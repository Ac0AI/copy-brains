## Master your mind: controlling the voice that wants comfort

David describes two voices in every head. One is the comfortable voice that offers "cookies and milk at nighttime" and says "you've earned this, you deserve this." He says that voice took him to 297 pounds, a loser's life and about $1,000 a month, and ran his first 26 years.

- He wants his mind to "know who's in charge." Once you take control, you make decisions for yourself instead of your mind making them for you.
- Every day he faces the pull toward "the path of least resistance," and says you don't get better by following it, you become normal.
- On bad days he talks himself out of quitting: "So you want to be normal, you just want to be like everybody else... being fine with being mediocre?"
- Example: on arriving in London he had no running route, so he used a hotel elliptical that powers itself. After two minutes he thought "I don't want to do this," watched his mind go there, redirected it, and stayed on for two hours and 45 minutes. He adds "I'm not saying do that."
- He frames it as owning the process, not hating it: "I don't enjoy it, it's necessary." He says growth only happens in the uncomfortable zone, so he visits it every day.

## Face your demons: go back into the dark hole and organize it

David says most people carry insecurities and issues they don't want to revisit, so they avoid them.

- His own turn came when he found the courage to "go back in there and organize that [stuff]," getting it ordered in his mind so he knew where to start.
- He compares his past to a collection of shells in a treasure chest: bad memories you hide because they remind you where you are. Once he started visiting them all, "I don't have any more shells." The flame of anger faded because there was no more fuel.
- He calls his childhood the "ultimate training ground" and says he is proud of it. Seeing the bad things that way changes how you see your past.
- Sources of pain he names: an abusive father, bullies, learning disabilities, stuttering, obesity, and repeated failure.
- His view on anger: it is powerful fuel but "can't overcome everything." He now asks what is left once the anger is gone.
- Self-knowledge process: get quiet, find the truth about who you are ("it may take months, may take years"), find your purpose, plan, then execute. He warns people love the planning phase because it is comfortable, and execution is where the real work, failure and failure again, begins.
- To do this he says to shut off technology, go dark, get away from people, and accept that being alone and even unhappy is okay.

## Own your weaknesses and build discipline from nothing

David had "zero self-discipline" and the military did not teach it to him.

- Starting around age 16, with a fourth-grade reading level and severe learning disabilities, he forced himself to sit at a table doing reading and writing. That discipline carried over to working out.
- He argues you must "triple down" on weaknesses to become a full human being. People run toward what they are good at (the runner only runs, the reader only reads) and avoid the rest. He dove into what he was bad at.
- He shares both strengths and failings openly and says owning both is why people relate to him.
- Small disciplines matter: in the military he folded shirts and made hospital corners on his bed, and says these small habits build the mind. He says the world has lost them (rushing, no customs and courtesies).
- He warns that technology makes everything easy, so people don't use "the most powerful weapon in the world, which is your mind," and then can't rely on it when things get hard.

## Daily routine: win the morning, prepare the mind

- David stretches for two to three hours every night. While stretching he plans the next day and the obstacles that could come up: "all things that could happen, might happen, probably will happen, and then the unknowns."
- He expects not to want to work out tomorrow and prepares his mind for it. His aim is a "mental armor" so life doesn't attack an unprepared mind.
- Winning the morning: make your bed, clean the house, eat breakfast, shower, shave, and don't hit snooze. Snooze means "you wake up already failing... behind the power curve."
- Don't grab your phone first thing, because bad news there starts your day out of control.
- Job-interview analogy: before an interview you lay out clothes, rehearse and bring your best self. After a few months you stop, and "the interview you is gone." Waking up like it's the interview keeps that standard.
- Winning the morning means you go into the world having already won something, so you can take the hits.
- Current methods beyond fitness: visualization, self-talk, deciding where he wants to be, making a plan "and I don't deviate from that plan," plenty of quiet time, and shutting out noise.

## The 40% rule and what suffering opens up

David describes his first 100-mile race, which he ran unprepared in 19 hours and 6 minutes (101 miles). He soiled himself, urinated blood, and had 30 miles left in the worst pain of his life.

- He says he "lived 100 years" in those 19 hours: all the highs, lows, trauma, failure and success.
- At mile 70, in terrible shape with 31 miles to go, he cracked open another part of the mind. His explanation, which he ties to his "40% room": when the mind knows it is not going to quit, "it opens up... it finds more focus, more power, more drive."
- The focus is heightened to a level few examine because few are willing to go to a place of fear, suffering, pain and doubt.
- He is not addicted to it and doesn't want to live there, but knows he can go there. The experience changes how he sees people, and he sees that others can go there too.
- He admires the free solo climber (he does not name him) not for the climb but for shutting out the whole world and being willing to sacrifice everything for a few hours of total commitment. He notes he is not speaking from personal knowledge of the climber.
- He says people label such athletes "crazy" or "special" as a "get out of jail free card" so they can stay in their comfort zone, when really they just found what they want.

## Failure, the cookie jar, and happiness

David says "failure is part of life" is a cliche when delivered by people not living it. He says he talks about failure because he is still failing today.

- You should set goals high enough that you might fail. He failed the pull-up world record attempt twice before getting it, and says everything he has done took three tries.
- Rocky analogy: watching a movie where someone keeps failing and finally wins makes you feel amazing, and he wants that feeling for himself. You can't get it without the failures. The satisfaction of success is "directly linked to the size of the failures."
- He starts to feel the success before he even gets there, and then puts that feeling in his cookie jar: "I'll come back and get you again" down the road. It can become addictive, to the point where you don't mind failing.
- His definition of happiness: overcoming yourself at all costs, reaching the point where "you don't care about being judged." You could be in a room of a million people who all hate you and not care, "not because you're angry at them" but because you know you have walked the walk and put yourself through hell.
- He says he is "the most peaceful person on the planet" because he is winning against the other voice in his head. His fun is bettering himself, though he also loves watching sports.

## Ignore other people's opinions, but keep an honest circle

David says most people fail because they fear what others think. Other people live in your head as "puppet masters."

- Example: the first recruiter he called about becoming a Navy SEAL looked at him at 297 pounds and said he wouldn't make it. David says negative people project their own limits onto you. He notes he was only the 36th African-American to make it through in over 70 years. He also admits he avoided the military as a kid because of what friends would think.
- Don't-care means not caring how people view how you walk, talk or dress. He says when you really don't care, "you become very, very dangerous."
- But you need a small, hand-picked support staff: "if your fiancée and your kids don't believe in you... you chose the wrong support staff."
- He wants honest, truthful, accountable people in his corner who will say "you're being a turd today, you're not getting after it," not people who "kiss your ass" or pat him on the back for waking up.
- He only respects people who work hard every day, and won't take advice from people who talk but don't grind. He also warns that advice often works for the giver: a publishing agent told him a self-published book would sell around 5,000 copies, and it sold a million copies in seven months.
- His rule for his circle: if you are not trying to go somewhere, he doesn't want to hear complaints. He wants solutions, not whining.

## Emotion, softness, and how he built the book and brand

- Emotion: you want to control your mind but you can choose when to let it out. At the VFW Americanism award, he cried for 58 seconds looking at his mom, and says that was a deliberate, earned moment. Emotional people focus on what they don't have, "pity party," and complaining produces no solution.
- Fear of public speaking: he still hates it, interviews and being on camera because of the stutter, and does it as a way of facing his fears. He prefers working alone.
- On society: he says the world has gotten "soft," with too little accountability and no acceptance that "everybody is not a winner." Toughness to him means accountability, not push-ups.
- No one is coming to save you: "the only person coming to save you is yourself."
- Business: "I'm not about money." Be authentic, have a real product (the product is himself, living what he preaches), and help people. Audiences see through fakers and the business is done. He wants to be for "the underdog."
- Book "Can't Hurt Me": he says it worked because he told the truth and was vulnerable as a "tough guy," which gave people permission. He says he is not the hero, the reader is. He was a practitioner, not a theorist, and the takeaways are basic and immediately usable ("just screw the light bulb in").
- Figures he cites: over a million copies sold in about seven months, number two behind Michelle Obama for 12 weeks, over 650,000 audiobook copies. The audiobook has him chiming in with extra stories and commentary between chapters read by a narrator.
- Best advice: his grandfather told him he wasn't going to amount to much "because of how you hang your head." He says the best thing you can do for someone is tell them the truth lovingly: "I'm watching you fail and I can't do it."
