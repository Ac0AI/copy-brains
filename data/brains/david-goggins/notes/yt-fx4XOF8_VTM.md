## Winning by default: why most people are weak

- David argues that "it's easy to be great nowadays cuz most [people] are weak."
- He says there is a big difference between 99% and 100%. He always thought he was giving 100% and wasn't, so he was only 1% away from the money, the life, and everything he dreamed of. That 1% is what people "sleep on": the morning they say "not today, maybe tomorrow."
- His tactic is to think about all the weak people taking a day off while he keeps working, and he says he "will come up real fast."
- During COVID, others used closed gyms and cancelled events as excuses. He says the people who kept training on a trail or at a playground pull-up bar "marched light years ahead."
- He calls this "maximizing minimal potential." If the average person has 10% potential and wastes nine of it, then using your 2% fully beats them. He says he was born with nothing but realized most people are lazy.
- Leaders who stop get passed. People rest, you don't, and you pass them.
- He says people look at him and see potential. He sees accomplishments, because he used every bit of what he had.

## Unbalanced life, front-loading, and having no finish line

- David says he decided about 23 years ago, when he called himself a "born loser," to go all the way in.
- He argues that to be in the top 1% you have to throw balance "in the wind." He tells his family: "I'll be home, but not today."
- He calls this front-loading. You grind early and bring your family fully on board at 40, 50, or 60, once you have made the money.
- He says an unbalanced mind does everything at a level others can't fathom. People see you grinding while they sit at a mediocre level, and you rise slowly.
- He is 47, a smoke jumper out of British Columbia, and says he has plenty of money but "ain't [enough]." He says there is "zero finish line."
- He compares himself to his bulldog, who will eat until she's dead: "I leave no scraps on the table for anyone to pick up."
- He says he wants to be "the hardest [person] ever lived." He doesn't know if he is, but putting it on the table keeps him closer to it.
- Whatever you think about, he says, "will become exactly who you are." Greatness starts in your head, and most people never put it on their list.

## Building belief, the unseen work, and the dog

- David says he failed the first 24 years of his life because he hadn't developed belief. He insists this is not "Sesame Street" talk like "believe in yourself."
- He builds belief through hours, days, and decades in the grind, and says he gained his "by going into the damn dungeon and staying there." He runs 100-mile and 200-mile races, breaks pull-up records, and jumps at 47 because he has to build belief at that level given the darkness he came from.
- When he fails, he goes back to the "belief jar" and pulls out the belief he built. It tells him he failed because he "didn't figure it out yet," not because he can't.
- He calls his daily work "the unseen work." Nobody knows what he actually does. People see a one-minute snippet, and on Instagram he dumbs things down because the truth would get him called a liar.
- He built an "insanely high pain tolerance" and a "dog" inside him, and says the pain, the lonely nights, and being unbalanced make you dangerous once you have the right people with you.
- He says belief can't come from a book or a motivational speaker.
- He says nothing in his journey needs changing: "I had to do all that to continue on with the belief I have to have to move forward every day." Everybody has a dungeon, some deeper than others.

## The one-second decision and trying one more time

- David's core advice is: "Try one more time."
- He failed SEAL training the first time, and also Ranger school, Delta selection, and a pull-up record, and got "really [messed] up" in smoke jumping training. He says look at where he is now.
- Hell Week is 130 hours, but he says it's the 450,000 seconds that make people fail. You can get 449,000 right and lose by one second.
- For a 100-mile run, he says not to think about 24 hours. Do the seconds. That is how much focus it takes in whatever you do.
- His own first 100-mile attempt was at about 300 pounds with no training, which he calls the dumbest thing he ever did.
- His advice to someone moving from 100 to 200 miles is to get scientific about recovery: "It's not so much the distance, it's the recovery." He did eight 100-mile runs in eight consecutive weekends.
- He gave a pull-up injury story: a doctor gave him a morphine shot in the palm of his hand and he kept going, and "that shot hurt like hell. For a second."
- On limits, he says they are all relative.

## Facing the old self, anxiety vs fear, and the tape recorder

- David says the 300-pound pest-control version of him never goes away. When he runs, the figure chasing him isn't a skinny shadow, it's that 300-pound man. He now turns around and faces it each morning and says, "I know you ain't going nowhere, but you have to control every decision you make in your life."
- He says it's not a permanent solution. The demons always want to break you back down, and that's why he keeps building belief.
- He wants to go to bed "net positive": "I'm in the black, not in the red." He may wake up negative, but before bed he puts the demon to bed.
- On fear, he says he studies it. Fear lasts a second (looking out of a plane at 15,000 ft), while anxiety, the day or two before a jump, lasts a long time. He studied parachutes, how they're packed, how they open, and the malfunction procedures, until he was "a genius at the [thing] I was afraid of" and the fear drifted away.
- His tape recorder trick for bad mornings: record how you feel when you wake up, then play it back and ask if it would get a friend off the couch, or whether his girl would sleep with him tonight. He says to listen to how weak and pathetic it sounds, then get up.
- Everything you say to yourself should energize you "to the core of your soul." The weak voice takes the path of least resistance, so smack it down fast. He says "even me" doesn't want to work that hard.
- He also described a "lab for my brain" where he does a "live autopsy," constantly dissecting why he stops short of the finish line. He says everyone has something holding them back, and you have to face your own shadow or hit the prison wall.
- He described the accountability mirror as a tool he used.

## The foxhole, empathy, and leading versus managing

- David's "foxhole mentality" says that when you go to war every day because you want to be great, you can't have losers from high school or college in your foxhole.
- He wants people who, when he's suffering, ask if he wants a banana, a drink, or coffee while he studies late, not people who give weak sympathy. When he's hungry they feed him, when he's tired they keep him up, and when he says he can't, they help him find a way and "never give me a way out."
- He gave the example of a woman who wanted to be a doctor and got stuck in anatomy. Her parents said she didn't need it, and now in her 40s she regrets it. A good foxhole partner would have said "I'll get you a tutor."
- He says there is a place for empathy "but not when you're trying to be great." He says "have about this much" of it, and staying in empathy too long ruins your team.
- A manager makes sure you clock in every day, and a leader knows you and doesn't baby you so you can become the best you can be. Teach your team thick skin, not empathy, because "it's not here to hurt you. It's here to help you."
- On joining a team as a lone wolf, he says he used to storm in with "let's go, and if you can't, [forget] you," and they resisted. Now he takes time to learn each person's ins and outs and what motivates them, then sprinkles seeds until "trees" grow in their brain. Each individual gets a different leader from you, and they follow because they trust you.
- He says the person who joins the lone dancer is the real hero, because they make it okay for everyone else. He says the first follower started his movement, not him.
- He says as the first to do something, you're setting the example and if you stop leading, no one follows. Leading "never ends."

## Discipline needs direction, goals, and a mission statement

- Asked by a 26-year-old who didn't know what he wanted and lacked discipline, David said you can't have discipline when you don't know where to put it. You must first have plans, goals, destinies, and a journey, and then the other questions fall into place.
- You won't find it at conventions or in a book. At around that age he sat down and thought about who he wanted to be and what that looks like.
- He told the man to go home, shut off the phone, the TV, and the world, and "tune into you."
- He tells people to write a mission statement covering where they see themselves in 1, 2, 3, 4, and 10 years. Don't live just to wake up, kiss the wife and kids, and punch the clock. On days you don't know why you're doing it, open it up. He says SEALs and Rangers have creeds for this reason.
- He says figure yourself out before attaching to a family, since if you're not your best self you give your family a half-assed person.
- When he runs out of goals, he goes through his "Rolodex" of what he hasn't done, and especially what he hasn't done at an elite level, and checks them off. He calls it a witch hunt for whatever makes you tick. Once you start "living civilized," he says, it's over, so keep the savage as long as you can.
- He says the goal is to be able to "operate without purpose at the highest level possible."
- Asked what goes on his headstone, he said a person "who just tried one more [time], every time." He says he's scraping dust because he front-loaded and lives with a paranoia that he's missing something.

## Overcoming setbacks and anchoring against burnout

- David's turning point was spraying for cockroaches at around 300 lb in a restaurant at 1 am, when he realized "this is the rest of my life" and didn't want to die a born loser. A recruiter told him he had a few months to lose 106 lb for the Navy SEALs. He says he "put everything on black, put everything on David Goggins."
- After a bad surgery on his leg he gave himself 24 hours to feel sorry for himself, then went back to the grind. He had a 3-month plan to find the best doctor. They sawed and reset his tibia and put in a plate, which normally takes 18 months to recover from. At 8 months he ran 100 miles, and he was jumping out of planes at 9 months.
- Asked what he'd do if he couldn't run, he said he would be the best cyclist on the planet.
- Before SEAL training's third Hell Week, which his instructor said was his last attempt, he went in with stress fractures and shin splints. He says that when you believe it's really your last shot, "you'll find inhumane [stuff]" in you and make it.
- For burnout, he uses words as mental anchor points, like climbing anchors that limit your fall to 10 to 12 feet. He says hope is not an anchor point and he doesn't live off hope. His rule: "One day you'll sleep, but not today."
- On a breakup and a divorce, he said to suck it up, stop the feelings, and move on if the person doesn't want you. He said you attract the same level you're at, and he was married three times while "in the dungeon." When he became who he wanted to be, he found a partner who also wanted to up her game.
- On marriage, he says he was honest with his wife for three years: "I'm a savage, this won't be fun, support me differently." Everything in the house has to be aligned and "you can't tightrope" it.
- For parents and moms who can't throw balance away, he says to use every free hour, such as when the kids and husband are asleep, and that if you don't, "you did not want it bad enough." He also says to give kids chores and responsibility to build mental calluses, since "if you don't teach them that, no one else is."
