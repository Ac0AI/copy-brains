## From addiction to ambition: how David lost and regained 106 pounds to chase SEAL training

- David joined the Air Force at 19, about 175 pounds, to become a pararescueman. He was uncomfortable in the water, hated it, and treated a sickle cell finding as "a way out." He quit training.
- After quitting he went from about 175 to about 300 pounds. He says he "started finding things that made me very comfortable," and that was the start of the downhill slide.
- He served about four years as an Air Force Tactical Air Control Party (TACP) airman, then got out and sprayed for cockroaches at about 297 pounds.
- A Discovery Channel show on Hell Week brought back how badly the water had messed with him mentally. His takeaway: when you run from something and don't handle it, it keeps bothering you. That pushed him to try to become a SEAL at 297 pounds.
- Recruiters hung up on him for about two weeks. He kept calling until he reached a Navy reserve recruiter, Steven Sal Joe, who "gave me a shot." "I was just looking for a shot."
- The requirement: lose 106 pounds in under three months.

## The weight-loss method: math, obsession and high-rep training

- David did the math: about 3,500 calories per pound, so stagnation was not an option. His mind "became very obsessed."
- He barely ate. He had a banana in the morning and otherwise "pretty much don't eat."
- At 300 pounds his legs hurt too much to run, so for the first roughly 50 days he rode a bike (even while watching TV) and spent a lot of time in the pool.
- He trained all day with extreme volume: 100, 200, 300, 400, 500 reps on one exercise. Example: about 10 lb on each side of a 45 lb bar, repped around 100 times, for a few sets. He credits this routine with keeping his skin tight, with little excess skin.
- He called the recruiter at odd hours (like 11 at night) to report workouts, and the recruiter at first did not believe he would do it.
- The cost: he went from 297 to 191 pounds and developed stress fractures before BUD/S even started, so he arrived "already broken."

## Failure, three Hell Weeks, and learning to stay in the fight

- First BUD/S attempt: stress fractures and double pneumonia, rolled back to day one, week one. Second attempt: he made it through Hell Week, but a fractured patella (suffered before Hell Week) kept him from keeping up, and he was rolled back again. He graduated on his third attempt, completing two Hell Weeks. He graduated August 10, 2001, in class 235, which lost a man.
- His old pattern: when something got hard in pararescue training, "the best thing to do... was just quit." He found an excuse, or lied (he blamed sickle cell).
- What changed in BUD/S: "the harder it was, the more it was challenged in my mind," so he had to find different ways to stay in the fight, and that made him tougher.
- He says he is happy he went through three Hell Weeks: "I actually needed it." Self-examination is something every person has to do, and Hell Week was one of the best places to do it.
- Earning the Trident did not satisfy him: "All I wanted was more... it started in Hell Week and it's still going now at 43."

## The first ultramarathon: Badwater, 101 miles on zero training

- Motivation: after Operation Red Wings, David knew the fallen men (Danny Dietz, Michael Murphy and Axelson through BUD/S or training) and was at military freefall school with Morgan Luttrell, Marcus Luttrell's twin brother. He had to give Morgan the news. The story moved him to raise money for the Special Operations Warrior Foundation, which covers 100% of college tuition for children of fallen special operators.
- At the time he was about 250 pounds and a powerlifter. He found the Badwater 135 (Death Valley, summer) and called the race director on a Wednesday.
- Qualification required running 100 miles in 24 hours or less. His resume (Ranger School, Air Force training, three Hell Weeks) did not matter, only that. A qualifying race was that Saturday, three days later. He had run about 20 miles in the whole year.
- Result: 101 miles in 19 hours 6 minutes on a one-mile track with "zero training."
- He says this is where he realized he was "scratching the surface of the human mind."

## Controlling your mind at mile 70: the step-by-step process

At mile 70 he was peeing blood, dizzy and lightheaded, in the worst shape of his life, past his previous furthest run of 26 miles. His process:

1. **Calm the emotion first.** "Emotion controls us, we cannot let emotion control us." He says his worst decisions came when his mind said "let's get the hell out of here, we're done, this sucks."
2. **Don't give yourself a way out right now, but let the mind believe it has a little control.** Tell yourself: "I might quit, but not now." He calls it giving your mind "a little cookie." If the mind knows you won't quit, it spazzes more.
3. **Fix the physical problem.** Dizzy meant hydration, salt, potassium and more nutrition. Then he stood up.
4. **Shrink the task.** He did not think about "30 more miles." He asked whether he could walk a mile, then two. At mile 81 his crew said he would not make the time, so he asked, "Let's see if we could run a little bit now" and ran the next 19 miles.
5. **Use the cookie jar.** After calming down you need something that fuels you and gets you out of the chair. He reflects on times just as hard as this, including his rough childhood (detailed in his book "Can't Hurt Me").

His broader insight: when we hit pain and suffering, "the wall gets bigger and bigger" and the mind says to quit. Finding ways around that wall is the work, and it took years of practice.

## Why he keeps doing it: leading by example and daily discipline

- Early on, the motivation was overcoming himself: he saw himself as weak.
- Now, at 43, he says it is about being a leader and setting the example. "I can't talk it and not live it." He will not talk about what he used to do; he has to still be that guy.
- Routine:
  - Runs every morning, no day off since December 2016. He wants to "win the battle in the morning."
  - Stretches two to three hours daily; missed about two days in roughly five and a half to six years.
  - Weights about four days a week ("you can't just be a runner").
- He calls this the "monotony" of his life and says it is how he builds discipline, while noting he is not telling everyone to copy it.

## Staying hard after the SEALs: "day one, week one" every day

- David served 21 years, retired, and says he misses nothing about the Navy because he left it all out there. Even on deployment, if the call came at 1 a.m., he was in the gym at 1:05 a.m. He also underwent two heart surgeries.
- He lived every day "like it was day one, week one of BUD/S," even after graduating and while recruiting and instructing, because even "the biggest baddest person in the world" gets comfortable once they finish something hard.
- Lessons he took from the Navy:
  - Teamwork is big, but you must be a good individual first and "triple down on your weaknesses." Your teammate may not be there, so be a jack of all trades.
  - Accountability: go back to boot camp in your mind. People are happy to leave boot camp and SEAL training, but those are what make you good. Over time people get comfortable, sag, and lose the "militant discipline."
  - Values and discipline are a muscle: "you stop going to the gym, stop running, you get weak." Keep up the discipline of whatever school or training shaped you.

## Advice on joining the military

- He thinks everyone should do at least two years in the military.
- Start with mindset, since everything in the military is about mindset. Most people only work on themselves "at a surface level." He urges going deep into your demons and what actually drives you.
- Handle your own problems before you enlist, because the military will expose them and push you to question why you're there. The military then "fine-tunes who you are."
