## Callousing the mind through deliberate suffering

- David's core idea is that the mind builds calluses the same way hands do: through repeated, deliberate exposure to pain. He calls it "callousing my mind through pain and suffering."
- At 16 he was an insecure, bullied kid in a small Indiana town, with a dad who "took our soul" and a mom working three jobs. No one helped him.
- That absence of help taught him the only person who could turn him around was himself, and the only way was "to put myself through the worst things possible a human being can ever endure."
- "To grow you must suffer." He argues most people reach a fork where left is easy and right is hard. Many take the easy route and live fine lives, but "the better life" is on the hard side, even if it costs 20 years of pain.
- He says suffering is "the true test of life," and that you cannot know who you are until it tests you.
- Example: he failed twice at the Guinness pull-up record and did 67,000 pull-ups in training before finishing 4,030.

## Why he runs what he hates: training for life, not races

- David says he hates running, and that this is the point. "I don't want to take showers and eat either. That's life."
- He argues the person who learns to do things he hates doing finds "greatness" on the other side. He was dumb in school because he hated school.
- He defines self-discipline as "the ability to not want to do it but still do it."
- He says he is not training for a race but for life: "I'm training for the time when I get that 2 o'clock in the morning call that my mom is dead." Body, mind and spirit are trained as one so he doesn't "fall apart."
- He says he did not like jumping out of airplanes or shooting guns as a SEAL, but chose to do it to change himself. If you choose a path, you may as well be the hardest person on it.
- Attitude on the door breach: "Open the [expletive] door and go in hard." He wants people to remember that he attacked, not that he "slowly opened it and peeked in."

## The accountability mirror

- He began shaving his head at 16 and found the mirror was the one person he couldn't lie to.
- In high school he had adopted many identities (saggy pants, pulled-up pants, different ways of talking) to fit in with groups. He calls that "pathetic."
- Every morning he reflected on lies he'd told and ways he acted to impress others, and asked "how did I attack today?"
- Tactic: if he did something he wasn't proud of, he wrote it on a sticky note and fixed it.
- Example: he wanted to sit at the cool lunch table. He wrote "[expletive] the table, sit by your [expletive] self." He did, and his table became a table people started sitting at, "because a whole bunch of people in that lunchroom felt exactly like I did."
- He says by senior year he was "a totally different David Goggins."
- Core point: "We write our own book every day, but we never read it." Read it honestly and own the flaws ("I'm a dumb [expletive]. Okay, Roger that. How do you get smarter? Educate yourself.") instead of blaming his dad, bullies or his health.

## The cookie jar

- The cookie jar is a mental store of past failures he turned into wins: failing, going back, finally succeeding, plus things that "kicked my ass."
- In suffering, he says even the hardest people "forget how hard we really are."
- When he feels the "poor me" mentality, he takes "one second," steps out of his life, and pulls something from the jar. Example: "You were in three hell weeks and finished," including one where a man died.
- Then he puts it back and remembers who he really is: not the kid called the n-word, not the scared kid.
- He says self-talk and visualization are the two keys to his success.

## His first ultramarathon: breaking 30 miles into pieces

- Setup: he'd never run a marathon. He wanted to raise money for the Special Operations Warrior Foundation after Marcus Luttrell's team died, and Googled the hardest races. He chose Badwater 135 (135 miles through Death Valley in summer).
- The race director said he had to qualify by running 100 miles in 24 hours. The qualifier was four days later. He was around 240-270 lb and hadn't run in over a year.
- He reached mile 70 in about 12-13 hours, then broke down: fractured shins, tearing muscles, peeing blood, unable to stand. He had been eating Ritz crackers and Mylanta, with tube socks and no water plan.
- His method: he was "driven," not "motivated" ("motivation comes and goes. When you're driven, whatever's in front of you will get destroyed"). He broke the last 30 miles into small steps: get nutrition, stand up, walk.
- At mile 81 his wife told him he wouldn't make the time. He ran the last 19 miles nonstop.
- He taped his ankles and shins with compression tape to get through. He says he first used this in his third Hell Week, taping from ankles to calves every morning for six months because of stress fractures.
- He says it became "personal": him against the race, against the kids who mocked him, against himself.
- Takeaway: "Your brain and your body once connected together can do anything."

## Using the dark side as fuel

- He says "Goggins" is a character who can take anything. Hell Week is designed to find your flaws, and he used rage to get through.
- He wanted instructors to go home after beating him and feel worse than he did, thinking about him still suffering.
- He insists this is not Kryptonite: "I'm using it for strength. I just flip negative into positive."
- He says he is "the happiest man on the planet" and that facing what's on the other side of the door made him happy. He warns against being overtaken by it.
- In the worst moment of the race he used the negatives as motivation: "Who the hell would even get out of that chair? You did."
- He swears on purpose: he says he can't convey Hell Week by saying it "was really hard," and that tap-dancing around the truth to protect "thin skin" stops people learning from you.
- On insults: "They don't own your life." Flip it, harness it, and thicken your skin.

## Finding your why and living inside yourself

- Advice to a lost young person: you live in an external world, so turn "yourself Inside Out." Stop listening to people calling you names; their insecurities are theirs.
- You won't find greatness in a book or in David's talk. He may give the spark, but you must be quiet, "shut the [expletive] up," search your soul and ask "why am I here on this planet?"
- First question he'd ask a kid: "Who are you at the core of your soul?" If he can't answer, "our conversation's over."
- He says greatness is not 200-mile runs or 4,000 pull-ups; it's whatever you dreamed up in your own mind. First create the vision, then ask how to get there.
- He calls most of this generation "softened," quitting the moment they're criticized, and says that makes it "easy to be great."
- Daily regimen: stretches two hours, doesn't drink or go out, oatmeal, run, weights, and keeps a very small circle of trusted people.
- Rocky I round 14 was a mental image for him, and he listened to one song on repeat for 17 hours during a pull-up record attempt.
