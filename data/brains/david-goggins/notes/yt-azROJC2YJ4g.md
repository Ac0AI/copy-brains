## The accountability mirror and owning your own story

- David's central premise is that "the most important conversation is the one you have with yourself." You wake up with it, walk around with it, go to bed with it, and eventually act on it, so the internal dialogue has to change before anything else does.
- The accountability mirror started when he was a junior in high school. His mom found his report cards after he had been hiding that he was flunking most of his classes, and she told him he was done.
- It was not a fix. It was a starting point for holding himself accountable.
- Being honest with himself meant admitting character flaws: "I've lied about this, I've cheated here, I'm insecure here."
- He had to stop building on a "fucked-up foundation" and get down to "the actual mineral soil of who I was naturally" before real growth started.
- Reframing vulnerability: he calls being vulnerable the one thing that made him who he is. Breaking himself down to rock bottom and telling people who he is became strength, not softness.
- He self-published so he could own 100% of his story. He did not want anyone telling him what he could or could not say about what he suffered.
- Many people are going through the same things and "just hide better than you did." Stop hiding and people can understand you.
- He is not here to judge. He says the problem is that people die with untapped potential because they think someone else is better than them.

## Journaling, the cookie jar, and the schedule

- Journaling: for several years he has kept small green military notebooks and records his day, then goes back and studies it.
  - It works as an accountability journal for physical, mental, and eating habits. He calls himself "a lab rat" who studies himself.
  - When he feels off or overtrained, he checks last week's entries to find where he messed up, such as sleep or time.
  - The journal has to be honest. "You cannot put bullshit in there."
- External accountability: circles of people matter at first. If you are a drunk, get away from the people who keep you drinking. Build a nucleus of people who say it's not okay to sleep in today.
  - The goal is to reach the point where you hold yourself accountable and those people fade, because "they're not always gonna be there to hold your hand."
- The cookie jar: in hard times even the hardest person forgets how badass they are.
  - He reaches into a mental jar of past wins: surviving three Hell Weeks, Ranger School, a brutal childhood, and seeing people die.
  - It reminds you that you can get through the current hard moment, because bad times consume the mind and make you forget who you are.
- The schedule: his answer to "I don't have time" is that you have not prioritized yourself.
  - Write down everything you do in one day. He says people discover they waste hours on phones, Instagram, texting and tweeting.
  - Everyone can find an hour a day. After that it comes down to willingness.
- His own schedule: his fiancée Jennifer builds it backward from fixed events. A 7 a.m. event an hour away means he is up by 4 a.m. to run, and sometimes 1:30 a.m. He prefers 7 to 8 hours of sleep but says the schedule sometimes allows three.

## Visualization, a new norm, and staying present

- Visualization: he says he can "see the end before the beginning even begins."
  - Before BUD/S he pictured himself walking across the stage to get his graduation certificate at 191 pounds, and replayed that feeling many times a day.
  - That imagined feeling made the suffering feel worth it. For a race he rehearses how he will feel at mile 50, so when it arrives "it's no surprise."
- Compartmentalizing: for the ASVAB he first needed a 50 on mechanical comprehension (his earlier score was 44). He then had to lose 106 pounds in a limited time.
  - He handled one task at a time. The weight did not matter unless he first passed the test.
  - He got exactly a 50. He describes it as putting everything he had on one bet, like pushing his last chips onto the table.
- Focus: he stays fully present with whoever or whatever is in front of him. He says multitasking means half-assing everything, and that being focused on the task at hand is a huge advantage today.
- Set a new norm: while repeating the first phase of BUD/S, he reset his mindset. "Your new norm is you wake up at 4 o'clock and you suffer."
  - Once suffering is the baseline, the hard thing stops feeling hard.
  - He applies the same logic to running: a two hundred mile run becomes the new norm.
- Day one, week one: he lives by that mentality, but says he made the mistake of trying to put it on people who did not want it.

## Taking souls and finding energy when there is none

- Taking souls means finding the tactical advantage in a hard situation, not hurting the person you are up against.
- David's reasoning: everyone has demons, and "the most alpha males are very fragile." Ego is serious. He grew up with a fragile ego and did a "live autopsy" on himself, so he figured it could be used in the instructors' heads.
- The Hell Week example: the boat crew was freezing, shaking, and jackhammering with cold. Instructors enjoyed watching them suffer.
  - He asked himself what he would hate to see if he were an instructor: students acting like it was just another day on the beach.
  - He told his boat crew (Chris Kyle was on it) they were going to "take some souls." They threw the boat overhead and caught it, and yelled while doing it.
  - He says the instructors' faces looked "numb," and the crew went on to win nearly every race.
- The takeaway: it is about finding energy and strength when you have none.
  - Energy does not have to come from outside, like a TV, podcast, or music. A lot of life is quiet, and those are the moments you want to run and hide.
  - You have to make up games and tricks to reach the next evolution, to stay in the fight until the second wind arrives.
- A related example: on Wednesday night of Hell Week, instructor SBG sent him alone to swim out through the surf zone at night with no swim buddy. He took it as his chance to show him and did it. SBG later told him he knew David would treat it as a challenge.

## Motivation, obsession, and why anger is not fuel

- Anger can start you off, but it is not sustainable. David says anger was never his fuel.
  - It got him to look at things and say he wanted to be somebody, and it built big ego chips.
  - When he was suffering and at wit's end in cold water, the anger was gone. He was thinking only about getting out of there.
- Motivation is only a spark, like the sparks that start California wildfires. A bit of water puts it out.
  - Kindling grows only if you keep feeding it. He wants it to become a big log that burns long.
  - Motivation has to become drive, passion, and obsession for what you want to become. He calls that "an inferno."
- Obsession is self-generated, cultivated deep inside. Someone else cannot fuel you into it.
  - He calls "obsessed" a compliment, though it can ruin your life.
  - To be great at something you may be misunderstood by everybody. "You've arrived when you are misunderstood to the point where people think you're psycho."
- On balance: balance matters for many people, but going to the edge takes every second of your life.
- On January 1 resolutions: he says the kindling usually burns out within 10 to 12 days. He stopped giving people workout programs because they want a program or a watch instead of getting the shoes and running.
  - His challenge is to see how far you can take it past 12 days. "There is no hack."

## Staying uncomfortable, avoiding complacency, and judging yourself only against yourself

- Sacrifice and discomfort: the culture pushes the idea that happiness comes from luxury, comfort, and ease. David says self-knowledge, self-esteem, and peace come through sacrifice and getting uncomfortable.
  - He says people want results in two days, and there are real plateaus, so patience matters.
  - Getting up when it's snowing and going to the gym is "miserable," and that is the point.
- Judging yourself: the biggest stumbling block is the "woe is me" mentality. People look left and right and judge themselves against others.
  - "We are judging ourselves against too many people." You have to race against yourself. He says the race is "David Goggins and David Goggins alone."
- Suffering strips away layers. In misery your internal dialogue is unfiltered and has no lies. That is who you really are.
  - Forcing yourself to stay in it builds calluses on your mind, and that is where growth happens.
  - His first hundred-mile race (Badwater) came with zero training. He says he lived five years in 19 hours of highs and lows. He did not quit, and it broke him down to nothing.
- Complacency: becoming civilized is, in his words, "the worst thing that can happen to a person." You get to the point where you believe you have arrived.
  - At 43 he cannot go as hard as at 20, but there is a new bar you must always set.
  - He keeps that bar high by returning to where he came from. He compares it to going back to the fundamentals in basketball.
  - He pays rent mentally on the $7-a-month place where he grew up.
- Going back to scratch: he is working as a wildland firefighter, which he says means being a nobody again on a fire line. He values being nobody and likens it to Ranger School, where titles are removed.
- He distrusts people who talk about what they used to do. He wants to know what you are doing today. "I hear so much talk, I don't hear a lot of work."

## Peace, purpose, and the fear of failing the mission

- Peace: he says he never found peace until he got really sick.
  - Peace comes from clarity, and there is no clarity when there is noise.
  - He found it by looking back at the dungeon he came from. "The greater the suffering, the greater the peace I've found."
- Taking a vantage point: inside a grinding schedule you need a point (he says 12 o'clock) to reset and survey what you have accomplished.
  - He and his fiancée now stop to appreciate what they have done. They were so busy grinding they lost sight of it.
  - If you do not step back, you never apply what you learned. He says he would have handled the second platoon differently if he had.
- Overtraining: after two heart surgeries and a return to ultras, he overtrained and his thyroid and endocrine system broke down. It lasted about five years, starting around 2013.
  - He calls it one of his hardest and most eye-opening times. He could not run or train, so he went to school and became a medic.
  - He was always pursuing his "hundred percent," whatever form it took.
- His father: he found peace by going back to where it started and forgiving a man who hated himself. His father never said sorry, and they last spoke when David was 22. He died four or five years before this interview.
- Being nonjudgmental: it had to be learned the hard way. He used to want to fit in and "swore" he wanted people to like him, which he says makes you lose yourself.
  - Now: "take a number and get in line."
- His faith: he believes in God and describes a voice in his head since he was a young kid that guided him.
  - He sees his past as the perfect training ground, with every domino lined up.
- His biggest fear: getting to heaven and being shown a chart of who he was supposed to be, and having under-achieved against it.
  - He wants whatever force knows what he is capable of to say it had "no idea he even had this in him."
