## The accountability mirror: face the raw truth, then fix one thing at a time

- David's accountability mirror started in his junior year of high school. He had a "1 point something GPA," missed lots of school, lied to his mom, and read at a third-grade level, which he hid by copying on everything for fear of being put in a "special" school.
- He looked in the bathroom mirror and called himself out honestly: dumb, lazy, fat, a fake "cool guy." He says he "didn't call myself out. I was just honest."
- Each day he asked: "What am I going to do today to change what I see in this mirror?"
- He argues that if you aren't "real and raw" about who you are, nothing changes. Soft language ("you're just a little big") keeps people stuck.
- Early actions were small: he stopped sitting at the cool-guy table, tucked his shirt in, and wore a "callous layer of skin" against whatever others said.
- The same honesty applied when he got fat again as an adult: "I called myself fat because I was fat."
- He says the biggest conversation of your life is the one you have with yourself: "You wake up with it, you walk around with it, you go to bed with it. Eventually, you're going to act on it."

## Total accountability and one-problem-at-a-time goal setting

- Accountability is a daily grind, not a five-step formula. He says "It's a practice... it's a habit."
- He says the journey is a "race that you run completely alone." Social media and comparison are poison, because they show "who we want to be, not who we are."
- His mirror was covered in sticky notes: year goals, weekly goals, daily goals, hourly goals. Notes compared him to his own yesterday, never to other people ("be better than John" was not on them).
- Example: he lied constantly as a kid to be accepted. His goal became "go one day without lying." When he lied, he went back to the person and said "I lied to you."
- Progress is patient and small. Losing three or four pounds counts as a "huge accomplishment."
- He says he runs before interviews because accountability means doing what he preaches: "Are you accountable for what you're doing? ... to the T."
- He wants people to "put your life on a billboard on the busiest highway in the world" and own how messed up they used to be.

## The list of excuses and the "bag of..." work: accept it before you fix it

- In the book he has people list everything working against them, every valid excuse and flaw. Reason: "You have to first accept it before you can fix it."
- Many people never start the journey because they live a fake life as who they want to be and haven't fixed the real problems.
- Once you fix these, you "no longer care about how people are judging you." Caring about judgment means no forward momentum.
- Method: don't stare at the whole list. Take the first item and make it a focal point, then strategize that one problem. Repeat until the list is done.
- Example, his learning disability: he can't retain information, so he built a method around his weaknesses. For his thousand-page dive manual he got the book 18 months before dive school, bought spiral notebooks from a dollar store, and wrote each page about 10 times. By test time he could recall "okay, page 71 was where I remember seeing this."
- He says to avoid trying to learn "like you" or "like anybody else." Find your own strengths and weaknesses and design around them.

## The 40% rule and the governor

- David believes most people live at about 40% of their capability. The mind has a "governor," like a car rated for 130 mph but limited to 91 at the factory. The car wants to go, but the governor stops it.
- It is a survival mechanism that protects you from pain and suffering. When it gets uncomfortable, the mind says back off, sit down, find something comfortable.
- The mind "has the tactical advantage" over you at all times because it knows your fears, insecurities and "deep, dark lies," and steers you toward comfort.
- Practical use:
  1. Go until the mind says stop. You must get to that point first.
  2. At that moment, take control back and ask: "Let me see if I can go 45%."
  3. Keep giving yourself a little more. The mind pushes back ("we're supposed to be going right and you're going left"), and you keep controlling it.
- He says there's no tidy formula: "That's all [BS]... It's a practice... it's a habit." He thinks experts who tell you your limits are less useful than people practicing beyond them.

## Creating the alter ego: the dark room and "Goggins"

- First, you must be unhappy with how you feel about yourself and stop running from the inner voice that says you're "wimping out." He calls the white lies we tell ourselves "okay" a trap.
- He faces the real David: a kid who stuttered and is still afraid of stammering on a show like this one.
- In the "dark room" you face who you are and then create another person to walk out. "Not like you have two different personalities. It is you. But you have to find strength."
- That person is "Goggins": someone who doesn't care about being judged, knows he's weak, knows he's afraid, and says "whatever you think about me, take it."
- He describes cracking out Goggins "like that Superman cape."
- David frames Goggins as a visualization tool, not a split personality.

## Turning insecurity, trauma and "no one is coming to save me" into fuel

- David's background: a father who beat him, a stepdad who was murdered, a mother who couldn't coddle him, being called a racial slur at school, a learning disability, and weighing 300 lb working as a pest-control guy.
- Key realization: no one was coming to save him. When the school principal waved off the slur ("they're just ignorant"), he saw he was on his own.
- His reframe: "I had to use all this negative [stuff] that was making me weak... as the power that now fueled me... The darkness is exactly what I need."
- Others start up high with a good foundation. He started with "no legs" and had to grow them, so surpassing them felt bigger.
- On passion: "We're all looking for passion. Passion is all around you... It's your insecurities." At 300 lb he had no drive to be a Navy SEAL. He found drive in his own fear and doubt, and says "when you can find drive in your own doubt, fear, insecurities, you become very unstoppable."

## Detest mediocrity, don't get civilized, and keep callousing the mind

- Mediocrity is "contagious." People surround themselves with others who tell them what they want to hear. David's example: someone who says "I don't feel good, take a day off, get pizza and watch the game" feels good; someone who says "get your gear on, we're running at 3:30" feels like a threat.
- Being the best of the mediocre people feels great, but it means you're still mediocre.
- Staying hungry: he doesn't sit on past accomplishments. Reaching the top of the mountain means "you got to build another one." Many people with a good resume are "one-timers."
- "Don't get civilized": comfort and retirement make people go soft (he cites retired athletes who let themselves go). He says you never retire from setting the example, because people watch you.
- Daily work: he still has to stretch for two and a half hours after the interview and hates it. "I'm constantly callousing over my victim's mentality... If you stop shooting the gun, you're not going to be a great shot." You must keep challenging the machine every day.
- He says he gains knowledge through suffering, and on the other side is "a world that very few have ever seen." At hour 19 of a 100-mile race he "found a whole another world."
- He doesn't listen to "theorists" but to people who say "I'm tired because tomorrow I got to do it again."

## Outwork the chart: the feeling he's chasing

- His motivation isn't trophies, love or followers. "I'm in search for a feeling": true victory for himself alone, and he doesn't care if anyone knows how hard he works. He says he lives an introverted life.
- If he learns someone is grinding harder, "I'll make sure that I up my game."
- Visualization he uses: when you die, a higher power has a chart of who you were meant to be. His chart said he'd be a SEAL at 185 lb and among the smartest people. He wants to arrive and have that power say "I didn't see this," so his job is to "outwork the chart."
- Regret test: you could look back and think "I could have lived a much better life if I just would have suffered a little bit more," held back by fear and the 40%.
- At the VFW Americanism award, thanking his mother, he nearly broke down. The feeling wasn't money, fame or awards, but "I worked myself so hard that I turned a person this [messed] up into this right here... off of sheer will."
- He says everyone has a personal "equation" (like pi is 3.14) and his book, *Can't Hurt Me*, helps you solve for yours. His goal is for people to stop being ashamed of what life made them, stop judging themselves against others who "have hidden it better," and see that "life is one big head game."
