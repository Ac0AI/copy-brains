## The war inside: who you are versus who you want to be

David says the real fight is internal. At 24 he was overweight, bullied, abused and hiding it, and he points to that guy as the baseline he fights every day.

- Change is not permanent. "I wake up every day" and go through the steps again to become Goggins.
- He asked himself who he wanted to be: "a guy that can endure... whenever he doesn't, he does."
- He painted that picture in his mind, then looked in the mirror each day until he felt like that man. The standard was how he felt, not how he looked.
- His advice: get off social media, stop measuring yourself against others, spend time alone figuring out what you want to be, and be that person.
- People change clothes, houses and partners but "most of us just don't change how we think." He decided to grow the day he chose to go after Navy SEAL training, and calls it a painful decision because the effort felt unbearable to comprehend.
- Dreams are not enough. "Dream big. But don't make dreams your master." The change happens when you put work into the dream. He had no mentor; if you fail, he says, own it and don't blame anyone.

## The 40% rule and the mental governor

David built the 40% rule out of his first 100-mile race.

- The governor is like the speed limiter on old cars: at a set speed the car stops giving more. Our brains do the same, except "we are the factory" and we install the limiters ourselves.
- The more pain, suffering and toxic experience in a life, the more it pushes you back to that governor.
- Release it by getting to a place that is uncomfortable, where the brain says "we're good," and then holding on a little longer.
- Proof: two weeks after the 100-miler he could barely walk, tried a quarter-mile run two days before a marathon and couldn't. When the gun went off he "took off like a bat out of hell" and ran a 3:08 marathon that qualified him for Boston.
- He says this capacity is in everyone, and many people in the audience think they can't because of limits they put on themselves. He asks readers of *Can't Hurt Me* to remember the first 125 pages: the kid with no self-esteem who lied, cheated and made excuses.

## The 101-mile race: pressure release valve and small stages

David describes his first ultra, a 24-hour track race on a one-mile loop he entered cold to qualify for Badwater 135.

- Setup: after Navy SEAL training (three Hell Weeks) he was a heavy lifter whose cardio was 20 minutes a week on an elliptical. He had days to prepare, ran with no nutrition or electrolyte plan, and ate Ritz crackers and Myoplex. He was about 225-230 lbs.
- At mile 70 he sat in a lawn chair, dizzy, with low blood pressure and unable to stand, and was soiling himself and urinating blood. He calls his ego "busted."
- Pressure release valve: when the mind is full of clutter, he told himself "I'm going to quit." That relieved the tension, but in the back of his mind he knew he wasn't leaving. He says he is not recommending this, but it worked for him.
- Then he took stages: clean up first, eat real food (peanut butter and jelly, Rice Krispies treats, water, Gatorade), then "one mile," then "just one more."
- At mile 81 his wife told him he would miss the cutoff if he kept walking. He ran the final 19 miles averaging a 10-minute mile and finished 101 miles in 19 hours and 6 minutes.

## Rebuilding from 297 pounds: the SEAL decision

David explains how he went from the cockroach-spraying job to Navy SEAL training.

- After the Air Force, he went from 175 to 297 pounds in about 18 months, depressed. He hid his weight from his best friend, who thought he had become a pararescueman. Living a lie, he says, haunts you if you have any self-esteem.
- His routine: a 42 oz chocolate milkshake, mini chocolate donuts, and at his mom's house eight cinnamon rolls, half a dozen eggs, almost a pound of bacon and cereal.
- A TV show on SEAL training (class 224) ended with the officer saying "We live in a society where mediocrity is often rewarded. These 18 men appear to test mediocrity." David: "I'm not even mediocre."
- Recruiters laughed at him. Recruiter Steven Saljo weighed him at 6'1" and told him he had about 3 months to lose 106 pounds. David said "Watch" and gave up the milkshake.
- The turning point came while spraying a restaurant: a dropped brownie, a wand pulled by his own arm, and roaches falling from the ceiling tiles. He walked out to the truck and quit.
- He recalls losing the weight (106 pounds in about two and a half months) as the hardest thing he ever did: no one cooked for him, he made $1,000 a month and worked relentlessly.

## Ego versus confidence

- David likes confidence, not ego. "Ego almost got me killed several times."
- Confidence means "I know what I know." It comes only from putting in time, work and effort, especially the unseen work.
- Ego is what you have when you haven't done the work. He says he was a Navy SEAL who had never run 100 miles and believed he could, so the race buried that ego.
- On self-esteem: telling kids "you're great" does nothing "if you don't put the time in and the work in."

## Leadership: "Who's going to carry the boats?"

David tells the origin story during Hell Week with a boat crew that included Chris Kyle.

- Boat crews have six men. The heaviest part is the front, so the strongest go there and the weakest at the back. David always took the front: "When you lead, you must carry the heaviest load of the group."
- One teammate kept "ducking the boat" so the weight rested on others' necks. David talked to him repeatedly, then asked whether he really wanted to be there. The man said no.
- David's lesson on teamwork: you talk to someone until you realize there is no more talking to do; they just don't want to be there.
- When David moved him to the front spot, the man tried to turn around and ask him to come back up. David refused: "I've been up there for 72 hours."
- When the man quit, the crew's energy left with him. David returned to the front and yelled, "When you leave, who's gonna carry the boats and the logs?" The crew got back under the log.
- A leader must look for the moment to re-energize people when things fall apart. "Be that guy that wants to stay when it's minus zero and snowing."
- He says the energy of his stage persona is the same one that comes out of him in the pain cave, and criticizes speakers who don't live what they preach.

## Advice for athletes: a war mindset

David's answer to a track athlete who wanted to raise her teammates' intensity:

- Frame it as a mindset: go to the limit "where someone's life ends and yours begins." In a race, you hear a rival's breath fade as you pull away, and that is taking their soul.
- Motivation is not enough. Give the team facts. Make them work so hard that they step to the line knowing nobody else has done what they did today.
- Look left and right at the start and think: "You don't even know who you're dealing with."
- Extra work must be visible to teammates for them to follow.
