## The "calloused mind": rebuilding yourself by choosing the path of most resistance

David says mental toughness is "not something that you sample, it's something that you live in every day." He treats the mind like a muscle that can be lost: "if you can lift 315 pounds and you stop going to the gym for a month, I guarantee you won't be able to pick the same weight up again." The knowledge he has gained disappears if he stops practicing it.

- He describes building a "callous" on the brain the way hands callous from work. Hard, repeated effort becomes part of your DNA instead of a one-time feeling.
- He distinguishes the physical standard from the mental one. At the SEAL prep program he trained recruits to be "bigger, stronger, faster," yet the same number quit at BUD/S. The standard that matters in life is a mental one: "you must know how far you've come."
- When hardship hits, people retreat to their insecurities and look for comfort, "that cookie that your mom used to give you." David says that is the moment to retrain your mind to think differently.
- He contrasts the four-lane highway with road maps, gas stations and a Cracker Barrel every ten miles against the harder route. He says the easy road is "doom" because it only buys a quick fix, while the hard road gives permanent results: "you keep a six-pack forever because you know the work that goes into it."
- He says the mind must be given no way out. Starting SEAL training broken, his only way out was death, and "when the mind gives it no way out, your body says okay, I believe you now."

## Morning "armor" routine and daily discomfort

David says life does not hand out "get out of jail free" cards, so he builds protection before the world gets to him.

- Mornings: wakes before the day starts, does at least an hour and a half of running, then goes to the gym for pull-ups, push-ups and calisthenics. The first five hours of his day are uncomfortable on purpose.
- He does not love running: "People think I love to run. I don't. That's why I do it." He knows he will do something he dislikes every morning, and it changes how the rest of the day goes.
- Rationale: once you leave your house, "the war starts." Phone calls, social media and disappointments arrive. If you hit snooze, run late and leave a messy house, life has already beaten you before it hits you. If you have already won the morning, you face disappointments "already in victory."
- He trains at the hardest moment on purpose: after a long run, dehydrated and low on sodium, he goes straight to the gym, because "life's not going to give you a glass of water when you're thirsty."
- At night he stretches two to three hours. He has missed only two days in about five and a half years. He says it lengthens the body and opens the mind, and it is his time to recharge. He thinks about nothing.
- He says he lives around the 90th percentile, sometimes 99, because "100 is dead."

## The 40% rule and never "arriving"

David says when you feel you have arrived, you start dying.

- After losing 106 pounds in under three months, learning to read and graduating, he realized he was "leaving so much in the tank." That is where his 40% rule came from. He says most people operate at about 40% of their potential.
- On finishing events: he used to skip the medal and ceremony and move straight on. He later learned to reflect, because he had never acknowledged how far he had come.
- His third "truth" at the end: when you finally get where you wanted to go, "be afraid." You are either getting better or getting worse. After summiting Everest, "the best thing to do is fall back down that damn mountain as fast as you can and start climbing."
- His biggest fear is getting civilized and comfortable, because "God starts to die." He wants to stay the creature "that can live in a sewer, that can eat rats, that doesn't need water."
- He insists he is not special: "There was nothing special about him. He wasn't talented, he wasn't gifted... and he made it." Labeling him as crazy or unique lets people off the hook from doing the work.

## Confidence, fear and self-examination

- Real confidence comes from hard work and facing what scares you, not from places that feel good: "You don't gain confidence by going to the spot that makes you feel good."
- His own example: years at a kitchen table learning to read and write. He had to write everything repeatedly in spiral notebooks, and a single math problem could take ten hours. That is where his work ethic came from: "I am dumb, but I have the work ethic to catch you." He says the notebooks were empty and three months later they were full.
- He stutters severely and says his confidence comes from no longer caring if he stutters. Facing it is the point.
- He calls the process a "live autopsy": examining yourself honestly. Billionaires call him saying they are still missing something, because they did what they were good at and never examined themselves.
- Fear is no longer a word he uses. It is "my ultimate guide" to where he is supposed to be, and anything that triggers it gets conquered.
- Own your insecurities. If you're fat, call yourself fat; if you're not smart, say so. Then use that as the starting point. Stop speaking "hollow words" and staying on the surface.
- He ties maturity to this. Self-examination gives you a throttle: he rates a provocation (a level-two or level-three engagement) instead of escalating by default, unlike the old him who would pick a fight.

## Suffering, stress fractures and the 100-mile race

- Before BUD/S he repeatedly failed and returned. For his third Hell Week he went in with stress fractures and double pneumonia. Each morning he duct-taped his feet from forefoot to mid-calf, put on two black socks and ran. The first 45 to 60 minutes were excruciating. Three months later the fractures had healed, and he says he has not had one since (he ran 7,000 miles in 2007).
- His motivation: if he could graduate starting broken among the "studs," it would change everything. "Life is one big mind game, and you're playing it with yourself. Is it true? I don't care. It got me through."
- He warns against copying this: "I'm not saying do what I did." The point is to be uncomfortable, not to injure yourself and be out for six months.
- His first 100-mile race: under-trained, four days' notice, hadn't run more than about 20 miles that year, raised money for a foundation. At mile 70, with 30 left and no one able to save him, he pulled tactics out of "the cellar of your mind." Afterward he lay in the tub, blood and brown fluid in his urine, and felt he had "cracked the code to human potential."
- Years later he got sick, had to pull out of Badwater (135 miles) at mile 50, and could not run a quarter mile. He plans to return and says he is only now in shape to go back.

## Delayed gratification, perspective and going back to "scratch"

- He values the years, not the finish: "It's not the race, it's the 12 years." Chasing one second at the finish line leaves people angry and moving to the next goal without reflecting. Perfectionism is "the worst thing that can happen to a person."
- Perspective: he works as a wildland firefighter at 43, digging fire line (18 inches wide, three miles long, $12 an hour) beside 27-year-olds. A 21-year-old coworker looked at the mountain and his hands and decided a pair of running shoes cost too many hours of work. David keeps that story as his reminder of where he came from.
- Going back to "scratch": the further you climb, the smaller your friction gets, because you're not hungry and the fridge is full. Friction creates growth, so he visits discomfort deliberately: "Take a day trip. Don't stay there."
- Reflection: he keeps a "cookie jar" self-talk, a constant reminder of everything he's done. He says growth needs quiet, since the world is too noisy to find your purpose.
- Greatness as he defines it: being haunted, years after a career ends, by every shot you missed because your mind drifted. It is "being so aware of the time of life... and not making that same mistake again."

## Lessons from his father, mother and the "three truths"

- Background: he describes an alcoholic father who abused him, his brother and his mother, with constant mental warfare. He grew up dyslexic, with a learning disability, a tutor and cheating, and faced racism, including graffiti on his car and a KKK presence ten minutes away in the mid-1990s. His mom worked multiple jobs and never saw a report card.
- From his mom: she never picked him up when he failed ("she never gave me that cookie"). Her stance was effectively "life sucks." He read that as a training ground and began choosing the path of most resistance.
- From his dad: "The biggest lesson he taught me was how not to be." Insecure people tear others down, and a fulfilled person only builds others up. He judges no one who bullies him because he knows where it comes from.
- His three truths:
  1. You are your own hero. Idolizing others leads to mimicry and disappointment, so hold yourself fully accountable.
  2. Never pick the easy road.
  3. When you feel you've arrived, be afraid; find the next climb.
