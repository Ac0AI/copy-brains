## From fat exterminator to Navy SEAL: the 300-pound starting point

- David tried to become an Air Force pararescueman and quit "for the fear of the water." He left the Air Force four years later at 300 pounds, up from 175.
- He worked for Ecolab spraying for cockroaches, earning $1,000 a month, and weighed 300 pounds at six foot one.
- He read a book on the Medal of Honor and says all he wanted was "to be an uncommon man." The stories of men who jumped on grenades showed him the kind of courage he lacked.
- The trigger for change came from a Discovery Channel show of men carrying boats and logs in Navy SEAL training. He decided to change his life that day and called a recruiter.
- He frames this as proof that he was "not always this strong guy" and went through "a lot of hard times" to get to where he stood.

## Getting past rejection to enlist

- The recruiter asked about his height and weight limits. Hearing six foot one and 300 pounds, the recruiter laughed at him.
- Seven other recruiters laughed at him as well. One finally said to come in, too busy to talk on the phone, and did not know how much David weighed.
- In person the recruiter told him "you're fat and you're black" and doubted he could be a Navy SEAL. David says there had been only 35 African-American Navy SEALs in over 70 years at that time.
- Because of his age, he had to lose 160 pounds in less than three months.
- He came back three months later, 160 pounds lighter.
- Takeaway in his own story: keep calling and keep showing up after repeated laughter, and let results answer the doubters.

## BUD/S and serving beside real heroes

- He spent 18 months going through BUD/S, which is a six-month program.
- He went through Hell Week alongside Marcus Luttrell, Danny Dietz and Michael Murphy, the last a Medal of Honor winner.
- He says: "I am NOT a hero. I served with real heroes."
- He stays humble about his own accomplishments and credits the men around him.

## Definition of courage

- David once looked for courage in Medal of Honor winners, and he says it is there.
- His redefinition: "courage is a man who's willing to put those boots on every single day of his life to go out there and fight for this country."
- That courage holds when "nobody even knows what the hell they're doing or what the hell they're at."
- The measure is daily, repeated commitment and what you do "for the man beside you," not a single heroic moment.

## Fundraising and the people who shaped him

- He raised over $2 million for the Special Operations Warrior Foundation.
- He thanks his grandfather, Jack Gardner, now deceased, saying the day would have been the happiest of his life.
- He credits his mother: she never picked him up when he fell; she "taught me how to get up." He also thanks his uncle for always being there.
- He calls the VFW Americanism Award the most meaningful honor of his life and says the room's combat veterans "have no idea how big of a deal" they are to him.
