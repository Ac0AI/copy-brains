## Childhood trauma, learning disability, and the starting point

- David was born into an abusive home in Buffalo, New York. His alcoholic father beat him and his mother. At about eight years old, after one of the worst beatings, his mother got him out, and they moved to Brazil, Indiana.
- He grew up in a small town near a KKK hub, one of about five black families, and was bullied and had a racial slur spray-painted on his car through high school.
- He had a severe learning disability and cheated through school. By his junior year he read at a third-grade level, and his mother never saw a report card.
- He says the toxic stress left him unable to retain information, and he stuttered badly as a kid.
- To pass the ASVAB (he scored a 20 on his first try) he had six months, four hours a month of tutoring at $15 an hour, and hand-copied entire manuals into spiral notebooks. Something that takes others an hour took him six or seven days.
- His point: he started from a "horrible foundation" and nobody was coming to fix it.

## The turning point: 297 pounds and "indestructible mental toolbox"

- After leaving Air Force para-rescue training (he lied about the medical situation to get out, and says he didn't tell anyone for years), he ballooned from 175 to 297 pounds.
- He sprayed for cockroaches at Ecolab for about $1,000 a month, working 11pm to 7am, then ate a large chocolate milkshake and a box of mini donuts on the drive home. "I was depressed and food felt good."
- He saw a TV show on SEAL training. The commanding officer said: "We live in a world where mediocrity is often rewarded. These men did test mediocrity."
- He realized no one was coming to help: "Everything was on me and solely on me." He decided to start building an "indestructible mental toolbox" and announced he would become a Navy SEAL.
- Recruiters hung up when he said 297 pounds. The one who agreed to see him told him he had three months to lose 106 pounds. He lost the weight in about two and a half months.
- He quit his job the same day after a cockroach-spraying incident, and says his best workouts come "when you're least motivated."
- "I had to stop thinking normal. I can no longer be a common man walking around doing common things."
- "Being a badass hurts real bad and that's why I didn't want to do it."

## The 40% rule and the governor on the brain

- At the 24-hour race in San Diego (needed to qualify for the Badwater 135), he sat down at mile 70, urinating blood and with his blood pressure collapsing. His ex-wife, a nurse, told him he was turning white.
- He called it the worst pain of his life, and he has done 21 years in the military and 60 ultra races.
- His tactic was to shrink the problem to the smallest possible step: "Let's not quit yet. I'm going to quit, but not yet."
  - Get nutrition, then quit after the peanut butter and jelly sandwich, "but not yet."
  - See if he can stand. Walk to the car. Get back to the chair.
- He then did 30 more miles and finished 101 miles in 19 hours.
- The governor analogy: a car factory-limited to 91 mph could do 130. We put a governor on our brain, and at that limit the body starts asking questions ("this sucks, let's stop").
- "If you can't answer the questions your brain is asking you, you quit." If you have a quick answer to "why the hell am I out here," the brain shuts up.
- He says the brain has a tactical advantage because it knows your insecurities, lies and fears. Even on stage, his mind still tells him to run off, as it did in a sixth-grade play where he stuttered. He counters by recalling his "resume," what he's been through.

## Callousing the mind and being a practitioner

- He says he is not a theorist: "I studied it by being a practitioner. I put my mind in hell."
- The analogy: he did 67,000 pull-ups in nine months, which calloused his hands against the bar. He used the same idea to "callous over" his victim mentality.
- Examples of what he endured while training: three Hell Weeks in one year (130 hours of continuous training, about two hours of sleep, restarting from day one after ruptured hernia, a broken kneecap, and 16 stress fractures).
- For the stress fractures he taped his ankles to his shins with duct tape and ran on his hip flexors for the first 45 minutes, until the pain subsided.
- He later ran 7,000 miles in 2007 to raise $2 million for the Special Operations Warrior Foundation, on a heart with a dime-sized hole (an atrial septal defect), which he says explains why doctors couldn't understand how he did it.
- On mental toughness books: "It's all crap" because they're cookie cutter. "We're all different equations." You must figure out your own equation first.
- His book is called Can't Hurt Me. "Life isn't fair... it's a trial ground, a testing ground built to make us harder." His line: "Nothing should be able to hurt you. If you believe that it starts to become true."

## The mirror, accountability, and facing weaknesses

- His turning point came from the daily bathroom mirror, where "you can't lie to yourself." He shaved his head young and lied constantly to be accepted.
- That inner voice "started gnawing at me so horribly" that he had to go down into the "dungeon," the sewer where his mind used to be, and talk to that guy.
- His goal: not money or fame but to impress "the guy in the mirror," and to die proud.
- He says the first step is "face yourself and be accountable for what you're not, so then you can be what you want to be."
- As a SEAL instructor he realized he was only building "bigger, stronger, faster quitters" because he changed external things and not the internal.
- He doesn't "triple down on strengths," he triples down on weaknesses. He hated the water, and facing it is what made him a SEAL and gave him a story. Fears are doors to another world that people keep shut.

## Motivation, noise, and the other side of suffering

- "Motivation is crap." It peaks when bills are paid and everything is good. The test is when everything falls apart and you still get it in. You need to be driven or obsessed.
- The world is too noisy (phones, computers) to hear yourself. "You have to be alone in a very dark place in your mind" to work out what you want and your purpose.
- The "box" analogy: the 40% rule keeps us in a comfortable box with highways, restrooms and gas stations. Outside it, "God gives you a shovel" and you dig your own path.
- "On the other end of suffering is a life that many people don't know even exist." He is now 185 pounds.
- Climbing analogy: he stopped fearing walls once he learned how to climb, with the ropes, shoes, chalk and harness.
- Inspiration: he believes he was put here for a reason, and that his suffering was to "unlock the code of human potential" and share it.

## Leaving nothing on the table (the heaven chart)

- What haunts him is the 24-year-old at 300 pounds who could have died as a 300 to 400 pound man in a minimum-wage job, "never knowing that I was this guy."
- His visualization: God has a chart showing what you were supposed to be. His would show 185 pounds, a 205-mile run, a pull-up record, and motivating people. He wants to have written "more" on it with a marker, to impress God.
- His book test: imagine giving your book to a stranger as you die. If it wouldn't change their life forever, rewrite it.
- He turned down a $300,000 publishing offer for Can't Hurt Me, saying he wouldn't sell his story, and self-published.

## Leadership, authenticity, and being your own hero

- Leadership: "being very uncomfortable all the time." You hold yourself to a near-untouchable standard, may lose friends, and may have an unbalanced life.
  - Example: if the team trains at 5am, the leader runs to work at 3:30 to remove every excuse.
  - "To wake up every day miserable and say 'God, I don't want to do this anymore,' that's when you know you're a leader." He calls it Groundhog Day.
- Authenticity: be able to put all your bad, not the good, on a billboard and walk around saying "this is me, but I'm fixing it." Not caring what others think.
- Role models: he respects the "uncommon among uncommon," people who stand alone, hold morals, and go right when everyone goes left. He dislikes yes-men.
- "We are our own hero." People let you down because they're human, so hold yourself accountable, look up to yourself, and don't try to mimic anyone: "Don't be them, be you."
