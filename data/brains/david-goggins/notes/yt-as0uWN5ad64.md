## Callousing the mind: from victim mentality to a calloused mind

- David grew up with what he calls a "cracked foundation": an alcoholic, abusive father (abuse of him and his mother), a move to a small town in Indiana with about five Black families where he was regularly called racial slurs, a stutter, social anxiety, a learning disability, and cheating through school.
- With no one to show him the way, he says he had to "mend that cracked foundation alone."
- He had a victim mentality: his dad, society and his mom had messed up his life, or he was "dealt a bad hand."
- He argues mental toughness was not enough. He developed what he calls a "calloused mind," and used it to get over the victim mentality by outworking his faults.
- His method for building it: do something every day that sucks, and "triple down on your weaknesses, not your strengths."
- He says the skill is not natural talent: "I'm not the best person to run... I'm not the smartest." What he learned was to outwork everybody around him, and that work ethic is how he became known as the hardest man on the planet.

## Setting your own standard and rejecting mediocrity

- David argues society rewards mediocrity as if it were a standard. Beating ten mediocre people in a race or at work still leaves you mediocre.
- Most people reset their standards to whoever came before them. He set his own standard and "reinvented the wheel" for himself instead of following the crowd.
- He doesn't try to be Michael Jordan or Tiger Woods: "I'm trying to be like David Goggins." In his view, true growth is finding yourself and conquering yourself.
- He stopped comparing himself to others and compares himself only to himself. "Don't worry about what everybody else is doing, worry about what you're doing."
- He also says everyone who judges you has problems of their own.
- On social media he posts his bad side and what he has to work on, because everyone has a side they show and a side they are not working on.
- He thinks more people relate to struggle than success: most people are underdogs, and very few are the one-percenters (CEOs, the best golfer). Everyone loves the Rocky story, but you have to work your ass off to create one.

## Staying uncomfortable: friction and sharpening the sword

- David says he doesn't live a glamorous life, though he says it's not because he is broke or lacks the means.
- His analogy: a boxer with a four million dollar house goes to the mountains for about two months before a big fight, living on a cot or the ground, to put his mentality in a ready state. David says he lives that way all the time.
- He is "always sharpening my sword": "my refrigerator is never full, I've never arrived," and every time he gets close to the top of the mountain he falls back down on purpose.
- He believes starting from scratch is true growth, and that "there has to be friction in your life for you to be able to move forward."
- On excuses: people triple down on their strengths (runners only run, readers only read), so when life presents a challenge they aren't good at, they quit. His alternative is to say "I'm not good at it, I'm going to be great at it."
- He says you can't fix anything in life until you fix yourself, and that most people avoid looking inside because what they find is ugly: lies, secrets and insecurities. Once you rip those layers away, the other end is who you truly are and you have no more excuses.

## The Rocky visualization (round 14)

- With little in the way of role models, David became obsessed with people at the bottom, and with Rocky in particular. At 43 he still returns to round 14 of Rocky I, which he first watched around age 10 to 12.
- The scene: a no-name boxer against the best in the world, Apollo Creed. Rocky is knocked down roughly thirty times, his coach Mickey tells him to stay down, and Apollo turns his back and raises his arms thinking he is done. Rocky pulls himself up on the ropes with nothing left.
- What David took from it: Apollo's look of "who the hell are you?" He wanted every obstacle and every doubter to look at him like that, and as he describes Rocky: "I don't care if I win... I just want to keep coming after you."
- He visualized that scene for years. For him it became a reality he had to live: "I had to become that guy who can run on broken legs going through hell week."

## The cookie jar

- David describes the cookie jar as a mental reminder to use when your mind and body say you're done. He says even the hardest person on earth forgets how badass they are when times get hard.
- Mid-race, with the mind spazzing out, take one second and go into the jar, which holds everything you have overcome, suffered through and fought through.
- His example: running 205 miles non-stop. At mile 100, after more than 24 hours, his body is broken with 100-plus miles still to go.
- His version of the self-talk: "you went through two hell weeks, you went through Ranger School... I've overcome a hell of a lot more than this."
- The purpose is to remind yourself you have the strength to persevere, not be mediocre and not settle for what is in front of you.

## Defying the odds: the "what if" mentality

- David was about 297 to 300 pounds when he set the goal of becoming a Navy SEAL. Nearly every recruiter told him there was no way, saying he had to lose so much weight so fast.
- One recruiter told him there were only 35 African Americans to ever become a Navy SEAL in over 70 years. David got into his truck and asked himself, "but what if I could?", and decided to try to be the 36th.
- He says people who can't imagine something reflect that doubt onto you like a mirror. He takes that mirror and discards it, and uses all their negativity as fuel.
- He says he could have stayed a 300-pound man spraying for cockroaches at 24, and that most people walk the earth as a fraction of what they are capable of.
- What drives him now is being his best, which he calls a never-ending journey, and "finding more of myself." He says he is the most driven person in the world because he realized he was leaving so much on the table.
- He holds that you open up another world once you peel the crust away and see who you really are. He thinks people who never try to get to the other side of what is in front of them are the crazy ones.

## Self-talk only works with the work behind it

- David's book Can't Hurt Me (out December 4 per the interview) is a self-help memoir. He says it is not a five-step program but is about getting to the root of the problem, which is yourself: "Master your own mind and everything else is taken care of."
- His mantra: "can't hurt me." He used it during SEAL training, Ranger school, fights and tests, and on a bully or a failure. He says it becomes true only if you put in the work to develop a calloused mind.
- During long events (for example mile 75 of 135 through Death Valley) he tells himself he is the hardest man God ever created. He says he doesn't care whether it's literally true, he believes it.
- His caveat: "Self-talk without the work is just lies." When he talks to himself in a bad spot, he is also recalling the 3 a.m. wake-ups, the 15 miles a day for 75 days with no days off, and the roughly three hours of training a day, so the self-talk is real.
- His analogy: telling yourself you'll pass a test you haven't studied for won't get you the result.

## Practitioner versus theorist

- A theorist, in his picture, is the old man in a library reading about how the brain is supposed to work. Theorists put invisible bars and barriers on what humans can achieve.
- A practitioner goes out and does the work: "A practitioner dives into suffering and as he's suffering he's writing the book."
- His example: Hell Week is roughly 130 hours, and at hours 48 to 72 your mind is a mess. It is easy to write about it in a 72-degree library, but a practitioner writes it in 50-degree water when everyone is quitting.
- His advice is not to read about it but to put yourself in the environment and listen to how you talk to yourself in the worst situations. That is how you build real self-talk, real visualization and real tools for hard times.
- His closing line: "You have to be willing to suffer to get to the other side. The other side of suffering is a whole other world that people have no idea even exists."
