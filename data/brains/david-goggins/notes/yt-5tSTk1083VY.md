## Early life: abuse, a "fake" persona, and the pattern of running from fear

- David grew up in Buffalo, NY. His father owned skating rinks and bars and ran a prostitution operation, and beat David and his mother. David worked the skating rink from a young age (open ~7pm, cleaning until ~10pm), slept in the office, and rarely went to school. He ended up with a major learning disability and social anxiety.
- After his mother left his father (David was about 8), they moved to Brazil, Indiana, where racism was constant. He had slurs written on his Spanish notebook and car, and the KKK marched in the 1995 July 4th parade.
- He describes living with "two people": a scared, insecure, stuttering kid and a fake tough persona who "walked around like my [ __ ] didn't stink."
- Internally, a "voice" or conscience kept telling him to get up and do something, while he mostly avoided it.
- He cheated in school from fourth grade through his junior year of high school. On the Air Force ASVAB he could not copy (different test versions), scored about 20, then about 18, and needed a 50 out of 99.
- His mom paid for a tutor one hour a week. He had roughly 6 months to study for his last allowed attempt, "studied my ass off," and passed.
- He says the lesson he later took from this: he blamed his dad, his mom, his learning disability, his skin color, and his town. At 24 he concluded "No one's going to come to help me... it's me against me, period."

## Quitting Air Force pararescue and gaining 125 lb

- David wanted to be an Air Force Pararescueman and was terrified of water. Special ops "water confidence" training (essentially simulated drowning) was his hardest part. He is negatively buoyant, which he links to bone density.
- About 6 weeks in, with roughly 25 of 150 candidates left and no sleep in 6 weeks, a blood test showed sickle cell trait and he was pulled for a week. Watching others struggle while he was out rebuilt his fear of the water.
- When told he'd have to restart from day one, he says he broke. He claimed sickle cell scared him, but "It was the [ __ ] water." He calls it quitting, even though he left with a medical.
- From ages 19 to 22 he took a TAC-P job (no water) and went from 175 lb to a peak of 297 lb. His observation: "the more things I found comfortable, the more uncomfortable my mind was."
- By 24 he was spraying restaurants for cockroaches overnight at Ecolab (about $1,000 a month), driving home eating a 42-oz shake and mini donuts.

## The turning point: seeing himself as "exactly what people said I was going to be"

- One morning he watched a Discovery Channel show on SEAL Class 224 Hell Week, with men quitting and ringing the bell, and others staying. It made him reflect on his fears and excuses.
- His decision: face "every [ __ ] fear I have. No matter what." He framed it as two options: stay a 300-lb exterminator and look back at 50 on the man he never became, or "fail and fail and fail until I succeed."
- Recruiters kept hanging up when he gave his weight (6'1", 297 lb). One reserve recruiter told him he'd need to lose 106 lb in under 3 months to make the class. He initially said no and went back to work.
- The next night he hit "the mother lode of cockroaches," said "this is my life... this ain't going to be it," left his spray canister in the restaurant, quit, and drove home.
- He then "invented a guy that didn't exist," one who could take any pain, suffering or insult, and built a "callous mind" through suffering. If it was raining or snowing at 3am, his instinct became "we got to go out there." Anything inhumane that he'd normally say no to, he did.
- He lost the weight, got into the class, and went through three Hell Weeks in one year (failed the first, finished the next two).
- His conclusion: the mind is what you create, and "the more doors I opened up... my potential is damn near endless."

## Day one of getting back in shape: the quarter mile and the grind

- His first run: planned 4 miles, ran a quarter mile, walked home, cried on his couch and again at his mom's. He was making $1,000 a month, rent was $810, and he had just gotten someone pregnant.
- He regrouped by watching Rocky (especially round 14, Apollo Creed's face when Rocky gets back up) and Platoon (Elias being shot in the back and getting up). He wanted to be the guy who keeps getting up: "I don't care if you liked me or didn't... this [ __ ] is going to keep coming after whatever is in front of him."
- He turned negatives into fuel with "what if" thinking and self-talk: "who at 297, who can't swim that great, scared of the water, would have the balls to quit a job and put everything on himself?" Eventually, "through time it became reality."
- Tactics he used:
  - Cycled heavily and rode while watching TV; swam with fins to offset his negative buoyancy; spent hours in the pool "to become one with the water."
  - Built running from 1 mile to 2, then 3, then 6 miles, 5 days a week (the warning order standard for BUD/S).
  - Used two-a-days when a run failed (a quarter mile, wait a couple hours, try a half mile).
  - Did high-volume weights: 200-400 reps of light bench press in one exercise to burn calories and build muscle.
- Reframe: when he failed he stopped saying "I'm just not good enough" and told himself "I'm going to make myself good enough." He treated bad days as "part of the process."

## The 40% rule and the cookie jar (first 100-miler)

- Origin: David knew nothing about ultra running. He googled the 10 hardest races, found Badwater 135, and called race director Chris Kostman, who told him he had to qualify. His shot was the San Diego 1-Day, a 24-hour run on a 1-mile track, four days away.
- He showed up as a roughly 230-lb bodybuilder with almost no running in the prior year, a Walmart lawn chair, Ritz crackers and Mylanta. He had squatted and deadlifted heavy the day before.
- Around mile 70 (about 12 hours in) he was dizzy, bleeding in his urine, cramping, with broken feet and 30 miles to go.
- The cookie jar: "A lot of us when we have bad times in life... forget how badass we are during that hard time." He takes a few seconds to reflect ("you've been through this, you overcame that") and reaches into his past accomplishments. He did not panic: he fixed nutrition, hydration and dizziness first.
- At mile 81 something clicked and "mind, body, spirit, soul, everything just connected." It was "me against me," no crowd or trophy. He ran the last 20 miles around a 10:30 pace and finished 101 miles in 18 hours 56 minutes.
- The 40% rule: "I thought I'd given 100%" at mile 70 and was nowhere near it. Our brain has a governor, like a car capped at 91 that could do 130. The moment we feel pain or discomfort we slow down. If you push through and gain a few percent, the 40% becomes 60, then 70, 80, 90. He says he doubts many people are at 100, and joked that 100% is "at death's door."
- Aftermath: he soaked in the tub, then wanted to enjoy the pain instead of numbing it. Kostman then told him the point of a 24-hour race is to run 24 hours and he'd only run 19, which put doubt in his mind about getting into Badwater.

## Racing volume, recovery, and the body breaking down

- About two weeks after the 101-miler he ran the Las Vegas Marathon (December 5, 2005), unable to walk beforehand, and qualified for Boston with roughly a 3:08. His mindset: "I just ran 101 miles. What is 26 to me now?"
- Next he ran the Hurt 100 in Hawaii (26,000 ft of climbing): 33 hours, ninth place, which qualified him for Badwater. He dropped from about 230 lb to about 190-195. His method was less protein and weights, more running.
- At Badwater he finished fifth the first year and third the second. For the 2007 season, he cited back-to-back 100-milers and 50-milers on his race log, including Badwater, Leadville and the Bear 100. He also ran a 205-mile, 39-hour race, his longest.
- Injury management during training and Hell Week: pronation caused stress fractures and shin splints. He duct-taped his feet and ankles ("cast myself") so the pain went numb after 30-45 minutes. Later he used compression tape, shoe inserts and a heel wedge.
- Later breakdown: after about 5 years of unhealthy output, he developed adrenal, thyroid and endocrine problems and went from running 205 miles to barely getting out of bed. Doctors medicated him without finding the cause.
- The fix: he found a very tight psoas (hip flexor) pulled on his T12. A bump on the back of his head shrank as he stretched. He now stretches about 2 hours a night, has skipped about 2 days of stretching in 5 years, and is on only a low-dose thyroid pill. His takeaway: stretch and do yoga, "a martial art you do against yourself" (quoting Nick Gregoriades), and the psoas is "almost worse than others."
- He also says natural flexibility is a myth people use to excuse themselves: you have to push through stretch pain.
- Surgeries: he had two heart surgeries for an atrial septal defect (a quarter-sized hole) with a patch, and was off active SEAL duty for about 3 years. After the second surgery he could only walk, so he became an "ultra walker." He did a ruck run the day before the surgery.

## Callousing the mind, living in suffering, and why theory isn't enough

- David argues that mental toughness training (visualization, self-talk, "eat an elephant one bite at a time," breathing) goes "out the door" in the first hour of cold-water Hell Week unless you live it daily. His example: you can only use the tools if you've practiced them under real discomfort.
- He rejects reading as a shortcut: "You can't read somebody else's book about some theory... sat up in their nice warm office." He recounts an MIT panel where an academic argued that being a certain way was something you had to be born with, and he contradicted him from experience: you can be very broken and change with the right mindset.
- His line: "You have to suffer. You have to make that a tattoo on your brain."
- Shortcuts like six-minute abs may give results "but they're not permanent."
- Quitting regret: he says he knows "what's on the back end of quitting. It's a lifetime of thinking about why the [ __ ] did I do that." After Hell Week he saw the graduating guys and thought "why did I quit?"
- The four-lane highway vs the shovel: most people take the easy four-lane highway with signs and restaurants. He picked up the shovel and made his own path, which is incredibly difficult but yields "glorious [ __ ] you can't even explain to people."
- Triple down on weaknesses: he says "triple down on your [ __ ] weaknesses," not strengths, because strengths are what you already know. Putting yourself in situations that suck is how you "find yourself."
- Staying uncivilized: "The worst thing that can happen to a man is become civilized." At 43 he was still doing 100-mile weeks and thousands of pull-ups and push-ups. He deliberately returned to hard training (like Ranger School as a SEAL, at 28 or 29, after seven denied requests) to avoid getting soft and complacent, and to be "uncommon amongst uncommon people."
- Self-accountability: "It's all on you. The self part is what's big." He says he had no trainer, no nutritionist and nobody waking him at 3am. Self-discipline is everything and "we count on people too much to get us through."
- Authenticity and motivation vs drive: he says motivation "comes and goes as how you feel"; the goal is to move from motivated to driven to obsessed. He says people can read through someone who preaches but doesn't live it.
- Truth as fuel: if someone calls you fat or dumb, "you might be." Take it, and use it as fuel. He credits his past suffering with making him appreciate happiness more: "Don't misunderstand the passion... happiest person in the world. But I'm not done."

## The pull-up record and doing what he hates

- He set the world record for pull-ups in 24 hours: 4,030 in 17 hours (he weighed about 207 lb, sets of five, with witnesses for Guinness). He did about 67,000 pull-ups in 9 months to prepare.
- He failed twice first: about 2,588 in September in front of millions of viewers, then again 2 months later, then succeeded on January 19. His hands took third-degree burns and he tore his forearm.
- At the finish he felt nothing but relief that he could stop: "I ain't got to do more [ __ ] pull-ups anymore."
- He doesn't enjoy running or pull-ups. He says he does them because he doesn't like them: "That's the only way to callous your brain." He says people who assume he loves it are putting a label on him: "Anybody's capable of doing [ __ ] like this."
- Visualization detail: he listened to the Rocky song (2 minutes 13 seconds) on loop for 17 hours during the pull-up attempt.

## Current life, habits, and mission

- Routine at 43: he runs every morning, lifts, and stretches at night. He stays off social media and phones to "silence the world," because he believes phones and cameras pull you away from your own mind.
- He plans to break a record crossing from Death Valley to the top of Mount Whitney as fast as possible.
- Business and brand: he runs Goggins LLC, does motivational speaking, and is investing his own money in the brand rather than chasing income. He lives on a small military retirement and calls himself a minimalist (backpack, pull-up bar, running shoes, Subway sandwich).
- He says he wants to grow it slowly and authentically, because he refuses to "mass produce" quotes or say things he doesn't live.
- His stated reason for sharing: he calls it his duty, "I'm a tool," to tell weak, scared people like his past self the unfiltered truth. He explains his heavy profanity as his refusal to water down how violent the struggle was.
