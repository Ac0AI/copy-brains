## Callousing the mind: how David builds a "warrior mentality"

- David says he was born with no mental toughness: abusive father, raised in a small Indiana town near Klan headquarters, called the n-word daily, learning disabilities, "zero" self-esteem, and a high school graduation where he cheated on tests and homework.
- A teen meeting with an Air Force special operator gave him a goal ("my real-life Rambo"). He saw himself as "the weakest human being on the planet" and wanted to become "the hardest man God ever created."
- He flipped his mindset: instead of "why me," he treated the bad hand as "the platform" for who he wanted to be.
- He deliberately designed crucibles (SEAL training, ultra races) to build a warrior mentality: "I was building that callous mind."
- He says he also callouses his brain the way pull-ups callous hands: by moving through hell, ignoring negativity, and running "to the fight" instead of from it. That is how he handled PTSD.
- He created "Goggins" as a persona. "David Goggins was a weak kid so I created Goggins." This was conscious and internal: "I don't care about money, awards, medals." The aim was to be proud of himself.

## The accountability mirror

- He started shaving his head and face at 16 and used the time looking at his reflection to tell himself the truth: he was dumb, a cheater, a liar, insecure.
- Core idea: "If you suck, you suck. If you tell yourself you suck, that is when you become great." He rejects friends and circles that keep giving positive feedback to someone who is dumb or fat.
- It is a daily practice he still does. If he tells a white lie, he confronts himself in the mirror next morning, like a daily inventory.
- He says to handle insecurities and jealousies before chasing a goal, because they surface when you are suffering in the crucible. "The ego will kill you every time."
- He ties staying power to purpose: leave the ego at the door and know why you are doing it.
- He is critical of participation trophies and "everyone is great" culture. Without them he says he would not have become who he is.

## The cookie jar and the first 100-mile race

- Origin: he called the Bad Water race director on a Wednesday in November 2005 and was told to qualify with 100 miles in 24 hours. Three days later he ran the San Diego One Day, a 24-hour, one-mile-track race, with zero training at roughly 250+ lb.
- Setup: a blue lawn chair, Mike and Ike candy, Ritz crackers, and his then-wife crewing.
- Around mile 50 he broke bones in his feet (stress fractures, shin splints). At mile 70 he sat down, and when he tried to get up he urinated blood and soiled himself. He says it was the worst thing he has endured, including SEAL training.
- The cookie jar: every time he failed and then succeeded, he put a "cookie" in the jar. When suffering makes you forget how great you are, he recalls where he came from with a calm mind and then solves the problem: salt, electrolytes, food, hydration, and fixing blood pressure first so he could stand.
- Pace: he was walking a 37-minute mile. At mile 81 his wife said he would not make the time, and he ran the last 19 miles. He finished 100 miles in 18 hours 56 minutes.
- He says this showed how far short human beings stop when they feel pain.

## Embracing suffering and the "what if" mindset

- At mile 70 he says he had to "go dark" into a "barbaric, garanimal mindset" and make the suffering work for him: "I embraced the suffering."
- He talked to himself with a "what if": what if I get through this while this messed up? He pictured finishing and the feeling afterward, which sparked his endorphins.
- He denies being a masochist: "I'm not a crazy guy. I'm a guy that wanted to see who the best me was." His journey was to find his limits and not settle for stopping at 70.
- Moments of extreme difficulty reveal character: "It's all about that moment and that decision you have to make when everything else is stripped away."
- Hard evidence of the breadth: he did the 100-mile Hurt 100 about two months later with the feet still broken, finished Jan 17, and then ran Badwater (fifth place the first year, third the second) with compression tape on his shins, training only 30 to 40 miles a week on feet that were broken.
- Cross-training for that: heavy time on the sauna, elliptical, and rucksack.
- In 2007 he says he ran many 100-milers in consecutive weeks (Western States, Leadville, Angeles Crest, Bear, and others) and treated each one like "another workout."

## Reframing failure and rejecting "freak" excuses

- Failure to David is just "more information on how to succeed." He fails, goes back to the drawing board, and tries again. You must be willing to fail 100 times to succeed once.
- Example: he failed a pull-up record attempt, took two months off, failed again, then set the record (4,025 pull-ups in 24 hours) in January. He did 67,000 pull-ups in nine months to train.
- He says people call him a genetic freak, but he has sickle cell and a hole in his heart, a low VO2 max and bad hematocrit, and says he operates on a fraction of capacity. Everything he did was "miserable" and he was destroyed at mile 50 of every 100-miler.
- He refuses to let people excuse themselves by putting him in a category. "You watered down my message by putting me in a category."
- 2013 Badwater: he walked 105 miles, spent about seven hours in the vehicle, and treated it as resetting the goal rather than quitting.
- Fun detail: his first Ultraman (borrowed bike, three weeks of bike training) ended with a flat and no clips, and he rode 75 miles on pedals in running shoes and missed first by about 10 minutes.

## Stop asking questions: want it and start

- David says people stall by asking endless "how" questions (what watch, what plan) and by saying "I wish I could." "Stop asking these questions... get an idea, start walking, and figure this out as you go."
- His examples: he qualified for Badwater in days and ran it, lost over 100 pounds in about 60 days to get into SEAL training (he says 105), and became a Ranger at 41.
- Time excuses: while a full-time SEAL he woke at 3 a.m., ran, then rode his bike to work. "If you want it, you will find time in your life to do it. If you don't want it, you will continue to give excuses."
- The passion that people read as anger is, he says, because he knows it can be done.
- Many people say they will do something "one day" and he says most do not want anything badly enough.

## Motivation vs. purpose, and changing how you think

- He says motivation is "crap" compared with being driven. The windchill example: a motivated person goes back inside; a driven person goes back in to get a new hat and runs anyway.
- Purpose requires quiet: tune out social media and others' noise and listen to your internal voice. Many people never listen to what they want.
- He says people change their car, underwear, spouse, or diet but rarely "the way they think," which keeps them stuck.
- Key message: "If you want to be better, you have to change your perceived limitations and take the barriers down." He is "working on how most people think" and tailors his message since people respond to different things.
- He says what you do for fulfillment: find "the truth of who you are" and take the hard journey rather than the "journey of least resistance."

## Keeping the edge, stretching, and health lessons

- Even when successful, he says "the worst thing that can happen to a man is he becomes civilized." He keeps his mind sharp "like a sword" and keeps "the refrigerator empty" so he always keeps an edge.
- He values a simple life (backpack, Subway sandwiches, push-ups, football in a hole-in-the-wall) and sees luxury as not for him after staying with the Itzlers.
- Health price: his adrenals are shot, and he has a heart defect, sickle cell, and spinal issues. He says he knew what he was doing to himself and sees it as worth it.
- Stretching routine: he stretched 8 to 12 hours a day at the worst point, then 6, then about 2, and has not missed a day in three years. He focuses on the psoas, hip flexors, and abductors, holding stretches 30 to 40 minutes, and says it fixed back pain and a bump at the base of his skull.
- Results he claims: his Rocket-style 41-mile race pace improved from 8:30 to 8:40 training pace to about 7:15 to 7:30, and he cut his time on a course from 5:30-something to 4:50. His advice is to do your own research and be your own guinea pig.
