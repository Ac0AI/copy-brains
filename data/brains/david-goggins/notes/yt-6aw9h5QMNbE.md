## Hell Week and the Voice That Says "You Don't Belong"

- In his first Hell Week, David was still doubting himself. Many SEAL candidates came from the Naval Academy or from families where dads and grandfathers were SEALs. He had taught himself to read and write, had quit Air Force training, and was now going for "the best."
- He had lost weight and rebuilt his self-esteem, but standing among "the most uncommon of uncommon people" he asked himself whether he belonged.
- He says the mind takes the path of least resistance whenever friction shows up. Friction causes growth, so when we turn the other way, we stop growing.
- When friction hit, his mind told him: "you're a fraud," "you're not real," pointing at where he came from and how little he could read and write. Whenever times got hard, it said he wasn't good enough.

## The Cookie Jar: Remembering What You've Already Overcome

- David calls it "the cookie jar." Everyone has gone through hard times, but in a hard moment the mind forgets what we have overcome.
- The tactic: when you want to quit, take one second and remind yourself "you endured this," then draw on past hardships you survived.
- He applies it to his own life: his upbringing became "the ultimate training ground." Most people ask why it happened to them; he flipped it and asked whether he was being trained to become one of the baddest men on the planet.
- Those experiences "beat me" but "didn't kill me," and he learned to work around them and figure out ways through.

## Flipping the Inner Voice in Hell Week

- Instead of "I don't belong here," he looked around and told himself he was the most trained person in the room. He had been through more mental torture than the others could fathom, and he thought of the other candidates as people who "can't hold a stick."
- He reframed the question: who else could be dropped into the worst place on earth and still climb to the top of the mountain with nothing but hard work, grit and figuring it out, without sending himself back to the dungeon every time something got hard?
- That became his daily self-talk: "you really are the baddest person on the planet." After a period of time, you start to believe it.

## Making Suffering Your New Norm

- By his second and third Hell Weeks, the pain became his baseline. What most people fear, such as never wanting to go through one Hell Week, let alone two, he treated as normal.
- His SEAL training ran 18 months when it is supposed to take six, and that became his "new norm."
- He says people do not do enough of this: constantly looking for a new norm. Being beaten, tortured, suffering broken legs and kidney issues became normal, and his mind changed to match the reality of who he wanted to be.
- The takeaway he gives the audience: past pain, once owned and examined, can be turned into preparation for future pain.

## Building Bigger, Stronger, Faster Quitters

- In his last two years of a 21-year military career, David was sent to Great Lakes, Chicago, to help with the Navy SEAL BUD/S prep program, designed because too many candidates were failing.
- Candidates trained for about two months on swimming, push-ups, sit-ups and more, but the quit numbers stayed exactly the same.
- His conclusion: "we were building bigger, stronger, faster quitters." If you do not train the mind, "the most powerful weapon in the world," physical preparation does not change who quits.

## Facing the Mental Dungeons Before the Pain Arrives

- Everyone is insecure about something: a mom, dad, sibling, spouse or kids. Even if you are not consciously thinking about it, that unresolved material surfaces when you are in physical pain.
- These "dungeons" and "trap doors" built by life are why most people quit. They have no mental tools to get to the other side.
- His advice is to start from zero with your pain, go back and examine it, and ask why you are doing the hard thing in the first place.
- Watching SEAL shows from a 72-degree house with lemonade makes it seem doable. In 40-degree conditions, he notes, a candidate died of pulmonary edema during his third Hell Week.

## Having an Answer to the Questions Your Mind Asks

- Out there your mind asks, "how can you go one more step?" If you do not already have answers, you will quit. He says nobody can hand you the answer; "you have to know why am I here."
- You must really want whatever you are going for. For David, in SEAL training, "I was willing to die," which gave him an answer to every negative question that came up.
- His closing point: people who cannot answer those negative questions will quit.
