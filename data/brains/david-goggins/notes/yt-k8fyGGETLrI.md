## Embracing suffering through a daily "live autopsy" of your past

- David says suffering is where the answers are. "In failure and in suffering, all the answers are in there. All the answers to all the test questions, the test is your life."
- He does not mean living in suffering all day. Visit it like a workout: about 1 hour a day, with the other 23 hours outside it.
- The "live autopsy": an autopsy cracks you open after you're dead to find out why you died. David says to do it while you're alive. "Crack it open while you're alive. Don't wait until you're dead to figure out why you died."
- Steps he describes:
  - Go into the suffering and pain of your life and ask "Why did this suck for me so bad? Why am I afraid of all this stuff? Why have I shut down the whole world?"
  - Study your failures, insecurities and self-doubt for at least an hour a day.
  - Use specifics: if you're afraid to jump off an airplane, ask what makes you afraid of it and study that.
  - He went back systematically and re-met every person and every situation in his mind, one by one, and "went one-on-one with them again."
- The answers sit in the scars. "They lie within the scars. Visit them... study them. And then you'll find out more about yourself. You will then grow."
- He says he became powerful in the mind because he saw he was weak and dove into the mind instead of running from it.
- Trying to forget is where people fail. "You will continue making the same mistakes if you don't go back." Pushing the memories under the rug means there is no growth.
- He wrote his book this way. "You don't forget that stuff. You remember it. Lift the rug up, you'll find them."

## Go back to the root: fixing the foundation, not the surface

- David says most adult problems trace to childhood. "The only way you're going to fix yourself is to go all the way back to the beginning, to your childhood, because that's where everything starts."
- His childhood: an alcoholic father who beat him, his brother and his mother about once or twice a week. Kids worked the family skating rink and bar from the time they could walk (age 4, scraping gum off the rink floor). He had a learning disability and a stutter, white splotches on his skin and patches of hair falling out from stress. At 8 his family moved to Brazil, Indiana, where he was the only Black kid. His mother worked three jobs and they lived in a $7-a-month place.
- Foundation metaphor: building on a bad foundation means "a little windstorm" can knock the house down. He looked strong but was one windstorm from breaking, and "nobody knew it."
- The turning point: he taught himself to read and write and built disciplines, yet the first real challenge pushed him back to zero. He realized he was "all surface" and needed to fix the cellar of his mind.
- He was about 24, had gone from 175 lb to almost 300 lb, and sat on his couch and realized "we got to go back," starting with his father.
- Fixing symptoms is like taking Motrin for a jacked-up knee. The swelling goes down but the knee is still damaged. "You got to fix the knee."
- He says people avoid this because they invent a version of themselves, lie about who they're not, and hide behind social media images. "That's not going to fix you."
- Doing this exposes the "scars" on the brain like scars on skin. "You have to break it open, let that blood flow... All that toxic stuff you have trapped in there has to start flowing out."

## The accountability mirror and brutal honesty with yourself

- David calls the first step the "accountability mirror." "It starts with yourself... that person in that mirror."
- Rucksack metaphor: a military pack that civilians carry as life's baggage. His held abuse, learning disabilities, stutter, insecurity, self-doubt, immaturity, and lies told to make friends.
- No one is coming to fix it. "No one's coming back to help me... You have to realize you are on your own now." He calls it a lonely journey.
- Ownership: you have to own what others did to you. "It's yours now to fix the problems that people did to you. It makes no sense. It's not fair. I get it." Staying in a "woe is me" mentality keeps you stuck.
- The conversation must be honest, with no kind euphemisms:
  - Not "I've gained some weight" but "you're fat."
  - Not "I have a learning disability" but "I cannot read. I have a fourth-grade reading level."
  - He calls himself stupid at times, "not in a way to put myself down," but because it's honest.
  - Others: "I look like crap. I feel like crap. I'm falling behind in school. I'm lazy. My house is a mess." Call it what it is.
- He says people surround themselves with those who "tell us what we want to hear, not what we need to hear," and the inner dialogue turns into "it's okay." His line: "It's not okay anymore."
- Calling yourself out for exactly what you are, and exactly how to fix it, is where it starts. He ties this to a culture that skirts hard conversations: "We have to break the barriers within ourselves."
- Rewrite your story daily: "You have to constantly rewrite your book every day of your life." The most important conversation is the one you have with yourself, and most people's conversations "keep us sitting in that toxic environment."

## The 40% rule and the mind's tactical advantage

- David says the mind is protective and "has the tactical advantage over you." It knows your scars, fears and insecurities and keeps you away from them.
- This is the core of the 40% rule from his book: the brain tells you to stop long before you've reached your limit.
- Examples he gives of its protective voice: don't jump out of a plane, don't go in the ocean where there are sharks, don't go back to the place where your dad beat you.
- That protection becomes a ceiling. "It protects you so much it doesn't allow growth." The mind takes the path of least resistance and wants to stay peaceful, in a "72 degree" room with a massage therapist, and away from scarring.
- You must take power over your mind or "your mind will guide you into all the soft places that your brain wants to live in."
- Watch for it mid-effort: when he revisits the past he knows his mind may let him wallow for 45 minutes, so he stays aware of the conversation he's having.
- The mind will also justify comfort: "Just sleep in today. You deserve it." David says, "Sometimes you don't deserve it... You really haven't worked hard enough."
- Aging alone doesn't create growth. People who repeat childhood patterns "strengthen a bad muscle": "I'm throwing a bigger tantrum at 43 than I did at 8."
- He agrees with Jay's monk framing that the mind is like a child and the intelligence a parent. The parent has to teach the child, not just shout at it.

## Hell Week: breaking suffering into the smallest moment

- David did three Hell Weeks in one year (finished two), which he says no other SEAL has done. Hell Week is 130 hours of continuous training with maybe 2 hours of sleep, "designed to break a man."
- It exposes anyone who only fixed the surface, because it brings out your demons.
- Structure he describes: the first hour is loud chaos (gunfire, no time to think). In the second hour comes "surf torture" in the roughly 49-50°F Pacific, arms linked, waves crashing, now quiet. In the quiet, the mind spirals: "I have another 129 hours."
- His takeaway on the mind: it can't process 130 hours of suffering, but it can process right now. "The brain can't handle hours and hours and hours of suffering. But it can handle right now, I'm in the Pacific Ocean and it's very cold." Break big painful things "down to the smallest molecule."
- Tactics he lists:
  - Stay in the exact moment. He does not think even an hour ahead, such as about eating.
  - Think about the people beside you. As a boat crew leader he checked on his five teammates ("How was John? How was Andy?"), and his mind stopped caring how cold he was. "If you don't think about yourself, there's no pain."
  - Get a different vantage point: "Get on top of that mountain top in your mind and look down on what you're doing... that world is beautiful." Spiritually get out of the suck and be amazed at how far you've come.
- He describes himself as a practitioner, not a theorist: "A theorist is a person who reads a book, doesn't do it. A practitioner... put yourself in hell repeatedly and study how you process it."
- He says he studied this on himself through Hell Week, Ranger School and other schools, and the tools came from watching how the mind speeds up in hell.

## Recovery, burnout and the "hard stop"

- David warns that the "think about others, not yourself" trick can't be used every day. It's like a lever that gets rusty and has to be oiled. "There has to be a recovery process for your mind, your body, your soul, your spirit."
- He says he lost about 5 years to overdoing it and is "just now back to crushing it." He talks about this in the last chapter of his book. He credits meditation, stretching, eating right and sleeping right over 5 years.
- Pushing hard is good, but "to push as hard as you can, you have to be mindful of the machine that's pushing. It is going to end up dying."
- Warning signs he uses:
  - You can't hear yourself think; your mind is "so amped up."
  - Every day is a schedule and you have no time for yourself.
  - You start saying "I don't have time for this anymore" and neglect your fiancee, your disciplines, reading, learning, workouts, diet, sleep.
- His rule: when he drifts too far from those self-disciplines, "it's a hard stop," because those disciplines are what made him who he is.
- He listens for an internal "unrelenting voice" (the dedication in his book is "to the unrelenting voice in my head that will never allow me to stop"). He calls it right-or-wrong voice, whatever name you give it. The more you ignore it, the quieter it gets until you hear only yourself. It often points toward the uncomfortable choice, like getting up instead of going back to sleep.
- His fiancee conversation is part of the check: he tells her "Hey, I'm doing too much."
- He balances pushing: "constantly grinding myself into a fine powder, but doing it in a smart way... There's a happy medium of grinding."

## No special people: stripping the superhuman label

- David says labels like "superhuman" or "1%er" give people "a get-out-of-jail-free card." He wrote his book raw so readers could see where he came from.
- "There are no special people. There are special minds, and the mind can be created on your own."
- The mind forgets quickly: by chapter 9 or 10 of his book, readers forget chapters 1-3 of the hell he came from. He says the same happens when he speaks: "By the time I leave... you forgot it already. The mind's already tricking you." So keep reminding yourself of chapters 1, 2 and 3.
- Daily mantra: "I used to believe I was the weakest man that God ever created. So now I believe that I'm the hardest human being that God ever made. I don't care if it's true or not, it's the most important conversation to me."
- Other points he makes:
  - Don't forget your roots as success grows. Return to the quiet place of the "dirty mirror" in the $7-a-month place where he grew.
  - Be wary of motivational speakers who don't live it: "Are you actually doing what you say you're doing?"
  - Let people evolve. Don't pull someone back to who they were. If someone's growth makes you feel bad, "fix yourself" and "change your own life." He cites Good Will Hunting: the friend who wants you to leave is the kind to keep.
  - His advice on advice: he never received any, so the best advice came from himself: study yourself, including the good, and grow. The biggest thing that can hurt him is himself, by letting his mind build a false reality.
