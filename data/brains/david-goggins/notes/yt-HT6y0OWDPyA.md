## Embracing suffering as the way to find out who you are

- David says he likes suffering "in the way that is competitive," because it brings out the absolute best in him and in everyone around him.
- He wants to be with the hardest men, suffering alongside them, and see what they are made of. His comparison: "almost like the Coliseum in Rome." The only way to find out who the baddest person is, he says, is to suffer; you can't do it by writing a paper.
- His central message: "once you change one thing, your mindset, you can attack everything."
- He used to see himself as a weak little kid and put elite people on a pedestal. Once he worked his way up there he realized "we can all compete," and he tells people not to put him on a pedestal either.
- He loves the men who get broken, say "[ __ ] you" and keep going, but says very few are willing to go there more than once.

## "I'm only going to do this one time" is how you cap yourself

- David says many people in special ops, and in anything hard, tell themselves they will only do it once. In his words, once you say that, "you [ __ ] yourself."
- He ties this to athletes and business people who sacrifice everything once, build their identity on it, and then retell the same stories for the rest of their lives.
- David's version: he is only happy when he is grinding for something and growing. He doesn't want to talk about what he used to do.

## Goggins vs. David Goggins: keeping the dungeon alive

- David describes two people: David Goggins, a calm, cool guy who used to be a weak kid, and "Goggin," the guy willing to tape up his legs and go after it. He says his dad created David and he created Goggin, and Goggin is who he loves.
- He says "since Can't Hurt Me came out" and he got on social media, he has been pulled into helping so many people that he lost himself. He calls this the biggest depression of his life.
- His reasoning: the book motivates people through the stories of what he did. "Once I stop living that, I'm no more." If he stops doing hard things, he is no longer the person people are being motivated by.
- He says he is now back to running 100 miles a week and back "grinding" himself, and he tells people not to get it twisted: "what makes me me is the dungeon."
- His method: "I have to be hard on myself... continue to grind myself into a fine dust." If things come easy, it isn't fun.
- He wants to fail at something repeatedly so that it haunts him, and feel he accomplished something only when he finally completes it.
- When he fails, he wants to say "Roger that," go to the dark place in his mind that people avoid because of social media and computerization, analyze what he did wrong, and work out how to get it done.

## Badwater 135 and the 101-mile race he wasn't prepared for

- After the military, David decided to attempt Badwater 135, the 135-mile race through Death Valley that he describes as the hardest in the world. His cardio at the time was 20 minutes every Sunday on an elliptical.
- Race director Chris Kostman told him he needed a 100-mile finish in 24 hours or less to qualify. Only two qualifying races remained before the deadline. He called on a Wednesday and the next race was Saturday: a one-mile track loop in San Diego for 24 hours.
- He showed up as "a big bodybuilder looking guy," shirt off, with a blue lawn chair, and his nutrition was Rice Krispies-style crackers and a sports drink: "no water." He says he never drank water on runs and didn't know better.
- At mile 70 he sat down for the first time and everything fell apart. He was peeing blood and had 30 miles to go, in the worst shape of his life.
- He says this race, which he did without training, coaching or a crew of experts, taught him more than all three Hell Weeks, Ranger School, or his time in selection.

## The cookie jar and "I might quit, but not yet"

- David invented the cookie jar. His explanation: "we forget how badass we are when we're suffering because our mind stays right there in the suffering."
- The cookie jar is the collection of things he has accomplished, failed at, or kept grinding through. Like a mom's cookie jar, you don't know exactly which one you'll pull out, but you reach in, grab one and go.
- At mile 70 he went back through his cookie jar.
- He told himself "I might quit, but not yet." His reasoning: with 30 miles left, your mind is spazzing, so "I'm giving my mind some space" because if you don't, it freaks out.
- Concrete steps he took: sat there for a moment, drank water, had his crew get food, and worked on simply standing up (he was dizzy and lightheaded at first). Once he could stand, he went back out.
- At mile 81 he was too slow to make the time. He says his body and mind connected and he "became a cyborg," running the last 20 miles at about a 10:15 pace. He finished 101 miles in 19 hours 6 minutes.

## After the race: pain as confirmation

- Afterward he got in the tub in the fetal position, with the shower on him. What he passed looked like dark brown dirt rather than blood.
- His mother's friend, a doctor, told him to go to the hospital immediately. David refused: "I want to sit here and enjoy this pain."
- He says he wanted no painkillers. The pain was "confirmation," proof of what he had just done with no training, no coaching and no cheering staff. It was the worst pain of his life and the best feeling of his life.
- He says in that moment he became "the truth." He was a kid who lied and cheated, and now he was a Navy SEAL, an Army Ranger school honor man, and had done all of it.
- He says you never want an "I've arrived" mentality because you stop, but this was the moment he realized he was the truth.

## People who want to drag you back down

- David says the people from your past, from when you were at your worst, are often mad that you're out. In his words, "they hate that they're still where they're at."
- His image: there is always "that [ __ ] in the sewer you came from that's grabbing at your ankle as you're leaving that sewer."
- He says they aren't willing to figure it out, and that "everybody has a different equation." His own equation was different from most people's, and it wasn't about being sadistic.
