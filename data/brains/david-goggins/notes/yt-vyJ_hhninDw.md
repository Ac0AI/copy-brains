## Callousing the Mind by Doing What You Avoid

David argues that comfort is the reason people stop growing. He says "we live in a world that we want to be as comfortable as we can," and that when the smallest thing gets difficult, people "cower and run away" because their whole life is built on the path of least resistance.

- He ties this to his own life: he became 297 lb because that was comfortable. Running, dieting and facing things he didn't want to face were what felt uncomfortable.
- At that weight he says he had no growth, because he was living comfortable.
- His fix was to face the uncomfortable things every day. Running was the one he "absolutely hated," so he did it daily to start "callousing my mind."
- His rule for mental toughness: "It's constantly facing the things that you don't want to face." Running from them means no growth and no toughness.

## Why Tactics Aren't Enough Without Practice

David says he could "give you a class all day long about self-talk, visualization, eat an elephant one bite at a time." But if you never put yourself in a situation to practice them, "you're never going to grow."

- Techniques only become real when tested under actual discomfort.
- Mental toughness is built by reps in hard situations, not by knowing the concepts.

## The Mind as the Battlefield

He says a warrior is not a person who carries a gun. "The biggest war you're ever going to go through is right between your own ears."

- Everyone is fighting a war in their mind.
- You have to callous your mind to fight that war and win it.

## How the Badwater 135 Goal Started

David uses a story to show callousing the mind in practice.

- Motivation: the book Lone Survivor and Marcus Luttrell moved him. He knew the men who died and wanted to raise money for the Special Operations Warrior Foundation, which gives 100% college tuition to children of fallen special operators.
- Choosing the challenge: he Googled the 10 hardest races in the world. Number one was the Badwater 135, a 135-mile race through Death Valley in the summer.
- His starting point: he was not a runner. He had run maybe 10 miles the whole year and came from bodybuilding and weight training. He had never run a marathon and assumed Badwater was a stage race.
- The qualifier: race director Chris Kostman told him he had to run 100 miles in 24 hours or less. He called on a Wednesday, and the San Diego One Day race was that Saturday, 4 days away.

## Hitting the Wall at the San Diego One Day

- The race was run around a 1-mile track for 24 hours. His supplies were Mile Plus (his drink) and Ritz crackers, plus a blue lawn chair and a crew person he saw every mile.
- Around mile 30 his shins became extremely sore and he developed stress fractures and shin splints. Around mile 50 he felt the metatarsals in his feet breaking.
- By mile 70 he was "totally destroyed." He sat in the chair and could not stand up, could not get up to go to the bathroom, and went where he sat. He was pale, discolored, dizzy and light-headed.
- He says this was the worst shape of his life, worse than three Hell Weeks and Ranger School. He thought he was literally dying.

## Making the Impossible Small: How He Got Out of the Chair

- He says the mind, put in a quiet, calm place, lets you ask honestly: "How bad are you really?"
- He calmed himself down and "had to make this enormous thing small." He broke the 30 remaining miles into small chunks.
- He fixed the immediate problems first: water, potassium, sodium, and the dizziness, so he could stand. After about an hour he could stand.
- He then moved around the track at a pace of 30-some minutes per mile. His crew told him at mile 81 that he would not make the time.

## When Mind, Body and Spirit Connect

David says that once the mind knows you won't quit, it "is going to try to give you more." When it realizes you won't take the path of least resistance and will stay until it's done, it finds more.

- He says his mind, body and spirit "became one for the first time ever."
- He ran the final 19 miles and finished 101 miles in 18 hours and 56 minutes, in the worst shape he'd ever been in.
- He calls it the final crucible after overcoming many obstacles in his life, and says it brought "such clarity" and was "the most amazing thing I'll ever do in my life."
