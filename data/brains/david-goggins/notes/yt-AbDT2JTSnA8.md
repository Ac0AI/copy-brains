## "Perform without purpose": training the mind to act with no goal in sight

- David coined "perform without purpose": people wait for a 5K, a 10K, or a "golden carrot" before they move, and that is what he used to do.
- For 20-some years he has not needed a target. The purpose, he argues, is you: "do you want to be a [ __ ] today, do you want to feel like a little [ __ ]... knowing that you could but you didn't?"
- He wants people to learn to get up and do the work when nothing is on the calendar and life "sucks" ("you're in the dungeon"), so they are ready when real purpose arrives.
- Event goals also create a finish line that lets people rest. As he gets better, the "ah" moment shrinks, and each day he re-earns his eight hours off by working first.
- Every day follows the same loop: it sucks, then you feel better. Each step of a daily run is "beating the demon again", even though he hates running.
- He also admits hesitation: some days he stares at his shoes for half an hour before putting them on, and he says people need to hear that.

## Mental aid stations and front loading

- **Mental aid stations:** instead of vacations, he treats the shower, the car and meals as moments when he thinks about nothing (no work, running or people). This lets him grind hard the rest of the day without burnout.
- **Front loading:** he realized he might not be able to do any of this at 47 or 50, so he packed his early life with work: a "fat" military resume, money made, hard races done, pull-up records.
- Payoff: "if you frontload it properly the back half of your [ __ ] life is money." He worked while his body could.
- When doctors told him he might not run again and mentioned knee replacements, he was fine with it. "I gave everything," so he has no regret.
- He has "always prepared my mind for the next chapter" and would swim or go to college if he could not run. He gives himself only "a couple minutes" to lie in it after bad news, then asks what's next.
- He says Kobe walked off the court without crying because he had given everything.

## Pushing through the knee ruin: ignoring feelings and the surgery story

- His knees were misaligned from birth (he also cites sickle cell and other health issues), not simply worn out by running.
- Timeline he gave:
  - Injured a leg around 2001 during BUD/S, kept going on it, got cortisone shots, and later stopped squatting but kept running.
  - Surgeon cleaned up a meniscus so hard it reportedly broke tools. Afterward his leg "dangled" and he was worse, getting knees drained and PRP.
  - Ran a 240-mile race at Moab in October 2020 (second place, 62 hours), with his knee drained a few days earlier for a baseball-sized Baker's cyst.
  - Dr. Gamal (New York) said he did not know how David ran 240 miles on those knees, and offered a high tibial osteotomy (HTO): break and realign the tibia, shifting load to his healthy lateral meniscus.
  - The surgery was about 17-18 months before the interview; he feels the plate on every step.
- His mindset: "Sometimes you have to ignore your feelings." Some mornings he says "[ __ ] your feelings," not literally, but meaning go beyond what you feel. Pain became his "new norm."
- He frames it as choosing between sitting down and seeing how far the human body can go.

## Studying the darkness and clearing the mental garage

- He says he "studies the darkness" because no answers come from the light. Happy times need no teaching; dark times do.
- Method:
  - Take mental "snapshots" during suffering ("I snapshot it because I know I can use that later").
  - Come home and write it out by hand on scratch paper. Most of his book was written that way.
  - Layer and break down what led up to each moment. His runs are his "mental lab."
- He writes from scratch, not from success: "I can't write at David Goggins now... I must write at David Goggins, [ __ ] man I'm a [ __ ]." Growth, he argues, is down in the muck, not up high, so he keeps going back to school and the basics (he is a wildland firefighter and smoke jumper).
- **Mental garage:** discipline tips like waking early or cold showers are "cookie cutter" because a mind full of clutter (kids, bills, boss, regrets) has no room for discipline or consistency. A cluttered mind "overloads like a circuit breaker." Clear the space first, then the habits can fit.
- Ultras compress learning: 62 hours equals "seven years" of knowledge, and you learn to chunk the race ("how the [ __ ] am I getting from mile 100 to 118").

## Going beyond talent and building belief

- Talent only takes you to its edge. After that it is a mental game, and most people can perform only to their talent. Fighters who are elite in the gym fall apart when tired and thinking about quitting.
- He had little talent, so he trained his mind early: "I invented Goggins" to out-strength people mentally.
- Talented people can coast early and never develop the mental game. The fix is to train past the talent level, into the "deep end," so you are ready if you ever need it. Long camps away from phones, comfort and attention (mountains, "Dark Matter") do this.
- Belief: because he started "so far down underneath soil," a 5-mile run could not build it. He built belief by repeatedly doing extreme things ("200 mile run... 100 mile run... late nights in the gym"). "Without belief you have nothing."
- The rock metaphor: each day you fight not wanting to get up and do it anyway, you chip another piece off the rock. Eventually you have a finished piece of art, and the journey becomes real.
- He was fat (about 300 lb) and ran past a graveyard thinking "don't die like this."

## Hard conversations with yourself: the morning meeting and the "part-time Savage" lesson

- The "morning meeting" with yourself: wake up and ask what you did wrong yesterday, where you were weak, and analyze your life. People hold meetings for their bosses but not for themselves.
- Around 2018 he slipped into being a "part-time Savage": bacon and eggs, protein shakes, two-a-days, about 50 miles a week, and he "stopped having those hard conversations."
- Fix: stop letting the "demon" quit at 10 miles ("there's somebody out there doing 12, 13, 14, going to do another one"). He resumed the hard talks.
- He says he would rather be hated and improve than liked and stay the same. He also applies this to others: he tells people hard truths he sees (weight, procrastination), because staying silent does them "zero justice."
- He rejects excuse culture: "It's okay" narratives are a "get out of jail free card." He asks people to work harder and discipline their minds, not to accept it. He was fat himself and knows the pull of cinnamon buns and milkshakes.
- Accountability: on failure he sees "attempts, not failures"; "not trying is failure." Even someone paralyzed trying one step has made a first attempt.
- He says "the only thing that changes your DNA is discipline."

## Haters, the SEAL lies, and being the standard

- He addressed a former SEAL Team Six member who spread lies (dishonorable discharge, never deployed, froze in combat).
- His facts: two deployments (Guam and Iraq), a medal from Iraq, Ranger school honor man, lead navigator on a PSD detail in Iraq's Red Zone. He missed more deployments because of two heart surgeries (hole in his heart) and recruiting duty. He put his proof in the book "Can't Hurt Me" for liars.
- Instead of suing after hiring a lawyer, he dropped it: "I'm not going to take money out of your kids' mouth." He studied the man and concluded he had demons.
- Views on haters:
  - "Crabs in a bucket": people judge themselves against someone who goes farther.
  - "You will never meet a hater doing better than you."
  - Entertaining haters steals mental bandwidth from loved ones and goals.
  - He reads hate mail and plays it back as motivation ("make a mixtape out of your [ __ ] ass").
- The uncommon road is lonely, so he surrounds himself with people who want to improve ("I'm your biggest cheerleader").
- A message from parents about a bullied boy who died by suicide reached him months late and drives him to be honest and better every day.

## Cold plunges, hard workouts and fighters who go to the edge

- He described his current approach: wake up, wear only underwear in 40 degree weather, lift the lid on a cold plunge with ice floating, and stay in 3 minutes before working out. "Many dreams die while suffering."
- Hard sessions he likes: a Cam Haynes run (20 miles holding about six-minute miles, then light-weight, high-rep supersets of push-ups, curls, pull-ups and triceps, then steak as a "mental aid station").
- On fighters: the mental game appears when the walls close in. He watches fights for the "one second decision" before a tap, such as when Volkanovski escaped a mounted guillotine. He says a fighter should write down the million thoughts that kept him from tapping, because that knowledge is "priceless."
- He says older athletes face "father fatigue" (the daily grind) more than father time.
