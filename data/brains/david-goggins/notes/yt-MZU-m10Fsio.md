## Callousing the mind and the discipline of tiny tasks

- David learned discipline from his grandfather, Sergeant Jack, who retired after 30-some years in the military and wore his uniform every day. Jack gave him no pity: "No one really gives a [expletive]" about what happened to him, so he could keep living or "become a statistic."
- Jack made David cut grass, rake leaves, and clean cars before and after school, and he never said "good job." Tasks were redone until they were right (one example: a thin white wall on a black tire).
- David learned to do the job right the first time rather than 20 times. The satisfaction became internal: "I don't need a pat on the back."
- His rule: "I see dirt, don't pass the dirt, clean the dirt." If a half-assed job doesn't bother you, he says, it speaks volumes about you, and you will half-ass your life.
- He wrote Never Finished from the "calloused mind" rather than from Yale, Princeton, or Harvard. He calls it a "diploma in hard knocks."

## The Goggins alter ego and visualization

- Discipline alone wasn't enough. In pararescue training he hit the water, which he hated, and found that his ordinary mind couldn't carry him through the highest levels of suffering.
- He went into his "mental lab" and visualized a person with a "cast iron steel" mind that is "never dull" and always sharp. He wanted a version of himself that stayed and "marinated" in fear and suffering instead of running.
- He built his own training ground before Navy SEAL training, saying that anyone who arrives unprepared will quit.
- In the pool, Goggins would appear when things got hard, and "smack" David down and drag him through.
- His point: "There's no David Goggins Goggins. There's just David Goggins." Only his mindset changes. The pain stays the same.

## The Moab 240 porta potty moment

- At about mile 200 of his second Moab 240 (240 miles, over 31,000 ft of elevation gain), he had a raw backside, broken feet, six layers of tape, and trouble breathing at altitude. He had 40 miles left.
- He lied to his pacer, Mike, about needing the bathroom, then sat in the porta potty wishing he would fall in and break a leg so he couldn't finish.
- Goggins appeared and reminded him that the course beat him in 2019, that he had trained on bad knees and visualized the course a million times, and that he was now lying to his pacer.
- He says he "knifed" David Goggins and stuck him in the porta potty, and Goggins came out. His feet and back still hurt and the pack was just as heavy. Only his approach changed: he stopped being a victim of the race.
- He ran 7-minute miles over the last stretch and dropped Mike. He used Mike's anger as fuel: "I want him to be mad at me so he can bring the best out of me."
- His advice is to keep what you want to be in the front of your mind, because you will lose it in the suffering. He says most suffering is psychological, not physical.

## Hope vs. belief, and who the devil is

- Hope is "[expletive]" because it isn't in your hands. In SEAL training, people hoped to be pulled from the cold water.
- Belief is not the "after-school special" kind. It is harnessed through hard work and dedication, and you know what you can do because you have gone to the dark places several times.
- He kept returning to SEAL training because he believed he could make it. He knew his past failures were on him, because he wasn't executing as he should.
- He grew up watching his mother go through horrific things and was never sheltered from them. He compares it to a front-row seat at a horror movie.
- He blamed his father as the devil. When he confronted him in Buffalo, he realized "I was the true devil," the one holding himself back and looking for an escape goat. His father and mother were broken and would not save him: "You got to save yourself."
- The drive home, he says, convinced him the rest of his life would be hard because he would finally start winning.

## Taking souls and haters

- Taking souls is rising above what a normal person would be in a situation where a normal person would be defeated, while the people around you know it. He took it from Hell Week: everyone is defeated by Wednesday, but he acted as if it were minute one, and instructors saw it.
- His boat crew thought "what one man can do, another can do" and elevated themselves, and the other crews' stunned faces became more ammunition. In his words, you "thrive in that environment" where most won't.
- People who aren't working hard can feel judged around him even though he says nothing. He compares himself to a metal detector on the beach: they run through their own "resume of life" and realize they haven't done enough.
- The hater's mixtape: he recorded everything people said about him into his phone, added beats and Eminem tracks, and played it on a loop as fuel. He studied the "mind of the weak" and concluded that you'll never meet a hater who is doing better than you. Successful people give criticism meant to help, not trash talk.
- Haters know more about him than his mother does, so he gives them "more fuel" to hate him.

## Having the right people in the foxhole

- He started life far behind: "negative grades," having to "dig out of the grave to get to first grade." So 24 hours feels like three to five days of catching up, and he describes going to "summer school in my mind."
- The right foxhole people don't ask whether a 3:00 a.m. wake-up is smart or tell him to take a break. He needs pushing, pulling, anger, and passion, not critiquing.
- He supports everything you do, not everything you don't do. In a firefight, you don't need someone "blowing noses and wiping butt holes."
- Early in life he attracted losers because he had no "self" (self-esteem, self-awareness, self-belief, self-motivation, self-discipline). His biggest regret is not building himself fast enough, which cost him years in the "dungeon": "Everything has an expiration date."

## Staying disciplined with success: being "feelingless"

- Success makes it harder to be Goggins: nicer beds and "silk sheets" make a 3:00 a.m. wakeup harder.
- His fix is to go back to the old school and become "feelingless" about wanting to be better. When the alarm goes off, in cold or rain, or when everyone goes home and one office light is on in a dark 15-story building, the feelings must go away.
- He says this doesn't mean turning off feelings toward family. It means turning them off in the grind.
- He didn't have talent: he shares only a birthday (February 17) with Michael Jordan and Jim Brown and can't dunk. What he could do was outwork everyone, so he gravitated to ordinary people who showed courage, character, commitment, and discipline. He calls the place where greatness begins the "blue to black line."

## Quitting, regret, and being a game changer

- When he's considering quitting, he lists every option in the situation and then tries pairing options together. Only after exhausting every option, in his words "the accountability way beyond some accountability mirror," might he say it's not for him. He says almost no one does this, so the answer is "almost never." It takes him years, not months.
- He "front-loaded" his life: at 47 he is "100% retired," and the payoff is choices. He can say no to 99% of offers and doesn't need the money.
- Never Finished is a deeper, more philosophical book than Can't Hurt Me, which he calls a "bachelor's degree." He wrote the first one so people would know who he was. He says a third book is almost done and "puts that book to shame."
- A game changer isn't arrogant. The belief is built through hard work, so you know your value and bring "the difference to the table." You've been "training my entire life to take the big shot."
