## Childhood abuse and the origin of his fight

David grew up in Buffalo, New York, with a father he calls "a devil," a violent drunk who beat him, his brother and his mother. He says his father "helped create this."

- At about seven or eight, he watched his father beat and drag his mother down the stairs by her hair. His brother hid in his room; David stayed and jumped on his father's back. His father beat him "from my neck down to my ankles like black and blue."
- Staying in the room and acting while scared became a pattern in how he handles fear.
- His mother later told him and his brother "you guys can come or you guys can stay but I'm getting the [ __ ] out of here." He went with her to Brazil, Indiana.
- At the VFW Americanism Award speech, he thanked his mother "for not picking me up when I was knocked down but teach me how to get up." He sobbed on stage for 58 seconds, then finished the speech.
- Later, as a man in his 20s, he went back to face his father. He saw a man still unchanged and reasoned that something had damaged his father and he never dealt with it, so he passed it on. David's conclusion: "I'm gonna deal with my [ __ ]," fix what he was handed, and not blame it.

## Trauma, learning disabilities and the accountability mirror

David arrived in Indiana hiding learning disabilities, a stutter and stress that made his hair fall out in patches. He couldn't read or write well and was held back in second grade. He was also one of five or six Black kids in a school of about 1,700, with a noose and a threat drawn in his notebook. A witnessed child death and the murder of his soon-to-be stepdad in the same period left him sleeping on his floor for months. He scammed his way through school while his mother worked three jobs and never saw a report card.

- His mother's letter from the school (he'd missed about 25% of classes and was going to flunk out) was the trigger. Her only advice was that he would flunk out.
- He saw that "no one is coming to help me," not his principal, his mom or his dad. Looking in the mirror he said he was "defeated," and started writing sticky notes calling himself out.
- This became his accountability mirror: the honest conversation with yourself where the only person responsible for the change is you. He says the problem was his own mind, not anyone else.
- He later let that mirror go dirty. Spraying for cockroaches, at nearly 300 pounds, he stopped seeing himself honestly. "You can't lie to yourself," even though he could lie to everyone else.

## Learning by repetition: the study grind

David's tutor could only be afforded for four hours a month for six months. He started at a fourth-grade reading level, and the tutor told him he'd have to write things down a thousand times to remember them. He took it literally.

- He bought spiral notebooks and wrote the same math equations over and over.
- For reading comprehension, he copied whole paragraphs repeatedly.
- He got the Navy dive manual a year ahead and wrote the book out about 14 times (Boyle's law, Charles's law, Gay-Lussac's law, Dalton's law). He can still recall a page and the act of writing it.
- His point: people think he became who he is from running. "No, I became this guy from [ __ ] study." What takes others an hour took him two days, so he put in the hours.
- He passed the ASVAB and got into the Air Force.

## Quitting, and why he admits it

David entered Air Force pararescue and taught himself to swim, but he was negative buoyant and terrified of the water confidence training. He wanted to quit every night and slept badly. He then got a sickle cell diagnosis and was pulled from training, which was a relief because he could drop without quitting.

- When the doctor offered to put him back in with a week and some change left, he didn't want it. He lied to his commanding officer, citing sudden death risk, and took a medical out.
- He now realizes the officer knew he was quitting: "as a man, you can tell another man where the man's done." He saw that look in the eyes of the quitters when he later watched SEAL training footage.
- Why he admits it: if he only told the badass stories, he'd be a liar and a superhero fake. "I wasn't born that. I had to make myself into this [ __ ]," and he wants people to know he was a scared, depressed, insecure kid.
- Fixing the surface wasn't enough: "I'm fixing everything on the surface, I'm not going into the dungeon." People want peace without confronting their own stuff, and he says you won't find it in a yoga room: "You're gonna find peace from going to war with yourself."

## The turning point at 297 pounds and getting into SEAL training

From about 175 pounds in 1994 to 297 pounds by 1998-99, David was out of the military, sprayed for cockroaches for around $1,000 a month and ate constantly (a milkshake from a restaurant account, a box of mini chocolate doughnuts eaten "like tic tacs," eight Pillsbury cinnamon rolls, scrambled eggs, half a pound of bacon, fruity pebbles).

- One morning he was watching a Navy SEAL "hell week" documentary in a towel with a shake. He saw guys quit and recognized his own look from pararescue.
- At graduation, a commander said "we live in a society where mediocrity is often rewarded" and that the 22 graduates detested mediocrity. David wanted to feel like those men. He was tired of lying to himself and others. He knew what he could be but was afraid the work would kill him.
- He quit his job (a roach-and-rodent dumpster night pushed him over) and started training. His first run was a quarter mile at 297, and he came home crying.
- A recruiter said he had a "lofty task": he had about three months to drop roughly 100 pounds. He dropped to about 215 on the way.
- He had to retake the ASVAB and get a 50 on mechanical comprehension. He begged the test sergeant for the score and got exactly a 50. He compares it to the stair run in The Pursuit of Happyness.
- His point: no single breakthrough fixed him. Multiple "changed" moments (the mirror, seeing his dad, the roaches) each needed follow-through. "Life is not a movie."

## Three hell weeks, the broken body and the duct tape

David went through SEAL training (BUD/S) three times because the commander rolled back anyone who failed back to day one, week one.

- First class: stress fractures, shin splints and double pneumonia rolled him back before finishing.
- Second class: he finished hell week but broke his kneecap, and it was done ("your patella is done"). He was determined not to get rolled again, but he was.
- Third class: he got to come back with only a few months to heal a broken kneecap and stress fractures. His body was swollen by Monday of hell week. Hell week ended early on Thursday when a classmate died of pulmonary edema.
- Walk week: the instructors, angry about the death, made them stay on their feet and ruck the whole time, so there was no healing. He left home early, went to the dive cage at about 4 a.m., put on a black sock and casted his shins and ankles with duct tape as tightly as he could. The first 45 minutes were "excruciating," then his legs went numb and he could run.
- He says his stress fractures actually healed by the third phase. He's "not saying do that," but that was the choice: do it or don't become a SEAL.

## The 40% rule and what separates finishers

David says the 40% rule is a belief he built through pain. A fat guy who quit on everything became one of the toughest men on the planet because he changed one thing: his mindset.

- The governor analogy: some cars have a governor that caps speed at 92 mph. The factory that put it there was his own mind, and you have to take it off or you'll keep hitting the cap.
- On what separates people who finish anything hard: you have to really want to be there, and you have to have lived the suffering in your mind a million times, not just visualized the graduation. "You gotta be there in the worst and the suffering that you know over and over again."
- When suffering hits, your mind starts asking why you're doing this. If you've already pre-lived it, you have the answers ready. If you don't want it that badly, you quit.
- Inspiration in a comfortable setting (couch, fridge, a movie about badasses) disappears when the "suck factor" is real.
- His warning about "only one time": telling yourself you'll only do the hard thing once is how you set yourself up to be done. Even Special Ops guys do this. He sees athletes and others stuck in an identity built on one great accomplishment.
- He likes competition and suffering because it brings out the best in everyone. "Once you change one thing, your mindset, you can attack everything."

## Badwater 135: the cookie jar and 19 hours of suffering

With no running base (his cardio was 20 minutes a week on an elliptical), David signed up for Badwater 135, a 135-mile race through Death Valley. The race director told him he needed a 100-mile finish in 24 hours or less, so he entered a one-mile-loop race in San Diego that Saturday.

- He ran in a hat with no shirt, fueled by Ritz crackers and no water. At mile 70 he sat down and fell apart, urinating blood, with 30 miles left.
- The cookie jar: "We forget how badass we are when we're suffering because our mind stays right there in suffering." The cookie jar is his stock of things he's accomplished and kept grinding through. He reached into it to remember.
- "I might quit, but not yet." He gave his mind space: sat, drank water, ate, stood up, and told himself he might quit later rather than panicking over 30 miles.
- He got moving again and ended up running about 20 miles at roughly a 10-15 minute mile pace, finishing 100 miles in 19 hours and 6 minutes.
- Afterward he sat in a shower, in the fetal position, passing dark brown fluid, and refused to see a doctor or take painkillers. He says the pain was proof that he'd done something unprepared, untrained and uncoached. "It was the best feeling I ever had in my life, not because of the pain, but the pain was confirmation."
- He says he learned more in those 19 hours than in three hell weeks, Ranger School and the rest, because it ran him through every emotion.

## Staying hungry, "taking souls" and who he's chasing

David says he has to keep grinding himself "into a fine dust." What makes him him is "the dungeon," and he's only content when failing at something repeatedly until he finally completes it. He warns against settling into a place of comfort or coasting on past achievements.

- On social media and fame: he didn't set out to be a motivational figure, and he doesn't want to be pulled away from what built him. Fame can be a depression because you lose yourself in helping people. He went back to running 100 miles a week.
- His motivation: the stories in his book come from when he was living them, not from telling them. If he stops, he's no longer the person people follow.
- The pull-up record: he failed twice and finally got it (4,030 pull-ups in 17 hours), listening to one 14th-round Rocky track on a loop for 17 hours.
- "Taking souls": in the Rocky fight, he takes Apollo Creed's look when Rocky keeps getting up as the thing he wants from doubters. "I wanted a lot of people that doubted me to look like Apollo Creed did in that 14th round because I got back up repeatedly." He wants opponents to be tired from his refusal to stop.
- He says he doesn't care about winning: "I just want to go the distance."
- People who knew him at his worst, he says, try to pull him back down and don't like where they are compared with where he got to.
