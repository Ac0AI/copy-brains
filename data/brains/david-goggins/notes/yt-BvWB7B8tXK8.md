## Callousing the mind: why David trains

- David worked out as a teenager for mental reasons, not physical ones: "I saw working out as a way for me to build calluses on my mind. I had to callous over the victim's mentality."
- He did not care about losing weight, speed, or the Olympics. He could barely read and write as a high school junior.
- He chose training that looked brutal (waking at 5 a.m., running a golf course) and made a list of things he didn't want to do, then did them.
- He argues repetition of what you don't want to do "armors" the mind: "We suffer every day, it's what we do, so when the suck stuff comes you're ready for it."
- This built a work ethic, and the work ethic built self-esteem. Once he had self-esteem he stopped caring about being judged.
- He says the benefit has nothing to do with how he looks: "I do it to become better mentally." He has no trainer, reads no exercise science, and keeps training to "continue to harden" his mind.
- On music: he never trains with it because it won't always be there. "What do you do when you have no external motivation? It's about the internal." He did say he listened to the Rocky "round 14" track on repeat for 17 hours during a pull-up record attempt, a deliberate exception he used to go to a dark place.

## Failure, the "sword in the fire" method, and the accountability mirror

- David says he is not a theorist. He put himself "in the fire repeatedly like a sword," beat it, and dissected his mind while it was suffering: "You can't dissect anything in 72 degree weather. You must put it in the freezer."
- Cycle: fail, reflect, reboot, repeat. Earlier fixes were surface-level (learning to read, passing the ASVAB). He never asked what made him a quitter, so he kept falling back to the start.
- He heard a persistent inner voice pointing him toward the hard place ("that rock pile in the corner where nobody's at, that's where victory's at") and says he avoided it because it meant looking in the mirror and being accountable.
- He describes the "accountability mirror" moment: he saw his persona (shaved-top haircut, backwards sagging pants, a toothbrush in his mouth) as a costume and a "clown" act.
- He says the mind "has the tactical advantage" because it knows your fears and insecurities: "You can't hide from it, so don't ever try."
- He says being honest about his flaws forces consistency: "You know you did it today, and you have to know you'll do it tomorrow." His biggest fear is that he can slip.

## Reframing adversity and forgiving his father

- David reframes his childhood (abusive father, a stepdad-to-be murdered, learning disabilities, bullying) as "the perfect training ground." He compares a hard past to walking in muck and not seeing the sidewalk just to the left.
- Becoming "a master of what I was scared of," his own mind, is what he says sets him apart.
- He says he got fixated on people with good upbringings who "didn't have to struggle," then realized everyone has a "jacked up life"; some just lack the guts to talk about it.
- On his father: he went back at 22, saw him "through a grown man's eyes," and later forgave him (his father died about four or five years before this interview; David did not attend the funeral).
- His method: study, don't judge. "Why did they do that? Not in a judging way. I want to learn from you." He traces his dad's behavior to insecurity that was never fixed and passed to the next person in line.
- He says holding on to the hate was half the reason he kept failing: "I had to start dumping off some baggage." Pain's origin is often elsewhere: "If your knee hurts it's usually not your knee."
- He also says to watch your company. People from your worst period will try to pull you back, and he says some stretches require being alone.

## 106 pounds in three months and the 100-mile race that opened his mind

- Weight: he weighed 297 lb at the Navy scale and was told to lose 106 pounds to qualify for SEAL training in about three months (he calls it "almost impossible"). He had been 175 lb in high school.
- He was spraying for cockroaches at the time and lay in his mother's pond in November in Indiana, then went for runs in his freezing, wet clothes.
- He says quick fixes like stomach stapling and "six-minute abs" don't last: "If you earn it you'll keep it."
- First 100-mile race: no training. He lifted heavy (squats, deadlifts, power cleans, bench) the night before, partly to show his old SEAL instructor what a human can do. He says the experience compressed "five years of struggle" into 19 hours, with highs and lows to manage. Afterward he was urinating dark "Coca-Cola" colored urine (likely rhabdo) and thought he wouldn't get out of the bathtub.
- A few weeks later he ran the Las Vegas Marathon on the strip with no marathon training and finished in about 3:08, after talking himself forward the entire way. He took away: "What have I been leaving on the table?" That is when he got fascinated with pain management and the mind.
- His rule on "when should I quit?": those questions come from people looking to quit. "The body will adjust."

## Taking souls and finding strength in misery (BUD/S Hell Week)

- In Hell Week, David noticed instructors work eight-hour shifts, judging the trainees, and decided to play mind games with them.
- Tactic "taking souls": have your boat crew visibly refuse to break. On Wednesday, when everyone is wrecked and the instructors have told you that you should be tired, David told his boat crew: "We own space in their head." They kept charging and got more rest because instructors stopped targeting them.
- He rejects being told how to feel ("Don't let these guys tell you how you're supposed to feel. It's day one, this is hour one.")
- Principle: nothing wants to fight something relentless. "You must break the soul of whatever is in front of you." One second of energy stolen from others is enough to fuel you.
- He learned to "control his adrenals" and find strength when everyone else is miserable, then lead others once they see you hold up.
- Open-mindedness for big tasks: when told the pull-up record was 4,020, he got a pen and paper and did the math, rather than shutting down. "Once you shut your mind down to the possibility, there's no way it can happen."
- He also says he trained through injuries (stress fractures taped up with duct tape and a sock, pneumonia) in BUD/S, and notes they healed by third phase. He says this "isn't real smart" and tells people not to copy it.

## Daily routine, heart-rate training, and recovery

- No days off: seven days a week.
- Typical week: at least a 7-mile run on a "light" day; calisthenics plus a gym workout about every other day (roughly four days a week); he never lifts without pull-ups and push-ups ("nickels and dimes"): for example 5 pull-ups and 10 push-ups, or bigger sets of 25 pull-ups and 50 push-ups.
- Stretching: about two hours every night while watching TV or sports. He holds stretches for long periods to restore full range of motion, which he says had been the root of his tightness, stress fractures, and tendinitis.
- Results: his zone-2 run pace dropped from about 8:30/mile to roughly 7:20 at the same heart rate after the stretching opened up his stride. He stopped needing heel wedges, compression tape and inserts.
- Heart rate training: he runs easy at roughly 147-152 bpm (he says his zone 2 was around 140), which burns fat and recovers quickly (back under 100 within two to three minutes). He checks his resting heart rate in the morning on a Polar chest strap or by finger. If it is elevated, he stays in zone 1 or 2 ("active recovery," about 6 to 7 easy miles).
- He says active recovery is movement, not rest: an easy bike spin plus hydration and carbohydrate drink to reload glycogen.
- He says long cardio quiets mental chatter: "Evil can't stay in a 10-mile run" and calls it "the great purge."
- Injury history he mentions: a hole in the heart (ASD) repaired by a catheter patch, which cost him about three to four years off jump and dive status; he also trained a day-of 10-mile ruck before one heart surgery.
- Pull-up record: he did about 3,000 pull-ups before landing in the hospital with rhabdo on his second attempt, was home that night, back on the bar about a week and a half later, and broke the record six weeks later. He had sickle cell trait and warns this was dangerous and not smart.

## No finish line, and why he seeks new challenges

- David says there is no "golden years" or finish line: "There's no end." People want to rest after the struggle, but you only appreciate rest if you earn it.
- He says growth requires friction. His fear is a judgment day where God shows him who he was supposed to be and he finds he left it on the table. He wants to "drain my soul of every bit of person I am" so nothing is left.
- He goes "back to scratch" regularly and "pays rent" on the $7-a-month place he lived in as a kid, to remember the fundamentals that made him.
- He took up wildland firefighting (hot shot crews) after 21 years in the military: a three-mile fire line, 16 to 17 hours a day, sleeping on a mat on the ground, for little pay. He was 43 and did two half seasons, and applied to smokejump next. He says it keeps him humble ("a rookie at 43"), keeps his hands calloused, and is about human growth, not money.
- His analogy for motivation: a spark leads to one-hour fuel, then ten-hour fuel, then big logs that burn for days. He seeks sparks, like running with 20-year-olds.
- He turned down a $30,000 book offer in 2012 and later a $300,000 offer, and says the biggest trophy is what's in the book, not sales. He says if you have to be on your own, with no crowd, trainer, or finish line, the discipline is internal.

## Authenticity, procrastination, and self-help that works

- David criticizes motivational speakers who don't live it ("fat as hell, big old belly"). He says he can't speak to people unless he is authentic; even a white lie bothers him.
- He says he procrastinates "like crazy" too: he sometimes stares at his running shoes for 30 minutes, thinks about the run all day, and goes anyway. Days without time to dread are "beautiful days" because you just have to go.
- Rejects "tricks" and shortcut language: "You can't Google how to suffer." Advice: "Don't overthink it. Put your shoes on and lace them up."
- He says admitting flaws shows more strength than a fake front of perfection, and that once he believed in himself he could tell his messed-up stories without lying.
- He says he told people he is not perfect and never will be, but is "better than yesterday." He blocks and deletes trolls and rest-and-recovery excuse-makers from his pages and wants a community of people helping each other get better (he mentions people losing 150 pounds and sharing stories on his site).
- He says people looking for a reason to quit ask when they should quit, and that criticisms of him often reveal the critic's own issues: "I know something about you."
