## Laziness is beaten by raising your standard, not by waiting for motivation

- David argues motivation is not a permanent fix. "You have to learn to perform without motivation. You have to learn to perform without purpose."
- Motivation is "kindling to the fire"; all it takes is a spark to burn a forest, but you must be your best self "when you're least motivated."
- Most people fail because they think they need motivation to work out or study. If they don't have it, they don't do it.
- His test for laziness: if you wake up and don't want to do the thing, you don't care enough about yourself. Research why you aren't doing it for yourself.
- Hard days stack up (cold, dark, warm couch, argument last night, hungover). He says that is exactly how you level up, and he is happy that so many people with ability refuse to get off the couch, because that gives him the advantage.
- "It's so easy to be great nowadays because most people are weak." Most won't go the extra mile because it is miserable and lonely, and he thrives in that loneliness.
- Performance without a purpose: even with no race, class or goal on the docket, keep performing at the highest level, because when the thing finally arrives you won't be ready unless you were already training.
- "You are always the purpose." The main purpose in life is to better oneself, and that is the only purpose he needs.

## The one second decision

- Pain pushes the brain into fight or flight, and it is hard to process information rationally while in pain.
- He uses SEAL Hell Week (about 130 hours) as the example. It starts as a pep rally with guns and grenade simulators, then goes quiet and marches you into the surf for surf torture. Your mind jumps from hour 2 to hour 130 and you forget every reason you wanted to be there.
- In that moment, all you want is warmth, food, and your girl holding you. That is the one second where people lose; lose it once and you lose the whole thing.
- His tactic: physically stay in the water (getting out is quitting), but mentally go stand on the beach with the instructors in their Parkas with coffee. Now "warm" mentally, think logically.
- Then ask: where are you going to end up if you quit? What will you say to yourself? He projects forward to the long-term shame and regret versus the pride of finishing.
- He says people who quit Ranger school, SEAL training or Air Force training still call him decades later, unable to enjoy their lives because they are thinking about what they could have been.
- "I don't look at failure anymore as failure. I look at it as my first, second and third attempt."
- Daily question: "Can I take one more step?" Usually the answer is yes. Not taking it when you can is real quitting, and "that's on me and I gotta live with that."

## Moab 240: re-qualify mental toughness and make the new level your norm

- Mental toughness is "a perishable skill." Going through training once does not last forever. He says you must recall it and re-qualify "every day, not once a year."
- Before Moab he had gone about 6 years without a 100-mile race, had heart surgeries and small injuries, and felt he had become "part-time Savage." He decided to push to the next level with the 240-mile race.
- First attempt: he got lost, got sick (pulmonary edema), slept about 12 hours, then got back in the race. A doctor told him that leaving the course meant he couldn't come back, so he left it. The race was already a DNF for him.
- Lying in bed, he heard a voice, "You're not done yet." He woke his wife Jennifer and two crew members, had her drop him at the spot where he left, and finished the last roughly 40 miles. He finished next to a telephone pole, not at the official line, for roughly 250 to 255 miles total. He says it was one of the best races of his life.
- He came back and did it better. His point: pushing a limit makes it your new norm. A 240-mile race became as easy as running 50 once he figured it out.
- At mile 201 on the second attempt his rear was raw like hamburger meat from chafing, and Jennifer applied the cream.

## Gaining knowledge through staying humble, capping success, and the mental lab

- Success can make you soft. His fix is to "cap success": stop hearing yourself talk, get off podcasts and social media, and "get back to the mental lab," because that is where his knowledge comes from.
- He says he does few podcasts, and after finishing a 10-day book push he said he probably wouldn't do another for a very long time. He goes back to firefighting to find the next level.
- He keeps growing at $12 to $15 an hour as a smoke jumper (Wildland firefighting in British Columbia, jumping into spots vehicles can't reach, with gear dropped separately, up to 7 days on the longest job). He turned down millions to do it, and says the growth is in the freezing, questioning-yourself moments.
- Trained humility: stay hungry but stay humble within the hunger. He cites William Crawford, a Medal of Honor winner who worked as a janitor at the Air Force Academy, cleaned for young cadets, and treated himself as no better than anyone.
- His message must not be cookie cutter. It needs to be broad enough that all 25 people in a room get something, which requires constant reinvention.
- "Take what I say with a grain of salt." His mission is different from yours.
- On the SEAL tear gas story and Elon Musk demanding hard work at Twitter: he doesn't judge. Some people want that environment, and society needs people willing to do those jobs. If you don't understand someone like him, it probably means he is in an area of life you're not in.

## Organizing the mind: the garage, meditation, and the daily routine

- Discipline and motivation don't work if the brain is cluttered. He compares the mind to a cluttered garage where you can't find your dumbbells. You need it organized (he calls it "mental zones") so discipline and consistency each have a shelf.
- Life stressors (kids, wife, job) all stuffed in your head are why people fall off the wagon.
- He meditates and stretches about two hours every night (one 4-hour block combined with stretching, per the conversation) to "reorganize the garage."
- Morning routine:
  - Up about 5 to 5:30.
  - Fasted run every morning, minimum 12 miles, in heart rate zone 2 at roughly 8:15 to 8:30 pace, about 1.5 to 2 hours. He is retraining from scratch after his leg injury.
  - Run is his "cup of coffee" because it's what he hates most. He calls it armoring: "armor plate my mind and body like a person going to war" before opening his phone.
  - Eats something small, then gym 45 minutes to 1.5 hours.
  - Stationary bike 3 to 4 days a week, plus stretching and meditation at night.
  - Sleeps 7 to 8 hours.
- This has been his routine for seven years, and he says the only reason he can fit it all is that he caps success.
- He doesn't carry his phone; it is the biggest distraction and he wants to be present.

## Building real confidence through proof, not affirmations

- Skeptical of pounding your chest and shouting affirmations in the mirror. What works for him is an everyday resume of real accomplishments: "the real calluses on my mind, the real calluses on my hands."
- He agrees with the idea of a "stack of undeniable proof": three Hell Weeks, Ranger school, Delta selection.
- "Built belief" versus "after school special belief." Confidence must come from the work you put in, not just being told to believe.
- Don't make dreams your master: people talk about being a Navy SEAL or astronaut and get a dopamine kick from the talking, then the 4 a.m. alarm goes off and they skip it. You become master of the dream when you do the laundry list of tasks (he lost 106 pounds in under three months to try to become a SEAL).
- Limited horizons: coming from a small Indiana town (about 8,000 people), his mind could only picture what he had seen. He credits being able to dream beyond those four walls.
- He found his first 100-mile race (San Diego 24-hour track, he was a bodybuilder doing 20 minutes of cardio a week) by Googling the world's toughest events to raise money for the Special Operations Warrior Foundation. He says the roughly 31 mile final stretch was the worst pain of his life, yet it showed him "what is possible" and that he had been "underachieving my entire life." He cautions it is a dangerous place (near rhabdo).

## Using haters, bullies and the past as fuel

- Hater mixtape: he reads comments, deletes and blocks the nasty ones from his page, takes a screenshot, and archives them. He turns them into audio and plays them on a loop while running as fuel. His reasoning is that haters are usually underachievers, so he'll keep succeeding to make them question themselves.
- Doesn't take the hate personally. "You'll never meet a hater doing better than you." He studies the haters, since he was once that negative person himself, and feels pity.
- Example: a former SEAL Team Six guy attacked and lied about him; he was ready to sue but after two months decided to feel sorry for him instead, noting the man was in a bad mental place. "Take that one second to pull back and study them" before reacting. People in a good place don't try to destroy you.
- Reverse role model: he watched his father, mother and brother as a blueprint for how not to live. He was the youngest and learned to study people and their energy.
- Facing his father: he drove to Buffalo to face the "unsolved mystery." He researched his father and learned that the man's own father had whipped him in front of an open furnace. He said he went hoping for an apology to justify being a "loser," and instead a voice told him "it's not your dad's fault" and that he would have to make it on his own.
- "Live autopsy": examining why you are dying while still alive. He says he was living every day but really dead until he did this work.
- Face your past: "Don't ever be ashamed of anything you've done in your life. Face it, fix it, make it better." Go through the archives of your life, study it, and use it.
- Chosen suffering prepares you for unchosen suffering: do something every day that sucks.
- Trauma should not become a "Jersey barrier"; it has to be something you can maneuver through quickly.
- On the Huberman VR shark test: it didn't bother him because VR is fake and he works only in reality; if a screen scares you while you sit firmly in a chair, study why.
- Pride and a personal mission statement: have pride in yourself, write your own ethos about who you want to be, and hold yourself accountable to it each morning. "If you don't stand for something, you'll fall for everything."
- "Roger that" in its best sense means "received orders, given, expect results."
