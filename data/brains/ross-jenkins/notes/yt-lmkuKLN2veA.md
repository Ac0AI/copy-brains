## Marketing for results: performance marketing vs. brand spend

Ross defines performance marketing as marketing with attribution: you can see where every sale came from. He contrasts it with brand-style marketing, where a company says "we spend 50 million a year" but cannot say what that returns.

- Brand-style spend: put in $50M, hope some sales appear, no data tying spend to outcome.
- Performance marketing: put in $10,000, track every click and every purchase, and report what came back out.
- Example he gives: a shop owner with a 15-year-old client list that has never been emailed. A $5,000 budget on an email to that list can return far more than it costs, and every spender and clicker is traceable.
- Results depend on the product and on how good the relationship with the audience is. He says there are "100 different variables."

## Winning large clients with case studies

Ross used past results to land UFC as a client (signed the day of the interview).

- He showed case studies of what clients normally invest and how it typically works, rather than pitching in the abstract.
- He treats it as a test case: UFC gave only a tiny slice of its database, but he is running it through ActiveCampaign, where they pay full price for the platform and he gets a large return. His aim is that delivering well opens up the whole list (he guesses around 50 million people).
- He says he has generated over $150 million for clients in roughly five years, and earned over $2 million himself. He started on Upwork when people barely saw freelancing as a career.
- Authority assets: guest-contributed about 40 performance marketing articles to Entrepreneur (he had hoped for 10 a month; the publication is very strict), plus writing for Upwork and ActiveCampaign, and a Forbes feature.

## Client red flags: who he refuses to work with

Ross became a freelancer partly because a former boss scammed him out of $75,000, so he screens clients hard.

His perfect client:
- doesn't try to negotiate on price
- understands his value
- doesn't demand things "tomorrow"
- plays by the rules, with mutual respect ("if you value me, I value you")

Red flags that get a client fired or refused:
- Pitches built on dubious ideas (for example, a person who bought a database and wants to push a crypto product that "tomorrow will be worth a million dollars").
- Mistreating their own staff. He fired a founder who had just sold a company to WeWork for about $12 million because of how he verbally abused his team: "I don't need your money, whereas the people who work for you do."
- Abusive behavior toward him. One client sent abusive messages every Wednesday. After the second occurrence he cut her off, even though her animation-video campaigns were making good sales.

His rule: "if you hit any red flag we're not going to work together, and no matter how much money you've got behind you, we're just not going to work." He sometimes turns down large offers on this basis.

## Cut bad clients quickly to protect your mental health

- Ross says he built internal red flags over four to five years so he can cut people out fast.
- Bad clients "ruin your mental health," and the point of freelancing is a better work-life balance, higher pay and flexibility, not being treated badly.
- Observation on pricing and abuse: at lower hourly rates he got more abuse; at higher rates he got less himself, but saw high-paying clients abuse others. He took that as a signal about the client's character.
- Leading by example matters: how an owner treats staff affects everyone who relies on them.

## The business case for hiring a freelancer over a full-time employee

Ross makes the cost argument for clients:
- A full-time hire in a place like Los Angeles at his skill level can cost roughly $400,000-$500,000 a year all-in (he says "4,000 maybe $500,000"; the transcript is unclear on the exact lower figure).
- A full-time employee also means insurance, equipment, maintenance and retirement contributions.
- A startup rarely has 40-60 hours a week of work for that role anyway. A freelancer can be used flexibly, on the hours actually needed, at a fraction of the cost.
- A freelancer can also be proactive, for example proposing "here's the email newsletter we should send this week."
- He wants freelancing to be seen as a legitimate model, and says the way people treat freelancers needs to evolve.

## Upwork, talent and reputation

- Ross is critical of Upwork's early "high American talent" advertising. He argues there is nothing in a country's DNA that makes its talent better, and talent exists everywhere.
- Examples he gives: Ukrainians as strong designers, Filipinos as strong assistants (caring, good with numbers, good at timekeeping).
- He wants Upwork to educate clients about global talent and to screen out bad clients and bad freelancers more strictly.
- He says there are great clients and talent on Upwork: his clients there have included a UFC partner and someone who ran for US president.
- Reputation system: every client rating counts, so one negative review hurts, which is another reason he avoids clients he can't satisfy or who show red flags.
