## Ross's background and how he got into email marketing

- Ross Jenkins is CEO of DigitalME (digital me.cc), a full-service agency that specializes in email marketing: setup, diagnostics, analytics and audience understanding.
- He has about 12 years in marketing. He started at a UK agency and kept working for agencies afterward.
- He learned what clients hate about agencies: "communication sucked" and agencies "take the money and then work out how you're going to deal with it." His view: "You don't sell a car and hope for the best."
- His first business was a friends-and-family investment called SMS to PayPal. PayPal was hard to fund for under-18s but accepted everywhere, and the product let them fund it by SMS. It worked well and he declined purchase offers.
- That business taught him how SMS works. He then moved to agencies and fell for the "gratification" of campaigns going out and working.
- Clients are mostly under 50 staff, plus some bigger names, including people who have run for US president.

## Going independent and pricing on Upwork

- In Ireland he was getting strong results for agency clients. He made a verbal deal with a CEO, hit his side, and the CEO didn't. He treats that as a lesson not to rely on verbal agreements.
- He tried negotiating a percentage of agency business, then decided to go alone once agencies saw his potential.
- He wanted something like escrow, because clients don't want to send $100,000 up front.
- He joined Upwork when freelancing was small and no one had earned over $100,000.
- Starting rate: $25/hour. Current rate: $365/hour. He reports just under $2 million earned on the platform.
- He stayed exclusive to Upwork on purpose, to focus on one goal. His logic: more earnings on Upwork means more trust and more hires. He avoided Fiverr because of its low-price reputation and the one-off-gig audience.
- Upwork suits his target clients: higher-end, ongoing work of 5-10 hours a week rather than one- or two-hour gigs.
- He still keeps his Upwork profile, accepting the fees, because it works as a marketing platform. Most clients now come direct as the brand has grown.
- Fixed-price quotes: he quotes about 20% over his estimated hourly cost. That covers scope creep and slow-paying clients.

## Channels he sells and why he sticks to them

- Core channels: email, SMS and WhatsApp.
- He avoids ads and other channels because "ads either work amazingly or it's a massive hole." He sticks to what he can build the best performance in.
- He does not do billboards or print.
- He used to offer SEO and web work but dropped them. Clients wanted the cheapest price and tools like Elementor let anyone build their own site. His reasoning: "What's the point in being in another space that we're not going to be the best at?"
- He works with outside specialists when a client needs more. For example, a Facebook ads contractor, paying for a landing page and opt-in form that feeds email on the back end. He trusts partners he has vetted and has spent tens of thousands of dollars on PPC with people he trusts.
- Stack: ActiveCampaign for email and SMS, with a custom tool built on top for WhatsApp.

## SMS and WhatsApp mechanics

- SMS has no spam folder, so messages deliver. The risk is carriers: "phone operators are like the mafia."
- Carriers (AT&T, Verizon in the US; Vodafone and others in the UK) judge by sender reputation. A new sender has none and can't build it without sending.
- One misstep can destroy reputation. Big senders like Domino's and Papa John's have earned a reputation that lets them send weekly. He says money and connections can buy that.
- One client has 10,000 customers who spend tens of thousands of dollars each. They only SMS them three times a year because carriers make it hard.
- Opt-out is a legal requirement for SMS and email. In email, a missing unsubscribe button pushes people to hit spam.
- WhatsApp: strong in the Middle East and Europe, where people don't open email as much but "will WhatsApp you all day." The US uses it less.
- He believes WhatsApp marketing will overtake SMS in the UK and Middle East, helped by Meta releasing an API. He is unsure how long Meta will keep it open, and it has similar anti-spam rules (spam thousands of numbers and they ban you).
- He notes WhatsApp runs over data or Wi-Fi rather than a carrier plan, and sends images better than MMS.

## Send-time optimization

- ActiveCampaign's predictive send picks each person's optimal send time.
- It doesn't just use when they open. Example: someone may open emails at 9:00 a.m. while skimming and deleting. At 11:00 a.m. at their desk they read, click and reply, and that becomes the optimal time.
- So one contact might get sends at 11:00 a.m. and another at 12:45 a.m., based on engaged behavior rather than raw opens.
- It's a fix for messages arriving at odd hours, like a 3 a.m. text.

## Retargeting by email with Retention

- Ross describes standard retargeting ads (Google/Facebook cookies) and a second approach: a tool called Retention, US-only.
- How it works as he describes it: a visitor to a client's site is matched against legally purchased US databases. The client gets the visitor's email and can email them without the visitor submitting anything.
- Segmentation can go down to the product viewed. For example, someone who looked at a recipe gets an email about the ready meal.
- Best fit: e-commerce stores. He says the results are "insane" and his clients see strong conversions. One client with five competitor tools says Retention beats them, getting about 2,000 contacts a day and roughly $22-25K in sales from that alone.
- Cost: he says under about $1,000 a month. Justified when the site has roughly 10,000 US visitors a month, or fewer if each customer is worth a lot (example: a $50,000 customer at 5,000 visitors a month).
- DigitalME's role: send clients to Retention, help install it, and build the email flows in ActiveCampaign or another CRM.
- He expects it to survive in the US given light regulation but is unsure.

## Who he takes on and what he won't do

- Bought lists: people come to him about every five to six weeks with a "phone book" of a million addresses from different niches that they have never spoken to. He turns them away. Mailing strangers lands you in spam and the client comes back upset.
- Minimum economics: if the product is about $5 and there are no existing customers, cost of acquisition versus margin doesn't work. His soft spot is roughly $200-300 per product or more. He's worked with clients selling $2,000-$10,000 courses.
- Enterprise example: a campaign to about 6,000 hotels worldwide for feedback on a fitness product can cost only a couple thousand dollars. Pure conversion goals cost more and take time, and his marketing budget advice "varies so hard."

## Top marketing mistakes he sees

1. Doing it yourself badly: owners who never email their list, or send emails with a logo the full size of the screen. He has also seen CEOs cut costs wildly.
2. No upselling: "Why haven't you got upselling on your website?" Especially for B2C. He cites Temu as an aggressive upseller and says traditional sites upsell far less than they should.
3. Not knowing your audience: build a persona covering age, gender, location, job, and so on. Different audiences respond to different channels (TikTok for 18-25, Instagram/Facebook for 35-55). Test with your own data, since many marketing speakers haven't marketed in 10-15 years.

His tip on upsell: the movie theater example, where offering a larger soda for 25 cents more won yes about 80% of the time, which helps both the customer and the business.
