## Choosing a platform by fit, not by hype

Ross argues there is no one-size-fits-all website platform. Every business has different online objectives, products and services, so the goal is to find the platform that suits its specific needs. He warns against picking the "best option" according to "some random blogger who more than likely has an affiliate link or two slipped into the article." Far too often, businesses end up on a platform that isn't up to par for what they need, or build on something that is complete overkill. He frames the choice around five factors: intended use, technical expertise, budget, design customization and ease of updating.

## Match the platform to the website's intended use

Ross starts with what the site needs to do.

- **Online presence only** (showcase products, services, company info): a WordPress site with a blog does the trick.
- **Direct-to-consumer selling:** Shopify is a plug-and-play option with low-cost plans and a very low barrier to entry.
- **Unique needs:** a fully custom build may be required.
- **No blogging and no SEO intent:** a simple single-page HTML website is enough.

He associates WordPress with blogs and small local businesses, and Shopify with ecommerce. Other players and fully custom builds also exist.

## Treat the website as a tool and invest in the skills to use it

Ross says platforms demand different levels of technical expertise, and many people don't consider this. Time spent learning a platform helps you get the most out of the site. A live website is only a start, because it is a tool for the business.

- A Shopify store that connects a domain, uploads some products and then sits there "isn't going to grow quickly, if at all."
- Someone with more technical knowledge can use the tracking and insights to drive more sales.
- That person can also use abandonment recovery and upselling, among other things, to lift sales.

His point is that knowing how to leverage a website to its full potential can significantly affect business success.

## Budget ranges from nearly free to a quarter-million dollars

Ross lays out the cost spectrum:

- **WordPress:** free to download, install and set up. Roughly $12 for a domain plus low-cost shared hosting at $5 per month.
- **Shopify:** build with a free template and be running for $29 per month.
- **Fully custom build:** custom databases, functionality and more. You could "drop a quarter-million dollars" on an elaborate one.

He cautions against discounting the low-cost options. They can handle large companies: some of the largest ecommerce brands in the world run on Shopify and generate millions of dollars daily on the same tech stack available to any small business.

## Decide how much design customization you actually need

Ross says the level of customization determines the build path.

- **Modest needs:** both Shopify and WordPress offer free themes that work well and allow some customization.
- **Paid themes:** thousands are available, priced from $39 to $200. They give affordable access to sleek designs and features that can be adjusted to match your needs and branding.
- **Custom animation and unique design:** hire a front-end and back-end team to build from the ground up. This one-off customization "will cost a pretty penny," but for some businesses the expense is worth it.

## Favor platforms you can learn and update yourself

Ross's final factor is ease of updating and the resources available. Popular DIY platforms such as Shopify, WordPress, BigCommerce, Wix and Squarespace all have plenty of online guidance, both from the platforms themselves (blog and video content) and from user-created content.

- If the budget doesn't cover an onsite development team to manage and update the site, pick a platform you feel comfortable and confident learning.
- He notes that today almost anything related to development can be found on YouTube.
