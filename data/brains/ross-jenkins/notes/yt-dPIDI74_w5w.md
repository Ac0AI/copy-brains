## Freelancing as a business: Upwork pricing, rates and positioning

- Ross joined Upwork in 2018 and started at $25 an hour. He now charges $365 an hour.
- His rule for raising rates: "when we see huge sort of uproars in demand in terms of my time we know it's time to increase the hourly rate."
- His first client was a pub owner in Los Angeles. Ross raised his price as demand grew until the work was no longer profitable for the pub. They parted on good terms, and the client may come back.
- He says hiring on Upwork is largely about confidence. If a freelancer isn't confident in their hourly rate, clients won't trust the work.
- He argues freelancers should build up their feedback at their real rate. A $30-an-hour freelancer who accepts a $6 job drags the visible rate on their profile back down to where they started.
- Ross says Upwork has a large share of freelancers who have never landed a job. He guesses around 80%, and flags that as a guess. Reasons he gives: no portfolio, unrealistic hourly rates (his girlfriend's brother listed at $10,000 an hour with nothing to show), and not applying to jobs.
- Upwork and Fiverr overlap, but he sees Upwork as the more upmarket marketplace. On either you can find an admin for $3 an hour or a specialist at a very high price.
- He recommends exams and certifications, both on Upwork and external ones such as HubSpot, to become "better than the people you're competing with."

## Specialize: niche down instead of being a generalist

- Ross's own niche is email marketing and CRM. He takes other work only when it falls within the scope of what the agency does well.
- Generalist freelancers get "generalist pay" and compete with people who "scrape the barrel" on price.
- He says crowded categories such as social media marketing, admin and customer support have far more supply than demand. Some people charge $2-3 an hour.
- His advice for those categories is to upskill and build a brand, or do something distinctive. For example, customer support freelancers can build Zendesk macros or FAQs for companies instead of just answering tickets.
- He cites freelancers who earn well in these niches, such as an Upwork admin making about $200,000 a year, because they made themselves the go-to person.
- He dislikes the "marketing manager with 20 roles" pattern (account manager, SEO, content, email, admin). Nobody is good at all of those.
- Example: for a Gravity Forms job he would hire one person to build the form and another to design it, because specialists beat a generalist.
- He warns against handing specialist work to an office manager or admin, such as cleansing and emailing the client database, which he calls a "gold mine." The admin is usually untrained for it and ends up resentful.

## Price on value, and turn away clients who only want cheap

- Ross wants freelancers to add at least double what they cost. If he pays someone $100 an hour, he wants them to generate $200 an hour for him.
- Freelancers need case studies that show this, and need to understand why they are worth their rate.
- He pushes back on "they can't afford it" thinking: you can't put yourself in the client's shoes. Focus on the value you bring and how you help them grow.
- When a prospect offers less than his rate, he tells them to go work with someone else. If they come back and ask for his rate after trying to negotiate, he still won't take them. They have already shown they want to pay as little as possible.
- He fires clients too, including one who spoke badly to freelancers in a shared Slack group and then spoke that way to Ross. His reasoning: for many freelancers a client is their livelihood, and treating them badly isn't acceptable.
- He says freelancers should value their mental health and their time. A client who demands work immediately when the freelancer has already used the booked hours is not worth keeping.
- Agency approach to pricing: "we're pretty Cutthroat" about who they take on. Past mistakes with misfit clients taught him which prospects to avoid.
- Commission deals: for founders and products he believes in, the agency will work partly or wholly on commission to give "a bit more drive." Ross says his door-to-door sales background makes this comfortable.
- He claims his team has made over $150 million for companies. As one example, a company with about 30 years in business and no email list before he started now makes about $4 million a year from email.

## Email marketing: personality, relevance and giving a reason to opt in

- Ross says email isn't dead. He sees a "massive pickup" this year, after growth since he entered the field five years ago.
- Email works for notifications, newsletters, sales and upsell emails, for both B2B and B2C. He thinks every business should have an email system.
- People give up too early: "no one wants to reply to a robot." Personality sells.
- His emails are personalized and come from a real person. They look as authentic as two people emailing each other, not from a no-reply address.
- Be relatable to the specific audience. His example: for CEOs, relate the pitch to something like an upsell at Starbucks. For moms, build an audience in Facebook groups with relevant messaging.
- Tell a story about why the email is valuable in this person's inbox. He has had clients with 30,000 contacts on paper and no idea what message they wanted to send.
- Show value in the first lines. He criticizes cold outreach on LinkedIn that gives no reason and shows no research.
- Email your past client list. He calls it a "gold mine" most companies miss, and "one of the simplest ways to increase your revenue." People are scared to hit send, but he says it's worth it.
- He is wary of adding people to a list without asking. His agency adds buyers to the list as part of purchase and makes opting out clear. Fewer than one or two people have opted out.
- For a first email to past clients, send something personal. Give an update and a link to join the newsletter if they want more.
- Sell the newsletter itself with a concrete reason to join. Example for an Amazon seller audience: tips on improving listing SEO, pricing to compete, and better product images.
- Scarcity can work for B2C: "I can only take a hundred people on this newsletter" or "I've given all my tips and tricks for the next 100 days."
- For B2B, state why they should be on the list, and consider a discount. Make it a bold statement and be "personable," not "personal."

## Cold email is relationship building

- Ross says cold email still works but takes time. You're building a new relationship, which is harder than emailing past clients.
- Clients who want sales "tomorrow" from a cold campaign will be disappointed. Relationships don't work that way.
- He compares the skill to driving a manual car in Europe: it takes practice, and people who try one email and quit never learn it.
- Between new audiences and existing ones, channel choice depends on age demographics. He mentions TikTok, Instagram, Facebook and X as channels that skew to different ages.

## Hiring and running a freelance team

- Ross uses freelancers across the world and has built the agency (Digital Me) around them. The team has ranged from about 12 to about 40 freelancers as he learned what worked.
- His first contractor is still with him.
- Reasons to use freelancers over full-time hires: a US web developer might cost around $100,000 a year plus insurance and a desk. If you only have about 20 hours of work, a freelancer is better.
- Hiring locally limits you. "The best email marketer in Chicago" is not the best in the world. Hiring globally lets you find top talent anywhere. He mentions graphic designers in Ukraine and developers in India and the Philippines.
- Specialists beat generalists at the same price.
- Give experts creative freedom. Ross asks developers how they would approach something (for example a HubSpot dashboard request) rather than dictating: "I'm the expert in marketing and they're the expert in that."
- He lets people go who keep missing deadlines or who charge but don't deliver. In one example, he was chasing a developer for late work and decided "why am I chasing you, you should be chasing me to give you work."
- Late work lands on him, because he is the one who goes back to the client.

## Quality control and acting on emotion

- Everything the agency delivers passes through two or three people, and Ross himself, who check the code or front end.
- Reason: after ten hours of work you can miss something, and a second or third reviewer catches it. This is a big part of why the agency is higher end.
- The agency won't do cheap work, for example a "$20 HTML page." Prospects who say they'll hire a cheaper developer or use Elementor are told to go ahead.
- On emotional decisions, Ross says to cool down before acting: "you don't want to make decisions based on emotions." He estimates 99% of hotheaded decisions are wrong.

## Tooling by building in-house, and the gig economy outlook

- Ross's agency recommended a contact-cleansing tool to about 50 clients, which he says generated over $400,000 for them. He then had a developer build the same script in a few hours and wished they had done it sooner. He attributes the delay to not giving freelancers enough responsibility.
- He keeps learning from Twitter and Social Media Examiner, looking for better tools and ways to be more effective.
- Outlook: he thinks the gig economy will keep growing. He points to labor shortages and high pay for low-wage work (McDonald's at over $20 an hour in California) as signs more companies will turn to freelancers.
- He expects some return to offices after COVID to cause a "rumble," but thinks freelancing is here for good.
- Employers once held back on remote work and treated people badly, which he thinks pushed workers toward freelancing.
- His background: small-town England, eight months of door-to-door internet sales, then agency work in Ireland, Qatar and England. He left because agencies took a huge cut and had gaps in communication and quality, then went to Barcelona alone for about six months to build his own business.
- He now splits time between Spain and Dubai.
