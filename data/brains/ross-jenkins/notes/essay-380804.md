## Choosing a freelance niche and platform presence

- Ross says to sell your service "in a place full of people who need it": create profiles on several freelance platforms (Upwork is his example) to widen your connections and get your name out.
- A strong profile lists skill sets, experience, education, portfolio and basic information, so clients can judge fit. Clients may also ask extra questions before hiring.
- He notes that platform traffic matters for getting hired, but "it all boils down to how you 'sell' yourself to employers."
- He calls being too broad a common mistake. A profile that says only "writer" or "graphic artist" is unattractive, because most clients want someone who can do a specific job repeatedly, not a vague one.
- His fix is to sell within a niche. A writer could position as "children's blog writing" or "informative article writing."

## Do not rely on platforms alone: use your personal network

- Ross tells freelancers not to depend only on the platforms they've joined.
- Make your services known to people you know personally: acquaintances, relatives, friends and friends' friends all become potential clients.
- Personal contacts already know your skills and output, so they have "little to no doubt" about you.
- He says never to underestimate direct family and friends as a source of recommendations and referrals that bring more clients.

## Being active, responsive and priced sensibly when starting out

- Ross argues you cannot create an account and wait. A new, inexperienced freelancer with an idle profile will lose clients, so you must do the pursuing and apply for gigs on the platform.
- When new, consider a lower hourly rate than most freelancers, but not too low, because clients may then assume you only produce subpar work. His rule: "Make it reasonable, yet lower than most freelancers."
- Respond as fast as possible to client queries and stay professional and polite.
- After being hired, give frequent updates to reassure the client the project is moving and will be delivered on time.

## Winning repeat work from existing clients

- Ross says to meet or exceed each client's expectations. When you deliver, tell the client you're open to working with them again.
- This only works if the job was done well, so always produce high-standard work.
- If a client's project turns out to need more work than first specified, create a new project with new objectives instead of stretching the old one.
- If clients are thoroughly impressed by earlier work, they are likely to trust you with what follows.

## Collaborating honestly on projects too big to handle alone

- Ross warns against overconfidence and overworking yourself. If a client hires you for a big project you can't do alone, tell the client you'll need to work with someone else.
- Do not take the credit yourself, because it "will backfire."
- He says clients "highly value honesty and transparency." Bringing in the right people to finish excellently shows commitment to quality, not weakness, so there's little reason for a client to decline.

## Long-term view: from freelancer to your own platform or company

- Ross frames the six steps (join a platform, publicize, specify a niche, stay active and responsive, seek repeat work, collaborate openly) as simple but effective for attracting clients.
- He expects that, over time, a freelancer becomes self-sufficient and may have the means to build their own platform or company to showcase and sell their services to potential clients.
- He stresses that success is not overnight: you can't wait for a miracle, you must "work with what you have and actively pursue your goals one step at a time."
- He notes freelancing is hard, with roughly as many people failing as succeeding, but offers benefits such as working at your own pace and widening your professional network.
