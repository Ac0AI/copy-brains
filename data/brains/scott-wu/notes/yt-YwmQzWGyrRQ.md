## Scott's path: competitions, Adapar, Lunch Club, then Cognition

- Scott started math competitions in second grade (age 7 or 8). He lost his first one, was "really upset," trained for a year, entered the algebra division the next year in third grade and won. He kept competing from there.
- He learned to code in fourth grade and started programming competitions in sixth grade.
- After high school he took a year-plus off to work at Adapar on the computational architecture team, computing large financial metrics. He wanted to learn what building real companies was like.
- He then went to Harvard for about two years. His goal was less to learn software engineering and more "to meet interesting people, take classes and develop personally." He dropped out to start his first company.
- That company was Lunch Club, started in 2017, before generative AI. It used AI to make professional introductions: users shared interests, goals, contacts and calendar, and the product chose who they should meet. It started in person and moved to video meetings when COVID hit. He spent about 5 years on it and it facilitated millions of meetings. He says it taught him the lessons he applies now.
- He argues that advanced math classes are what got him here, and that technical education is where everything starts. He finds it sad that schools are dropping honors math.

## How Cognition started: a hacker-house research project, not a company

- Cognition grew out of Scott's competition network. Years of math and programming contests left him close to many people who now work in AI.
- His co-founders: Steven was the first engineer at Scale AI, and Walden was the first engineer at Cursor.
- It began as a research project, not a company. In November 2023 they rented a house in the Bay Area, gathered a group for two weeks and hacked on ideas.
- In December they got another house in New York. In January they got another house, and in February they kept the model going. The company was incorporated later.
- The team is about 30 people. About 19 or 20 have founded a company before. Scott says this brings "a lot of fire," and each founder takes initiative and runs with it.
- He describes it as "a hackathon for the last year and a half." The team regularly works past midnight, and plays math games like the 24 card game.
- He says the math and programming crowd runs AI because the balance in AI "swings a lot more technical." If you solve hard technical problems, it is clear how to turn that into a business. He cites OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind as run by people from these communities.

## Skating where the puck is going: the agent bet behind Devin

- Scott's strategy was to "skate where the puck is going."
- Around the end of 2023, Cognition was working on AI for coding. They judged things would move to agentic systems, before "agents" was a common term.
- The idea was to move from pure text interfaces to AI that interacts with the world: debugging, pulling up logs, reading documentation and testing code itself.
- Devin is an AI software engineer, an autonomous system you work with like a human engineer. You assign it a bug or task. It knows your codebase, tests itself, iterates, and produces a pull request for you to review. You treat it as a junior engineer.
- The Devin demo went viral. Scott says he hoped it would do well but did not expect that much.
- Newer capabilities he mentions:
  - Humans and AI interactively plan and scope a task, then hand it off.
  - Every ticket filed in Slack, Linear or Jira can be picked up automatically by the coding agent.
- Scott says the answer to "what is the future of software engineering" changes every couple of months as capabilities reach a new tier. That question anchors all of Cognition's product and strategy decisions.

## Enterprise sales: look for 10x use cases, not 10-20% gains

- Large enterprises, including multi-billion-dollar adopters, use Devin. Scott's approach is to find use cases where Devin delivers "not just 10% or 20% gains... but more like 10x."
- He says they routinely see 6x to 20x efficiency. One engineer hour on that use case with Devin equals 6 to 20 hours without it.
- In practice the engineer reviews the code Devin produces and signs off.
- The best fits are the tedious, repetitive tasks engineers don't want to do. Humans keep the higher-level strategy and creative work.
- Scott says startups often "get caught up in fancy tactics." He prefers to start from the future and the value you will provide.

## Why code is the fastest-growing AI use case: feedback loops and RL

- Scott describes two waves of generative AI: imitation learning, then reinforcement learning (RL).
- His view of where RL leads: you can solve any benchmark. If you have a clean environment, a good feedback loop for correct versus incorrect, and the context needed to decide, you can train a model that aces it.
- Code grows fast because it has a great success/failure feedback loop: "you can run the code." In healthcare or law it is much harder to say whether the job was done right.
- Many model benchmarks are the math and programming competitions he did as a kid, because they cleanly show reasoning on hard problems.
- What comes next is largely defining the benchmark: which use cases and tasks to solve, what tools an agent needs, and how success is judged.
- Rate of progress: the amount of work an AI system can do between human interventions has doubled about every 3 months in code over the last 3-4 years. That is 2x every 3 months, 16x a year, about 4,000x over 3 years. It went from tab-completing a single line to hours of autonomous work (fixing a bug or building a feature). He says it shows no signs of slowing.
- He cites Google's silver medal at the International Math Olympiad and OpenAI's result reaching roughly the top 100 competitors in the world in competitive programming. His takeaway: "intelligence actually isn't the bottleneck." Much of the work now is making models excel at your specific hard problems, such as a particular library or debugging a specific error.
- He cites Andrej Karpathy's "jagged intelligence": AI is superhuman at some things and bad at others. That means humans plus AI is the way to go for the foreseeable future, and he expects the long tail to take quite some time.

## Why demand for software, and for engineers, keeps growing

- Scott says there are about 30 million software engineers and engineers are paid multiple trillions a year. Around 1999-2000 there were fewer than a million.
- Since 2000, Python, React and cloud made software much easier to build, and the number of engineers still grew, because people did far more with software. He expects the same pattern with AI.
- He says no engineering team has told him "we're done, no more software." Every team has a hundred things to build and picks four per week.
- His products-by-engineering-hours argument:
  - The best products (YouTube, Instagram, TikTok) have roughly 100 million hours of engineering behind them. Scott calls this "call it" a rough figure.
  - Each order of magnitude down is noticeable in bugs and unintuitive flows. Banks and healthcare apps have tens of millions of hours, and a local school website sits at the bottom.
  - Conclusion: "we're only bottlenecked by how much we can build."
- On the future of the job: skills will change, but a great engineer is not someone who types fast. They think in abstractions, understand every case and detail, and make high-level architecture decisions. He expects engineers to spend more time there.
- He expects software engineering to become "you telling your computer here's exactly what you need to build," for example adding a page, adding a table, or making a button rounder, and it just happens.
- Both non-technical and technical people benefit. Non-technical people can already build sites and apps from plain text. Deep understanding will still matter for design trade-offs, much as almost nobody writes assembly except for high-performance work.
- He still tells parents their 16-year-old should study computer science. The concepts (logic, algorithms, what a computer can and cannot do) make you better at deciding what to build. He says "it'll always be up to us to decide what to build."
- Economic framing he agrees with: he calls AI coding a multi-trillion-dollar opportunity. He expects each person to have their own team of AI assistants. He compares the future to moving from "Minecraft survival mode" (a line from co-founder Walden) to "creative mode," with people pursuing their passions.
