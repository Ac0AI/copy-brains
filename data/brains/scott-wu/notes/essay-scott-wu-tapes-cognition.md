## Cognition's mission and why Scott stays "Switzerland"

- Scott says Cognition's one goal is "to make it much easier for everyone around the world to build software." He frames the larger aim as moving people from "Minecraft survival mode" to "Minecraft creative mode" (a line he credits to co-founder Walden Yan).
- He argues a company can have different functions and verticals but only one goal. Cognition's goal differs from a foundation lab's, whose goal is advancing research itself.
- He believes company DNA is "kind of rigid, more so than people usually expect." The mission can change over time, "but not as much as you think." Customers, products and business model all root in that one thing.
- Why Cognition stays model-neutral ("Switzerland"):
  - It wants to be the trusted infrastructure partner that helps companies ship much more.
  - "Signing away our trust to a specific model would be a scary thing to do. Nobody even knows who's going to have the best model in 12 months."
  - Working with all models lets it use each where it is best and advise partners on which to use.
- He rejects Silicon Valley cynicism that new companies can't be built from scratch because incumbents "are going to go eat everything." He calls it "totally misguided" and says building something new is also "just more fun this way."

## Devin as an AI coworker, not a tool

- Scott says Cognition always framed Devin as a coworker. It is a full agent with its own machine that does its own work, lives in the same systems (Slack, Jira, etc.) and works alongside you.
- The name was deliberate: "there's a reason we decided to call it Devin as opposed to naming it like a tool." He thinks of it as "its own being that can go and do stuff."
- Two years earlier this was an aspiration needing heavy handholding. Now, he says, "you really can just work with it as a coworker."
- A decent fraction of customers' Devin sessions are no longer started by humans. They are triggered automatically, or run in loops that look for problems in products and fix them. Devins now find the tasks as well as do them.

## How Cognition started: timing, forcing functions and RL

- Scott's path: Addepar (performance engineering, some parts about 100x faster), two years at Harvard (dropped out), then LunchClub with Vlad Novakovski from 2017. He left LunchClub in June 2022.
- He treats the stretch after as a sabbatical: exploring crypto, security and zero-knowledge with Steven Hao and Andrew He, then getting deeper into AI after ChatGPT (November 2022).
- Why he started Cognition:
  - He followed research papers and saw reinforcement learning working on math and code, so "the logical next step was actually kind of obvious, but the next step wasn't published."
  - The thesis: reasoning is getting better, RL is working, so now is the time for a new lab.
- Forcing function: on November 17, 2023 (the day Sam Altman was fired) he was pitching the idea over lunch. He decided "if there's ever a time to do it, we should probably just do this right now."
- His view of founding: "The moment of just actually committing yourself... we've all started companies before... but this one is the big one. This is the one for life."
- Execution:
  - He organized a hacker house in Burlingame over Thanksgiving 2023, weeks after his mother died, and canceled family plans to do it.
  - He messaged AI people he knew and invited them to hack together.
  - A second house followed in December, and they kept the house going until January 2026.

## What actually drove AI progress, and how to read benchmarks

- Scott sees models beating humans on the AMC (a hard high-school math competition) in early 2024 as a proof of concept. Hard problems require logical reasoning, creativity and long chains of reasoning that all line up. Models doing that with enough RL and the right data suggests practical tasks are achievable.
- He says the last two years had no huge breakthrough in attention or RL itself. Progress came from:
  - More compute and data.
  - Tackling real-world problems: training models against rubrics for what is good for a given task.
  - Deploying in the real world and building product experiences. He calls this "messy, practical work" and credits it, more than researcher techniques, for the explosion in AI usage.
- On "jagged" intelligence: he thinks models will always be jagged relative to humans, because they are trained on internet tokens and so excel at knowledge work first. He notes coding is literally "talking to a computer," so it is unsurprising it was the first great use case. It "almost says more about human intelligence than it does about computer intelligence."

## Where humans still have the edge, and what's coming

- Scott's near-term view: AGI is "much more of an ascent" than a binary. Humans keep an edge in high-context, general-purpose work needing "soft context": decisions, principles and the history of why systems were built a certain way.
- He says retrieval is what humans do best, such as a memory triggered by one file of code ("four months ago I was working on this and we had this bug"), even though our context window is short (his joke: we could not handle 10-digit two-factor codes).
- Two AI problems he names for closing the gap: embedding search over all past conversations and code, and continual learning, where the AI updates its own weights as it goes. He expects both to be solved "maybe not so long from now."
- On the long run he does not think any single capability will stay uniquely human. Past "AI will never do X" claims (chess, Go, math, code, new theorems) were disproven.
- The METR result he cites: agents have moved from seconds to minutes to hours of human-equivalent work, doubling every few months. He asks what happens if that continues to days, weeks, then a year, and says "I think that is where we will get to."
- His framing of the shift: the last 200 years automated raw physical strength, and now AI is on the verge of automating raw mental strength. "The bottleneck to turning our dreams into reality will dramatically narrow." AI is "the lever that will ensure our dreams don't have to forever remain dreams."

## The 10% / 90% of building software, and automating drudgery

- Scott's test for drudgery: ask how many ideas a person has in a day and how many they actually get to do. He guesses it is "probably like 5%."
- In software, he says no engineering team ever finishes everything they want: "you have 85 projects and you have to pick six because that's how much bandwidth you have."
- He splits the job into about 10% self-expression (trade-offs, architecture, what product to build) and 90% "code monkeying and execution." When people say they love building software, they usually mean the 10%.
- His prediction, which applies to every field: people will get to "do 10 times more of the 10% you actually love."

## Risks Scott worries about

- His biggest worry is not AI itself but the friction from how fast it moves. He compares it to past revolutions (electricity, the internet, mobile, cloud), which are similar at a high level, but this one is faster. The Industrial Revolution played out over generations, and in earlier revolutions adopting two to five years late was still fine. "In this case, it has to happen faster."
- The bad outcome he names: a long period where only elites in SF have access and everyone else does not.
- A specific example: strong model cyber capabilities where much of the world isn't using them for defense while attackers use them for offense, which makes the balance "pretty tricky."
- His remedy is education and getting everyone on the technology at the same time.

## The founder profile: intelligence plus hunger, and the competition network

- Scott says two qualities matter in his field: intelligence and hunger. The competition community selects for being "super smart" and "salty and want[ing] to beat everybody up." If you analyze what went wrong and adjust constantly, motivated enough to do it for years, you can usually get good at the downstream skills too.
- He says startup founding is going through a "Moneyball" arc like poker and chess, shifting from the Steve Jobs-type profile to a technical one. In AI especially, deep technical background is "extremely valuable" because the problems are deeply technical.
- He credits a network effect: Alexandr Wang was the first of their group to start a company, and "since we were all so tight... it was very much like, we should all go and do this." He and Alexandr kept a doc of startup ideas in middle school.
- Cognition itself was started with many friends from that competition network. He says "we were all very, very lucky to have each other" and notes many people do not realize entrepreneurship is an option.
- Habits he describes about himself: he thinks intuitively rather than memorizing theorem names, and he wanted to beat everyone regardless of age (third grader winning the ninth-grade division). His mother told him he had the potential to be the best, rather than comparing him to others.

## Personal drive and what he's choosing to do

- Scott describes his mother's death on October 6, 2023 as one of the biggest events of his life. He spent over a year at home during COVID and moved back in 2023 as she got sicker. He says the period made him think about his values and his life's work. Cognition started the next month.
- On his purpose: "There's a chance AI is the most important event in human history, and coding is how it will learn to act and build things in the real world. If I was meant to do something... I think it's to be the guy who teaches AI how to code."
- His fear is not failure but not trying: "the world where I actually couldn't live with myself is the one where I took this thing that was a perfect match for everything I've always naturally loved... and just didn't give it my all."
