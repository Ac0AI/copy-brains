## Scott's view on what AI changes: humans decide, agents execute

- Scott is "very bullish on the AI capabilities." He expects AI to get to logical, decision-making work, including math problems, but says that is different from AI ruling the world.
- His model: humans decide what to build, what to do and what to create. AI helps with execution. Otherwise "the humans aren't in control anymore."
- Humans stay accountable, which adds a layer of trust software can't carry. He agrees with the point that the human is responsible in a way no system can be.
- He argues there should always be a human-AI communication layer. The products that matter sit in between: what information the agent shows the human, how the human gives thoughts back, and how they iterate together.
- Capability alone doesn't deliver value. You still need thoughtful product experiences, education and onboarding for people to use agents.
- His framing: AI moves life from "survival mode" to "creative mode" (a Minecraft analogy). In survival mode you mine every block and build every shelter yourself. In creative mode you have all the tools and can build whatever you want. He expects AI to remove barriers so people can pursue their passions.

## Building Cognition: what changed between demo and real deployment

- Cognition builds Devin, the AI software engineer. It works with big companies, startups and everything in between on shipping software faster.
- Scott's founding bet was that the technology would get much better. He says it was still surprising how stark the improvement was over two years.
- He saw the background agent take off in 2026. Consumer agents with the same form factor now exist for many use cases.
- Early on it was "much more of a demo or a prototype." It did fun hobby tasks, but working on a real code base is "very different work."
- The company had to learn what deploying at a real company involves: big code bases, real use cases and integrations.
- Enterprise deployment demanded sophistication fast. Scott says Silicon Valley fixates on raw intelligence, but getting it into the real world and into everyone's daily work takes much more.

## Speed-running company functions

- Scott says Cognition had to "speed run building all these functions" in two to three years.
- Example: no designers in-house until about 8 or 9 months before this interview. He says they should have hired one much earlier. The product had cool things but "definitely did not look good" until recently.
- Marketing was the same: Cognition only started ramping it up much more recently.
- Takeaway he implies: don't wait on design and marketing because the core tech is the star.

## Brazil as an international market

- Cognition is opening offices in Brazil and growing internationally in a few places. Brazil has been a meaningful hub.
- Early partners included Nubank and Itaú. The first international trip after founding was to Brazil, and Cognition held a developer conference there the following year.
- His demand logic: Brazil has huge demand for software and not enough software engineers. The fix is making each engineer much more effective with AI.
- He calls Brazil an early adopter across the board.
- Brazilians online call the product "Divino." That started organically, with folks talking about it online and natural demand.
- Cognition's response: "We should do more," and build out its own local presence on top of the organic community.

## Handling AI hype and uncertainty

- Scott says he goes through "AI psychosis" himself every few months.
- Part of the hype is true: the world will be very different and many things humans do manually will go away. Part is overblown, like the idea that there is no worth in living and we become slaves.
- His stance on predictions: "anybody who claims that they know everything about what's going to happen in AI has been wrong basically every time."

## Investing: stay balanced and hedged

- Scott says he has no idea how AI plays out in markets. He thinks AI as a whole still has a long way to go.
- Rule one: bet on AI broadly, not on one individual company.
- Rule two: be prepared for uncertainty. He says it's not wise, especially now, to go all in on one or two things, since a new model capability could reshape an industry.
- Keep a balanced portfolio and be ready to adapt to change.
- He believes the total market will go up because "we'll all be building much more in AI," but picking a single stock or pick would be "guessing."
- Method: decide which directional trends you believe in for the long term, then hold a mix of exposures that is still reasonably hedged.

## How Scott manages his own money

- The large majority of his wealth is illiquid.
- Outside that, he applies the same logic. On where AI value accrues (chips, frontier labs, application layer, inference technology), he doesn't claim to know which wins. He sees a decent chance all of them accrue value and favors balance across those layers.
- His investments in friends' companies and others in the Valley follow that same spread view.
- His liquid money is largely a mix of tech ETFs, total market ETFs and some amount of Treasuries.
