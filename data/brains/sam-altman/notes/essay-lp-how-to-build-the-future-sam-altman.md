## Why this is the best time to start a tech company

- Sam calls it "the best time yet" to start a tech company. Each major technological revolution lets people do more than the last one.
- He argues big companies have the edge when things move slowly. Upstarts get their edge during platform shifts: the semiconductor revolution, the internet, mobile, and now AI. He notes it has been a while since the last one.
- He says "the world is still sleeping on all of this to such an astonishing degree."
- Platform shifts are usually built by young people with no prior knowledge. He points to Facebook almost missing mobile because it was good at web software, then having to buy Instagram and WhatsApp.
- Abundant intelligence and abundant energy are, in his view, the two key inputs to everything else. His essay predicts superintelligence may be "a few thousand days" away.

## Startup advice: bet on the AI trend and use speed as the edge

- His advice to founders starting now is to bet on this trend. He says "we are not near the saturation point" and the models will get much better, quickly.
- What a founder can do with AI versus without it is "wildly different."
- Big companies, mid-sized companies, and even startups a few years old run on quarterly planning cycles. He says a startup's edge is "speed and focus and conviction and the ability to react to how fast the technology is moving." He calls this "the number one edge of a startup kind of ever, but especially right now."
- He recommends building something with AI, and acting when you see something new: "build something that day rather than put it into a quarterly planning cycle."
- He expects billion-dollar-a-year companies with fewer than 100 employees, maybe 20, maybe one. His shorthand is "one person plus 10,000 GPUs."

## The rules of business still apply, even with AI

- Sam warns against thinking "because I'm doing something with AI, the laws of business don't apply to me." He says you still have to build a moat, a competitive edge, and a better product.
- Embracing a new technology faster than others can produce "short-term explosions of growth." That is not the same as a durable business.
- "Everyone can build an absolutely incredible demo right now." He says "building a business, man, that's the brass ring."
- His summary is that you can build faster and better than ever, but "you still have to build a business."

## Picking one bet and concentrating

- OpenAI was far less resourced than DeepMind and others. His reasoning: "they're going to try a lot of things and we've just got to pick one and really concentrate, and that's how we can win."
- The two core beliefs were that deep learning works and that it gets better with scale. Both were heretical at the time.
- Other efforts were "trying to outsmart themselves in too many ways." OpenAI did the thing in front of it and kept pushing.
- The norm was to spread resources across many bets. He says "most of the world still does not understand the value of a fairly extreme level of conviction on one bet."
- On scale: "when in doubt, if you have something that seems like it's getting better with scale, I think you should scale it up." He adds that "more is more."
- An early whiteboard listed three goals: figure out unsupervised learning, solve RL, and never get more than 120 people. He says they missed on the third.

## Conviction, pushback, and staying truth-seeking

- When the world pushes back and the pushback doesn't make sense to you, "you should do it anyway." Eminent field leaders told the OpenAI team they were wrong and that believing this was a bad thing.
- He says there are no "adults in the room" with all the answers. You can "just do stuff" and iterate quickly.
- Conviction has to be paired with honesty about evidence. "Conviction is great until the moment you have data one way or the other." Many people hold on past the moment of data.
- OpenAI was high conviction and wrong many times, and tried to embrace it fully once it saw the data. He lists "the ability to get punched in the face and get back up and keep going" as one of its strengths.
- There is a long stretch where you must operate without data and "run on conviction." You still have to show your work.
- He admits the story was not a short path. They started with robots, agents, and video games before language models. He says "if we knew then what we know now, we could have speedrun this."

## Recruiting and launching a team around an audacious mission

- Sam says OpenAI said from the start it would go after AGI, at a time "you weren't allowed to say that." The statement got the attention of the best young people and drew derision from mediocre older ones, which he took as a good sign.
- Recruiting was one-on-one. He saw a video of Ilya and wanted to meet him. When Ilya didn't respond to his email, he went to a conference where Ilya was speaking. He already knew Greg Brockman from the early Stripe days. It took about nine months to come together.
- OpenAI announced in December 2015 and started in January 2016. At the first meeting in Greg's apartment, with about 10 people, the question was "so what do we do now?" He compares it to founders who raise a round and realize "it's not time for popping champagne. That was actually the starting gun."
- He says the original ideas were "incredibly right" even after many detours.
- On peers, he says "no one is immune to peer pressure, so all you can do is pick good peers." He advises young people to find an inspiring peer group as early as they can. At Stanford, peers competed over investment-bank internships, and the YC culture changed that for him.

## Product-market fit: how GPT-3, 3.5 and 4 landed

- With GPT-3, founders said it was an incredible demo, but "with the possible exception of Copilot, no great businesses were built on GPT-3."
- With 3.5, YC startups began doing interesting things, and it "no longer felt like we were pushing a boulder uphill."
- With GPT-4, people asked how many GPUs they could get, very quickly after getting access.
- He cites Jake Heller of CaseText, who sold the company for $650 million after chopping prompts into small workflows on GPT-4.
- His caution: "you never really know if you have a hit product on your hands until you put it in customer hands." Teams are always too impressed with their own work, and users are the real test.

## Scaling a company fast and aligning everyone on a few things

- Sam says OpenAI has "speedrun" a company arc that normally takes a decade, and ChatGPT is under two years old. The cost is a lot of painful change.
- Management teams turn over as a company scales, because people who are good at zero to one are not necessarily good at 1 to 10 or 10 to 100.
- He says this is the first time OpenAI feels it knows what to do. Research and infrastructure paths are fairly clear, and the product path is getting clearer.
- He says "the degree to which you can get everybody aligned and pointed at the same thing is a significant determinant in how fast you can move." For a long time OpenAI was a pure research lab and found it hard to act on conviction with so many good options.
- On his first startup, Loopt, he says it never found product market fit or escape velocity. Even so, he says nothing has a higher rate of generalized learning than doing a startup, and he calls his 20s an "apprenticeship."
- Loopt gave him firsthand experience of a platform shift and how messy the beginning is.
