## Shifting from building a product to building a company

- Before product-market fit, the founder's number one job is to build a great product. Around 25 employees, the main job shifts to building a great company, and it stays there. Sam calls this "probably the biggest shift in being a Founder that ever happens."
- Most of the items he lists matter between months 12 and 24, or around 25 people. Until then, founders can ignore everything except writing code and talking to users.
- Being a founder for "nine of the 10 years" means mostly building the company. He says that if you don't want to be the long-term CEO, you probably shouldn't start one. He is not totally sure about this and allows exceptions.
- He recommends never hiring a professional CEO. The most successful tech companies are run by their founders for a very long time, and Larry Page came back to be CEO again.
- Building a company that does repeatable innovation is "the single hardest thing in business." Most companies do one great thing because the founder pushes it through, then fail on follow-on products. Founders need to plan for version 10 and 11, as Apple did over 30 or 40 years.

## Management structure and the four founder-as-manager failures

**Structure**
- Up to 20 or 25 employees, everyone reporting to the founder is the best structure for productivity.
- Lack of structure "fails all at once." What works at 20 people is disastrous at 30.
- The fix is simple. Every employee knows who their one manager is, every manager knows their direct reports, and people are clustered into sensible teams. Clarity and simplicity matter most.
- Don't innovate on management. Innovate on product and business model. Avoid both "no structure" and convoluted circular matrix structures where people report to different people for different things.

**Four failure cases**
1. **Fear of hiring senior people.** Early on, senior hires are usually a mistake, because aptitude and willingness to work hard beat experience. When the company scales, experienced executives become valuable. Almost every founder who hires a great executive who takes over big pieces of the business says "wow I wish I had done that earlier."
2. **Hero mode.** A leader of customer support works 18, 19, then 20 hours a day to lead by example, and won't stop to hire because tickets will pile up. The fix is to accept falling behind for two or three weeks, hire three more people sized to the growth rate, and hire ahead of demand next time. The wrong answer is to stay in hero mode until burnout.
3. **Bad delegation.** The bad version is "research it, bring me the data and tradeoffs, I'll decide." The good version is "you're really smart, that's why I hired you; here's what to think about, here's what I think, but you make the decision, I trust you, let me know." Steve Jobs got away with making every decision himself, but for 99.9% of founders the second method works better.
4. **Weak personal organization.** Founders need a system to track what they need to do, what everyone else is doing, and what to follow up on.

## Writing down the how and the why

- Founders wish they had written down how things are done and why, earlier. Early on this happens at lunch and dinner, but it doesn't scale.
- The "how" covers things like how the company builds product, pushes to production and handles support. The "why" is the cultural values.
- If the founder writes it on a wiki and every employee reads it, the founder "get[s] to basically write the law." If not, the culture becomes random oral tradition from whichever hiring manager or friend tells the new hire.
- Sam calls this one of the highest-leverage things founders skip. He cites Brian Chesky's talk on values.

## HR, compensation, equity and hiring process

**HR basics**
- Founders say people are their most important asset but "we don't want any HR." Good HR is not sitcom HR. It means clear structure, a career path and frequent, simple performance feedback.
- Feedback should link to getting people out if they do badly and to compensation if they do well.
- Set compensation bands early (for example mid-level and senior engineer ranges, and how you move between them). Everyone will eventually learn everyone else's pay, and wildly varying pay causes "a complete meltdown disaster." Bands also save crazy negotiation.

**Equity**
- Keep giving a lot of equity to employees all the way through. Investors, though not YC, give bad advice here because they are short-sighted and don't want to dilute themselves.
- YC data shows the most successful companies, and the ones where investors do best, give out stock year after year.
- Sam tells founders to expect to give out 3-5% of the company every year for the next 10 years. Individual grants shrink, but in aggregate it is a lot.
- Plan refresher grants early. Never let an employee reach three of four vested years and start thinking about leaving.
- Structures he mentions: six-year vesting with big grants (his preference, because companies take a while to build), pyramid vesting (back-weighted, so year four vests much more than year one), and continuous forward vesting (grants auto-renew every year at the same number of shares).
- Move off an Excel spreadsheet to option management software around this point. He has seen mistakes cost companies or employees tens of millions of dollars.

**Other HR and hiring items**
- New HR rules, such as harassment and diversity training, start around 50 employees.
- Watch for burnout. Product-market fit is a sprint, but scaling is a marathon. Burning out the whole company at once is often company-ending.
- Hire a full-time recruiter once things are working. Doing it too early means hiring too fast, which usually implodes, but most founders are late.
- Announce every offer on an internal mailing list before making it, until about 300-400 employees. About half the time someone knows something good or bad about the candidate.
- Build an onboarding program, including a first-week plan and a buddy.
- Think about diversity before months 12-24. Teams that hire all men for the first 15 or 20 engineers get a culture that takes on a life of its own, and founders regret it.
- Early employees: the company can outgrow them (for example, an early engineer who wants to be VP of Engineering). Plan a path for the first 10 or 15 employees and talk to them directly about their careers.
- On diversity versus hiring people you know: "diversity of backgrounds but you don't want diversity of vision." Hire people you trust who share the goal but bring complementary skills. A monoculture often causes problems later.

## Keeping the company productive and aligned

- Productivity falls with the square of the number of employees unless you work at it, because every pair of people adds communication overhead. Start building systems at 25-50 people.
- The key word is alignment. Companies become unproductive when people are not on the same page or are working against each other.
- Alignment starts with a clear roadmap and goals covering the next 3, 6 or 12 months. Sam's test is to ask whether 10 random employees would name the same top three goals. Founders always say yes, but in practice no two employees give the same three goals in order. Keep repeating the message, because almost no founder does it enough.
- Set values early so smart people make consistent decisions.
- Stay run by great products, not process for its own sake. One easy tactic is "ship something every day."
- Rhythm of communication:
  - A weekly management meeting of the CEO's direct reports.
  - All-hands at least monthly, covering results and the roadmap.
  - A quarterly plan for the next three months, tied to the annual goals.
  - Offsites, meaning a weekend at a cabin with the best people to ask what the company wants to be and what it should be doing or not doing. He says everyone who does them finds them well worth it.
- Personal system: one sheet of paper with 3-12 month goals, read every day. One page per day for short-term goals, flipping forward seven pages for something due in a week. A list per person with what they're working on, what to tell them and what was discussed last time.

## Back-office, legal and finance checklist

- **Accounting:** around month 18, once things work, get books in order. Use an outsourced person, start annual audits and set up an accounting firm.
- **Legal documents:** collect every agreement the company has signed, such as leases and employee PIIAs. It's easy to fix now and hard to fix in the middle of a financing.
- **Founder (FF) stock:** a special class that lets founders sell in a later round without affecting the common valuation. Asking for it at seed became a bad signal, because founders obsessed with early liquidity tended to fail. Sam says it can safely wait until a later round, around Series B, to set up, with sales years later. Most founders don't want to sell until the company is worth something like a billion dollars.
- **IP and trademarks:**
  - A patent can be filed within 12 months of announcing. At about 11 months after launch, file a provisional patent.
  - A provisional costs about $1,000, holds your place in line and buys another year to decide on a full patent.
  - File trademarks in the US and major international markets, and grab all the domains.
- **FP&A:** hire someone to build a great financial model so you know where the knobs are. He cites PayPal CFO Roelof Botha, whose top sheet ran 1,500 lines. Most companies wait until hundreds of employees, and Sam says it is worth doing earlier.
- **Full-time fundraiser:** after the B round, hire a great internal person whose job is to raise money, for example targeting a C-round valuation double what it would otherwise be. Sam says this beats an investment banker, costs much less and takes "literally half the dilution."
- **Tax structure:** spend some time on it once things work. He says he finds it boring, but the IP-in-Ireland structure can only be done relatively early, and it matters when competing with companies that did it.

## Founder psychology, focus and acquisition offers

- The emotional swings get worse as the company grows. The highs are better but the lows are worse, so manage your psychology early.
- Success turns you from underdog into a target. Internet commenters, then journalists, will criticize you. Make peace with it early or it will bother you all the way through.
- Few founders think long term. Most think a year ahead and plan to sell in three years. Committing with your cofounders to a 10-year strategy gives a huge advantage and is "a very high leverage thing."
- Take vacation. Running for three or four years without more than a day off leads to nasty burnout.
- Loss of focus is a symptom of burnout. Founders start doing easier, more gratifying things like advising and going to conferences. The most common post-YC failure is being focused during YC, then doing many other things afterward. Focus is what made you successful, so fight to keep it.
- Acquisition talks are "one of the biggest killers" of companies. They distract and demoralize, and if an offer comes in low you've already mentally checked out and take it.
  - Don't start a conversation unless you'd sell at a pretty low number.
  - Don't take meetings hoping for a miracle offer. A truly high offer would arrive before you met.
- Founders giving up is what kills startups at the root. Sometimes quitting is right, but mismanaging your psychology and quitting when you shouldn't is the final cause of death for most.

## PR, business development and deal-making

**PR**
- Press won't save an early startup, but once the product works, founders should spend a little time on it.
- Write the key messaging yourself and never outsource it. Once the press decides how it talks about you, it's very hard to change.
- Get to know key journalists yourself. PR firms try to prevent this because they need a reason to exist, but no journalist wants to talk to a "PR Flack."
- Sam calls not hiring a PR firm "the biggest PR hack." Pick three or four journalists, build close relationships, and contact them directly. They will cover every story you give them, which beats a firm blasting 200 contacts.

**Business development (five points)**
1. Nothing matters without a great product.
2. Build a personal connection and actually care about what the other person gets. Don't treat them as a one-off transaction.
3. Create competitive dynamics. If you don't do the deal with party A, you'll do it with party B. This is the single thing that makes deals happen and gets good terms.
4. Persist, and go beyond your comfort point.
5. Ask for what you want, even if it feels aggressive. Most of the time you won't be thrown out, and you might get it. He says he still struggles with this himself.

## Timeline of a startup, failing well and early fundraising

- The Airbnb founder's drawing of the YC process has two gears, product and market, and the way to close the gap is to meet users and get very close to them.
- The adoption curve:
  - A launch press spike falls to nothing.
  - At least once, things look about to die.
  - A long "trough of sorrow" follows, with wiggles of false hope.
  - In Airbnb's case, it was a thousand days before the graph began to tick upward, and growth came about three years later.
- Fundraising timing:
  - Wait until the idea is figured out and shows initial promise.
  - Raising puts time pressure on you. If you've raised and the idea fails, you pivot to the first vaguely plausible idea, which is bad.
  - Without money you can flail and pivot until something works.
  - Ideally avoid raising more than $100-200,000 until things work or point toward working.
- Failing gracefully:
  - He doesn't agree with "failure is great." Failure still sucks and you should try not to fail.
  - If it happens, tell investors early. Don't surprise them or run out of money entirely. Avoid unpaid debts and employees finding the door locked.
  - Shut down gracefully, help employees find jobs and give two to four weeks of severance.
  - Investors expect to lose on most investments, and the winners pay for it "with a factor of 100."
- On a market you don't know: you can jump in, or work in the space for a year or two first. He leans slightly toward the second, but either works if you get "uncomfortably close" to users.
- Location: he still thinks Silicon Valley is the best "by very significant margin." He says it is maybe weakening slightly because of cost, and that he would still pick it himself.
- YC data: in the latest batch, 41% of founders were born outside the US, from 30 different countries.
