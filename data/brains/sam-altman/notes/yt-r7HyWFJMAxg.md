## Hard tech startups follow the same rules as software startups

- Sam's main point is to do as little differently as possible. The common mistake is thinking "I'm different because I'm saving the world, the normal rules of startups don't apply to me."
- Founders in that mode take a long time, use a lot of capital, skip finding a customer, and sit in a lab for five years without talking to anyone.
- YC briefly tried running hard tech companies as a separate group. Sam calls that "really bad" and stopped.
- Hard tech companies now sit in the mix with all other YC companies, whether they build nuclear reactors, synthetic biology or photo apps.
- His reasoning: "the market does not care" about a noble purpose. If you don't build a real thing for a real customer that is better and cheaper, or ideally both, you fail.
- The problem with hard tech companies is that they don't look enough like successful software companies, not that they look too much like them.
- In his view the rules apply "even a little bit more" to hard tech.

## Ship something in three months and improve 10% every week

- Sam asks founders with a seven-year nuclear reactor plan what can be done in three months. They first say nothing, then find something, for example getting the heat system running.
- By the end of YC, companies "are always able to do more than they thought they could."
- Momentum "becomes a product of itself." Small incremental wins build on each other.
- He calls getting 10% better every week "the whole secret of software startups." Facebook started small and not very good, then improved 10% a week for years.
- With Ginkgo Bioworks, the first meeting was spent finding what they could make 10% better every week, ignoring how fast they thought they could move.
- The key step is a small initial project with a real test in the world that you complete on a short timeframe.
- Startups "survive on their own momentum." A startup that is winning keeps winning, and one that starts losing keeps losing. A founder should make the company win from the start, even with tiny wins.
- The common mistake is to "sit around and do nothing and talk a lot." Companies that win start doing things and get laughed at for how small they are. They build a culture of winning, and the critics eventually stop mattering.
- Momentum attracts good people and makes them work harder. If nothing ships for three years it doesn't matter whether you start today or tomorrow, but if something ships this week each day counts.

## Cost and cycle time decide whether a field suits a startup

- Sam says the two factors that let software startups dominate are low cost and fast cycle time. Unit economics also help: another copy is usually free, and scale arrives fast enough to build network effects and monopoly.
- To judge a new field, check whether cost has come down dramatically and cycle time has gotten much faster. If so, startups can overcome their natural disadvantages. Genomics is his recent example.
- If you can't see how your work drives cost down and iteration speed up, "you have to think really hard about starting a startup there."
- Both variables are changing in almost every field, so he calls this a wonderful time to start a company in any of them.
- Time will always be against a small company. If you can get weekly improvement, big competitors won't match it and "you can really win."
- He says not to make excuses. Life is not fair and big companies don't play by fair rules, but complaining won't win. It is the founder's duty to find a way to get 10% better every week.
- Software founders can change course quickly, so they are specific about what they'll do. Hard tech founders are usually vague.

## Why hard companies can be easier to start than easy ones

- Sam says it is often easier to start a hard company than an easy one. The 11,000th photo sharing app makes people roll their eyes, so hiring, investors and press are all hard.
- A synthetic biology or similar company draws people who want to help. He thinks this is "really badly underestimated."
- Few people try. He says there were about four private nuclear fusion companies in North America, which is an automatic competitive advantage.
- Cruise Automation (from MIT, YC) is his example. Founder Kyle was pressured to build another video streaming site and chose self-driving cars instead. The company didn't exist about two and a half years earlier, and GM signed an acquisition deal for a huge amount.
- SpaceX and Tesla had little competition for the same reason, since few people were starting rocket or electric vehicle companies.
- He argues good ideas are undervalued. Maybe 70 or 80% of venture capital chases the current fad (the next Facebook, then the next Workday), with founders moving like a school of fish.
- Taking time up front to find a great idea and a new technology you can explain gives an automatic advantage.
- He argues people misprice risk. If you work hard on a startup for a couple of years and fail, you still have nothing but great experience. The real risk is never trying a great idea.
- His advice on market choice is not to worry about competitors but to ask whether you believe you can build a $10 billion company in that market.

## Take detours with real customers

- Founders hate detours but they are "worth it." Sam says to hold yourself to building something you can give a real customer in a short timeframe, which prevents the mistake that has killed most hard tech startups.
- Tesla built the expensive, "okay" Roadster, then the expensive but great Model S, and then used that revenue to build the Model 3. The mass-market electric car was released the month before this talk.
- SpaceX wants to go to Mars but resupplies the ISS and may build an internet network in space on the way.
- It is hard to go from zero to a colony on Mars with no intermediate steps.
- More hard tech startups fail from a grand plan that sounds good in the press and never produces anything than from a first project that was too small. Finding the "Goldilocks" project is "somewhat of an art."
- For a one-goal company like nuclear fusion, list the biggest risks and the risks you can test first, then find where they intersect to set the order. A first goal might be a stable plasma for a set time.
- Rigetti (quantum computing) is his example. In their first office hours Sam and Chad filled several whiteboards with steps to a working 40-qubit system. The first milestone was a software proof for the design, planned for three months and done in two.
- He also recommends government as a customer. He says it went out of fashion because of VC groupthink, that government is slower but its people want things better and cheaper, and that more YC companies now sell to it.

## What kills hard tech startups: short-term money, negativity, too much cash

- **Short-term investors and partners:** Many say they are long-term and then push for a sale in a year. "The more time an investor spends trying to convince you that he or she is a very long-term oriented shareholder the less you should believe it."
- Sam keeps a separate list of investors willing to hold shares past 10 years, the typical VC fund lifetime.
- If you want a 15 to 20 year company, make sure the people around you will sign up for it. He has no problem with starting an easy software company first and doing the hard thing next.
- **Negative people:** Someone who says all day that it won't work will wear you down however tough you are, so remove them.
- **Too much money:** It can ruin culture and focus. YC data (small sample) showed companies that raised $40 million or more in their Series A failed far more often than the average Series A company, and he thinks the pull toward slowness is stronger in hard tech.
- **Only science skill:** Someone already on the team must get good at business. Sam says it is "not hard" and "less fun," and it is better than bolting on a business person later. He recommends YC or hands-on practice over business school.

## Funding, team and company-building answers from Q&A

- **Co-founders:** The best sources are school and working together. Co-founder meetups are the worst. Startups bring out the worst in people, and a prior relationship lets you keep working together after expected value dips below zero. He advises being a solo founder over starting with someone you just met at a meetup, and trying a small project together first.
- **Control:** VCs learned from Google and Facebook that they make the most money when founders keep control. In today's climate you shouldn't be asked to give up board or voting control, and at most an investor lets you vote their shares.
- **Investors and small wins:** If an investor doesn't care about small wins, you don't want them. The right ones understand this is the only path for these companies.
- **Management at OpenAI:** What worked was getting the right people and setting short-term milestones against a chosen attack vector on how to build AI. His preferred theory is to get smart, motivated people who believe in the same mission, so you don't have to manage much.
- **Patents:** YC advises every hard tech company to file a provisional patent. It takes about an afternoon and about $1,000 and buys a year before filing the full patent, which costs $15,000 to $20,000. He says it is a rare worthwhile task outside talking to users and building product.
- **Hardware and location:** Launching hardware and delivering the first thousands of units gets easier every six months and is no longer the problem that kills companies. YC started in Boston and moved to the Bay Area. Sam's concern is that Bay Area rent is very high (about $5,000 for a one-bedroom) and that people there think in two-year stints. Boston and Silicon Valley both work, and a natural reason can justify other places.
- **Lifestyle businesses:** They are fine, but YC's model loses money most of the time and relies on hits, so it does not fit them.
- **Fear of failure:** It is normal and "failing sucks," but nobody thinks about you as much as you do. Silicon Valley judges you on your biggest success rather than your biggest failure.
