## YC's mission and what Sam added to Y Combinator

- Sam wants YC to be "the organization that enables the most innovation in the world." He says that is Google today and he hopes it is YC in the not so distant future.
- His logic is that economic growth is needed, growth requires more innovation, and so YC should maximize innovation, including things that may not make money.
- Programs he added after taking over (he had been president for about two and a half years):
  - Funding hard-science companies: self-driving (Cruise, later acquired by GM), nuclear energy, biotech, space and aircraft. These need huge capital and existing investors would not fund them, so YC started doing it.
  - A growth fund, to keep supporting companies that come out of the portfolio.
  - The YC Fellowship, a light-touch early-stage program. The goal is to eventually touch 10,000 companies a year, because the core program is too small.
  - YC Research, a nonprofit group funding important technology that does not fit a for-profit startup (AI, basic income, education, possibly synthetic biology).
- He objects to analogies like "university" or "DARPA" for YC. He thinks it is a fundamentally new structure that will take about 50 years to fully figure out.
- He would invest in things that take 100 years: the biggest investor advantage is "a longer time horizon than everybody else" and a willingness to take more illiquidity and volatility.

## Core startup lessons: build something a few people love, and protect momentum

- The old YC motto was "make something people want." Sam says it should be updated to "make something people love."
- Choice for a startup: a small number of users who really love it, or a large number who only like it. Large-audience, low-love products get taken by big companies.
- The best companies in the YC portfolio all share this trait: some initial users loved what was built, became evangelists, and told others to use it (Stripe, Airbnb). He says they all had this, "full stop."
- YC's process: build a product a small number of users love, then work out how to grow it.
- Growth matters for two reasons. It shows people actually love the product, provided it is real growth with retained users and not users who churn. It also builds momentum.
- "Momentum is everything in a startup." Losing it is extremely hard to recover from. Startups that win keep winning, and startups that lose keep losing. Startups live and survive off their own momentum.

## Seed to Series A mistakes: retention, new responsibilities and compensation

- Founders put huge effort into recruiting and almost none into retaining talent, then pay for it later. Retention must become as much of the job as recruiting, usually learned only after losing half the team.
- Sam cites Zuckerberg's rule of hiring only people you would report to. Good people have as many options as you, so if the role is not good enough that you would stay in it, you will struggle to keep your best people.
- Retention tactics (none are hard, they just take time):
  - Give your best 5-10 people time with the CEO. Some of the best CEOs take each of their top people to a one-on-one dinner or drinks every month. Sam calls it a big commitment of dinner slots but says it works, because those people crave it and want to be asked their opinion and listened to.
  - Keep giving people new tasks and more responsibility. Slotting VPs into one function and never moving them makes them leave. New or additional tasks each year keep them happier.
  - Proactively re-up compensation for the top 5-10 people. Many founders are bad at this and many YC companies have failed at this transition.

## Delegation: don't burn out on one bad hire, and keep your critical function

- Founders get delegation wrong in both directions.
- Under-delegating usually follows one bad experience (a bad VP hire) after which the founder swears off delegation. Sam's advice is to trust that delegation is still a good idea despite one disaster.
- Over-delegating happens when a board member says to hire five VPs for five areas and the founder hands off the area they care about most.
- His rule: whatever the one or maybe two most critical pieces of the business are, and whatever you enjoy most, do those yourself.
  - A product-loving founder should not hire a VP of Product, because both will want the same thing, you will dislike how they do it, and they will dislike your interference.
  - A finance-loving founder should skip the CFO and hire a controller.
- Keeping your critical piece also makes you better at delegating everything else.

## Fundraising: don't push every round to the limit

- Problems come from overextending: taking the highest possible price without being able to beat it in the next round, or ramping burn to the maximum while keeping only 9-12 months of runway.
- He applies the "margin of safety" idea from investing, which everyone understands except startup CEOs raising venture rounds.
- Advice:
  - Don't take a difficult-to-work-with investor just because they beat your best offer by 3% or 10%.
  - Don't run a plan where you run out of money three months before you begin your next fundraise.
- Founders fixate on the headline pre-money valuation because it is easy to compare, while terms and investor quality are hard to compare. He ties this to competitive founders optimizing the one quantitative metric they can win on.
- "Fundraising does not have to be hard" if the company is great and does not depend on "gigantic monster outside capital to survive."

## Choosing investors: diligence them like a VP of Sales

- Founders typically make 12 reference calls before hiring a VP of Sales and 0-2 before adding a board member.
- Sam says to treat a new investor with at least as much care as a senior hire.
- Call the CEOs for whom things went badly (the "awfulest" ones), including ones the firm did not recommend you talk to. He says founders have walked away from deals they were about to take after these calls.
- Spend many hours together in person before adding someone to the board or cap table. A VP can be fired but an investor is extremely difficult to remove.

## Picking winners and judging founders (YC's 10-minute interviews)

- YC decides in 10-minute interviews because of the volume of companies it must see. The only question is whether the person has "some seed of what it takes to be the next Mark Zuckerberg," not whether they definitely will be.
- Traits assessed, in rough order of ease:
  - Intelligence: easy to tell, in much less than 10 minutes.
  - Determination: the hardest trait to read, and the main source of YC's misses in either direction.
  - Clarity of vision, communication skills and non-obvious brilliance of the idea: quite a lot of data is available but not always right.
- New YC partners can be trained to do this in a year or two, so he thinks it is not an incredibly difficult skill. He notes YC had not missed many future billion-dollar companies, though at its current scale it has surely rejected some.
- Predicting breakouts: winners tend to do something new, so rules of thumb are hard, but he looks for:
  - Users who love the product.
  - Founder quality (the traits above).
  - A market that is small today but growing fast. He calls "how big is your market" a terrible question: you would not decide on current revenue, you want to know how it will grow.
  - Capturing a large share of a small market already, for example Airbnb with a significant amount of existing New York apartment renters by the end of YC, and Stripe with nearly 100% of YC batch companies that needed to process payments.

## Judging a team as an investor: meet the direct reports and test the recruiting pull

- As an investor before YC, Sam would not make a large investment without meeting all of the founder's direct reports. He calls it incredibly high signal and says it was the most effective non-obvious thing he did, and that it changed his decision both ways.
- Reasoning: the team you build is the company you build.
- To judge a founder's ability to recruit: notice how tempted you are to join the company after spending time with them. Naturally great recruiters leave everyone they meet with the feeling that they should go join.

## AI: the next platform and Sam's stance on risk

- AI is at minimum the next computing platform, and new platforms are where great investments come from. He also calls it possibly the next life form, the "children of humanity."
- Timeframe may be 10 or 100 years, but he says that is a blink in human history. Everyone agrees it is coming and argues only about timing.
- It could be "the best or the worst thing ever to happen to humanity." He is working to make it the best but says he would be lying if he claimed to be 100% confident in the plan, since controlling or understanding something more powerful than its creator is hard to guarantee.
- His hope: AI is widely distributed, with agents serving individuals the way the neocortex serves the limbic system, so the collective of humanity can stop any individual's misuse.
- He expects an economic shift on the scale of the agricultural and industrial revolutions, with short-term turbulence. He believes human demand is limitless so new work will appear. YC is running a basic income experiment to help ease the transition.
- A possible long-run outcome is global GDP growing around 100% a year for some number of years.
