## Mindset and the traits of great founders

- Sam says the two phrases top founders would use to describe themselves are "I always figure it out" and "I never give up." He puts this ahead of intelligence, domain expertise, network or connections.
- He calls the trait determination, or "relentlessly resourceful" (Paul Graham's phrase). Many people have everything else, so this is the rare, limiting trait.
- He thinks it can be learned by practicing it, as with any other skill.
- Three things he has seen successful founders do to get things done: focus, self-belief, and personal connections. Momentum is a "crazily self-fulfilling prophecy."
- Focus means a relentless single goal and ignoring shiny objects. The connections matter for recruiting, selling, raising money and talking to the press.
- Silicon Valley's strengths, in his view, are a relentless belief in the future, a critical mass of people who will not mock ambition, density, and a culture of paying it forward.
- He says people elsewhere should not grade themselves on a curve. Talent is evenly distributed, and apart from access to capital, everything else (office space, hiring engineers) is harder in Silicon Valley.
- He advises finding a few people you can call late at night with a wild idea who will engage with it instead of saying "that's dumb." Most ideas are bad, but the rare good ones get killed if everyone around you cuts them down.
- Live in the future: Alan Kay tries to think 30 years out and build what is 15 years out. Sam notes that this can look crazy in the present, and you will be mocked for it.
- He recommends working very hard early in your career. Hard work "pays off like compound interest," so doing it in your late teens and 20s gives it the most working years to pay off.

## Communication, vision and recruiting

- Sam says he did not used to think clear communication mattered this much, but now does. Almost all the best startups' founders could explain what they do in about 25 words from the first meeting. Founders who can't tend not to succeed.
- Recruiting the first employees is very hard, and "it is easier to start a hard startup than it is to start an easy startup." An inspiring, exciting vision attracts people, while a fine but forgettable business does not.
- He says to be genuine rather than charismatic. Help people even without an immediate payoff. Sam describes this paying off years later in investments, partner hires and relationships.
- He holds up Brian Chesky as a model of communicating a vision, saying he makes Airbnb feel important.
- His advice on hiring: be realistic that you will only hire a few people and will get less done than you want. Hold the bar very high, because "two or three great people" beat "ten warm bodies." YC is always behind its hiring plan.

## Hiring framework: values, then aptitude, then skills

- Sam's hiring order is values first, aptitude second, specific skills third. He says most people reverse this, and that "I need an engineer with a background in node" is the worst way to choose a co-founder.
- Treat the first ~10 hires as nearly co-founder level. They should believe in the mission, share your values and be very talented.
- He has "almost never gone wrong" hiring inexperienced but committed, talented people, and expects them to be a bit less productive for the first month. He has bet on many leaders without domain experience. The exception is a role like general counsel, where he would not hire a non-lawyer.
- He hires slowly and spends a lot of time with direct-report candidates: four or five meals, many hours in the office, and talking through life stories and goals. He says he cannot judge someone from two one-hour meetings.
- YC makes new partners go through roughly a year: office hours, then part-time partner for 6 to 12 months, then a trial period. Even with a friendly candidate, YC says no if it does not feel right.
- Example: YC set up a growth fund to invest $50M+ at a time. Everyone advised hiring an experienced growth-equity investor "in cufflinks and suits," but every candidate he met failed the values test. He hired the Twitter CFO, who had never invested, and it "worked out fantastically well." Specialists were hired under him.
- He is skeptical of founders who lack experience themselves but say they will only hire people with 20 years of experience.

## Ideas, timing and waves

- Sam says people overrate the idea. There are many good ideas, but few people willing to put in years of effort. Still, a bad idea is also bad.
- YC funded 21 companies whose founders were strong but who had no idea. All 21 failed in the first year, the worst experiment they had run. Wait for an idea you believe in so passionately that a startup is the best way to pursue it.
- Original thought is rare, and copying is disastrous. After Facebook there were over a thousand social networks, and after Instagram over a thousand photo apps. The first mover usually wins. The year he joined Helion's board, there were 10,000 photo startups and one nuclear fusion startup.
- Good sources of ideas: problems in your own life (a high percentage of transformational companies began this way), and spotting a "great wave" of technology early.
- Founders can't create a technology wave, but they can notice one coming while others call it a toy and serve it. Waves explain clusters in time: the late-90s internet wave (Amazon, Google, Yahoo) and the post-2007 mobile wave (Uber, Airbnb, Snapchat, Instagram, WhatsApp). He says Facebook straddled both. He asks what the current wave is.
- His one bet he feels confident enough to name: machine learning applied to every vertical is "as close to a guaranteed shot as you can have," though it is brutally competitive and there will be $100M companies in it. He tells people to trust their own convictions over his.

## Co-founders and company culture

- YC has a significant preference for co-founders, and about 10% of funded companies are single-founder. Sam says a solo founder is better than a bad co-founder, and cites Amazon as essentially a single-founder company.
- He wants shared history, like a relationship, so that goodwill carries you through the stressful stretch where a startup's expected value dips. He says co-founder pairs formed just to get into YC fall apart when stressed.
- Co-founders pull each other up on low days. Solo founders have no one to do that, so they need strong internal conviction.
- Don't pick a co-founder on technical skill alone. Pick for determination, and pick humble over entitled. A company reflects its founders' culture for a long time, and Sam says arrogance is poisonous.
- He says he has seen introverted founders conclude they need "a really outgoing, kind of arrogant" co-founder because that's what success looks like in movies, and he says that mistake hurts.
- On boards: a board's role is "advice and consent." It should fire a bad CEO, but otherwise give the CEO wide latitude. If you don't respect your board, fix the board.
- Good co-founder teams compromise heavily and each knows what the others care most about. He says a power-drunk CEO usually drives co-founders out. A good team staying together 10 to 20 years is very valuable.
- On decision-making at YC (18 partners), he describes explicit rules: things any one partner can do, things needing three partners, domain owners, and full-partnership votes for rare big decisions like raising the growth fund. He suggests spelling out up front who owns what.

## Product, users and iteration speed

- Sam says "nothing but a great product will save you." The common startup mistake is doing well at marketing, hiring and fundraising while ignoring the essential task of making a great product, then blaming "conspiracy theories" when it fails.
- He cites Paul Buchheit's advice: a small number of users who love you beats a large number who like you a little. Get users who love it, then find more people like them. He says this is counterintuitive, since most think they need many users on day one.
- How many is enough depends on the product: for an enterprise product paying hundreds or thousands of dollars a year, ten could be enough, while a consumer product probably needs thousands.
- On the "magic moment," Alex Schultz of Facebook talks about the one thing that makes users love a product enough to tell friends. Airbnb asks how to deliver an "11-star experience" and then works back to a 6-star one so good people tell friends. Sam says there are trade-offs: a magic feature that makes the rest suck is probably not worth it.
- Iteration speed compounds. If your product improves 10% per cycle, a one-week cycle against a competitor's three months leaves them in the dust. He says most hard-tech startups fail because their cycles are about three months, "which basically rounds to infinity."
- He says hard-tech founders arrive saying they can't get anything done in 90 days, and leave saying it was the most productive 90 days of their lives. Ginkgo Bioworks' founder said doing YC at the start could have compressed their first seven years into one.
- Be a "strong ideas, weakly held" company: have one idea you believe is best, and change your mind on new data. Don't say "we could do these seventeen things."

## Evaluating hard-tech momentum: the Cruise example

- Sam says investors are right that momentum matters, and that it can show up as fast growth or as continual breakthroughs in the lab. In hard tech you identify it by spending time in the lab, and he can often tell from one weekly research meeting.
- He sets a goal with each hard-tech startup for the end of the YC program. Founders who engage in a genuine brainstorm about an aggressive milestone are good, while those who say there's no intermediate milestone for three years usually fail.
- Cruise: YC funded the self-driving car company in January 2014, before self-driving cars were cool. It was run by a former Justin.tv co-founder with no car expertise. Investors thought it was crazy, and Google would win.
- They compromised on a goal: a car that drives itself on the highway from San Francisco to Mountain View by demo day. It did, with computers hanging out the back, 90 days later.
- Later investors passed on the A round (self-driving "isn't going to be a thing"). Sam wrote his biggest check of the year, and about seven months later GM acquired the company. Investors who had passed then asked him for more self-driving companies.
- His lesson: you can evaluate progress by going to the office, even without a daily-active-users chart. Most hard-tech companies, he notes, are not in fact progressing quickly.

## Pitching and investing advice

- Sam gave live feedback on a pitch from Lucid Labs (AI skin cancer diagnosis). His first point was to state what you do in one simple sentence early, e.g. "We use AI to diagnose skin cancer better than the best human doctors." He says that 95% of pitches miss this and that investors want it most.
- Lead with the strongest proof, that the product works and beats human dermatologists, before the problem and market statistics. Replace long problem slides with a quick statement of the error rate and death toll.
- What you leave out matters as much as what you say. Show the roadmap for the next two years: how to get from here to clinics, revenue and impact.
- He says the competitive-landscape 2x2 where the startup sits in the upper right is "always terrible," and that no one ever says "we're a small market." Show how you win the market, e.g. get into 90,000 clinics.
- Put the big vision (an AI doctor for everything) at the end, after the focused starting point.
- On his own investing: his edge is a very long time horizon (holding 15 to 20 years), which most investors claim but do not really have. His personal system is a nightly list of three big tasks plus about 50 small ones, with mornings for creative work. He has used it for 15 years, and he says there's no secret beyond knowing what to get done and guarding your time.
