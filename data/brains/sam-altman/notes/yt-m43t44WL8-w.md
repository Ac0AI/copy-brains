## Sam's case for not raising money early

- Sam says a technical founder who also understands what the product should be and how to build it "is forced to be reckoned with" in Silicon Valley. He says this is valued more here than anywhere else, and that founders have more leverage over investors than they realize.
- If you can avoid raising, or wait, he says that is "hugely to your advantage". Raising lots of money can work well, but it can also slow you down.
- Good reasons to raise:
  - The company needs a lot of money to execute.
  - Funding will help win sales or partnerships by showing partners you will be around.
  - The money will help scale the business.
- The longer you wait, the more likely you are to have figured out a product that works, and the better your valuation.
- Raising money on day one is "almost always a mistake".
- Bad reasons he says he hears more and more:
  - Legitimizing the company, or wanting a VC behind you just to feel real.
  - Getting great PR from the funding.
  - "Funding will make me successful." He says funding does not make you successful, and that raising VC is "the beginning of a very long process".
- Sam says Loopt raised its Series A (NEA and Sequoia) "probably earlier than we should have". The reason was legitimacy with the wireless carriers. After Y Combinator and as college sophomores, the company was out of money by the time it signed.

## Angel vs VC, and how to run the process

- **VC vs angel:** VCs almost always do more work for you than angels, whatever the angels say.
  - Fundraising is a VC's full-time job.
  - They have large networks, and the whole partnership works to help you.
- **Exit options:** An angel round leaves the "sell early" option open in a way a VC round does not. Founders must decide whether they want to build something big or flip it quickly.
  - If an early exit is the strategy, he would be disinclined to do a VC round.
- **Angel terms:** Angels usually, but not always, invest faster and on slightly more favorable terms.
- **Starting materials:** a one-page executive summary and a 10-15 slide deck. Use the summary to get intros to firms.
- **Focus:** Go after the good firms. Good companies are rare, so a good team doing something interesting has an easy time raising.
- **Number of firms:** Six to eight firms is what works most often.
- **Run the process in parallel,** for two reasons:
  - Investors only give you leverage-relevant attention when they fear losing the deal. "The more alternatives you have the better", which he says is true of all deal making.
  - Going serially (first choice, a month of process, then second, then third) means three months gone if the first three pass. Competitors will have "blown past" you, since "speed is the key to a startup". You also now have a shopworn reputation, because later VCs will see that good firms passed.
- **Timeline and meetings:** The number of meetings varies. Sequoia met Loopt 10 times before funding, while other firms issued term sheets with zero meetings.
  - You meet more and more partners, then reach a Monday partners' meeting, and they decide.
  - During diligence, help them: let them talk to users and customers, and give financial projections, past finances and product and technology detail. User adoption data matters most.
- **Herd behavior:** If one primary firm wants a deal in a space, every other firm wants one too. Getting your company talked about at the Friday-afternoon drinks scene is a net win. The key to deals is making everyone feel you have a great alternative you will probably take.
- **Term sheets:** The first one typically arrives four to six weeks in. Once word leaks, many more arrive. He never figured out why a business is a good deal today but was not yesterday.
  - Multiple offers let you negotiate. Valuation and ownership percentages move little, but other terms move a lot.
- **Stay focused:** Put one person on fundraising, spending about half their time. He has seen companies die because all three or four people agonized over the process.
- **Closing takes longer than expected:** After signing the term sheet, expect four or five or more weeks for lawyers to reach definitive documents.
  - Loopt had no idea definitive documents existed and had no money in the bank. They only closed when they called both sets of lawyers and said they needed to close by the next night because they were out of money.

## What investors look for and the pitch

- Sam says investors look for a great market, a great team, and a product that addresses the market. VCs increasingly want revolutionary rather than incremental businesses, because of how their model works.
- **Big growing market:** "You need to surf someone else's wave." He says startups cannot create markets, and probably cannot create a market opportunity.
  - Example: fast internet, lots of bandwidth and a good Flash codec let you build YouTube.
  - You need a big, fast-growing market for a 10x or 100x return. Without it, the business probably does not suit VCs.
- **Customers with their hair on fire:** such customers overpay, act fast, take a risk on a young company, and do not mind bugs in a first version.
- **Unfair advantage:** The VC must be comfortable that competitors will not fund someone exactly like you who then crushes you.
  - Examples: technology no one else has, a smarter team, a unique partnership, or built-up network effects. It is probably not patents, though a case can be made.
- **Team and product:** A great team and a great product "get deals done". He thinks a great team can correct product deficiencies more easily than a product can make up for a team.
  - Most VCs say one of team, market or product comes first, and which one they say is instructive about the VC.
- **Demand by proxy:** In a new market with no data on what works, this is the second-best thing. Loopt's example: it showed where your friends are on a map, and "where are you" is the most common text message sent in the world.
- **The story to convince them of, in four points:**
  1. This is an exploding market.
  2. Nothing currently good addresses the customer's needs.
  3. We have the best solution.
  4. We have the best team.
- Convince an investor of those and "you will be most of the way to a term sheet".
- Tell a big story. VCs do not want to hear something incremental. They want to hear how it will become a massive deal.
- He says there are about 450 VC firms in Silicon Valley and only the first 15 or 20 make a profit, so go for good investors.

## Terms: valuation, ownership and option pool

- Sam says technical founders usually understand terms poorly, but this is not an area to spend much time on.
- **Definitions:**
  - Pre-money is the value an investor assigns before investing.
  - Post-money is the value after investment.
  - The option pool is what you earmark for employees.
- **Option pool:** Make it as small as possible. At a funding stage it comes out of only the founders' share, not the new investors'.
- **Think in ownership percentages,** not pre-money valuations, even though investors will push the valuation framing.
- **Raising more raises valuation:** Investors have a minimum ownership target. A professional VC Series A needs 20-25% and cannot go below.
  - Raising more money can mean a higher pre-money valuation too, with the same investor percentage.
- **Raise twice what you think you need.** Raising is time-consuming and things rarely go as planned.
- **Worked example:** With a $7M pre-money valuation, $3M invested and a 25% option pool, you might expect to own 70%, but you would not.
  - The investor's ownership comes out of post-money. Add the investment to pre-money, then divide investment by post-money.
- **Don't fight over fractions:** Do not focus on tenths or hundredths of a percent. It wastes a lot of time, and you are better off with the right partner and a little less equity.
  - Giving up a little more equity for a better partner is better than a second-rate partner.
- He says that if outside investors own more than 50% after a couple of rounds, "it's not such a bad thing".

## Terms: board, vesting, liquidation preference and watch-outs

- **Board composition** is a major term. Current practice is three or five seats.
  - Three seats for smaller rounds: two founders and an investor.
  - Five seats for bigger rounds: two founders, two investors and one outside director.
  - The board decides change of control, going public, and can fire the CEO, so pay attention to it.
  - Founders should have veto rights on change of control.
- **Vesting:** Standard is four years. Founders who have been working a while often get one year accelerated up front, with the rest over three years.
  - Ask for some acceleration on a change of control.
- **Liquidation preference:** how much investors get before anyone else.
  - A 1x preference on a $5M investment means the first $5M of a sale. He thinks that is fair.
  - Some investors ask for 3x or 4x. With multiple term sheets you should be able to negotiate this down to 1x.
- **Preferred vs participating preferred:** Participating preferred is "double dipping". Investors take their preference first, then also share in the rest.
  - Push them to plain preferred.
- **Anti-dilution and pro rata:** It is fair for investors to maintain their ownership.
  - Avoid "super pro rata", where an early investor gets the right to buy all of the next round. It scares off new investors, who will not talk to you if they know one investor can take the whole round.
- **Founder-shares:** Founders sometimes set up a special stock class (such as Series FF) they can sell in the B round, set up ahead of time so it does not distort common stock strike price.
  - It is controversial. Pushing hard for it can signal that the founder wants a quick flip and not to build value.
  - He has seen investors pass on deals over it. If it matters to you, think about why you want it and how you ask.

## Leverage, investor selection and keeping going

- Alternatives are "the name of the game". Loopt started by talking to just one VC with no alternatives. As soon as word got out that they were talking to a few, "the whole thing changes".
- **Two-firm deal:** Loopt did one, almost by accident, and calls it wonderful.
  - You get two firms' connections and two sets of advice.
  - The two investors keep each other in check and stop either from behaving badly.
- **Reference-check the investor:** Investors do heavy diligence on you, and you should do the same.
  - Talk to CEOs the investor funded, and look for ones who failed to hear what working with that investor was like.
- **Getting intros without connections:**
  - NEA gets about 10,000 business plans a year over the wire and has never funded one. Sequoia has funded a few.
  - Events like Startup School are the route. You can reach any VC through 10 different paths by talking to people there.
  - Ask people directly: "I'm looking to get to these VCs, this is my idea, if you think it's exciting could you help me out?"
  - VCs need you. You will have a successful company with or without funding, but they have no job if they do not find companies to fund.
- **Don't get discouraged:** The summer of Y Combinator, Loopt looked like it would blow up every day. Sam says it was a joke: "what can be the worst thing to happen to us this week, it'll probably happen". Stay focused on a great company, product and team, and "more often than not things work out".

## Carrier deals and justifying how much to raise

- **Carrier deals (Sprint and others):** Sam says they are very difficult, with no way around the red tape.
  - Loopt raised early partly for legitimacy: Sequoia's backing said "this is a real company, they're not going anywhere".
  - The first use of the money was hiring an experienced carrier business development executive, someone doing it for 20 years.
  - High-level relationships and press matter, since carrier executives read The Wall Street Journal and The New York Times.
  - A compelling product where you do most of the work and the carrier just turns it on and markets it is very appealing.
- **Justifying the amount:** VCs want financial plans, but they can be rough: how many people, the average cost, marketing spend, "we need to buy a thousand servers".
- **Don't inflate the number.** Raise more by asking for a longer horizon: "we want it for the next two, two and a half years" so you do not need to raise again, and because spending ramps up.
- Loopt did not have financial projections until late in the process, and Sam says they did not know how to use Excel until then.
- He notes that if you are going to raise, the current economy (2008) is a reason to do it then.
