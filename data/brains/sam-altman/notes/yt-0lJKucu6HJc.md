## The core test: a product so good people spontaneously tell their friends

- Sam calls this the number one lesson for startups: success approximates "the degree to which you build a product that is so good people spontaneously tell their friends about it."
- He says founders always want the secret to be something else because this is hard. If you get it right, "you have done 80% of the work" needed to be a very successful startup.
- Evidence: for Google, Facebook and similar companies, most people heard about them from a friend who said "you've got to try this."
- Indicator: the product is simple to explain and easy to understand.
  - If you can't explain what you do in a few words, and at least some people don't respond "that's pretty interesting," that is usually a mistake.
  - He reads it as a sign of unclear thinking, or of a need that isn't big enough.

## Picking markets: growth matters more than today's size, and real trends vs fake trends

- Startups should look for a market that is already in exponential growth or soon will be.
- He ties this to a common investor mistake. Investors ask about the startup's growth rate and forgive small revenue if it grows fast, but they don't apply the same logic to markets.
- The most important startups often begin in small markets that grow very quickly. Eleven years before the talk, the market for iPhone apps was $0.
- Thinking only about today's TAM is "a big mistake." The goal is to find a market that grows every year and "ride that up elevator."
- **Real vs fake trend test:**
  - A real trend: a new technology platform arrives, and early adopters use it obsessively and tell their friends how much they love it.
  - A fake trend: people may buy the product but don't use it, or don't use it enough.
- Real example, the iPhone: it sold only a million or two in its first year and many dismissed it. Owners used it for hours every day, it became central to their lives, and they told friends to get one. Sam says that was the signal to bet on mobile apps.
- Fake example (as of August 2018), VR: he believes it will be big someday, but most people he knows with a headset use it never or very rarely. Many talk about it and buy it, but he doesn't see intense per-user usage among early adopters, which he wants before a big bet.

## Ambition, an evangelist founder and a definite view of the future

- A startup needs at least one evangelical founder, usually the CEO. This person recruits, sells the product, talks to the press and raises money, and must "infect with enthusiasm the whole world." It is very hard to succeed wildly, or even build a team, without that.
- An ambitious vision helps. Don't be grandiose, because that turns people off, but let the vision grow more ambitious over time and organically.
- Sam's 2018 claim: in Silicon Valley, "it's easier to start a hard startup than it is to start an easy startup."
  - Capital is relatively easy to raise, but everything else is hard. There are so many startups that gathering enough talent in one place is difficult.
  - With a modest problem, the first two hires are easy (you can give them lots of equity), but then it gets hard. Why would employee number 20 join, and why does it matter to the world?
  - Working on something that matters if you succeed helps with both talent and mindshare.
- The best founders have a "confident and definite view of the future." He suggests being confident and flexible: they may be wrong, but they have courage in their convictions and lead clearly ("we're gonna do this") even in the face of doubt. He says this correlates with success.
- The startup ecosystem is set up to back companies with a low chance of success that are huge if they work. Going after something huge if it works attracts the best people.

## Building the team: recruiting, optimism, idea generators and bias to action

- Sam quotes Vinod Khosla: "the team you build is the company you build." Other than the market and the product, he calls team building the most important thing a founder does.
- He has met only a handful of founders who spend enough time on recruiting. He names Mark Zuckerberg as one.
- Every successful founder transitions from building a product to building a company, and building a company is about the team.
- Non-obvious traits to look for:
  - **Internal fire of belief.** The world will say why you'll fail. You need people who say "it doesn't matter what the haters say, we're gonna figure this out," with a spirit of optimism that the problem must be solvable.
  - **Idea generators.** A handful of people who constantly throw out new ideas, most of them bad. You don't want too many, because that's more ideas than a company can execute, but having some is "super important."
  - **"We'll figure it out."** His favorite thing to hear from early team members. Situations are dynamic, and people may be unqualified on paper or face a problem that feels like it will kill the company.
  - **"I've got it."** In big companies people say "that's not my department." In a startup you want people who step up and say "I'll do it, don't worry about it."
  - **Bias toward action.** Startups often win by moving fast. You never get as much data or time as you'd like, so you want people who act on less certainty and, when it fails, adapt quickly and try something else.
- **Blessing of inexperience:** many YC startups did incredible things because nobody told them it was hard. He cites a Steve Wozniak quote that his best work came from having no experience and no money. Sam doesn't say the team should be all inexperienced, but you can take more bets than normal on inexperienced, high-potential people.

## Never lose momentum

- Sam says one of a founder's most important jobs is to never lose momentum. For the first few years you never get to take your foot off the gas.
- He is honest that startups are "not the best choice for work-life balance at all," especially early on.
- Why: startups "survive on their own momentum." With momentum, people deliver beyond what they think they can. If you lose it, it is very difficult to get back.
- Tactic: keep a cadence in which the startup keeps winning on a short, predictable cycle. It is the founders' job to hold onto this.

## Basics every startup needs: competitive advantage, business model, growth plan

- **Long-term competitive advantage.** More and more YC applicants look surprised when asked about their long-term monopoly effect, competitive advantage or network effect. Sam says all the really great businesses he knows have an answer, and "the better they are the more they pretend not to." Have a plan.
- **A sensible business model.** You don't need it all figured out at the start. But if founders look surprised when asked "how are you ever going to make money," that's a bad sign. He says this happens more often than you'd think.
- **A growth plan.** If a startup looks surprised when asked how it will get users, that is also a bad sign.
- He cites Paul Buchheit's distillation of the traits of the best founders: **frugality, focus, obsession and love.** Sam thinks it is a good list and that founders should be able to say these things about themselves and their work.

## Where startups beat big companies

Sam says it is hard for startups to beat big companies most of the time, so he suggests checking whether an idea fits one of three patterns:

1. **Ideas that sound bad but are good ("one no vs one yes").**
   - A big-company product manager needs yeses from everyone up to the CEO, so one no kills the idea.
   - A startup can pitch at demo day, and any one of thousands of investors saying yes gives it a shot.
   - Startups usually beat big companies in this category, so look for ideas where one yes is much more likely than a full chain of yeses.
2. **Fast-changing markets.**
   - Speed and agility are the startup's edge. The more a market changes, the more decisions and tweaks to product and strategy there are.
   - Optimize for the number of decisions a competitor must make. A big company makes them on average worse and much slower, so you compound your advantage.
3. **Big platform shifts.**
   - Startup clusters usually follow a major platform shift. After mobile apps arrived, many now-valuable companies started.
   - Large companies work on roughly an annual cadence and are bad at a big enough strategic pivot ("the Battleship just turns too slowly").
   - A startup can wake up, decide the world is fundamentally different from six months ago, and go all in on the new direction.
