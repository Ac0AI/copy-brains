## Where OpenAI is and why the models "clicked" (day 119 of the takeoff)

- Sam says it does feel like "we are somehow in the takeoff" and accepts Patrick's joke date (day 119 since January 1) as a reasonable guess.
- Models got very good for coding, and for general work, starting late last year or early this year. Every week now feels different from the one before, and that correlated with the models crossing a threshold.
- He attributes the coding jump to several things together: raw reasoning horsepower, a feedback loop of people using models for code, enough data, and the fact that "once you know something's possible, it's much easier to go do it with vigor."
- He says it is hard to explain why a given release is the one that works. GPT-3.5 was the model where people went from "not that impressive" to "going to change the world," and he cannot say why not one model earlier or later. "You just kind of feel it."
- He had two inflection points with Codex: GPT-5.2, then a bigger one in the last few weeks (latest app updates and 5.5), when "this is going to be the primary interface to a computer for me."
- The most adamant Codex users are still coders, but a "tidal wave" of new people is arriving. The ambition is for it to cover all the work done in front of a computer. He puts non-coding at "maybe like 10% of the way there" and expects the company to improve quickly now that real users are pushing it there.

## What AI does next: automating computer drudgery, and OpenClaw

- Coding is special because the world needs far more code than gets written. The next coding-like shift is not one domain. It is people realizing how much time they waste clicking between messaging apps, copying and pasting, and answering boring things they could automate once.
- He says watching an AI do the drudgery makes work more enjoyable: the little tasks "drag me down" out of a flow state, so the quality-of-life gain is "huge."
- He calls OpenClaw "one of my biggest... magic AGI moments ever in the field." His point is that when models cross a threshold and the product designer gets "a handful of critical ideas really right," the experience is far more magical than its description.
- His first test of any new AI system is home automation, which he says "never works." OpenClaw was the first setup he was happy with. He also used it to automate his morning message triage and later moved that to a messaging app he built with Codex.
- On the gap between hype and experience: it is "hard to explain" what it is like when it all works and you trust that it will.
- He sees a large overhang. Many people have not grasped that you can ask the system for almost anything and it will probably happen, and he admits he still doesn't trust it enough himself.
- He is excited about new computer interfaces: Codex is amazing, but it feels "very broken" to tell it to use a computer full of things built for people, not AIs. He thinks "there's a whole new internet protocol to make too."

## Management style and the three phases of OpenAI

- He describes himself as "definitely not a hands-on manager": get great people, give them a very high-level thing to point at, and let things happen.
- Phase one was a research company trying to build AGI when it "sounded completely crazy." Phase two added building a product company. Phase three adds building "this mega mega scale token factory for the world," which he frames as a new utility.
- For that utility, the goal is intelligence as smart, cheap, abundant and easy to use as possible, which he says requires deep full-stack integration and a massive infrastructure build-out.
- Lesson from the phase one to two shift: he "didn't really appreciate... how much my management style had to change." Running a research lab and running a product company are very different, and he expects phase three to be different again.
- He thinks phase three is not a natural fit for his style. His options are to hire one or a few great people, change how he works, or "build an AI that can manage this new thing."
- Unusual practice: he messages "a few hundred people at the company a day" via Slack or text, one or two quick messages each, done by him and not an agent. The context he gets is diffusely very helpful.

## Business model: be a low-margin infrastructure utility aligned with customers

- He rejects the idea that AI labs will climb the stack and swallow every customer's value. He says some labs want that, and "we don't."
- He admires Stripe because it is aligned with its customers: when customers make more revenue they pay Stripe more. He says Stripe's partnership at ChatGPT's launch was critical and that no one else could have scaled that quickly.
- He wants OpenAI to work the same way: an infrastructure provider, "happy for us to be like a forever low margin as long as we can be huge and grow in fast business," supplying "an intelligence meter" that companies can buy to automate and accelerate things or build products, and that people can take with them.
- He believes switching costs in AI are low and it will be "hard to have like huge margins in AI anyway." He cites how easily people recently switched from a competitor's coding product to Codex, and says smarter AI makes switching easier ("Hey agent, go do this thing for me").
- Demand is elastic: it goes up more than linearly as the price per unit of intelligence, and the time to get it back, drops. He thinks demand for intelligence at a low enough price is "effectively un-capped" and has no good answer for "what is enough."

## Compute capex and the "bubble" question

- He calls this the most expensive infrastructure project the world has undertaken. Revenue is ramping to meet it, and efficiency gains are letting them get "way more out of each GPU than I thought."
- He does not think it is a capex bubble given the demand he sees, while noting he's no expert.
- On calling bubbles: people and journalists love to proclaim them, and sometimes they are right, but he never figured out how to tell the time someone calls a bubble from the time you are actually in one. As an investor he tried to build a framework for when to deploy capital and failed. Smart people who "called it exactly right" had said the same thing repeatedly in prior years.
- Planning horizons: OpenAI has signed 20-year power and land agreements, but has a clear product vision only about 2 years out, hazier after that.
- On 10-year venture time horizons with a possible singularity in 3 to 5 years, he says acting on a 10-year horizon "requires a real suspension of disbelief," but you should still "live as if stuff's just going to keep going in an understandable way for a long time."

## Managing elite talent and the co-founder relationship

- A book author told him his unique skill was getting many people who each thought they were the most capable, and that everything had to go their way, to work together long enough to find the breakthroughs. His answer on how: "a lot of pain."
- What held people together was a few deeply shared convictions: belief in scale, concentrating resources, and doing "this one thing," important enough that people set aside personal conflicts.
- Concentration example: when training GPT-3, the vast majority of the whole organization's compute went into one research program. Recruits from DeepMind said it would create a toxic culture and that compute should be divided equally. OpenAI's approach was to "bet with conviction," accept it wouldn't feel equal, and ignore the distractions.
- On co-founders: at Y Combinator he saw one of the biggest predictors of success was whether the founders had known each other a long time. Teams that met "7 days before applying to YC on a co-founder matching site" rarely worked.
- He and Greg Brockman had known each other a long time before OpenAI, with shared values and history, "deep mutual respect and complementary skill set." Doing an intense startup without a deeply trusted co-founder is "really hard."

## Founders and startups in the AI era

- Previously the "idea guy" with no technical skill was mocked and rarely succeeded, and YC found teams without technical founders hard to make work. He calls the current moment "the revenge of the idea guys" and says he is "here for it."
- For a long time the most important thing he and YC looked for was technical talent. That is still important, but he now wants to fund people who "deeply understand their users and can't code at all."
- On startup durability against model improvements: he has held the same view throughout. A business should be on the side of hoping AI gets smarter. A "GPT wrapper" patching a weakness the next model will fix was sad when the model improved. Products that benefit from intelligence get happier.
- Harnesses: he treats "data center, model, harness" as one cluster producing usable intelligence. Build things where you want that whole cluster to improve, and if you secretly hope it won't because you're patching a weakness, "probably the next model crank turn somewhere in that stack is just going to solve it."

## What the best AI-using companies do

- Top-down CEO commitment. Tobi Lutke of Shopify was the first CEO he knew to go all in on AI in how the company runs. He got his hands dirty building AI automation and made his team do it. It wasn't a token leaderboard or "gamified hackable thing."
- The pattern that works: the CEO says "we're going to automate ourselves, accelerate ourselves," holds people to it, and ideally does it personally.
- A new OpenAI experiment: sending an FTE to work hands-on with a company's CEO to automate as much of that CEO's job as possible. He expects a "nice fractal effect" through the company if the leader does it.
- Being "uncomfortably permissive with data access": record meetings, give the AI access to the code base, every Slack message, every email, and let every employee use it. He explicitly stops "short of recommendation" because of privacy and compliance risk, and notes it's easier for small startups. He says regulations and tradeoffs around data privacy versus AI efficiency will have to change.
- Small startups of two or three people with AI doing everything are "amazing" to watch. For larger companies he thinks "one more abstraction" is missing for how humans and AIs interface at scale.

## Advice to Stripe, and why he invested

- Why he invested in Stripe: the founders were among the most impressive he had seen at any age; they were solving a problem for themselves; and it fit his belief that commerce would move online in a huge way with lots of startups. His rule, which he says still holds: "if you can find really smart founders and a market that's going to be big, you should just invest."
- Advice on durability: investors who said Stripe would be commoditized once customers got big and built their own were wrong. If you make a great product and are a "good reasonable ecosystem partner and don't do crazy things," people stick with you.
- Advice for the AI era: adopt AI internally and use it to build better products, but "don't assume that the entire socioeconomic system completely reconfigures." He thinks the world has gotten "a little bit delusional" about this, and money will still have to move even if agents pay each other.

## Science, technologies he's excited about, and iterative deployment

- Science: he hopes this is AI's most important contribution to quality of life. Since late-2025 models and especially 5.5, excellent scientists say models help them find better ideas and make small but important discoveries. A decade of science in a year would compound enormously.
- The OpenAI Foundation: focused on science and "AI resilience," funded with money, expertise and technology. He says it will likely be the biggest foundation in the world, and it recently made a grant to the Arc Institute, which he calls the best AI and bio effort.
- Technologies he's excited about: the physical layer (energy, robots, data center infrastructure), brain-machine interfaces, biotech (both afraid and excited; "defensive biotech is about to become very important"), and new computer interfaces.
- Overlooked area: materials science, which he calls "a beautifully AI-shaped problem" (new catalysts, for example) with very little attention.
- Predictions: first profitable nuclear fusion reactor "in the next 5 years," depending on electricity prices pushed by data center demand; commercial hypersonic travel more than about 10 years away.
- Iterative deployment: he calls it the most controversial decision in OpenAI's history. The prior consensus was to keep AI locked up among a small set who understood safety, and release only fruits. He opposed this power concentration and says building for the world, with reasonable guardrails, gives people a gift on which the world builds a much bigger one.
- His claimed biggest contribution: "pushing for this to be a democratized technology that people get to use and build on," based on a belief in democratization and agency, that "people are mostly good and mostly do amazing things with tools."
- Anecdote: for about 8 months after finishing GPT-4 training and before release, almost no one outside OpenAI knew about it, and staff wondered if they were in "collective psychosis" with no outside feedback.
