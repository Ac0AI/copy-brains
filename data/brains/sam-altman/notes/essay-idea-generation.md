## Starting from ideas: why founders must be able to generate them

- The two most common questions from prospective founders: how do I get startup ideas, and do you have any ideas for my startup.
- Sam says handing a founder an idea "almost always doesn't work."
- He treats having ideas as "among the most important qualities for a startup founder to have," because running a startup requires generating lots of new ideas continuously.
- YC ran an experiment funding seemingly good founders with no ideas. Sam thinks every company in that no-idea track failed.
- His conclusion: good founders have lots of ideas about everything. Someone who wants to found a company but can't find an idea should first get good at idea generation.

## Environment and people that produce ideas

- Be in the right environment, around people who:
  - have a good feel for the future
  - will entertain improbable plans
  - are optimistic
  - are smart in a creative way
  - have a very high "idea flux"
- These people think without the usual constraints, have few filters and don't care much what others think.
- "The best ideas are fragile." Most people never voice them because they sound silly.
- Seek people who don't make you feel stupid for mentioning a bad idea, and who never feel stupid for doing so themselves.
- Avoid world-weary people who belittle your ambitions. Sam notes this is most of the world, but they hold on to the past and founders want to live in the future.

## Thinking backwards from 20 years out

- Project yourself 20 years into the future, then think backwards from there.
- Sam says to trust yourself: 20 years is a long time, so it's fine if your ideas about it seem pretty radical.
- Sam later noted this applies to AI development too, particularly the paragraphs on environment, the future, tectonic shifts and "possible this year, not last year."

## Spotting tectonic shifts and real vs fake trends

- Ask what the most important tectonic shifts happening right now are. How is the world changing in fundamental ways? Can you identify a leading edge of change and the opportunity it unlocks?
- Most recent significant example in 2018: the mobile phone explosion from 2008-2012. He said "we are overdue for another!"
- In such shifts the world changes so fast that big incumbents usually get beaten by fast-moving, focused startups.
- Get good at telling real trends from fake ones. A key differentiator: whether the new platform is used a lot by a small number of people (real signal), or used a little by a lot of people.

## Timing signals for a startup idea

- Anything possible this year that wasn't possible last year deserves attention. It may be the seed of a great startup, especially if next year will be too late.
- A good sign is being able to say: "I am sure this is going to happen, I'm just not sure if we'll be the ones to do it."
- Sam's Uber example: after his first ride, it was clear people wouldn't be calling cabs much longer, but he wasn't sure Uber would win the space.

## Screening ideas: could it be huge, and does it have an accumulating advantage

- Early on, ask: "could this be huge if it worked?"
- Many ideas are good, but few have the inherent advantages that make a startup massively successful.
- Most businesses don't generate a valuable accumulating advantage as they scale. Think early about why your idea might.
- It's obvious for Facebook or Airbnb, but it often exists in more subtle ways.

## Founder/company fit and the contrarian test

- Consider what you're well suited for. Pure introspection is hard here, so ask a mentor or people you've worked with what you're particularly good at.
- Sam says: "founder/company fit is as important as product/market fit."
- A good test for an idea: you can articulate why most people think it's a bad idea, but you understand what makes it good.
