## Software revolution: three great revolutions, and why this one destroys jobs

- Sam names three great technological revolutions: agricultural, industrial, and the software revolution, which he says "we are now in the middle of."
- Great revolutions change what most people do every day and how society is structured.
- He says the industrial revolution was an anomaly. It created lots of jobs because the technology needed huge numbers of humans to run it. That leads people, "perhaps subconsciously," to assume technological revolutions are always good for most people's economic status.
- His view is that software will do what technology usually does: "create wealth but destroy jobs."
- He expects people will probably find new things to do to satisfy limitless human demand. Even so, "we should stop pretending that the software revolution, by itself, is going to be good for median wages."
- He is a fan of the software revolution and feels fortunate to have been born when he was. His worry is that the wrong lessons were learned from recent examples.

## Technology as leverage: wealth concentration and inequality

- Sam argues that technology "provides leverage on ability and luck," and in doing so concentrates wealth and drives inequality.
- He thinks drastic wealth inequality is likely to be one of the biggest social problems of the next 20 years.
- He says wealth can and will be redistributed. That still doesn't solve the real problem of people needing something fulfilling to do.
- He grants that life is better in absolute terms than 100 years ago, even for very poor people. He also grants that highly paid people make inexpensive services for the poor.
- He argues that ignoring quality of life relative to other people alive today "ignores what makes us human."
- His position on inequality:
  - It is a good thing when some people make thousands of times as much as others.
  - He doesn't resent paying his taxes.
  - We should do much more to help people who are actually poor.
  - The social safety net "will have to trend up with the development of technology."

## Jobs: don't cling to worthless ones, plan for fundamentally new ones

- Sam calls trying to hold on to worthless jobs "a terrible but popular idea."
- Finding new jobs for billions of people is a good idea but very hard. The new jobs will probably be so different from anything today that meaningful planning is almost impossible.
- He criticizes the current strategy, which amounts to "pretending that Travis is kidding when he talks about self-driving cars and that Uber really is going to create millions of jobs forever."
- His point is that founders' own statements about automation should be taken at face value rather than dismissed.

## Power concentration: why software differs from the atomic bomb

- Sam's second major challenge of the software revolution is the concentration of power in small groups.
- The atomic bomb is hard to build mainly because enriching uranium takes huge amounts of energy, so effectively "the resources of nations" are needed. Restricted knowledge matters less than that.
- He says this was an idiosyncrasy of nuclear development, not the normal course of technology. Like the industrial revolution and job growth, it taught bad lessons.
- Technology usually makes more power available to small groups, and he expects the software revolution to do the same.
- Footnote: he notes the enrichment barrier may be eroding, citing laser isotope separation ("Or at least, one used to").
- He gives rockets as an example. Building one used to take the resources of nations. Companies can now do it, partly enabled by software, and "a rocket can destroy anything on earth."

## Existential risks from AI and synthetic biology

- Sam sees AI and synthetic biology as two of the biggest risks of the software revolution. Both may put tremendous capability to cause harm in the hands of small groups or even individuals.
- On biology, he says it is probably already possible to design and produce a terrible disease in a small lab.
- On AI, an AI that could end human life "may only require a few hundred people in an office building anywhere in the world, with no equipment other than laptops."
- He says the new existential threats won't require the resources of nations to produce.
- He finds it "astonishing" that no serious efforts are underway to combat threats from synthetic biology and AI development.

## Strategy: legislate safeguards, but make the good side's edge stronger

- Two approaches Sam rejects:
  - Making the knowledge illegal and hoping that works.
  - Trying to stop technological progress.
- His recommended strategy has two parts:
  - Legislate sensible safeguards.
  - Work very hard so that the edge technology gives the good side is stronger than the edge it gives bad actors.
- He argues for defense using the same technology:
  - "If we can synthesize new diseases, maybe we can synthesize vaccines."
  - "If we can make a bad AI, maybe we can make a good AI that stops the bad one."
- He calls the current strategy "badly misguided." His view is that "it's not going to be like the atomic bomb this time around, and the sooner we stop pretending otherwise, the better off we'll be."

## Historical framing of technology

- Sam notes that many smaller revolutions have also mattered, including writing, cannons, the internal combustion engine, atomic bombs, fishing, and the hand axe.
- He calls the hand axe "the longest-serving piece of technology in human history."
- He mentions that many people believe fishing is what allowed humans to develop their current brains.
