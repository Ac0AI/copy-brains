## Concentrating on one bet instead of spreading across many

- OpenAI was far less resourced than DeepMind and others. Sam's reasoning: "they're going to try a lot of things and we've just got to pick one and really concentrate and that's how we can win here."
- OpenAI knew one thing worked (deep learning, which got better with scale). It concentrated there and did "the thing in front of us" while some other efforts were "trying to outsmart themselves in too many ways."
- The norm at the time was to spread resources across many bets. Some critics thought OpenAI's concentration was wasteful or even "vaguely immoral."
- Sam says "most of the world still does not understand the value of like a fairly extreme level of conviction on one bet."
- Focus also helps internally. Once research, infrastructure and product paths are fairly clear, you can point everyone at the same few things. Sam says that alignment is "a significant determinant in how fast you can move."

## Conviction, evidence and being willing to be wrong

- When the world pushes back and the pushback doesn't make sense to you, he says to do it anyway. Startups show this pattern again and again.
- Conviction is not stubbornness. The rule he describes is "believe whatever the results told us." OpenAI was high conviction and wrong on many things, and it tried to fully embrace being wrong as soon as it knew.
- "Conviction is great until the moment you have data one way or the other." Many people hold on past that moment.
- There is a long stretch where you have to operate without data, and there you run on conviction. Then you "show your work."
- OpenAI started with wrong assumptions about the technology, the company's structure, how AGI would go and the shape of the product. It began with robots, agents and video games.
  - Language models were not the plan. GPT-3 came a few years later.
  - Sam says one strength is "the ability to get punched in the face and get back up and keep going."
  - If they had known then what they know now, they "could have speedrun this whole thing." You don't get to be right at every guess.
- His biggest unlock from startups was dropping the belief that "adults in the room" have the answers: "you can just do stuff, you can just try stuff." Nobody will tell you exactly what to do, so you iterate quickly.

## Betting on scale and picking a contrarian thesis early

- OpenAI's core beliefs were that deep learning works and that it gets better with scale. Both were "somewhat heretical" then.
- People already knew bigger neural networks got better. The leap was a near-religious belief that it would not stop. The data on how predictable scaling was came a few years later. It started as a hunch.
- Leaders in the field said it was not really learning or reasoning, and that it was "a parlor trick." Some said the belief would cause an AI winter.
- At some point you look at the scaling laws and say "we're going to keep doing this."
- Sam is interested in the emergent properties of scale in startups, deep learning models and other things, and he thinks scale is underappreciated. "When in doubt, if you have something that seems like it's getting better with scale, you should scale it up." He adds, "more is more."
- Early flip-chart goals for OpenAI:
  - Figure out unsupervised learning.
  - Solve RL.
  - "Never get more than 120 people" (the one they missed).
- The big picture of the early ideas (a large unsupervised model, then RL) turned out to be "incredibly right," even though there were many detours.
- Alec Radford noticed one neuron flipping positive or negative sentiment in a model trained on Amazon reviews. That led to GPT-1, and others scaled it into GPT-2.

## Recruiting and launching with a bold, "irresponsible" mission

- OpenAI said from the start that it was going after AGI, when the field didn't allow that word because it seemed crazy and borderline irresponsible.
- Sam says that got the attention of Ilya, Greg and "all of the good young people," and drew derision from "the mediocre old people." He took the derision as a good sign.
- Tactics:
  - He went around meeting people one by one, then in different groups. It took about nine months.
  - He emailed Ilya Sutskever after watching a video. Ilya didn't reply, so Sam went to a conference where Ilya was speaking.
  - He knew Greg Brockman from the early Stripe days.
- OpenAI was announced in December 2015. The team gathered at Greg's apartment in early January 2016, about 10 people. His comparison is a founder who finishes a fundraise and thinks they are done: "that was actually the starting gun."
- Sam says startups have an edge in a technology shift because big companies run quarterly planning cycles. Founders can "see a new thing and build something that day."
- Being able to write the early cheques for OpenAI himself mattered. He thinks it would have been hard to get someone else to fund it at the very beginning.

## Validating a model or product: only users tell you

- GPT-3 was an incredible demo, but "with the possible exception of copywriting no great businesses were built on GPT-3."
- GPT-3.5 changed the feel: YC startups started to show interest, and it no longer felt like "pushing a boulder uphill."
- With GPT-4, OpenAI quickly got the "how many GPUs can you give me" reaction.
  - Jake Heller of Casetext was an early commercializer of GPT-4. Casetext sold for $650 million.
  - Jake chopped prompts into small workflows and built large test cases around them.
- Sam on internal enthusiasm: "you never really know if you have a hit product on your hands until you put it in customer hands." You're "always too impressed with your own work," and "the real test is users."

## Startup advice for the AI era

- Bet on the technology trend. "We are not near the saturation point. The models are going to get so much better so quickly."
- The startup edge is "speed and focus and conviction and the ability to react to how fast the technology is moving." He calls this the number one edge of a startup, especially now. Big companies and even mid-sized ones are on quarterly planning cycles.
- Build something with AI, and when you see a new capability, build with it that day.
- The caution is not to think the laws of business stop applying because you use AI. You still need a moat, a competitive edge or a better product.
  - Embracing a new technology faster than others can give short-term explosions of growth.
  - "Everyone can build an incredible demo... but building a business, that's the brass ring."
  - "The rules still apply. You can do it faster than ever before and better than ever before, but you still have to build a business."
- Big incumbents have the edge when things move slowly. Upstarts win in platform shifts like mobile, the internet and semiconductors. Platform shifts are "always built by the people who are young with no prior knowledge."
- He expects companies making billions per year with under 100 employees, maybe 20 or even one person. His example is "one person plus 10,000 GPUs."
- He says "the world is still sleeping on all of this to such an astonishing degree."

## Company stages, management turnover and clarity of direction

- OpenAI has "speedrun" an arc that would normally take a decade. ChatGPT was less than two years old at the time of the interview.
- As a company scales, it goes through management teams at some rate. People who are good at zero to one aren't necessarily good at one to ten or ten to one hundred.
- OpenAI changed what it was going to be and made plenty of mistakes. He wants the best decision at every stage and expects calm periods and dynamic ones to alternate.
- He says it is the first time he felt OpenAI knew what to do. There are known unknowns and a lot of work, but they know "what to shoot at and what we want to optimize for."
- For a long time OpenAI was a true research lab, where it was hard to act with conviction because so many other good things competed for attention.
- Sam's five levels of AI:
  1. Chatbots.
  2. Reasoners (o1, which he says OpenAI reached earlier that year).
  3. Agents.
  4. Innovators.
  5. Organizations.
  - At a YC o1 hackathon, the startup Camper (CAD/CAM) built something that iteratively improved an airfoil until it generated competitive lift.
  - That demo and others convinced him that "you can get a huge amount of innovation just by using these current models in really creative ways." He had expected level 3 to level 4 to be much harder.
  - He thinks "things are going to go a lot faster than people are appreciating."

## Peer groups, platform shifts and early-career lessons

- His biggest advice to young people is to find an inspiring peer group as early as possible. He says no one is immune to peer pressure, so "all you can do is pick good peers."
- The YC peer group was what made it special. It was people who believed you can just do stuff, more than any single person telling you so.
- Sam left Stanford after seeing the YC vibe. At Stanford, peers competed over investment-bank internships.
- Looped, his first startup, was a location-sharing mobile app started at 19. It never found product-market fit or escape velocity.
  - He still calls it valuable: it was a platform shift (pre-App Store, pre-iPhone) from the inside, and he learned management and enterprise sales.
  - "There is nothing that I have ever heard of that has a higher rate of generalized learning than doing a startup."
  - He quotes Paul Graham: "your 20s are always an apprenticeship but you don't know for what."
- He also says he was "kind of wasting my time" on Looped and thinks he could have picked a better idea, since AI was always what he most wanted to work on.
- Some of his early YC Research thinking: allocate capital to smart people, accept that some of it won't work, and try anyway. Not every bet was a good one.
