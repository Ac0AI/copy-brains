## Why YC exists and how the network effect works

- Sam says YC funds about 250 startups a year in its core program, plus a new Fellowship program: one tenth of the money and less advice, built to scale far beyond the core batch.
- He describes YC as "somewhere between like a product company and a traditional investing firm."
- Its goal is to give a founder "a 100x leg up" through advice, connections and an alumni community of 2,000+ funded founders.
- He calls YC a network-effect business at the earliest stage of funding. Alumni help each other fundraise, buy each other's products and give advice.
- Of the eight accelerator-born companies worth $8B+, all eight came from YC, out of roughly 2,500 accelerators worldwide.
- Founder loyalty to investors falls fast with each round. The first check-writer gets the strongest bond, so YC founders give back to the community more readily.
- YC builds the community on purpose: an annual camping trip, shared office space and private online networks. Founding is lonely, and peers matter.

## Why Sam preferred seed investing to venture investing

- After his company was acquired in 2012, he raised a venture fund and learned he did not want to be a venture investor or "public equity picker."
- Venture felt like allocating capital in a crowded field, trying to spot the best companies slightly sooner and pitch slightly better to win a deal.
- Early seed checks of about $15,000 in 2009-2010 felt like real impact: founders had no other options, and without the money the company might not exist.
- He took the YC job because he wanted "maximal impact," and says running YC is much more like being a CEO than an investor.
- He still says a great board member in the Series A is the most important thing to optimize for. YC itself is not good at that stage.
- A leadership handoff worked because he knew the organization and was YC's first check. He says a successful new CEO must "refound" the company.

## Founder qualities YC tracks

YC rates founders on traits and watches what correlates with success. His list, in no particular order:

- **Clarity of vision.** Can the founder explain exactly what they do and why? If not, they cannot recruit, sell or talk to the press. Airbnb's founders explained the airbed idea and the end-state vision clearly even though it later evolved.
- **Determination and passion.** Founders "bend the world to their will" and do not turn around at the first impediment. The best companies are mission-oriented.
- **Raw intelligence.**
- **Speed of getting things done.** Quick, decisive decision-making correlates almost exactly with YC's successful founders. Many on-paper strong founders fail because they lack it.
- The ability to solve novel problems fast, which he compares to the novel The Martian: a stream of problem-solving.
- YC calibrates its interview questions on data from about 20,000 applicant companies a year.

## Co-founders, conflict and hiring

- YC prefers 2-3 person founding teams but funds solo founders. Dropbox and Instacart each had a co-founder before or soon after YC.
- Reasons for a co-founder: the workload, and the psychological toll of going solo, which even tough people underestimate.
- Few $10B+ tech companies lacked co-founders by six months in. Four founders is harder because someone usually leaves within a couple of years.
- Bad co-founder is worse than none. "Bringing on a co-founder because they think we'll like it" ends in heartache "100% of the time."
- Diversity of skills and backgrounds is good. The harmful kind is diversity of vision: different beliefs about culture, decisions, selling, hiring and handling disagreement.
- Conflict arises when founders want different things for the company, and when both want the same thing for themselves (for example both want to be CEO).
- The current best pattern is two strong technical people, one owning product and one owning technology.
- On hiring: "our best startups hire the least." Airbnb and Stripe took about nine months to hire their first person, and Dropbox took longer.
- Early hires add burn, organizational inertia and politics. Hiring before the vision is clear creates communication overhead.
- Founders should not hire for status or force diversity hires.

## Ideas, conviction and pivots

- Sam argues the best ideas look bad. Great ideas that look bad are undervalued assets, whereas obvious ones get brutally competed.
- Investors in 2006-2008 chased the next Facebook, and in 2009-2010 the next Facebook again when the real winners were Uber and Airbnb. Today's "Uber for X" startups are thin verticals; the next $100B company will be something new.
- Ask what has shifted in the world that enables a giant new company. For Uber, it was that everyone suddenly had an internet-connected device in their pocket.
- Ways to build conviction: launch quickly, so users who love the product give you proof, and be able to explain which change in the world others missed.
- He says you can ignore everyone except users. If users say the product sucks and you cannot find others who like it, listen.
- On hard tech, YC applies the software cadence: find a core metric and improve it about 10% a week. "If you compound... 10% a week is a lot."
- On pivots, he says Silicon Valley "gets the pivot thing wrong" and that bragging about pivots is bragging about failing.
- Pivots he has seen work: (1) what the founder wanted to build all along (Instagram, from Burbn), and (2) something discovered while building the first thing (Airbnb, Slack, Stripe).
- Pivots that fail: sitting at a whiteboard to invent a new idea. If you have neither kind of pivot, shut the company down, return the capital and wait for an organic idea.
- Learn more from success than from failure. Failure should be tolerated but not celebrated.

## Focus on product, growth and fake work

- With about 70 productive hours a week, spend almost all of them on talking to users and building product.
- Focus on love, not like: "get the product to the point where some people would be really really bummed if it went away." Every successful company he has worked with had a small group of users who loved it.
- His board member's line: "sales solve all problems," later refined to "growth solves all problems."
- After YC, founders try to stop growth to fix technical, organizational and cultural debt. His advice is to spend about 10% of bandwidth on those, not 50-100%.
- Employees sense a loss of momentum before founders do. The prime directive is "never lose momentum."
- He warns of "fake work": fundraising, press, 30-under-30 lists, conferences, networking events, lawyers and logos. "No company has ever become great because they were great at fundraising."
- The real work is boring: coding, user interviews, sales meetings. Triage by letting small fires burn.
- Career advice: join the most successful, fastest-growing company you can find, and learn from watching it scale.

## Firing, valuations and other advice

- The biggest mistake great founders make is waiting too long to fire bad people. Firing early hurts the person less.
- His method: treat them as a human, be firm that the decision is made, and make it look like a win for them.
- He has found a job for every person he fired before they left, and they announced a resignation plus the new role.
- Be generous with severance. Say "this isn't working," let them choose when to leave, and avoid the lawyer-style termination packet, which makes it adversarial.
- On valuations, he says intermediate valuations are "complete fictions" and he ignores them. They matter only when buying equity or joining as an employee, where liquidation preferences make the headline number misleading. Ask what the company will be worth in 5 years and what your ownership will be.
- YC counsels startups not to chase the highest valuation, and sometimes not to raise.
- On Bitcoin, he said it lacked product-market fit because few people use it daily, and he does not invest past YC stage in companies without it.
- Founders should be generalists. School length does not correlate with success.
- Do not start a startup for its own sake. Join a company you believe in, or start one only if the idea matters.
