## Sam's initial metric: "do any users love our product so much they spontaneously tell other people to use it?"

- Sam says the "current fashion of Silicon Valley startups" is an unhealthy extreme: a weekly growth goal set before founders have any strong idea of what they want to build.
- His proposed first metric is a binary question: "do any users love our product so much they spontaneously tell other people to use it?"
- Until the answer is "yes", he argues founders are generally better off focusing on this instead of a growth target.
- The very best technology companies sometimes take a while to figure out what they are doing. When they do, they usually pass this binary test before turning all their energy to growth.
- He calls it "the critical ingredient for companies that do really well."

## The right order of operations for an early startup

Sam's sequence for a startup's first stretch:

1. In the first few weeks, figure out what you are doing and why.
2. Build a product some users really love.
3. Only after that, focus on growth above all else.

- He still holds that startups need to grow quickly. He calls that "true, and very good advice."
- His point is about timing. Growth comes after the product is loved, not before.

## Why premature growth targets backfire

- A startup that targets a growth goal too early often ends up with a nebulous product that some users "sort of like."
- Founders then paper over that weakness with "growth hacking."
- This sort of works. It can fool investors for a while, until they start digging into retention numbers.
- Then "the music stops."
- His conclusion: if you don't first build something users love, "no amount of growth hacking will make you into a great company."
- Retention is where the weakness shows up. Growth numbers that hide poor retention won't hold up under investor scrutiny.

## Product love and sense of mission

- Sam notes that startups that skip the step of building a product some users love "also seem to rarely develop the sense of mission that the best companies have."
- By implication, the discovery of what users love is tied to a company's sense of purpose, not only to its growth.

## Monopoly as the second early ingredient

- In a footnote, Sam says the best companies have a second thing that "also usually gets figured out early": "some sort of a monopoly."
- Together, the two early ingredients are a product some users love and some form of monopoly.
- He gives no detail in this essay on what form the monopoly takes.

## Context

- The essay first appeared on Sam's blog in 2016. It was republished by The Founders' Tribune on June 29, 2025.
- Sam was then president of Y Combinator, a role the republished byline calls "former CEO of Y Combinator."
- Y Combinator's standard advice to startups is that they have to grow quickly. The essay is a correction to how founders apply that advice.
