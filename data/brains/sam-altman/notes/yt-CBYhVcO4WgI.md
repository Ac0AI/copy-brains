## Startup success is a product of four factors, with luck dominating

Sam frames the outcome of a startup as roughly: idea × product × execution × team × luck.

- He says luck is "a random number between 0 and 10,000". He means this literally.
- The four areas to get right are a great idea, a great product, a great team and great execution. They overlap, but he treats each separately.
- If you do well in all four, you have a good chance of at least some success.
- He calls startups a fair playing field. Young and inexperienced founders can win, and so can older, experienced ones.
- Some things that are bad elsewhere, such as being poor or unknown, can be strengths in a startup.
- The advice in this course is for founders who want to grow massively and build a very large company. He warns that it often won't work in big or non-startup companies.

## Why to start a startup: only when a specific problem compels you

Sam is reluctant to teach "how" because nobody should start a startup just to start one.

- There are much easier ways to make money.
- Every founder says they could not have imagined how hard and painful it would be.
- Start one only if you feel compelled by a particular problem and believe a company is the best way to solve it.
- Passion should come from the specific problem first, and the startup second.
- He says all of YC's big successes followed this pattern.
- He handed the "why" half of the lecture to Dustin Moskovitz, co-founder of Facebook and Asana.

## Ideas matter: why the "ideas don't matter, just pivot" view is wrong

Sam argues the pendulum has swung too far toward dismissing ideas.

- The fashionable view says to write ideas on the wall, test what sticks, and not spend time on whether the idea would be valuable if it worked. Pivoting is treated as the solution, and more pivots as better.
- He grants some of this is right. Things develop in hard-to-predict ways, and you can't know everything until the product is in users' hands.
- He also says great execution is "at least ten times more important and a hundred times harder" than a good idea.
- But a bad idea is still a bad idea. Great execution on a poor idea "won't get you anywhere". There are exceptions, but most big companies start with a great idea, not a pivot.
- Successful pivots are almost always toward something the founders themselves wanted, not a random contrived idea.
- Example: Airbnb started because Brian Chesky couldn't pay his rent and had spare space.
- In general, the track record of pivoting does not produce big companies.
- Sam used to believe ideas didn't matter much. He now says he is "pretty sure that's wrong".

## How to evaluate an idea: a broad definition that includes market, growth and defense

Sam defines "idea" broadly. Evaluating it means thinking through all of these, not only the product:

- Market size and market growth.
- The company's growth strategy.
- The defense strategy, meaning how the business is defended over time.

His reasoning:

- If the idea works, you will work on it for ten years. It deserves real time early on.
- Think about the long-term value of defending the business.
- "Planning" itself has little value, but the practice of planning is valuable work. Sam says it is largely missing from most startups today.
- Thinking long term is the part he stresses most.
