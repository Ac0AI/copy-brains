## Sam's pre-company stage: project first, company second

- Sam says the foundation of a startup is critical: get it right at the start and it works, get it wrong and "it's usually fatal."
- He warns against starting a startup for the sake of having a startup, then hunting for an idea. People who sit in a co-working space thinking "I need a startup" and "I need my idea" have a much harder time.
- Declaring something a company instead of a project creates pressure to settle on an idea quickly. The idea also can't sound too crazy once you have to tell people about "my startup." Early ideas are delicate and need room to develop.
- His advice: spend time figuring out what you are passionate about, let it be a project before it is a company, then make the company the vehicle to support it.
- The founder can force the process by spending a lot of time thinking about ideas and building projects. The moment to call it a company is when you have conviction: "this may not work but I really believe in this."
- Raising money before you know even generally what you will build is usually problematic. Investors make you feel you owe them progress.
- The thinking phase takes a while. Once you commit to building, you go as fast as you can.

## Growth as the organizing metric

- Sam says the best startups set growth goals from the earliest days, and that startups and growth are "incredibly interrelated."
- Airbnb's founders drew a forward-looking projected growth graph and put it on their bathroom mirror and fridge door. They tried to grow 10% every week.
- A growth target shapes the company. It gives one metric to optimize and "one way to answer every question."
- Startups that do this usually succeed. Startups that don't "argue about everything else."
- In a later Q&A he confirms that 7% to 10% weekly growth is the benchmark YC watches.

## Customer understanding and the limits of Lean Startup

- Sam calls "understand your users" one of the most important pieces of startup advice. A startup that doesn't do it will fail, and one that does often succeeds. He agrees with deeply understanding users and iterating very quickly.
- What he thinks lean methodology can miss: very ambitious startups often take a long time to work, or a long time to look ambitious.
- Examples he gives:
  - Reddit began as a joke of an online forum and became a "weird collective consciousness."
  - Facebook began as college profiles and became universal connectivity.
  - Something like a nuclear reactor company simply takes a long time to get going.
- He thinks it is important to allow that kind of slow start to happen.

## Speed of execution as the main founder signal

- Sam says the best founders "execute so quickly." One way YC identifies great founders is how much progress they make between meetings.
- Mediocre founders, seen a week later, are still doing the things they said a week ago. The best founders, seen the next day, have done all of it and more.
- Slow founders always have detailed, good-sounding excuses: the product wasn't good enough, lawyers found a problem, a PR issue, a competitor moved, a VP got sick.
- He says "the hours matter" in the earliest days, not the weeks.
- Speed can be taught to a degree. YC can often take a slow, limp mover and make them a fast, fearsome one by focusing on decisiveness. Some people never get there, which he calls a disposition issue.
- His own time management: over the previous six months he got good at saying no to things that were not a high-impact use of his time. His list of priorities as YC president:
  - grow YC
  - get the best companies in the world to apply
  - pick them well
  - advise them well
  - develop the YC network

## What YC is, how it recruits, and how it selects

- He doesn't like the word "incubator." He describes YC as a source of funding, advice and a network, with YC as "a passenger in the back seat." Companies are their own and don't live in a dorm.
- He says the differences are:
  - the strength of the alumni network
  - full-time advisers rather than a pool of mentors
  - a track record of funding many billion-plus-dollar companies, which he says no other accelerator had done
- Recruiting works through several channels at once: the alumni network, Hacker News, and putting great content online. A class he taught with guest lecturers the previous fall got millions of views, and applicants rose roughly 40% to 60%.
- Outreach moves diversity numbers more than anything else. YC funds women, minorities and international founders at about the rate they apply (for example about 3% of applicants and about 3% of funded founders were black). Outreach included visiting HBCUs and holding a conference for women considering startups.
- The batch had 114 companies and high-30s countries, up from one country in the first batch.
- YC is deliberately unfiltered. It requires no intros and no network, sees tens of thousands of applications a year, and spends 90% of its time advising founders. So the interview is about 10 minutes. He admits it is imperfect and that YC rejects companies that would have been great.
- Criteria he lists: clarity of vision, clarity of explanation, determination, passion, and evidence of past accomplishment.
- YC tracks rejected applicants with custom software. He said it had not yet missed a billion-dollar company that applied, but was sure it would.
- On what to fund: "fund the smartest most determined people you can find with the clearest vision even if you don't understand yet why it's going to be a big deal." YC does best funding things others don't think will matter yet, and likes when other investors question a pick, then beg to invest two years later.

## Raising capital and staying lean

- When to raise: "either when you have to or when it's really easy." That means the company desperately needs money, or someone offers easy money on good terms (often at the end of YC), which can then be used for good things.
- He thinks the culture has gone wrong: founders who haven't raised a lot don't think they are a real startup. When YC started it invested about $12,000 to $20,000 per company, and people now say they need a million dollars to build a product.
- He says it is still "an incredible win" to operate on small amounts of capital and build the product yourself rather than hire developers.
- Technical founders: it is very hard, from YC's data, to build a company with no technical founder. It is "not impossible but a significant headwind." Such companies find it harder to get a product built cheaply and to hire and evaluate engineers. His advice for non-technical founders is to spend whatever time it takes, even in another job, building a working relationship with a technical cofounder, not picking someone random.

## Location, scale and company structure

- Silicon Valley: he expects it to stay dominant. People there want to get rich but will wait 8 to 10 years, and equity in a startup is the top prestige path, unlike finance in New York or film in Los Angeles. He noted that as far as he knew New York had not produced a $5B-plus tech company since Bloomberg. Housing cost is the main risk.
- Many foreign founders still want to come to the Valley for the 3-month batch. YC doesn't push them to stay, and some return home to their users.
- Talent is spread around the world, and he found Beijing especially impressive. He singled out Waterloo for engineering skill and a focus on what users want.
- YC could in theory run a batch outside the Valley, but it is not high on the list.
- He has no desire to run a public company and hopes YC never goes public. YC invests its own money with no outside LPs. He doesn't want a public shareholder or a bank calling about missing quarterly earnings "by a penny."
- Ethics policy: if a founder violates it, YC takes back the equity (the founder keeps the cash) and removes them from the program. He said it had happened twice.
- Government as a market: he sees the government as the largest enterprise customer in the world and says that bigger customers move slower. His advice is to start with small, bite-sized projects that agencies can act on quickly. He expects the area to become big and noted YC had funded many government-related startups in the past year.
- Mission: he argues growth must return to the economy and that startups are the main remaining driver of innovation, so there is a "moral imperative" to make more startups happen.
