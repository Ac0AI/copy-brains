## Sam's morning problem: overnight fires pollute deep-work time, and "Dots" fixes it

- Sam says early morning is his favorite working time and his peak creative window. He used it for the day's deep, complex projects.
- Running a company this big, things often break overnight. If he doesn't check his phone right away, a problem can get worse within an hour.
- The catch: once he checks his phone, he "pollutes" his mind with everything else and loses the deep-work window.
- Now he trusts his Dot (the assistant OpenAI launched at DevDay) to judge what he needs to see. He looks at something only if it is truly urgent. Otherwise the Dot handles it.
- Sam says this gave him back that creative space, or time with his kids. He calls it "simple" but "a massive improvement" in his life.
- Example from DevDay: 5 minutes before a keynote demo, another exec's Dot messaged him that it saw a production issue likely to break the demo, and offered to fix it.
- Example from his own day: just before the interview, his Dot flagged one specific thing to watch in a short gap between meetings. He says he would have missed it and it would have caused a mess.
- His Dot's name is "Dotty". He tried "Alfred" before and is still looking for a better name.
- Sam says the system needed a model smart enough, which he credits mostly to "Astra", along with many other things that had to be built.

## Using a Dot as a continuous idea-to-prototype loop

- Sam had a new feature that was hard to explain in enough detail for others to build, and he wasn't sure which of many approaches to pick.
- He kept dropping raw notes to his assistant whenever an idea hit, such as on a walk. The assistant built five or six versions and he gave feedback and refined them.
- Feedback ran whenever he had a spare moment or a new thought.
- He also leaves a long voice message while falling asleep. In the morning the work is built, and the assistant has also done the next seven steps.
- He says this works for any category where the idea is fuzzy, or building it takes a lot of effort from him or someone else.
- He calls the ability to dump raw ideas and get continuous feedback "amazing", and "a really great way to express things".

## Dots as a background agent that finishes tasks Sam gave up on

- Sam often uses Codex to search Slack, since he says Slack search is clearly bad.
- He spent 10 to 20 minutes searching for something he was sure he'd seen. He tried different approaches, failed, and assumed he'd dreamed it. Codex doesn't usually fail, so he gave up.
- The next day his Dot told him it had noticed he couldn't find it, kept thinking about it overnight, and found it.
- It turned out to be a screenshot, not text. Sam had forgotten that. The Dot went through the images and analyzed each screenshot until it found it.
- Sam's takeaway: the Dot stayed patiently looking for something useful to do, and he "would have given up" on that task.
- He also says a Dot can pull context from his whole life (the example given was that it read his Slack, not just his calendar). The context-gathering is among the most useful things about it.
- He frames this as an agent that helps against the flood of information and attention-hijacking. After a few weeks on Dots, he realized social media, phones and notifications hijack attention much more than he had understood.

## "Living documents" and the personal dashboard

- Sam describes a new idea he says has no good name yet: "living" documents. They update automatically as people interact or the world changes, and many people's assistants can work on them together.
- The point is not only sharing information. Assistants for seven people can work together to create something new that was latent in everyone's heads but emergent.
- He says this has been fun while thinking about new features to explore.
- On writing: for a genuinely new idea he still prefers to write it the traditional way, because writing helps him think. He may then have his assistant (or others) follow up.
- His own use case is the company dashboard. Before, he shared one with many people. Now he has a personal, constantly changing business summary based on the evolving things he does or doesn't care about.
- It reads all his Slack messages, builds something new for him, and keeps updating it.
- What he cares about shifts. Six months earlier he cared more about revenue, growth and traditional business metrics. Lately his focus has been the safety and security alignment challenge.
- He'd be curious to see what his Dot thinks he cares about most each day, tracked over years. He says he hasn't been able to get his Dot to find a good way to visualize it.

## Spaces and why software needs rethinking for human-AI collaboration

- Sam's team launched three office-suite-style tools in one release, which he calls a big bet. It wasn't the original plan.
- His reasoning: software as a whole wasn't designed for collaborating with AI. You can use it yourself or have the AI use it for you.
- For close collaboration, especially with multiple people and multiple AI systems working together, many of today's abstractions "fall apart".
- He calls human-AI collaboration an important area to explore.

## Platform strategy: a renaissance of builders, not one giant company

- Sam says AI model companies have two schools of thought: one believes or hopes there will be only one company in the world, and the other (his, and OpenAI's) expects a renaissance in the number of companies.
- He expects a big drop in the bar for individuals, one-person companies and small teams to create new products and services, and wants to go all out on the platform to enable that.
- He says OpenAI hasn't found the equivalent of the app store yet, but has figured out how to help millions of people build valuable products and companies.
- He lists what they opened up at DevDay:
  - Apps inside ChatGPT.
  - Letting users take their subscription anywhere.
  - An app store.
  - The same tools OpenAI uses internally.
- Reasons: there are now many ideas to build, and what's missing is the creative spirit to invent them. Sam believes there are far more good ideas outside OpenAI's building than inside.
- He wants to put that creative energy in people's hands and connect developers with the people who will find the technology useful.
- He is confident there will be apps or plugins he'd love that nobody at OpenAI can imagine today.
- On bringing a ChatGPT subscription everywhere: he says OpenAI wants this itself. He hates subscribing to 100 services, and when he tries a new app he wants it to have full AI power.
- On "OpenAI will crush a startup" tweets: he says they perform because people like pessimism, but it "never actually happens". He says OpenAI is committed to pushing the boundaries of model access.
- He says OpenAI did several things at DevDay no other AI lab would, and that it likes developers partly because of his startup background.
- He compares the new enabling power to fire: "maybe fire or something". OpenAI may have carried it down the mountain, but wants to give it to everyone.

## Renaissance versus industrial revolution, and the future of work

- Sam's framing: the Renaissance was centered on humans, the Industrial Revolution on machines.
- He says the Industrial Revolution had large positive effects on the world, but many people did not have a purely positive personal experience. In many cases they became "a cog in a machine" doing narrow work cut off from much of what makes life good.
- He is sad when people say AI will automate everything, humans will have no role, and "we'll be like house cats" while AI decides how to reward us. He says that is "definitely not" what OpenAI believes.
- His hope is a huge new wave of scientific discovery, art and company-building for anyone who wants to start something. People should share in the economic growth, and also regain control over their lives and how they spend their time.
- On jobs: he was less pessimistic than others, and was pleasantly surprised by the size of the latent demand in the world and by how much humans stay at the center of what happens with AI.
- Some work needs little creativity and nobody cares who does it, and AI will do much of that. But he says there will be no shortage of new things to do.
- On why humans stay central: "we just don't care that much about what machines do". He says people are obsessed with other people, and want to know the person behind the art, company, idea or product.
- Example: 10,000 agents worked for 4 days, perfectly coordinated, on solving Navier-Stokes equations. Public reaction was mostly about interpretation and the people involved, not the solution itself.
- On builders: people want more stuff and expectations are high. Developers never could do more before, but there was also no higher ceiling. He says it is a beautiful thing in society.

## Startup advice, speed, and cost of intelligence

- Sam says he would go back to being a startup investor. He calls this a "golden period".
- His investing approach has always been founder-focused: find the best founders working on ambitious projects, and be willing to take a lot of risk with them.
- What stays the same: "you have to make something people want". He says this was true 100 years ago and still holds.
- What changes: how much one or two people can do. He says the Silicon Valley startup "pipeline" hasn't yet adjusted enough to that.
- He believes that if you lower barriers a bit, people build far more.
- He is considering building something himself, such as a plugin, and releasing it, and says anything feels possible now. He noted DevDay's volume was only possible with AI help. Next year teams will be assigned work while he builds something too.
- On speed: he runs all his prompting on Ultrafast and finds it hard to go back. He cites a talk by Bret Victor from 15 to 20 years ago, with the idea that creators need immediate connection to what they create. He says fast responses changed his iteration loop and made a "massive" difference.
- On cost and speed roadmap:
  - Historically OpenAI focused on cutting the cost of intelligence, with more capable models getting much cheaper every few months or year.
  - Speed is now a new important vector, and OpenAI is investing in it. He says its own chip will be a big contributor to very fast inference for everyone.
  - Near-term, he believes OpenAI can offer 8x faster speed at a great price, then 100x faster.
  - There will always be real limits at a higher price on intelligence or speed, plus new top models such as "GPT-7 Galaxy".
  - He says to bet that OpenAI keeps relentlessly increasing value per dollar, "putting Moore's law to shame".
- On the Decisions API: the first request for Luna was cheaper and faster, which shows people always want lower price, more speed and better quality. OpenAI aims to be fast and cheap for common use cases, and to make the best, cheapest, fastest and most capable model at every point on the cost, speed and modality Pareto curve. He says it can't cover every new architecture or approach.
