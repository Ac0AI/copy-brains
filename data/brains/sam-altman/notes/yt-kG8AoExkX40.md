## Sam's role: research and compute over product

- Most of Sam's effort goes to research and compute. He says "the most important thing that we can do is to create smart models and to be able to run them efficiently and abundantly for a lot of people. If we can get that right, I believe that everything else will follow."
- His rule: find the high-leverage, difficult problem that continues the exponential. For OpenAI that means models and compute.
- Scaling compute is a complex problem: supply chain, partnerships, financing "probably already or at least rapidly becoming the most expensive infrastructure project in history," chip design, fabs, racks, and power systems.
- He admits he wishes he had more time for product. OpenAI has great product people, but he spends his own time where the leverage is highest.
- He frames the AI product phase as "smartphones before the iPhone". The technology pieces exist but the product ideas that change how people interface with computers do not yet. He calls his own habit of using computers the old way "mostly a product failure."

## Startup investing and research management follow the same power law

- Sam says the closest thing to startup investing in his career is managing a research program.
- Both are power-law games: "your best investment will outperform all of your other investments put together," and AI research works the same way. Brains are not naturally wired to think this way, so you have to reprogram them.
- High-risk bets are fine if the ones you take are super valuable when they work.
- In 2015, people said AGI was nearly impossible. Later, focusing on large language models drew a second round of criticism ("completely ridiculous").
- Who he backs:
  - People with non-consensus, fresh, high-energy, "non-standard" thinking who will stand by unpopular convictions.
  - Not founders with "a thin veneer on the same idea that everybody else has."
- Example: in late 2015 there were very few AGI efforts (DeepMind and one or two others), while thousands of founders were starting photo-sharing apps.
- Same rule for researchers: many chase whatever last worked, a few hold high conviction on a new idea. OpenAI aims to be the best lab for the second group.
- He thinks there is a small chance an outsider approaches AI from an angle nobody has considered.

## Why investor-then-founder helped

- Sam says investing first is unusual (the common path is founder, then investor) and strongly recommends it.
- As an investor he watched many crux moments across companies: strategy shifts, firing executives, founder mistakes. A single operator only gets a handful of these.
- He now pattern-matches on them when running OpenAI: "this is what happened when this company had a similar thing."
- He says running a company is far harder than investing.
- He learns more from successes than failures:
  - Failure lessons tend to be generic (grit, what not to do) and causation is hard to pin down because "most things don't work."
  - Success lessons, such as understanding which parts of YC or OpenAI worked, are easier to apply going forward.
  - Lessons taken from failure "either were fairly generic ... or got in the way of something else."
- Entrepreneurship advice is unglamorous and repetitive, and founders need constant reminders: talk to users more, ship earlier, get more feedback, raise the hiring bar and hire faster.

## Focus: kill good ideas for great ones

- Sam calls sacrificing good ideas for great ones "the hardest lesson for any entrepreneur or business to learn," and says he is bad at it himself.
- Last year OpenAI killed Sora (a good, fun product that used a lot of compute and mattered less than Codex, where the compute went) and killed its Atlas web browser (a great browser, but the talent was needed elsewhere).
- The decision: with limited compute, people, and resources, general intelligence for knowledge work and eventually science is the most important thing. Everything upstream of that (chips, data centers, infrastructure software, model training) is in. Offer the AI as a service and avoid many other things.
- Platform strategy:
  - One interface (a ChatGPT and Codex merger that started as a chatbot and moves toward a persistent agent) plus one API.
  - "Sell great AI at every point on the cost-performance curve": high-end for science, cheap for high-volume work.
  - "I don't think we should go build every product category. I don't think we should compete with all our customers." The aim is 100 million new businesses and 8 billion people building on it.

## Peter Thiel's advice on ChatGPT: double down on what works

- About two months after ChatGPT launched, growth was fast but felt unstable and "low value." Many people inside the company wanted to pursue five or six other directions.
- Sam asked Peter Thiel. His answer: it is an obvious mistake to do anything other than double down on the thing that is growing, which is rare.
- Thiel's reasoning:
  - It has "the power of the Google text box": type anything and it does the right thing.
  - It lacked what Silicon Valley wisdom demanded (feeds, network effects, lock-in, and this was before memory), but the market had chased the Google model for 20 years and this was the first thing like it.
  - "Why don't you just double down on that."
- Sam went "super hard onto it." He says Thiel and Paul Graham are the two people he calls on non-obvious problems because he can't predict what they'll say.
- Principle: when something is working, going deeper beats spending time exploring alternatives.

## YC lessons: ship embarrassingly early, move fast

- Sam says Paul Graham pushed the startup ecosystem toward "ship a v1, be embarrassed early," because customer feedback improves the product faster than polishing does.
- He shipped ChatGPT knowing it was early and embarrassing. He didn't ask Paul whether to launch because he knew the answer.
- Looking back across YC founder data, "how much the ability of moving fast and being iterative correlates with success" astonishes him.
- He applies the same approach to safety: put products into the world, watch where they break, learn. "That is the way you make a good product. That is also the way you make a safe product."
- He compares it to aviation: robust, clear-eyed accident reporting and public postmortems, and sharing learnings with other AI builders.
- He says he and OpenAI would not exist without the ecosystem YC created: capital flowing to young technical founders and ambitious work without a proven resume.

## Building a research lab with no customers (the exception to YC advice)

- It took 4.5 years from founding to the first product, the opposite of YC advice. He had no pattern for how to replace customer signal.
- What they did:
  - Spoke to people with experience at past great labs (including Alan Kay).
  - Used a leaderboard during the Dota 2 RL work so people could see how ideas performed objectively.
  - Used external demos for eminent people researchers wanted to impress.
  - Tried fake deadlines, which didn't work.
- Early days: on the first day, around January 2016, 11-12 people met at Greg Brockman's apartment. They got a whiteboard and then had no idea what to do. They wrote papers and tested ideas. Over years of "mostly chaotic stumbling" they found a rhythm for making and evaluating research bets, then the big discoveries (unsupervised sentiment paper, GPT-1, scaling laws).
- He says his dominant memory of the early period is trying and failing to raise money. They had no Bell Labs-style cash machine.

## Adoption is slower than expected, and the opportunity for small businesses

- Sam admits he expected software to be "up for grabs" much sooner after GPT-4 (2023). He now thinks the economy's inertia and force of habit make adoption slower and the transition smoother. He disagrees with Toby Lütke's view that 2026 is the year every business is up for grabs, but not with the direction.
- Some businesses (sports, authentic experiences, human-connection products) will become more valuable, since people stay wired to care about other people.
- He praises Toby for using the tools himself and sending detailed product feedback, and notes that being hands-on is the only way to get a real feel for what the models can do.
- He admits to using his computer the old way despite having Codex. His view is that the bigger limit now is the context AI has on him, not model intelligence. He wants an agent that reads Slack, customer stories, and papers at a scale no person can.
- He expects "the greatest boom in people starting smaller businesses that we have ever seen." He says the AI field has not talked enough about empowerment and autonomy, and that a pitch of giving up control for a cure for cancer is "a terrible sales pitch."
