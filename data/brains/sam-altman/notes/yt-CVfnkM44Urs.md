## How to spot a market that will keep growing fast
- Sam says students have an edge here. Usually they only need to trust their intuition.
- Older people have to study which new technologies young people are adopting. Young people grow up to become the mainstream market.
- His method is to look at your own habits and your friends' habits. Ask what you are using more and more, and what new things people around you are starting to use.
- Those things are probably where the market is heading. Founders who are young already know the new market trends better than older people do.

## Coping with the workload of a startup
- Sam's answer to the question of how to handle exhausting work and stay productive is blunt: you keep going even when you are exhausted.
- School is different. You can skip a class or fail it. A startup is real life, and you have to grit your teeth and persist.
- The usual advice is to take a vacation. He says that usually isn't possible for a founder, because the startup is everything.
- He tells founders to lean on the people around them for support. "Startup depression" is real and serious, and you need people who encourage you.
- To avoid burning out, face the challenges head on and fix whatever practices are going wrong. He says it gets somewhat easier over time.

## Team and execution only matter if the idea and product are right
- Sam repeats the point from the previous lecture. If the idea and product positioning are wrong, nothing about team-building or execution will help.
- This lecture covers building a team and executing decisions.

## The cofounder relationship is one of the most important in the company
- Sam says the relationship between cofounders is one of the most important in the company.
- The standard advice applies, including at YC. Watch for cracks between cofounders and resolve conflict quickly.
- The most common reason startups fail is that cofounders turn on each other.
- Many founders pick a cofounder more casually than they would hire an employee. Sam says "don't do this". It may be the most important decision for the company, so treat it carefully.

## Don't pick cofounders casually
- Students often go wrong by picking someone at random with a pitch like "I want to start a company, you do too, so let's do it together."
- Founder-matching meetups, where everyone is looking for a cofounder and knows nothing about each other, are a bad way to do it. Sam calls this "crazy".
- He argues you wouldn't hire an employee this casually, so you shouldn't choose a cofounder that way.
- Picking someone you barely know, or who isn't even a friend, is almost always a disaster. When things get hard, you have no shared bond from going through tough times together.
- Evidence from YC:
  - In one batch of 75 teams, nine added a new cofounder after the YC interview.
  - All nine of those teams fell apart within the following year.
  - Sam's rule of thumb is that cofounders who don't know each other well have a hard time succeeding.

## Where to find cofounders
- College is a good place to meet cofounders.
- If you are out of school and don't know anyone, go and work at an interesting company.
- Working for a while at a company like Facebook or Google makes finding a cofounder there almost as easy as at Stanford.

## Solo founders
- Sam says a bad cofounder is worse than none, but going it alone is still a poor choice.
- YC's data shows that all of its 20 most valuable graduates have at least two cofounders. He allows that the number may be off by one or two.
- YC invests in roughly one solo-founder project for every 10 it funds.
- His preference is a cofounder you can trust. Founding alone is not impossible if you have no such person.
- Partnering with someone you only casually know is "extremely bad", even though many students make this mistake.
