## Hire for startups: time, auditions, and sourcing

- Sam says hiring is "probably the most important thing a founder does." Companies "are a product of the team the founders build," and a mediocre hire can't be managed into doing good work.
- After the vision and product-market fit are settled, he says founders should spend between a third and a half of their time hiring.
- It can't be outsourced. The founder identifies people, gets candidates to want the job, and meets every person who interviews. He cites Keith Rabois's view that the CEO/founders should interview every candidate until the company has at least 500 employees.
- Learn the role by doing it before hiring for it. His example is a hacker-CEO who hires a VP of Sales to avoid getting his hands dirty: "This does not work." The founder should do the job first, then lean on the board or investors for opinions on the final few candidates.
- Hiring is a long-term, always-on activity, not something started when a role is urgent. If you find someone great for a role you won't need for two months, hire her now.
- Don't hire for the sake of it. Companies generally work better when smaller. Figure out the least work you can feasibly do and use the smallest team that can do it. "Hire because there is no other way to do what you want to do."

## Evaluating candidates: smart, effective, and auditions

- Smart and effective are table stakes for every role. Hires who lack them predictably fail in the early days of a startup.
- Interview tactics:
  - Ask about their most impressive projects and biggest wins.
  - Ask how they spend an average day and what they got done in the last month.
  - Go deep on what they personally did, since it is easy to take credit for a successful project.
  - Ask how they would solve a problem you currently have in that role.
  - Combine this with the right reference-check questions.
- Intelligence is usually clear by the end of an hour-long conversation. If you learn nothing, that is bad; if you are bored, that is really bad. A good interview feels like a conversation, not questions and responses.
- Smart, effective people are adaptable, which matters because anyone hired is likely doing a new job within three to six months.
- Sam calls auditions "the most important tactical piece of advice I have." Have candidates audition for roles instead of interviewing for them:
  - It is hard to know what working with someone is like after a few interviews, but easy after working with them.
  - Almost always possible: have the person do a day or two of work, at night or on weekends.
  - Developer: write code for a real but non-critical project.
  - PR person: write a press release and identify reporters to pitch it to.
  - Have them sign a contractor agreement and pay them like a normal contractor.
  - The candidate also gets a feel for what working at the company is like.

## Sourcing candidates and building a recruiting pipeline

- The core rule: "use your personal networks more." By at least a 10x margin, Sam says friends and friends of friends are the best candidate source he has seen.
- Go after the best people relentlessly even if you doubt you can get them. If it works 5% of the time, it's still worth it. The best startups keep hiring this way far longer than seems possible, and bad startups make excuses for not doing so.
- Once you're sure a new hire is a star, sit her down and "wring out of her the names of everyone that you should try to hire." It may take real effort.
- Great people are never looking for jobs, so recruiting often means poaching. He admits he has no great answer on poaching from acquaintances, quoting a friend: "Poaching is the titty twister of Silicon Valley relationships."
- Other channels:
  - Technical recruiters are pretty bad and job boards are generally worse.
  - Conferences can be good.
  - Hosting interesting tech talks can help with technical hiring.
  - University recruiting works well once the company is reasonably established.
  - Don't limit the search to your area; many people want to move to the Bay Area.
  - Tell investors in update emails what kinds of people you need, and use their networks.
- Treat sourcing as a long-term investment: you may spend time with someone for a year or more before talking about a job.
- A recruiting product idea he would back: a service that shows how everyone in your company is connected to a candidate and searches employees' personal networks. He says LinkedIn is probably good for sales hires but not for developers.

## Selling the mission and closing candidates

- You need a mission to hire well. Candidates must believe in it, meaning why this job matters more than the others they could take. A mission that excites people is probably the best thing you can do to get a great team before runaway traction.
- Founders assume everyone will be as excited about the company as they are; "in reality, no one will." Expect to spend a lot of time selling.
- A good mission plus good selling lets you land slightly overqualified people. In a fast-growing startup they quickly end up in a role they feel not quite ready for anyway.
- Use the board and investors to help close candidates.
- Once you decide you want someone, switch into closing mode. The person she will report to, and ideally the CEO, should do everything possible to close her and talk to her about once a day.

## Culture, fit, and not compromising

- Hire people you like. Sam cites Stripe's "Sunday test": would you come into the office on a Sunday because you want to hang out with this person? He has only a few times seen a very good candidate he didn't like, and the one time he hired anyway "was a mistake."
- Want some diversity of thought. Some attributes need uniformity (integrity, intelligence) and others need coverage of the full range (for example, both nerds and athletes).
- Decide your cultural values deliberately, make sure the whole company knows and buys into them, and hire for them. Treat them as "articles of faith" and be willing to pass on an otherwise good candidate who doesn't fit.
- He quotes Andrew Mason: "Values are a decision making framework that empower individuals to make the decision that you, the founder, would make, in situations where there are conflicting interests (e.g. growth vs. customer satisfaction)."
- Diversity of opinions is good; diversity of values in a startup is bad. Some people are so set in their ways they will never get behind your values, and you'll probably end up firing them.
- Avoid remote employees in the early days, while culture is still gelling.
- Don't compromise, especially early. You'll always need someone yesterday, but "a single bad hire left unfixed for long can kill a company." It's better to lose a deal or ship late than hire someone mediocre. Great people attract great people, and one mediocre hire can unwind that.

## Compensation and equity

- Be frugal with nearly everything in a startup except pay for great people.
- Be most generous with equity. Ideally pay slightly below to roughly market salary plus a very generous equity package, and pay just enough that people don't stress about cash flow.
- Experienced people often have higher personal burn rates and may need more pay. But great companies are not usually created by experienced people, with a few exceptions where it matters a lot.
- His line: if you want an above-market salary, go work at a big company with no equity upside.
- Equity rule of thumb for the first 20 hires: about double what your investors suggest. Rough numbers he has seen for a company on a good but not absolute breakout trajectory: about 1.5% for the first engineer and about 0.25% for the twentieth, with huge variance.
- Example: a very successful YC company pays effectively all engineers one flat salary, lower than market. It works because those people enjoy the work and believe in the stock, and they are likely to earn far more than with higher salaries elsewhere.
- Expect to negotiate a little, so learn how. Don't materially break your compensation structure to land someone, because word gets out and everyone is upset.

## Process, red flags, firing, and retention

- Red flags: focus on title, and worse, on "how many reports will be in my organization." These usually signal the person won't succeed at a startup. "If you have a difficult-to-articulate desire to pass, pass."
- Add some rigor to the process:
  - Every interviewer commits to a hire/no-hire decision and writes up their thoughts, which is useful to review if you get it wrong.
  - Hold a brief in-person discussion with the whole interview team after each candidate leaves.
  - Take the candidate out to lunch or dinner.
  - Insist everyone is on time and prepared, and that every candidate leaves with a positive impression.
  - One person coordinates the whole process.
  - Set a consistent decision framework, such as whether you need unanimous consent.
  - Teach people how to interview, since great team members may not be great interviewers.
- Fire fast. He has never met a newbie founder who fires fast enough, or a founder who doesn't learn this after a few years. When it's obviously not working, it's unlikely to start. Parting quickly is better for everyone, and a short stint is a non-issue in future interviews. The rest of the team usually knows before you do.
- Retention:
  - Check in with people and be a good manager.
  - Hold regular all-hands meetings.
  - Keep people happy and challenged.
  - Keep a sense of momentum, which is important to retaining talent.
  - Give people new roles about every six months.
  - Keep bringing in talented people, since that alone makes good people want to stay.
  - Always identify and promote new talent.
