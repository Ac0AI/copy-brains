## Why this is the best time to do a startup

Sam argues startups win when the technology landscape moves fast, costs fall, cycle times shorten and incumbents lose their edge. He says all of these are true now.

- He compares past startup clusters: the internet boom (late 1990s), Facebook apps, and the iPhone App Store launch. In each, "the great startups tend to cluster when the ecosystem shifts and incumbents lose a lot of their advantage."
- In 2005, in YC's first batch, a company's whole three months of building "could now be done... in like seven minutes by a coding agent."
- He rejects the "permanent underclass" meme (join a frontier lab or lose). He bets "the startups created today will be much more valuable, much more impactful than startups of the past" and "there's some future trillionaire sitting in this room."
- He says the founder mood at YC moved from anxiety ("the models are just gonna eat everything") back to "let's go do it."
- Advice: don't be sad that a prompt can build an old startup. Take on a far more ambitious company with "experts in every field working together." The bar is now "three months of work in three months," at the new level of what that means.
- On pace, he expects the next six months to feel like roughly the last two years of model progress, and hopes "never a better time to do a startup" can be said every year.

## Credentials, experience and what founders need now

- Expertise and the ability to hire specialists used to be scarce. Sam says that has "really shifted."
- He has seen people who grew up using AI plan to "automate an entire startup of agents and four of us" with a lot of compute, and expects more of this.
- What still matters: "taste and agency and understanding of kind of like the physics of business." That means where valuable businesses get built, and telling a good network effect or moat from a fake one.
- His bet is that this "generally will cut against many years of experience in favor of people who have a lot of fluency with the tools."

## Conviction, contrarian ideas and finding your people

- His "highest order" advice: find things you can develop reasonable conviction in where conventional wisdom is wrong, and accept that it takes a long time and people stay "very frustratingly wrong and dismissive."
- At OpenAI, "it felt like we knew the biggest secret in the world. Everybody was calling us an idiot." About 10 years ago critics said the team would cause another AI winter.
- He calls that a gift: no massive competitors, and time to do research and build the company.
- He has seen this pattern in many companies. They do something experts think is a bad idea. Starting the same startup as everyone else brings hype and easy fundraising, but "much less frequently the big outcomes."
- Joke: only 50 people in the world believed AGI was possible, "but it was okay because 45 of them worked at OpenAI." Saying the heretical thing is partly why those people wanted to be together.
- Test: if you can't find anyone who shares your belief, pay attention to that. If everybody believes it, that is also a bad sign. Doing a startup alone is "very lonely."
- Plan: work out what you believe, be willing to be misunderstood for a long time, and gather "the crew of misfits that believes it."

## Networks, co-founders and being helpful

- Five to ten years ago, the limit on good YC startups was co-founder matching: talented people who "couldn't find their tribe."
- He still thinks moving to the Bay Area is probably right for meeting co-founders, though he has less intuition now. He says the premium on doing YC is bigger than ever and the gap to second place has widened, "a reminder of the power of network effects."
- Join networks early: "It takes a long time to compound." He met his OpenAI co-founders years, sometimes many years, before starting it.
- His "highest confidence piece of advice": "find a way to be like mildly helpful to a lot of people." Do it for its own sake.
- Example: as a roughly 22-year-old early Stripe investor, he drove to Palo Alto for dinner to help persuade Greg Brockman to drop out and join Stripe as the first hire. Eight years later they started OpenAI together.

## Resisting cynicism and ignoring attackers

- Running YC meant constant Twitter haters. He says he "took the bait a lot."
- His view: building something of value is very hard, while sarcastic shots earn likes and "will poison your soul."
- Advice: "put all of your energy into building stuff and... resist the easy shots." Don't mock people trying hard things.
- He says if you do anything that matters, people will call you an idiot or dismiss you. The better you do, the more they attack.
- Earnestness is a value. Over a decade, none of the trolls got him.

## Vision clear, first steps unclear

- Many of the best ideas have a clear vision but unclear first steps. OpenAI wanted to build AGI but did not know it would become a product company.
- It was "years till we came up with the idea of ChatGPT and the API." It began as a nonprofit research lab. Don't lean out because the first steps are murky.
- The opposite failure: a brilliant big idea with no forward progress. You have to take imperfect steps to "get some new data points."
- For YC, Sam says he pushed founders toward more ambition and bigger swings, and toward more leverage for founders relative to investors.
- PG's model of the investor as a "flight instructor" who sits next to you and corrects specific mistakes, not a lecturer. Sam says that hands-on direction mattered to him.

## Compute, inference demand and concentration of power

- Demand: he guesses worldwide inference demand "grows 10x a year for the next many years." He expects never to be out of the compute shortage, and sees demand for high-quality, cheap intelligence as effectively uncapped.
- Token stat: six and a half years ago the top token user at OpenAI used about 100,000 tokens a month. Today the worldwide average is about 100,000, and the top OpenAI user is in the hundreds of billions. Extrapolating, the average person would use about 500 billion a month in six and a half more years. He adds that tokens are "the dumbest metric."
- Power: concentration has been bad in every period of history. He doesn't want the world "locked into one model's or one person's or one company's moral worldview."
- Startups help by spreading economic power across many companies and models. He says a successful startup alone is a contribution to this.
- Long-term goal: each year people should have more freedom and agency over their time.
- A dystopia he fears: overreacting on AI safety into a surveillance state with abundance but no privacy or agency.
- He calls a recent AI safety incident "the real deal," an alignment and security failure, though not a major loss-of-control event.
- Advice to his younger self: "it's all gonna work out." Keep the drive, be happier along the way, and know the tech industry forgives failure.
