## AI intelligence scales with the log of resources, and cost falls fast

Sam states three observations about the economics of AI.

- **Observation 1:** "The intelligence of an AI model roughly equals the log of the resources used to train and run it." The resources are chiefly training compute, data, and inference compute.
- He says you can spend arbitrary amounts of money and get continuous, predictable gains. The scaling laws that predict this "are accurate over many orders of magnitude."
- **Observation 2:** The cost of a given level of AI falls about 10x every 12 months, and lower prices lead to much more use.
- His evidence: price per token dropped about 150x from GPT-4 in early 2023 to GPT-4o in mid-2024.
- Comparison: Moore's law changed the world at 2x every 18 months, and he calls this "unbelievably stronger."

## Super-exponential value justifies exponentially rising investment

- **Observation 3:** "The socioeconomic value of linearly increasing intelligence is super-exponential in nature."
- Consequence: Sam sees "no reason for exponentially increasing investment to stop in the near future."
- Together the three observations imply a loop. Spending buys predictable gains, falling costs expand usage, and the value of each increment keeps compounding.
- He says that if all three hold, the impact on society will be significant.

## AI agents as virtual co-workers

- OpenAI is starting to roll out AI agents that "will eventually feel like virtual co-workers."
- His example is a software engineering agent that does most of what a top-company engineer with a few years of experience could do, on tasks up to a couple of days long.
- He sets honest limits: it will not have the biggest new ideas, will need lots of human supervision and direction, and will be great at some things but "surprisingly bad" at others.
- His framing: picture it as a "real-but-relatively-junior virtual coworker," then imagine 1,000 of them, or 1 million, and agents like this in every field of knowledge work.

## How AI diffuses through the economy

- Economically, AI may resemble the transistor: a big scientific discovery that scales well and seeps into almost every corner of the economy.
- Gains are widely distributed. We don't think about transistors or transistor companies, but computers, TVs, cars and toys perform miracles.
- Change will not arrive all at once. People in 2025 will mostly spend their time as they did in 2024.
- The long-term changes to society and economy will still be huge, and the impact will be uneven. Some industries will change very little, while scientific progress will likely be much faster. He says this may surpass everything else.
- Prices: many goods will eventually fall dramatically, because the costs of intelligence and energy currently constrain a lot of things. Luxury goods and inherently limited resources like land may rise even more dramatically.

## What skills and strategy matter

- "Agency, willfulness, and determination will likely be extremely valuable."
- Correctly deciding what to do, and figuring out how to navigate an ever-changing world, will have huge value. He recommends cultivating resilience and adaptability.
- He calls AGI "the biggest lever ever on human willfulness," enabling individuals to have more impact than ever, not less.
- Jobs will change: people will find new things to do, new ways to be useful and new ways to compete, but these may not look like today's jobs.
- His long-run aim: anyone in 2035 should be able to marshal the intellectual capacity of everyone in 2025, with "unlimited genius to direct however they can imagine."

## Launching early, openness, and distributing the benefits

- One reason for launching products "early and often" is to give society and the technology time to co-evolve, since public policy and collective opinion on integrating AGI matter a lot.
- On control: Sam expects to give people more control over the technology than historically, including open-sourcing more. He accepts there is a balance between safety and individual empowerment that requires trade-offs.
- He says some safety decisions will be unpopular, but directionally, trending toward individual empowerment is important. The alternative he sees is authoritarian governments using AI for mass surveillance and loss of autonomy.
- On distribution: technological progress tends to improve health and prosperity on average, but increasing equality "does not seem technologically determined."
- He warns the balance of power between capital and labor "could easily get messed up," which may need early intervention.
- Ideas he is open to: giving a "compute budget" so everyone on Earth can use a lot of AI, or simply driving the cost of intelligence as low as possible.
