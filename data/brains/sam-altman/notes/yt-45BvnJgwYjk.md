## Getting the first 20 B2B customers: expand your network so customers come to you

- Sam says the question assumes you must reach companies outside your network. The approach he has seen work best is the other one: expand your network and pull customers into it.
- The right channel depends on the company:
  - If all your customers go to one trade show, go there and meet them.
  - If there are only about 10 possible customers in the world, find any way to reach them: cold email, referrals, or anything else.
- Founder-to-founder sales tends to work well, so he suggests asking how to meet more entrepreneurs, or how to build a bigger network that reaches your early customers.
- At YC, the community was built so that most B2B companies get their first 20 customers from other YC companies. He says this has been "extremely valuable".
  - Almost all YC B2B companies sell almost exclusively to YC companies early on.
  - This was somewhat less true when the network was smaller, and is still less true for companies in a niche that is rare in the YC community.
  - It usually starts with Bookface (the YC internal network) or emails to YC founders, selling to the YC community.
- Even if you are not in YC, he says to think the same way.

## Building a network from scratch, and why a great product beats outreach

- Sam did this himself at 18 or 19 in Silicon Valley, when he knew nobody. He messaged founders he respected in his city and proposed meeting, for example by booking a table for ten and inviting nine of them.
  - Some of those people stayed in touch and became close friends 12 or 13 years later.
  - He says it is harder outside a startup hub, because these relationships are easier to build in person.
- Silicon Valley has a strong culture of helping others, with startups as a dominant economic engine. He says not to be afraid to email people you want to talk to.
- On conferences and meetups:
  - Some are good, but most are not.
  - Ask people you respect who have attended before you go.
  - He is shy in big groups and finds most attendees not very special.
  - Speaking at a conference is easier for an introvert because people come to you, though it may still be a waste of time.
- His key point is that if you build something impressive, your network comes to you.
  - "Hi, I'm starting a company, please reach out, I'm trying to find customers" makes you look like many other people.
  - "Hi, I built this great product, here it is, take a look" gets you taken seriously.
  - That puts you in the top 1% of people sending cold emails.

## Advantages and disadvantages of being young, and whether college matters

- Advantages of young founders:
  - Fewer life obligations such as boards and families, so more focus and stamina.
  - Boldness, including willingness to move across the world for a good opportunity.
  - Used to living cheaply. Sam had rats in his kitchen and a mattress on the floor until he was 25. Low living costs are a big advantage over startups whose founders need a lot of money just to live.
  - The biggest advantage: they lack the scars of experience. They are willing to try again and again. Founders often say they would not have started if they had known how hard it would be, how the industry would turn out, or how long it would take.
  - Young people are often at the front of new technology shifts and have a better-than-chance sense of the next big wave.
- Disadvantages:
  - Probably not good at managing people.
  - Very lacking in business experience.
  - Probably not as good an engineer as they will be later in life.
  - So bring in experienced people for specific areas.
- On college: he did two years, loved it, learned useful things and is glad he went. He says most people at YC do not know where he or most others went, and that it never comes up as a topic.
- He still thinks Silicon Valley's network is worth the cost, and says you can still live cheaply in hidden corners of the Valley. He calls the idea that you can't succeed there "a myth".

## Non-technical founders: what's needed and how to pick a technical cofounder

- Sam says Evan Spiegel and Brian Chesky are not fully non-technical. Both know a little. Brian is a great designer who built the first version. Both are great at product.
- A non-technical founder needs:
  - Strong product skills and great leadership.
  - A very strong idea and attention to fine detail.
  - The ability to convince really capable technical people to follow them. You need only a little technical or design ability.
- How to evaluate a technical cofounder:
  - Only start a company with someone you already know.
  - Look at things they have built. Even if you can't read their code, you can see that they built something good, built it fast, and kept improving it.
  - Work on a project together first.
  - Minimum "known for a while" is 6 months.
- To get more technical yourself, take a few computer science classes. There are also many free online options, some from YC companies. He learned Python "the hard way".
- How technical you need to be depends on what you build:
  - A nuclear reactor needs deep engineering and nuclear physics.
  - A not-especially-complex iPhone app needs little for the first version. Hire world-class technical talent later.
  - What matters early is building a product people love. Code quality in the first version matters little at first, but matters a lot later.

## Choosing what to work on: form your own convictions and explore broadly

- Sam says it is a bad strategy to let someone else dictate what is important and then go do it. Develop your own convictions, because you understand and care about them, and the other person may be wrong. His advice to a high schooler:
  - Study the world and history.
  - Look at the latest in technology.
  - Read what smart people say.
  - Analyze it all and make your own list of the most important things. You can make your own predictions.
- He studied AI and computer security in college and thinks both remain very good areas.
- Areas he names as promising:
  - Synthetic biology or biotech, which is where he would have gone if he'd stayed in school longer.
  - Cheap, clean energy. He chairs two nuclear energy companies. He argues quality of life and energy cost are tightly linked, and that the planet is at risk without cheap, clean energy. Cheaper energy over 20 or 30 years could replace current forms.
  - Education, which he calls huge and under-tapped.
  - VR and AR, which will be an important computing platform.
- How he found his own path:
  - He took one class in everything, read one book on every topic, and made smart friends in very different fields.
  - He tried many things. He was an investment banker for a day and disliked it. He built a lot of software and became interested in AI and mobile.
  - He loved theoretical physics but wasn't smart enough for it.
  - He ended up loving investing in tech companies and helping founders.
  - His summary: breadth-first, then depth-first search works. He ended up deeply specialized in a few areas.
- Needs versus value ("value" being a polite word for money):
  - Many world needs are obvious from history, science and economics. If you identify an obvious need, it is probably a real one.
  - Some things are great but unpaid.
  - He once wanted to be a writer but realized the world doesn't need or value the "7 millionth novel", and it is hard to earn enough from it.
  - It also works in reverse. Being very good at something is satisfying.

## Spotting the next platform wave: use intensity of use, not shipments

- Sam says transformative companies tend to be founded in clusters when a new platform rises:
  - The internet, 1996 to 2000.
  - Mobile after the iPhone in 2007, roughly 2007 to 2011. He puts Facebook mostly in the mobile category.
- Many people think VR and AR are next, and he thinks it's still a bit early.
- His test for when to focus: look at usage rate per individual user, not total shipments.
  - Smartphones existed before the iPhone, but people used them only a few times, did little beyond calls and a calendar, and didn't carry them every day.
  - The iPhone didn't sell much in its first year compared with the number of phones Nokia and others sold in 2007. But owners loved it and used it all the time.
- So he ignores VR/AR shipment totals and waits until someone builds a device or software that people use heavily every day once they have it.
- This holds regardless of price. The first iPhone was expensive, but owners loved it, and prices eventually fall.

## Equity crowdfunding for startups: why it fell short

- Sam calls it disappointing. He wouldn't call it a failure, but it hasn't had the success he hoped for.
- What still goes wrong: it is easy for companies that don't need it, because they already have known investors.
- So far the public hasn't been willing to back companies without professional investor support. It has become a way, though not the only one, to increase the money raised from full-time professional investors.
- He still believes crowdfunding should matter, but says the right product hasn't been built yet.
- As an investor he wants to know the company and founders well and in person, and probably wouldn't invest in people he'd never met through a crowdfunding platform.

## Surviving an acquisition as a key non-founder employee

- Sam says most acquisitions don't go smoothly, so go in knowing it will be hard.
- The most important thing he has seen is an agreement before closing about the target company's autonomy. He urges founders to secure one that lets the team operate as independently as possible.
- Build bridges. You don't want to be caught between warring factions.
  - Want the acquirer to support you and you to support them.
  - Want people to get along.
- As a key leader, make it your personal job to build strong relationships with as many people as possible at the acquiring company, and be the bridge when tensions inevitably rise.
- On how long to stay: six to nine months is enough before deciding it won't work.
