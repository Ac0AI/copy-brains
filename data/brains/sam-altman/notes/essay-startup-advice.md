## Two non-negotiables: team, market, and product quality

- Sam says "make something people want" and treats product/market fit as the thing that matters. Most other advice conflicts, so "just make a decision and get back to work."
- A great team and a great market are both critical. He calls the debate over which matters more "silly."
- Founders should mostly write code, talk to users, and build the company (hire the best people, get the culture right, fundraise, close sales). "Most other things that founders do are a waste of time."
- Obsess about product quality. Care about execution quality.
- It is better for a few users to love the product than for many to "sort of like it."
- It is easier to sell painkillers than vitamins.
- Execution beats ideas: "far more people have good ideas than are willing to roll up their sleeves and get shit done." He says you are more likely to die from bad execution than from a competitor.
- You can win with the best product, the best price, or the best experience.
- Value can come from breakthrough innovation, incremental refinement, or complex coordination. Great companies often do two; the very best do all three.

## Ideas, focus, and how to pick a market

- Good ideas are underweight in a "pivot-happy world," so it is worth the time to think one through.
- Don't start a startup you aren't willing to work on for ten years.
- Startups are very hard anyway, so "you may as well go after a big opportunity."
- Many of the best ideas "seem silly or bad initially." You want the intersection of "seems like bad idea" and "is good idea," and you must be "contrarian and right, not simply contrarian."
- "Surf someone else's wave."
- Set a clear, easy-to-understand vision and make it a mission people believe in.
- Stay focused and don't do too many things at once. Eliminate distractions.
- Startups should require as few miracles as possible, "but at least one."
- Pivots: do them fully and with conviction. The worst outcome is a bit of the old and a bit of the new. "It's better to make a decision and be wrong than to equivocate."
- Keep it simple and be suspicious of complexity.

## Growth, momentum, and customers

- "Growth solves (nearly) all problems." Focus on it, but occasionally check you are growing toward something valuable.
- Momentum is critical; don't lose it.
- Move fast. Speed is a main advantage over large companies.
- You make what you measure.
- Be suspect about buying users.
- Have a direct relationship with customers and great customer service.
- Learn to sell your product, but don't let the company be run by a sales guy.
- Be suspicious of work that isn't building product or getting customers. It is easy to get sucked into "an infrastructure rewrite death spiral."
- Journalists like hearing directly from founders. If you hire PR people, resist their urge to control all contact. Ignore the fact that "the press loves [you]."
- Meetups and conferences are generally a waste of time.
- Get on planes in marginal situations: in-person beats "tele-anything."
- Don't forget to make money.

## Hiring and team building

- Hiring is the most important thing a founder does. Spend at least a third of your time on it. "Hire slow; fire fast."
- Hire smart, effective people "committed to what you're doing."
- Hire friends and friends of friends, and go after them "like crazy." Other sources are OK, but he always got bad results from technical recruiters.
- Value aptitude over experience.
- Hire people you could describe as "animals."
- Occasionally think about why the 20th person will join.
- Don't hire professional managers too early.
- Don't have a diverse culture in the early days.
- Keep salaries low and equity high. Include a table in offer letters showing what the grant could be worth in various scenarios.
- Keep the organization as flat as possible. Stay small and nimble.
- Be suspect of anyone who says "process" too often.

## Running the company and culture

- Overcommunicate with the team. Most founders are bad at this. "Transparency is your friend."
- Share financial results and key metrics with the company every month.
- Hold a staff meeting at least once a week.
- Keep an operational cadence with short projects and regular releases.
- Set goals and motivate people toward them. Build a culture that rewards output.
- Always praise good work. Celebrate wins (for example, t-shirts for big milestones).
- Lead by example.
- Very small companies should work out of an apartment or house.
- Pay close attention to the cofounder relationship, especially if more than one of you wants to be CEO.
- If the founders seem to care more about being founders than about the specific company, go join another company.
- Find a mentor who will teach you how to manage.
- Be formidable: "do not be easy to push around."
- Schleps are good. Be relentlessly resourceful.

## Fundraising, investors, and cash

- "Don't die." Be frugal and keep burn low until you're sure everything is working.
- Watch cash in the bank and don't run out. Raise a bit more than you think you need.
- Startup success is usually pass/fail, so worry more about passing than about "an extra point of dilution."
- Raise money on promise and on clean terms.
- Create a competitive situation in any deal (fundraising, partnerships).
- "Investors love companies other investors love."
- Good investors are worth a reasonable premium. Prefer a few highly involved investors over many lightly engaged ones, and give them something to do.
- Do reference checks on investors. Ask other founders how they behave when everything goes wrong.
- Founders typically keep board control in the first round.
- The board's role is "advice and consent." If the CEO lays out no clear strategy and asks the board to set one, it "will usually end in disaster." Board observers are usually a headache.

## Founder resilience and personal habits

- An almost crazy level of dedication is needed to succeed. Sometimes you can win through "sheer force of will."
- "All startups are fucked in at least one major way. Keep going."
- Stay externally optimistic when things melt down. On bad days, "tomorrow will be better."
- Being CEO "is miserable more often than it's good. But when it's good, it's really good."
- Most things are not as risky as they seem.
- Listen to everyone, then make your own decision.
- Keep a daily to-do list with the one or two big things at the top.
- Sleep and exercise.
- "Get lucky."
- The best startups are defined by exceptions. The rules are probably breakable, "but probably not all at the same time."
