## The idea and testing it

- Sam asks founders what they're building and why, and wants "clear, concise answers." Complex ideas are "almost always a sign of muddled thinking or a made up problem." A good idea should excite at least some people the first time they hear it.
- He asks who desperately needs the product. Best case: the founder is the target user. Second best: the founder understands the user extremely well.
- If there are users, he asks how many, how fast they grow, why growth isn't faster, and whether users love it. "Love" means they tell friends without prompting from the company.
- With no users, he works backwards from the perfect experience to find the smallest kernel to build first.
- Test consumer ideas by launching and seeing what happens (people say they'll use it but it won't cut through clutter). Test enterprise ideas by selling first: get a letter of intent from a customer before writing code. For biotech and hard tech, talk to customers and build the smallest subset of the technology.
- He asks how the company becomes a monopoly (Peter Thiel's term): businesses that get more powerful with scale and are hard to copy, without unethical behavior toward competitors.
- Market: how big now, how fast growing, why big in ten years. He likes technological shifts that most people haven't noticed yet. The best answer is "a large part of a small market."
- He prefers something new to something derivative. One acceptable definition of new is 10x better. It is easier to do something new and hard than derivative and easy, because people want to help and join the former.
- "The best ideas sound bad but are in fact good," so don't be too secretive. There are a thousand times more people with good ideas than people willing to do the work.
- Don't force an idea. A YC experiment funding promising teams with no ideas failed for all of them, because once you have a startup you rush to a plausible but derivative idea. Instead learn widely, notice inefficiencies and tech shifts, work on interesting projects, and hang around smart people.

## Founders, cofounders and equity

- Mediocre teams do not build great companies. Top founder traits: unstoppability, determination, formidability, resourcefulness. Intelligence and passion rank highly. All matter more than experience or "expertise with language X and framework Y."
- The best founders are low-stress to work with because "he or she will get it done, no matter what it is." They are unusually responsive, a sign of decisiveness, focus and intensity.
- Founders who are hard to talk to are almost always bad. Communication is "the most important rarely-discussed founder skill."
- He wants rigidity on the company's core mission and flexibility on almost everything else.
- A tech startup needs one founder who can build and one who is good at sales and talking to users (can be the same person).
- Pick a cofounder you know well, not someone met at a "cofounder dating thing." Cofounder breakups are a leading cause of early startup death, and frequent when founders met to start the company. Existing relationships keep people going when expected value dips.
- Ranking: good cofounder, then solo founder, then (far worse) a bad cofounder. Part ways quickly if it isn't working.
- Set equity early; the conversation gets harder with time. Nearly equal is best. With two founders, one extra share for one person prevents deadlocks.

## Product: the only real secret

- "Here is the secret to success: have a great product." Founders look for tricks, but "startups are the point in your life when tricks stop working." Eventually growth hacks stop working and growth comes from people wanting to use the product.
- Build a "product improvement engine": talk to users, watch them use the product, find sub-par parts, fix them, repeat. Improving 5% every week compounds. The faster the cycle, the better the company.
- During YC, founders should build product and talk to users, and little else besides eating, sleeping, exercising and time with loved ones.
- Get close to users: watch them, sit in their office, value both what they say and what they do. Put no one between founders and users; founders do sales and support.
- "Do things that don't scale": recruit users one at a time (Ben Silbermann approached strangers in Palo Alto coffee shops for Pinterest), build what they ask for. Big press announcements almost never work.
- Ship tiny and early: minimal surface area, small pieces, no big batched release, little long-range planning. Keep product and company simple.
- Diagnostic questions: Do users use it more than once? Are they fanatical? Would they be truly bummed if the company went away? Do they recommend it unprompted? For B2B, are there at least 10 paying customers? If not, the product isn't good enough. He is skeptical of most excuses for not growing.
- When unsure or in any internal disagreement, talk to users. Great founders care "a little bit too much" about quality, and "product" includes support and sales interactions.
- Better to have a small number of users who love it than many who like it: "it's much easier to get more users than to go from like to love."

## Growth and momentum

- "Growth solves all problems, and lack of growth is not solvable by anything but growth." Growth keeps morale up and creates new roles; without it people fight over responsibility and leave. Prime directive: "Never lose momentum."
- Make growth the top priority and pick one metric the company optimizes: "The company does what the CEO measures." Examples: Airbnb's founders posted a forward-looking growth graph on the fridge, desks and bathroom mirror. Facebook created a prestigious growth group when growth slowed.
- Keep a list of what blocks growth. For each decision ask "Is this the best way to optimize growth?" (a conference usually isn't).
- Share metrics and financials internally; companies focused on metrics do better. Avoid vanity metrics: signups without retention. Retention matters as much as acquisition.
- Keep a "drumbeat" of features, customers, hires, revenue milestones and partnerships. Set aggressive but borderline achievable goals, review monthly, celebrate wins.
- Fast growth with everything broken is good: fix it and get more growth. Friendster is his one example of dying from technical debt. His favorite investments are fast-growing but un-optimized companies.
- Only think about 10x your current scale; figure out massive scale when you get there. Early great customer service creates passionate users and shows what to fix. Bad unit economics early are OK only with a real reason they'll work out later.
- Remind the team that humans are bad at exponential intuition, and every giant company started small.
- Growth traps that "effectively never work": deals with other companies and the big press launch. Instead build a loved product, recruit users manually, test many channels (ads, referral programs, sales and marketing) and do more of what works. Ask customers where to find more people like them.
- Sales and marketing "are not bad words." At least one founder must get good at asking people to use the product and pay. For B2B, track monthly revenue growth; early months look ugly because of long sales cycles (selling to startups first can help). He recommends Alex Schultz's consumer growth lecture.

## Making money, pricing and unit economics

- Get people to pay more than it costs to deliver; "people always forget" the delivery cost.
- Free product: don't plan to grow by buying users (very hard for ad-supported). Make something people share.
- Paid product with LTV under $500: you generally can't afford sales. Test SEO/SEM, ads, mailings, and try to repay CAC in 3 months.
- Paid product with LTV over $500 (net to you): you can generally afford direct sales. Sell it yourself first to learn what works; he recommends the book Hacking Sales.
- Reach "ramen profitability" fast, so you control your destiny and aren't at investors' whims. Watch cash flow obsessively; founders have run out of money without noticing.

## Focus, intensity and what the CEO does

- Two words: focus and intensity. Best founders say no a lot and don't start the next thing until they've dominated the first. "You can do far fewer things than you think." Doing too many wrong things is a common cause of death.
- His own routine: a daily pen-and-paper list with about 3 major and 30 minor tasks, plus an annual list of goals.
- Great founders are decisive: listen to all advice, then decide fast. Use Paul Buchheit's rule: 90% of the value for 10% of the effort. "The market doesn't care how hard you work—it only cares if you do the right things."
- "I have never, not once, seen a slow-moving founder be really successful." Obsessing over quality while moving fast is a tell of a great founder. Don't believe you're exempt; rocket and nuclear companies still move fast.
- Early success: skip networking events, panels, publicists and "personal brand." Press eventually finds who is winning.
- CEO jobs: set vision and strategy; evangelize the company; hire and manage, especially where you have gaps; raise money; set the execution quality bar. Also stay engaged in the part you love.
- It's intense and not balanced: you can have one other big thing (family, triathlons). Be responsive, decide quickly on things that block others, do unpleasant schleps. Still eat, sleep and exercise; this takes years.
- No excuses ("if only we had more money"); feel upset for a minute, then solve it. Distort reality for others but not yourself: be persuasive outwardly and paranoid inwardly. Be persistent and optimistic. Find CEO peers and a mentor (books work less well).
- Write mission and values down early; he praises Airbnb's cultural values. Don't innovate in HR, marketing, sales, financing or PR; do what works there and spend creativity on the product. Don't hire an "experienced manager" to do the work; it's "a graveyard for failed companies."

## Hiring, managing and competitors

- First advice: don't hire. Employees are expensive, add communication overhead and inertia, and change the things you can say. Don't derive self-worth from headcount. The best people want rocketships, so you can hire them once you're winning.
- Be generous with equity, trust and responsibility, and go after people you think you can't get.
- After product-market fit, spend about 25% of time recruiting; the CEO's top activity by time.
- Never compromise on quality, even when desperate; mediocre early hires rarely recover. "If you have doubt, then the answer is no." Skip chronically negative people. Value aptitude over experience; try a project together before hiring full-time.
- Managing: avoid "hero mode" (doing everything yourself); accept late projects to keep a functioning team. Keep everyone in one office. Fire quickly, and fire toxic people however good: "Culture is defined by who you hire, fire, and promote."
- Competitors are "a startup ghost story": "99% of startups die from suicide, not murder." Ignore them 99% of the time, especially when they raise money or get press, until they beat you with a real shipped product. He quotes Henry Ford on fearing the competitor "who never bothers about you at all."

## Fundraising

- Raise when you need it or when it's available on good terms. Stay frugal: "having too much money is almost always bad."
- The secret is a good company; optimizing the process matters about 5% of the time. Investors want companies that will succeed regardless but grow faster with capital, and returns come from big wins. Always explain why you could be huge.
- Don't raise before you're in shape; it burns reputation. "Believe the no but not the reason," and anything but "yes" is a "no."
- Run conversations in parallel; investor fear of losing the deal drives action. One founder fundraises. Metrics convince most.
- Insist on clean terms; don't over-optimize valuation. The first check is hardest, so focus on whoever loves you most. Always have a plan that includes raising nothing.
- Pitch minimum: mission, problem, product/service, business model, team, market and growth rate, financials. Each round's bar is higher.
- Small-check investors usually don't help (beware party rounds). A great, involved board member is worth a lower valuation. He recommends Paul Graham's fundraising essay.
