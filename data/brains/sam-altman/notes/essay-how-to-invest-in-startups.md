## Sam's three-part model for investing in startups

- Sam says doing well as an investor takes three things: get access to good opportunities, make good decisions about what to back, and get those companies to choose you.
- He admits the best investments "will do quite well without you".
- He notes the market is hard for investors. Supply and demand have pushed valuations up and the best deals are flooded with interest. A friend told him it is easier to raise LP money for a seed fund than to get a meaningful allocation in a hot deal.

## Getting access to deals

- Sam calls access the easiest of the three: "you can just work hard". He says most investors don't, which is a bug to exploit.
- Networking works if it isn't just trying to grab coffee when people have time. Help investors you respect and help good founders, and deal flow follows.
- For a new full-time investor, he suggests making your job helping your future sourcing network. Instead of asking contacts for deals, offer to spend a day a week helping their best company.
- Early-stage investors can help with closing candidates, future fundraising, customer introductions and generic advice.
- A brand is the other route to access. He favors long-form writing (hard, few do it well) over tweeting (easy, everyone does it fine).

## Picking founders

- Great founders are the key to great startups. One way to win is to predict who will be great before they are, because the market rewards finding great but inexperienced people. Proven founders cost more in share price.
- Meet people in person, several times. If someone meets you three times in three months and visibly improves each time, pay attention. Rate of improvement often matters more than current ability.
- His main question: would I work for this person? The second: can I imagine them taking over their industry?
- Traits he looks for:
  - scrappy and formidable at once
  - mission-oriented, obsessed, relentless
  - extremely smart (necessary, not sufficient)
  - decisive, fast-moving, willful
  - courageous, high-conviction, willing to be misunderstood
  - strong communicators and infectious evangelists
  - able to become tough and ambitious
- Toughness and ambition can change quickly. Speed seems harder to change: people tend to be slow or fast movers. He has "almost never made money investing in founders who do not respond quickly to important emails".
- The successful founders he funded believed they were eventually certain to succeed.

## Picking markets

- Startups can win wherever costs can be low and cycle time fast. They do especially well in industries with rapid technological change, because their advantages over large competitors are speed and focus.
- He cares about a market's growth rate and eventual size, not its current size. He doesn't know why most investors fixate on current size, and sees it as an opportunity.
- The best companies lead the market by a couple of years. To tell a real trend from a fake one, check whether the few early users use the platform a lot and love it. His example: the iPhone was derided for few users in its first year or two, but owners raved about it as they never had about earlier smartphones.
- The very best companies ride a new, important, fast-growing platform.

## Traits of the best companies and the power law

- The best companies he invested in usually share most of these:
  - compelling founders
  - a mission that attracts talented people
  - a product people spontaneously tell friends about
  - a rapidly growing market
  - a network effect and low marginal costs
  - the ability to grow fast
  - a product that is fundamentally new or 10x better than the alternatives
- Limit yourself to opportunities that could be $10 billion companies if they work, meaning at least a fast-growing market and some pricing power. Failures and small successes matter little; "the giant returns are where everything happens". He admits he has violated this many times.
- His central career learning: "you can and should scale up things that are working." He thinks about the potential energy of future scale for every investment, and sees most people as terrible at this.
- For early-stage investing, good ideas mostly help identify good founders. His hardest chronic mistake to correct is investing in bad founders with good ideas. His second is chasing deals mainly because other investors like them.

## Winning the deal (close rate)

- The better the opportunity relative to valuation, the harder it is to be chosen. Traditional sales tactics work: spend lots of time with the founder, explain how you'll help, and have founders you've backed call them.
- Reputation matters most: being above-and-beyond helpful and accessible, founder-friendly, and above all having founders say "that person was my best investor by far". A strong brand also helps, since taking your money can make a company be taken more seriously.
- Decisiveness helps. Most investors wait for someone else to act, and founders appreciate fast, early decisions. His two recent examples:
  - telling previously backed founders he'd do their Series A before they finished describing the idea
  - offering to do the seed round of founders he'd never met at the end of a one-hour meeting
- He doesn't recommend that often, but says that when conviction is strong, let it show.
- The surest way to a poor close rate is not treating founders as peers. Founders sense whether you'll act like a peer or a boss, and acting like their boss fails "an intelligence test".

## Helping founders after you invest

- The most important help is making founders more ambitious, with bigger thinking and more self-belief. Set goals that are ambitious but achievable. Most people set goals just out of reach, which demotivates. He prefers continuously setting goals you can just barely hit, since momentum is self-reinforcing.
- Second is specific tactical advice rather than general strategy. His example: "it seems like you've figured out yourself how to do sales for this company, so here is where to look and what to look for in your first sales hire, and here is the sales tool you should use".
- Intros, hiring help, finding other investors or an office are good, but wait to be asked.
- Exception: proactively say so when you have very high conviction a founder is about to make a big mistake, especially once things work and they aren't set up to scale.
- He warns against trying to give founders new ideas. PG is a "sui generis idea generator", and even most great investors are bad at telling founders what to work on. Be self-aware.
- When founders call asking for vague help, they usually want emotional support from a friend. Invite them over, make tea or pour a drink, and listen.
