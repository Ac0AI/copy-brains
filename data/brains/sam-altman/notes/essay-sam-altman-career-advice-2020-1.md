## Sam's career principles: what to work on and how to think about success

- Sam says the most successful people, "judged by history, not money", keep looking for the most important thing they can work on, and then do it. They avoid local maxima and don't deceive themselves when they find something more important.
- They stay on it for a long time even if others don't get it. "'Important' does not mean 'popular.'"
- He advises finding "the thing that only you can do": something no one else can do, or hasn't thought of, in a way that is hard to copy. Being just like everyone else gets you "ok but certainly not great."
- Follow your own curiosity and look internally rather than externally for answers. Be honest about the intersection of your skills, your passions and what the world values.
- To have valuable ideas, understand the whole landscape of a field and ask what can emerge now that couldn't before.
- "Authentic, high-conviction vision is rare and valuable." Double down when you find it, or find people who have it.
- Optimize for being "spectacularly right some of the time, and low-stakes wrong a lot of the time". The best work you ever do is what counts.

## Scale, power laws and compounding

- Sam says you can almost always scale things up more than you think, and the benefits are "almost always bigger and more surprising than you think". This holds for technical systems and for companies.
- Scale benefits, network effects and the power law are so strong that people usually delude themselves into thinking otherwise. They are often the best way to make it hard for others to compete with you.
- Compounding "in all ways" is a very powerful force. Long-term outlooks and long-term commitments are the easiest way to outperform others.
- For an early-stage startup, compounding success means growth. "Growth takes care of a lot of problems", particularly hard internal ones.
- To get rich, he says the route is building something and owning equity, "not salary".

## Execution: willfulness, speed and hard work

- Sam says that if there is a single key to success, it is being able to make things happen in the world. He lists willfulness, determination, execution focus, not giving up at a roadblock, solving any problem that comes your way, and self-belief.
- "Almost everyone underestimates the value of fast movers, in almost every context." Work with them and be one.
- People at the top of almost any field worked very hard to get there. He says to be skeptical of anyone who claims you don't have to work hard for an exceptional career, unless they have one themselves.
- Doing good work requires being optimistic, exceptionally determined and intellectually curious. He advises having faith in a positive outcome.
- His closing rule is to focus on what matters and "cut all the BS".

## Getting people to bet on you, and who to surround yourself with

- Early in a career, getting someone to bet on you (hire, promote, invest) is very helpful. Sam's method is to first do whatever you can to help them.
- The second-best way to get people to help you is to be working on something interesting.
- Spend lots of time with people who constantly produce new ideas.
- Ignore the haters early. Don't become one or surround yourself with them. It's fun short-term, but they are "almost never successful at anything other than social media".
- Avoid office politics. He calls getting caught up in the parts of a job that don't matter "a dangerous trap" that many people fall into. Let others play political games.

## Managing stress, goals and your own productivity

- Low-stakes things should be low-drama. High-stakes things can be high-drama if they must be. Use your "stress budget" on the few things that matter.
- Goals should be ones you hit "but just barely". Always-too-stretched goals are demoralizing, and people want to be on the winning team. Write goals down, professionally and personally.
- To protect your time, commit one day per week to a long uninterrupted block to catch up on the last week and prepare for the next. For Sam this only works on a weekend, and some people use Fridays.

## Running a team: standards, values and meetings

- Set and maintain high standards. If you have to be hard on people, do it "with love and a genuine wish for them to improve". Praise people when they hit the standard.
- Strong teams have a lot of diversity of thought but "do not have much diversity of values or goals".
- Have long staff meetings and short 1:1s, which is better for information flow and alignment.
- Don't overschedule. Avoid long meetings except for really important topics, and then make them very long.
- Avoid large meetings. If you need one, make part of it written, and ask whether an email can replace it.
