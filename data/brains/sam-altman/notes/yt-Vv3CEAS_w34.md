## Startups win when the ground is shifting, so build for where models will be

- Sam says startups have their biggest edge "when the ground is shifting the most" and when costs and cycle times are falling fast. That is happening in many places at once.
- He says a 10-week-old startup today can look like what used to take a year. He met a roughly 2-week-old startup that had rebuilt an office productivity suite (documents, presentations, spreadsheets) for AI as a first-class user. A 10-week-old startup that looks like one from 10 years ago "is in bad shape."
- He is surprised that more founders do not take on the "crazy thing" with the new tools. Most pick "AI agents for enterprise vertical X." That will work and be competitive, but he thinks it probably won't be the era's defining startup.
- His central advice to founders: internalize that scaling laws will continue. Plan for what is not possible or economical this month but will be in 2 or 4 years. It is fine to start on things that need smarter or cheaper models.
- He argues the market has not adapted to this, just as it still under-bets on high-growth young founders. He calls that "free money left in the market."
- He thinks a startup today still mostly looks like one from 10 years ago because that is the received wisdom. Hiring fewer people and spending more on tokens for Codex "doesn't seem like enough." He has met a few people building startups in a completely different way.

## Trusting exponentials and operating in chaos

- Sam says he has developed "a great trust in exponentials" in people, companies and models. Plotting where someone is when he meets them, then checking their progress later, builds that trust.
- He says handling chaos can't be taught, only learned through reps. You need emotional peace with "this is not going to be the thing that kills you and you don't quite yet know how you're going to solve it."
- He calls this a real weakness of young founders. At YC office hours he could tell first-batch founders from experienced ones by their emotional state when talking about a problem. They learn it eventually, but "at great pain and cost and unforced errors."
- The first company-killing event feels like the world is ending. By the tenth, it's much less bad.
- He reframes bad days. The opposite of a bad experience is no experience, so he tries to be grateful for them. He cites Naval: with a fast-forward button on your life, "your life would be over."

## Planning: a few convictions, planned forward

- Sam does not plan backwards from 20 years out. He holds "a small number of strongly held convictions about the future" as directions to head in, then plans forward from the current state: what to do now and this year. Some things need a 5 to 10 year horizon.
- He thinks many people hold too many beliefs and a rigid worldview. They end up chasing, like "space companies turn into AI companies."
- His advice is to deeply believe a few things, stay flexible on the rest, and stay true to the core.
- Critical path for him has been clear for a long time: abundant intelligence, with broadly shared prosperity and no power concentration. He has not been tempted to reconsider the goal.

## Mission-driven scope and supply chain alignment

- A clear mission plus a deep understanding of the problem point to what to work on. You still sometimes over-expand or under-reach.
- OpenAI's mission is to make AI extremely abundant, cheap and powerful, and to put it in everyone's hands. That means building the platform: chips, energy, data centers, robots. He says OpenAI has "no desire" to build every vertical on top of it. He wants a decentralized economy where OpenAI produces "units of intelligence."
- He names the biggest bottleneck as "Transistors and then electrons in that order."
- To keep suppliers on OpenAI's timeline, he talks to them a lot. He doesn't just demand delivery dates. He shows core suppliers the upcoming model, what it enables and the research behind it, so they believe in the mission. Then he aligns incentives as much as he can.
- On the supply chain, OpenAI brought chip design and model design together. He doesn't think it needs to own electron production, which is a commodity. Execution means doing whatever step the problem requires. Financing a fab build-out, assembling a chip team and running the supply chain are each a different approach.
- He thinks there is too much focus on "algorithms that create better algorithms" and not enough on data centers that can build more data centers. A data center's thinking power could drive robots to make more data centers.
- He sees the joint stock corporation as the key invention of the industrial revolution. It aligned incentives, gave liability protection and let people pool capital.

## Focus: kill good projects to scale the one that works

- Sam says killing a failing project is easy. The hard part is killing good projects when one thing is working very well.
- Examples: when GPT-3 worked, OpenAI shut down robotics work. When coding agents worked, it shut down Sora and the browser. Sora "would have been super successful," but compute and energy were better spent on coding agents.
- The call is "a somewhat gradual realization," not a single meeting. People accept it because they understand the mission and the stakes, even if they are unhappy in the moment.
- He admits his biggest ambition miss was under-investing in compute. He was "psyched out by the financial markets," and says he won't make that mistake again.
- He expects three waves of form factors. The first was chatbots. The second is coding agents. The third, coming soon, is persistent agents (chiefs of staff, co-workers).

## Asking for the hard thing: the Codex example

- Sam's rule is to ask for what you want. You don't always get it, but when you do, amazing things can happen.
- Codex is his example. OpenAI was way behind Claude Code, and the consensus was that you don't try to win a category when someone else has momentum. OpenAI judged coding strategically critical, partly because of its role in recursive self-improvement (RSI) and its economic value. It asked a team to take on an extremely hard mission, and they delivered something "very rare in the history of business." He says it is now the product most of the best coders he knows use.
- To learn a new area fast, he finds experts and talks to them, and reads as much as he can. He says experts are willing to help if you ask.
- He says people overestimate the risk of most actions. When someone calls a decision high or low risk, he asks them to say why out loud. That often gets past their intellectual blocks, since people are usually wrong one way or the other.
- Buying a lot of compute was a decision he held extremely high conviction on.

## Leadership, hiring and building ambition

- Sam says organizational pace is "90% the people you put in leadership roles." He asks whether each is a fast mover or a slow mover. Operating rhythms and management techniques matter less.
- He thinks executives should mostly be promoted internally. When hiring externally, he spends a lot of time talking to the candidate, reference checking, and trying to work together casually first.
- He rejects the meme that you can only hire in areas you deeply understand. He says he isn't strong at product or design but can tell Jony Ive is great within 30 minutes.
- He believes in getting better at your strengths. Obsessing over fixing weaknesses is a "huge trap."
- Some skills can be learned but not taught. Asking someone to explain them doesn't work. Studying them closely and sitting in meetings with them does.
- To raise ambition, he says corporate-background founders have "catastrophically low" ambition because they were punished for thinking big. The fix is "small repeated wins": try something that feels too ambitious, see it work, then try again. He doesn't give inspirational speeches.
- He admits he has gotten worse at patiently talking people through decisions and wants to go back to "let's really talk it through."
- He texts or talks with 300 to 400 people a day. He calls it a bad habit but says it gives him huge short-term context, such as knowing about an impending research breakthrough that changes a customer or supplier decision.

## Design, real vs fake trends, and ChatGPT's launch

- From Jony Ive, Sam learned that great design is "way more about understanding the problem than the flash of insight." Rushing toward or locking into a solution gives worse results. Ive studies a domain exhaustively before designing.
- He tested TikTok while building the Sora app. It pulled him in for hours, so he deleted it. He also turned off most notifications. He doesn't want to build products that absorb time without benefit.
- He says real trends differ from fake ones by persistence: people design their lives around the product instead of leaving it on a shelf (VR is his example of a fake one). He developed this by watching many startups at YC and analyzing the data.
- Reading ChatGPT's launch: usage rose to a higher peak each day, and the researchers called it a flash in the pan. Sam recognized from YC the "spectral signature" of organic growth. On day five it crossed a million users, and he knew OpenAI was about to become a big company very fast.
- Running the research lab and running the product company felt like "two almost completely unrelated jobs." The research lab didn't prepare him for the product company.
- On the 28-country, 35-day world tour, he got on planes in "marginal situations," following Brian Chesky's advice. He prefers bundling international travel into 7 to 10 day chunks instead of spreading out one-day trips.
