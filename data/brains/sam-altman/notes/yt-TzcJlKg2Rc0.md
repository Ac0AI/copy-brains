## Selecting founders: what Sam looks for at YC

- Sam observes that most great founders come from stable, upper-middle-class backgrounds, not the very poor or the very rich. He calls this "a real shame" but says you need enough stability, a great education and loving parents to compound through early life long enough to start a company.
- YC interviews started at 40-45 minutes (his own batch-one interview) and were cut to about 10 minutes. He thinks 5 minutes would reach decisions "90 percent or 95 percent as good", but says that isn't socially acceptable.
- Much of what matters shows up in the first few minutes: how someone thinks and how they react when pushed on their idea.
- A good interview feels like the company's first office hours: "let's talk together about how to build this company into something really big."
- Open contempt between cofounders is a sign to "walk away slowly". He says there are no secret physical tells.
- The trait he looks for most is clear communication. About 95% of a CEO's job is communicating and evangelizing: hiring, retaining, selling and fundraising. "If you cannot clearly communicate an ambitious vision we will probably not fund you," and it is hard to fake.
- YC has a cottage industry of interview coaching, but Sam says they can usually tell who has used it, and it doesn't help.

## Age, work ethic and honesty about the founder life

- YC's best investments have mostly been founders in their mid-20s to mid-30s. The same batch once included a 15-year-old, with parents signing documents, and a founder around 78-80.
- Age matters less than evidence of excelling at every step: "whatever hand life dealt you, you have done as well as anyone we can imagine." He grades a 40-year-old harder than a 20-year-old.
- He says age is often shorthand for willingness to work extremely hard. It is "a real disservice" to pretend you can build a hyperscale startup without making it the priority of your life for some period.
- YC tries to be upfront about this. The only time founders regret YC is when they feel they weren't told how much they would have to sacrifice.
- "One of the worst things that can happen to you is that your company succeeds when you're really young." It takes over your life more than people believe.

## YC's real innovations and the funding climate

- Sam says YC has had maybe five total innovations. Two he names:
  - Anyone in the world can apply, and YC puts in the effort to find the good ones. Before YC, you needed a business school pedigree, a business plan, experienced managers and a warm intro from "the right economic class".
  - YC publishes all its advice and keeps nothing secret.
- Together these increased the number of good companies in the world that people decide to start.
- Most of YC's current growth is outside the US. One success in a new market, such as Rappi from Bogota, sends a flood of applicants from that country. He thinks YC has "an easy 10x" of growth ahead over 10-20 years.
- On funding, if he could control the market he would not have YC companies raise millions or tens of millions at the end of YC. He says that happens without enough diligence or discipline on price, and it hurts companies in their next round. He expects capital to normalize over time.
- A question he now asks early is how the company will concentrate enough really good people. Capital is abundant, so "capital concentrated talent" is the scarce resource.
- On the next Silicon Valley, he says there will never be another one in a single place; talent will diffuse everywhere.

## Sam's self-assessment and who he relies on

- He says his main strengths are thinking further into the future than most people and appreciating exponential curves more than most.
- He calls himself a good leader but a bad manager. He doesn't want daily one-on-ones about feelings. He prefers "there's this interesting thing, let's go figure out how to get it done."
- He says YC's success was mostly Paul Graham and Jessica Livingston's work and that he "was basically handed a thing that all I had to do was not screw up".
- He says that as you become prominent, people filter and pre-plan what they tell you. He therefore relies on a small set of people for honest life advice: his parents, his partner, and Paul and Jessica.
- His mom pushed him to pick the computer science path he wanted over a full-ride scholarship elsewhere.

## OpenAI's mission, business model and capped-profit structure

- OpenAI's goal is building safe AGI. Sam believes some group will build a system smarter than humans in every way within decades, and it could quickly become a million or billion times more capable. He says the timeline debate is a distraction.
- The company had about 90 employees and no revenue, and no current plans for it. The "soft promise to investors" is that once they build a generally intelligent system, they will ask it to figure out how to generate a return.
- He says capital needs are unknown: "billions for sure, tens of billions maybe, hundreds of billions I sure hope not." Funding had mostly come from individual investors, and he expects institutions as capital needs grow.
- The first-round investors' cap is 100x. Later rounds get a lower cap, and he hopes it "will come down to hopefully 1x near the end".
- His reasoning is that if AGI works, a group could "capture the light-cone of all future value in the universe", which one group of investors should not own. A 100x return from a seed-stage bet seemed fair to him.
- Hiring is hard because they are "badly outgunned financially" by big tech. The pitch is the mission, working on behalf of the world, and the thrill of discovery rather than optimizing ad clicks.
- OpenAI's differentiators, in his words, are that they "do the best research in the world" and "care the most about how it gets deployed".
- At this stage, all work is pure research: reinforcement learning, robotics, video games, and unsupervised learning such as a text model that learns to predict the next word without labels.
- He believes the best route to solving climate change is to build strong AI first. He is open to working with the military depending on conditions.

## Staged release of GPT-2 and AI risk

- OpenAI did not release the full GPT-2 weights and instead released increasingly large models in stages. He says society hasn't built "antibodies" for fake text the way it has for photoshopped images.
- The academic ML community objected because it went against the norm of open sharing. Most of the rest of the world found it reasonable.
- He expects to release less and less as systems approach general intelligence, and says people will be upset. He argues "I'd much rather be a little bit too early than a little bit too late" with something you can't un-push.
- He says much of AI progress will come from scaling systems bigger and bigger. The criticism that GPT-2 was just 10x compute is, in his view, partly true.
- On press attention, he says OpenAI produces approachable, interactive blog posts and demos, but doesn't call journalists. He was caught off guard by GPT-2's attention.
- His near-term risks are autonomous weapons and cybersecurity, which he calls "eye-wateringly bad and not taken particularly seriously". He says systems get roughly one order of magnitude more powerful per year.
- He believes the tech industry has "failed the good faith test" on assuming it will be thoughtful, so OpenAI must show it will be.

## Economic growth, immigration and policy views

- He says "democracy only works in a growing economy" and that most people are not enamored with the tech industry as winners win bigger.
- He believes only something on the order of general AI's impact can restore global economic growth equitably.
- To stay less insulated, he travels and stays in touch with people outside the Silicon Valley bubble.
- If he could pick one policy for the global economy, it would be to fix immigration. Immigrants are a high share of tech founders, and concentrating talent in the US has been a major advantage.
- On facial recognition, he is more concerned than not and says details matter. He thinks society needs a real conversation about how privacy and speech rights should evolve when everything is digitally captured.
