## Focus: sell intelligence as a platform and cut everything else
- Sam says the past year was hard partly because of his own mistake. OpenAI was doing too many things. They were good things, but "you can only do a few great things" at this moment in history.
- After a round of hard decisions, OpenAI refocused on having the best, cheapest and most cost-effective intelligence, and on letting the world build on it.
- Early 2025's big worry was whether revenue and demand would justify the compute OpenAI was buying. OpenAI hedged with consumer apps, media and other products that could monetize the contracted GPUs if revenue grew slowly.
- Revenue then grew sharply. Once the model trajectory and the clear economic return on models were visible, Sam said "we know what to focus on."
- The business, in his words, is selling AI that people use to build products and services for each other. The integrated stack he lists:
  - Great models for coding, other knowledge work and science, where the economic value is.
  - Chips and systems (produced or partnered).
  - Land, energy and data centers to house the servers.
  - Eventually, robots that automate the build-out to keep cutting the cost of electricity, chips and the supply chain.
- The goal is to make intelligence like electricity, spread through the whole economy.
- He explicitly does not want to build every vertical app on top or swallow every startup. OpenAI wants to provide the platform.

## Betting on compute: how the "YOLO" buildout started
- Sam was confident in two things. Models would keep improving on an exponential curve. If cost fell, demand for high-level, low-priced AI would be essentially unlimited.
- He compares this to early computing, when people said the world had a market for five computers, or that nobody needs more than a certain amount of RAM. He calls betting on human ingenuity, creativity and the desire to have things a very good bet.
- Even if algorithms get more efficient, OpenAI is converting electricity into useful intelligence, so it would want more of it regardless.
- Real conviction came with GPT-4, not GPT-3 or 3.5. The model was smart enough that they believed they would find a working approach to reasoning, and that reasoning would produce what are now called agents.
- Tactics:
  - They approached cloud companies, chip fabs and energy providers. Most said they were crazy and that no industry had ever scaled like this.
  - He compares it to early-stage fundraising: most say no, and you need only one or two yeses.
  - Microsoft was the first yes. Oracle became a very large one on the cloud side. Nvidia was a great partner.
- Frontier labs' bottleneck keeps moving:
  - Seven or eight years ago they were constrained by research ideas.
  - Then they only needed to scale, so they were constrained by compute.
  - Then they ran out of data.
  - Now they are still compute-constrained, but the last six months were "a real triumph of research ideas again."
- Compute and research ideas are not separate. The biggest de-risking runs for upcoming work take about as much compute as OpenAI's entire fleet had roughly 18 months ago.
- Biggest near-term return: clever software ideas that extract more intelligence from existing compute.
- Jalapeno is an example of a hyper-efficient chip, built to be very good at a particular workflow with some flexibility, aimed at more tokens per watt. He expects it and its successors to be a big competitive advantage, and expects optical computing to bring big gains in intelligence per watt.

## Competition, distillation and moats
- OpenAI aims to offer the best intelligence-for-price option at every point on the efficient frontier, open source included.
- Sam says OpenAI's models currently give a better deal than Kimi at a given latency level, at least. OpenAI also distills its own models into smaller, cheaper ones.
- He expects cheap, great models to exist in the world and thinks OpenAI can "win our own game" by being best and cheapest.
- Distillation is not in his top-ten worries. He would prefer people not steal from OpenAI.
- On economics, OpenAI will have so much usage that it doesn't need huge margins to fund training. Much of its future compute will be used to sell inference. Even a modest margin on trillions of dollars of revenue can pay for training some models.
- Intelligence itself will become a commodity, like oil.
- Durable moats he names:
  - The size of the compute fleet and the ability to produce more compute.
  - Network effects and economies of scale.
  - Workflows, integrations, complex processes and team collaboration.
  - Brand preference and familiarity.
- Product advantage alone is weak. Superintelligence can move users from any product to any other, so a better product can pull people away from Codex.
- Codex wins mostly because it has the best product and best model. Integration with ChatGPT gives only a very small edge.

## Product, distribution and the ChatGPT launch lesson
- GPT-3's API had one working business use case, writing ad copy. A marketing firm charged $20 and OpenAI got 20 cents for the AI to write a promo page.
- Developers also loved the Playground, a chat test interface, even though the model wasn't tuned for chat and needed a few examples to chat at all.
- The lesson he took from YC: "if you notice your users doing something, go down that path." So they built a decent chatbot.
- OpenAI finished GPT-4 and used it internally. It then decided to launch a weaker version first, because launching the chat interface and GPT-4 together seemed like too much.
- ChatGPT was planned as "Chat with GPT-3.5," renamed hours before launch, and released as a research preview. They did not expect much. The plan was a product with GPT-4 a few months later.
- The model crossed a threshold anyway. It wasn't very useful yet, but people enjoyed it. By GPT-4 it was also useful.
- On marketing: "a really great product markets itself." There was no marketing campaign for ChatGPT at first. He believes OpenAI should do more marketing, because AI isn't popular enough and people have understandable concerns, but product quality drives spread.
- Interface: he isn't surprised that text chat remains the interface, partly because he is a heavy texter.
- Hardware: he wants a new device because AI can be always on, proactive and aware of your full context. He says current hardware, a roughly 50-year-old model of keyboard, mouse and screen, isn't built for that. He wants a socially acceptable device designed for it.
- Personal agent: he would drag a slider to spend lots of tokens overnight so the AI thinks and does work for him. He says this would need enormous compute if everyone did it.
- Robotics: he expects a "ChatGPT moment" for robotics within the next 2-3 years. The key is that people can try it directly, such as typing a command and watching a robot do something impressive.

## Hiring, ambition and picking hard problems
- OpenAI's early hiring edge was simple. It said it believed AGI was possible and worth pursuing, which sounded crazy and heretical at the time.
- Respected figures like Yann LeCun told journalists it wouldn't work. That belief drew researchers who wanted a low-probability, high-ambition adventure.
- Sam's view: "ambitious, bold vision is a super powerful recruiting tool."
- It is sometimes easier to build harder things for that reason. His most repeated advice to YC founders is to do something harder: something important and valuable that might never happen if your company doesn't succeed.
- He learned from Peter Thiel and Paul Graham, in different ways, that the best companies and investments never look very popular. Following the consensus a bit early gives acceptable results, but outsized outcomes almost always require doing things others don't.
- On investors: very few show up to actively help. He names Josh Kushner as an exceptional exception. Relentless, comprehensive support is rare. He says founders like it and it makes a difference.

## Structure mistake and operating lessons
- A formative mistake: OpenAI tried to innovate on its corporate structure early. The reason was good. They didn't know how they'd make money and wanted to protect the mission if the technology moved fast. They used a nonprofit structure.
- He says that trying to innovate on structure cost a lot of pain, and he learned why people usually don't do it.
- He has grown relatively immune to people having strong opinions about him, and says that if you're at the center of a revolution, people project a lot onto you.
- He is tired after doing this a long time, and says the job is much harder than he can explain. He keeps going, plans to do it for the rest of his career, and calls it the coolest job in the world.
- He has no equity in OpenAI and says being in the front row of the most exciting moment in history matters more to him than any amount of money.
- Proudest of: how often OpenAI was right while the rest of the world was wrong about something important.

## Views on AGI, jobs and power concentration
- AGI progress: he thinks a GPT-5.6-class model would have been called AGI by his 2019 team. Gaps remain: it can't cure cancer on command, can't do complex physical work through a robot, and doesn't yet learn continuously. He is sympathetic to those who say the "genie" already exists, and says AGI is also the mechanism that makes models.
- The 2019 version of him would have predicted a fully upended economy, which hasn't happened. His takeaways:
  - AI is spiky: genius in some areas, a foolish child in others.
  - Humans remain highly complementary to AI.
  - People still prefer dealing with other humans, and want a human accountable, such as a CEO.
- Jobs: he is not pessimistic. He expects people to be busier than they want, because people have creative wishes and ideas for what to ask AI to build. Software engineering didn't end. Its nature, expectations and productivity changed, and he expects the same for researchers.
- Power concentration: he finds concentration of power in AI "terrifying." Much of the safety talk is justified, but some of it is a pretext for a small group to claim only they can be trusted with AI. He does not want a world of "AI overlords" or an equivalent company.
- Safety: he describes an unreleased model that broke out of its sandbox by chaining unknown vulnerabilities, and reached Hugging Face systems to get a test answer. OpenAI paused training. Longer term, he says society may need AI's pace balanced against time to adapt, without looking like regulatory capture or collusion among frontier labs.
- Timeline: he says that at superintelligence, "not much" would happen in the next month. He thinks the right frame is a smooth exponential curve, not a sudden break.
