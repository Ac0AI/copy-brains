## Picking what to work on

- Sam looks for the intersection of three things: what you're good at, what you enjoy, and where you can create value for the world.
- Without some overlap of those three, he says, "it's hard to really have an impact."
- Most people "just fall into what they work on." Trying things is sometimes the only way to learn what you like, but he thinks the choice deserves upfront thought, because it decides how you spend most of your waking time.
- He refuses to name a specific field. People have to work out for themselves what they believe is high impact.
- His sequence: think hard about where you can contribute most, go meet people to work with, learn as much about the field as you can, then take a risk and focus on it.

## Finding people to work with

- Sam says finding your tribe is "really hard" and admits he has no perfect answer.
- Be willing to move. Most fields have pockets around the world where similar people do great work, and it's worth going to be near them.
- He defends YC asking founders to move to the Bay Area. Bringing people interested in startups together has been valuable for many of them.
- He has repeatedly helped people for no immediate benefit and with no intention of getting anything back. Years later it paid off, for example by letting him invest in their startup, which became a huge success, or by working together on OpenAI.
- Later in a career you are limited by how many good people you know and can work with. So help many people and spend time with many people, then see who is impressive and gets things done.

## How big things get done

- He loves a Charlie Rose quote: things get done through a combination of focus and personal connection.
- Sam adds a third factor, self-belief. You need deep conviction that the thing "a lot of people say is really stupid" will work and matter.
- Focus, personal connections and self-belief are how things get done in the world, in his experience.
- He wants people to be doers, not talkers. "History belongs to the doers."
- He criticizes people who dabble in many projects without going all in on one, and people who spend time on thought leadership conferences and organizing groups instead of building.

## Working hard early: compound interest

- Sam says he doesn't work as hard as many people, but he works pretty hard.
- Compound interest is his metaphor. If you get a bit better every day, week and year, and learn more and meet more people, the effect compounds.
- It is far better to put the time in at the start of a career, because you benefit for all the remaining years you work.
- He tried to meet every person he had time for and go to everything he could.
- The beginning of a career sets the trajectory for the rest of it. You shouldn't work all the time, since your 20s are your 20s, but you should work harder than most people think you should.
- He calls this one of the most valuable pieces of advice he never got.

## When to quit and what keeps you going

- Sam thinks most people give up far too early. The satirical version is the 23-year-old who has started 14 startups and quit each before it could succeed.
- Hard things take a long time and attract critics. YC also has the "trough of sorrow," where nobody even bothers to say it sucks because nobody cares.
- Most successful founders he has spent time with stayed on their idea for a long time, when many others would have quit.
- His framework: the decision to quit should be internal, not external. Users not using it, or people saying it's bad, is not alone a reason to stop. Pay some attention, since they might be right.
- The best entrepreneurs he knows stop "when you have run out of ideas and something is not working."
- Motivation that lasts comes from enjoying the work, an intense belief that it matters, and ideally liking the people you work with.
- He says it's fine to start out wanting money or fame. Within a few years, many people find a deeper mission. His own motivation has shifted so that money and press are no longer big factors. He now works only on things he finds interesting and important.

## Momentum, burnout and crises

- Sam used to believe burnout comes from working too hard. In his own experience it comes from failing and things not working.
- "Momentum is really energizing. The lack of momentum is super draining." He has almost unlimited energy for things that interest him and are working, and almost none for things that don't.
- Founders who fail often assume they lack energy or passion. His advice: shut the company down, go on vacation, and try again. Many then find huge energy when working on something that works.
- Each crisis gets less scary than the one before. Company-killing events usually get survived and the world doesn't end. "On the 19th major crisis you're like, well, I survived the first 18."
- He says you can't be taught this; you have to live through it.
- Ask for what you want. You'll get told no a lot, but sometimes it works.
- Entrepreneurs shoot themselves in the foot by not asking someone to quit their job and join them, or not asking a big company to do a deal. Being somewhat aggressive is an important entrepreneurial trait.

## Risk, conviction and the long term

- Sam says people have terrible risk calculus. They are wrong about what is risky, and most don't take enough risk, especially early on.
- Being young, unknown and poor is a gift for risk-taking that people don't capitalize on.
- Real risk is doing something you'll regret for the rest of your life. People regret what they didn't do more than what they did.
- Starting a company on an idea you're passionate about, even at the cost of a couple of years of steady income or being called a failure, is a good risk. The wrong risk is not committing because you overrate failure, reputation damage or embarrassment.
- If you fail, you'll be in about the same place you are today and can try the next thing.
- Time is one of the few arbitrage opportunities left in the market. Markets are good at high-frequency trading and short-term pricing, and worse at long-term value.
- His belief is that he should hold stock in the best companies he invests in "forever." Meanwhile he has held his grandma's birthday Apple shares and never sold one.
- Founders get paid for the wealth they create for others. The best companies create massive value and capture far less than they create, over a long period.
- A founder should pick something they can commit to for a very long time. Make that commitment to yourself and your team, because you earn more over the long horizon than from short-term moves.
- Strong opinions, flexible details: he distinguishes refinement from pivoting. Facebook kept the same core idea (a social graph and online directory) while the product changed a lot. He doesn't believe in startups on their 11th unrelated pivot.
- Successful people he knows hold "strong opinions strongly held about the future." They can be convinced by new data, but the bar is fairly high.
- He rejects the idea that the future is unknowable, and says it's okay not to be fully diversified in your investments, career or life.
