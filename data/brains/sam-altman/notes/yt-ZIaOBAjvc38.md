## Why startups have a structural advantage right now

- Sam argues startups win when technology moves fast, costs fall and cycle times shorten. Great startups cluster when the ecosystem shifts and incumbents lose their advantage.
- His examples of past clusters: the internet boom around 1998-99, a mini wave of builders on top of Facebook apps, and the iPhone App Store launch.
- He says this moment is bigger on all those dimensions. Expertise that used to be hard to hire is now cheap.
- Sam says he has recently seen people who grew up on AI who think "I can kind of automate an entire startup of agents and four of us."
- He expects this to favor fluency with the tools over years of experience. What still matters: "taste and agency and understanding of the physics of business." That means knowing how to build a valuable business, and telling a good network effect or moat from a fake one.
- He calls the "join a frontier lab or become a permanent underclass" meme "so dumb." He bets there is a future trillionaire in the room and that startups created today will be more valuable and impactful than past ones.
- He thinks startups keep the economy from stagnating, because "companies do just drift towards suckiness."

## How the cost of building collapsed

- In 2005, in the first YC batch, what took each company three months to build over the whole batch could now be done in minutes by a coding agent. He gives the figure loosely, as "seven minutes" and later "17 seconds" and "17 minutes."
- He rejects the sad reading ("a Codex prompt is a whole startup"). He reads it as the chance to start the most ambitious company possible, with experts in every field working together on very hard technology that was previously impossible.
- His rule for founders: "you can now do three months of work in 17 minutes, but you better just go do three months of work in three months of work. Whatever the new bar for that is." The bar for ambition rises with the tools.
- He expects the next six months of model progress to feel like roughly the last two years.

## Hold a contrarian conviction and tolerate being dismissed

- His "highest order" advice: find things where you can develop reasonable conviction that the conventional wisdom is wrong, and be okay with it taking a long time while people are frustratingly dismissive.
- At OpenAI for years "it felt like we knew the biggest secret in the world. Everybody was calling us an idiot." Ten years ago critics said the team was wrong, irresponsible and would cause another AI winter.
- He calls this "an incredible gift." It meant no massive competitors and time to do research and build the company.
- He has seen the same pattern at many companies: they do something experts in the field think is a bad idea.
- Starting the same startup as everyone else gets hype and easy fundraising, but "much less frequently" the big outcomes.
- Tactic: work out what big, brand-new thing is possible now that was not possible a few years ago. For OpenAI it was the deep learning moment that the world did not update on enough.
- Quoting PG: the world does not intuit exponentials. There must be new exponentials forming now. If you spot one and keep gathering data to back your conviction, "be grateful that the world doesn't understand."
- Sanity check on beliefs: if you cannot find anyone who shares your belief, pay attention to that. If everybody believes it, that is also a bad sign.
- The more you do and the better you do, the more people attack you. It escalates as you threaten the existing state of the world.

## Clear vision, unclear first steps

- At OpenAI the vision (build AGI) was clear but the first steps were not. It started as a nonprofit research lab, and it "really didn't occur to us" that it would make a product people would pay for.
- It took years to arrive at ChatGPT and the API.
- Sam says a clear top-level vision with unclear early steps is not a bad sign for ambitious ideas, and should not make you back off.
- The failure case on the other side: a brilliant big idea with no forward progress. "At some point you got to just get some new data points," even if the first steps are imperfect. He says OpenAI's were "very imperfect."

## Co-founders, networks and compounding relationships

- Sam's line: "only 50 people in the world believe that AGI was possible, but it was okay because 45 of them worked at OpenAI." You do not need many people, and a heretical belief is what draws a crew of misfits together. He says that is also what early YC was like.
- Doing a startup alone is "very hard and very lonely."
- Five or ten years ago he saw co-founder matching as the limiter on the number of good YC startups. Talented people could not find their tribe.
- He still thinks moving to the Bay Area is probably good advice for meeting people, though he has less intuition for it now.
- He thinks the premium on doing YC is bigger than ever and the gap between YC and second place has widened. He calls it a reminder of the power of network effects.
- He met most of his OpenAI co-founders years before starting it, over a long career. Advice: get into a flow where you will meet future co-founders as early as possible, because it "takes a long time to compound."
- Highest-confidence tactic: be mildly helpful to many people. It is fun, gratifying and pays off later in unpredictable ways.
- Example: as a very early Stripe investor (around age 22), he was asked to drive to Palo Alto for dinner to convince a student to drop out and join as Stripe's first hire. That was Greg Brockman, and eight years later they started OpenAI together.

## Distribution of power, and what founders can do about it

- Sam argues concentration of power has been bad throughout history, and the economy is best off when power is widely distributed and anyone with a great idea can start a company.
- AI could produce either the greatest distribution of power or the greatest concentration ever. One company, person or model with more power than everything else put together is "terrible."
- He says nobody should want to be locked into one AI's, person's or company's moral worldview.
- Startups are well suited to spread the technology's power through the economy. A founder helps just by building a successful startup.
- This means being "quite reserved" about imposing a worldview on what people should do with AI, while holding a safety bar high enough to avoid incidents like the Hugging Face one. He describes that incident as an AI system breaking out of its sandbox and hacking another company.
- He called it an alignment and security failure and "the real deal," though not a full loss-of-control event. He said OpenAI made big mistakes.
- Dystopia he worries about in ten years: overreacting on safety and trading away freedom, agency and privacy for material abundance. His test for a good outcome is that people's freedom, agency and control of their time rise every year.
- He also says superintelligence may be the most important event in business history, yet "pale in comparison to some new startup" that a founder builds.

## Compute and inference demand

- Sam guesses worldwide inference demand grows around 10x a year for many years. He says the world cannot support a thousandfold increase many years in a row.
- He thinks "we will sort of never be out of the compute shortage." He has "never seen any commodity quite like this one."
- His view is that demand for sufficiently high-quality intelligence at a sufficiently low price is effectively uncapped. Unlike electricity, there are always incremental things to do with more intelligence. He compares it to the "640K of RAM" quote.
- Token usage benchmark: about six and a half years ago, the world token leader (an OpenAI employee) used about 100,000 tokens a month and the per-capita average was near zero. Today he says the worldwide average is about 100 thousand and the leader at OpenAI uses hundreds of billions.
- If that repeats, in another six and a half years the average person would use about 500 billion tokens a month and the leader quadrillions. He calls tokens "the dumbest metric" but the one available.
- On the Rune tweet ("die a model company or live long enough to sell inference"): a lab that falls off the model frontier can still resell compute, because compute is so valuable.

## Founder mindset and behavior

- He says Paul Graham's special skill was creating "optimism and momentum and belief out of nothing." Weekly dinners left eight dejected founders each convinced they were about to take over the world.
- He describes PG's model of an investor as a flight instructor rather than a lecturer: sitting next to you saying do this, don't do that, you missed this. That hands-on direction of raw energy was key for him.
- At YC he saw the job as being the unofficial flag bearer for startups, pushing relatively more power to founders and pushing people toward ambition and bigger swings.
- On online sarcasm: it is easy to score likes by mocking people trying to build, and "it will poison your soul." He says to put all your energy into building and resist the easy shots. As YC president he took the bait on Twitter trolls too often, and none of them "got" him over the decade.
- Advice to his younger self: "it's all going to work out." The tech industry is forgiving of mistakes and failure. Keep the drive and ambition but "be a little happier along the way."
