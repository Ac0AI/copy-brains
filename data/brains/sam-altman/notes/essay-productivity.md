## Compounding and picking the right work

- Sam treats productivity as compound growth: "If you get 10% more done and 1% better every day compared to someone else, the compounded difference is massive." A small gain held over 50 years is worth a lot.
- He says picking what to work on is "the most important element of productivity and usually almost ignored." Speed in a worthless direction is worth nothing.
- He ends the essay on the same point: "productivity in the wrong direction isn't worth anything at all. Think more about what to work on."
- He warns against "productivity porn": people spend too much time perfecting their system and too little asking whether they're on the right problems. The right goal is "to allocate your year optimally, not your day."
- He leaves time in his schedule to think about what to work on. His methods are reading books, hanging out with interesting people, and spending time in nature.

## Conviction, learning and the people around you

- The most impressive people he knows hold strong beliefs about the world. If you always agree with whoever you spoke to last, that's bad.
- He says you will sometimes be wrong, but you should build the confidence to stick with convictions. That lets you be courageous when you're right about something important that most people don't see.
- He argues you can learn anything you want and get better quickly. It feels like "an unlikely miracle" the first few times, but you learn to trust it.
- Great work usually needs colleagues. Be around smart, productive, happy, positive people who don't belittle your ambitions.
- He avoids the opposite kind of people where he can: "the cost of letting them take up your mental cycles is horrific."
- You must both pick the right problem and do the work. There are few shortcuts, and in most fields the biggest prizes are heavily competed for, so important work usually takes working smart and hard.

## Working on what you like, and delegation

- He can't be productive on things he doesn't care about or like. He tries not to be in a position where he has to do them, by delegating, avoiding, or something else. Disliked work is "a painful drag on morale and momentum."
- Delegation rule: everyone is most productive doing what they like. Work out who likes, and is good at, which tasks, and delegate on that basis. "Do what you'd want other people to do for you."
- If you dislike what you're doing for a long period, seriously consider a major job change. Short-term burnout is normal, but if time off doesn't fix it, find work you're more interested in.
- He says he found work he likes so much he'd do it for free, which makes high productivity easy.

## Prioritization system and lists

- His system has three pillars: "Make sure to get the important shit done", "Don't waste time on stupid shit", and "make a lot of lists".
- He keeps lists of what he wants to accomplish each year, month and day. Lists focus him and reduce what he has to hold in his head. If he's not in the mood for one task, he can find another he's excited about.
- He prefers paper lists. They're easy to add to and cut, and usable in meetings without seeming rude.
- He re-transcribes lists often. This forces him to think through everything on the list and gives a chance to add or remove items.
- He skips categorizing or sizing tasks. The most he does is star the really important items.
- He prioritizes to generate momentum: the more he gets done, the better he feels, and the more he gets done. He likes to start and end each day with something he can make real progress on.
- He is relentless on his most important projects: "if I really want something to happen and I push hard enough, it usually happens."

## Saying no, meetings and scheduling

- He is ruthless about saying no and does non-critical things as fast as possible. He admits he likely takes this too far, e.g. being terse to the point of rudeness in email replies.
- He generally avoids meetings and conferences because the time cost is huge. He gets the most value from time in his office.
- He still keeps space for chance encounters and new people and ideas. An open network is valuable: roughly 90% of random meetings he takes are a waste, but "the other 10% really make up for it."
- He schedules most meetings for 15-20 minutes or 2 hours. The default one hour is usually wrong and wastes time.
- The first few hours of his morning are his most productive, so he lets no one schedule then. Meetings go in the afternoon.
- He takes a break or switches tasks when his attention fades.
- On valuing time: he's surprised by people who make $100 an hour yet spend a couple of hours on something they dislike to save $20.
- He overcommits slightly. He generally gets done what he takes on, and a bit too much makes him more efficient and trains him to avoid distractions. Overcommitting a lot is "disastrous."

## Physical factors: sleep, light, exercise

- He says the optimal setup differs per person, so you must experiment. His took a little time each week over a few years. He estimates doing all of it well makes him at least 1.5x more productive.
- Sleep is the most important physical factor for him.
  - He likes a sleep tracker; the only things he's consistent with are "set-it-and-forget-it", and he likes the Emfit QS+Active.
  - He wants a cold, dark, quiet room and a great mattress (he used the Tempur-Pedic Contour Elite). He resisted spending on a mattress for years, "which was stupid."
  - Not eating a lot in the hours before bed helps. Skipping alcohol helps a lot, though he won't do that all the time.
  - He uses a Chili Pad to stay cold, with the cooler unit outside his room because it's loud.
  - When traveling he uses an eye mask and ear plugs.
  - He says this is likely controversial: when he can't sleep he takes about a third of a normal dose of sleeping pills, or a very low dose of cannabis. He's a bad sleeper, especially traveling, and notes it has tradeoffs but so does poor sleep. He wouldn't recommend it to people who sleep well.
- Light: he uses a full-spectrum LED light most mornings for 10-15 minutes while catching up on email. It's "a ridiculous gain" and the one thing he'd tell people to try if they try nothing else.
- Exercise is second. After trying several programs for a few months each, the best was lifting heavy weights 3x a week for an hour, plus occasional high intensity interval training. It also makes him feel best overall.

## Nutrition, caffeine and workspace

- He rarely eats breakfast, getting about 15 hours of fasting most days (apart from an espresso on waking). He says it's contrary to most advice and probably not optimal for most people.
- Sugar makes him feel worst, so he avoids it and keeps junk food out of the house because he has little willpower for sweets. He also avoids foods that upset digestion or raise inflammation, like very spicy food.
- Caffeine: one big espresso on waking and one after lunch, roughly 200mg total a day. He tried other setups and this worked far better. He avoids other stimulants, but drinks more coffee if very tired and needing to finish something.
- He's vegetarian and takes methyl B-12, Omega-3, Iron and Vitamin D-3. He settled on the list after about a year of quarterly blood tests and retests every year and a half or so. He drinks many protein shakes, which he hates and wouldn't do if not vegetarian.
- Workspace wants: natural light, quiet, no interruptions unless he wants them, long blocks of time, and comfort. He has a desk with two 4k monitors but spends almost all his time on his couch with a laptop.
- He wrote custom software for annoying tasks he does often, and learned to type fast and use keyboard shortcuts.

## Motivation dips and not trading off life for output

- He has weeks-long stretches with no motivation, possibly nutrition-related. He hasn't found a fix beyond waiting and trusting the fog always lifts.
- He avoids people and situations that put him in bad moods.
- Don't neglect family and friends for productivity. He calls it "a very stupid tradeoff" and likely a net productivity loss because you'll be less happy.
- Don't neglect things you love or that clear your head.
