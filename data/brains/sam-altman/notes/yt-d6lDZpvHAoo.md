## Founding YC: breadth, speed and long time horizons

- Sam notes YC gets about 15,000 applications a year and funds about 200. He estimates roughly half of the applicants are fundable, high-quality companies. YC has not yet figured out how to fund 7,500 a year, but he says it needs to be able to fund 1,000+ per year.
- The YC model (speed of iteration, doing things quickly and cheaply) has worked beyond software. YC has funded four or five nuclear energy companies, plus rocket, quantum computing and synthetic biology companies.
- His stated vision is for YC to be a "Hub of innovation in the world". He wants to put more new innovation into the world than Google does, and he is flexible on how that looks.
- He says there is a big premium for being willing to hold an illiquid security for a long time. Most people try to trade around earnings and get less value than someone holding 10+ years.
- YC is not a trader. It uses its own money with no limited partners, so it can hold shares on a 20-year outlook, which a venture fund with a 10-year life cannot do.
- He has never sold a share in a company unless a founder asked him to (for example, to provide liquidity). He hopes YC still holds its Airbnb shares, bought six years earlier, another six years and well beyond.
- He calls this "infinite time Horizon" investing one of the secrets of YC's success, and the reason YC can back nuclear energy while most investors can't.

## Don't dismiss ideas as small or stupid

- Sam's pet peeve is journalists and others who never create companies complaining that YC and founders fund "small" companies.
- His examples: Google was mocked as a 13th search engine and not even a portal. Facebook was "a social network of college students who cares". Airbnb was mocked constantly: "who's going to like stay on like a air mattress in some stranger apartment", and he admits it didn't sound that appealing to him either.
- His reasoning: "exponential growth is this very powerful force", and people wrongly call an idea small or stupid because they can't see how it will evolve.
- His advice: ignore people who don't build anything, start whatever company you want, and "continue to pull at that thread and see where it goes". Sometimes it stays small and sometimes it becomes Google, Facebook or Airbnb.
- He avoids criticizing what others work on. His experience is that if you bet on smart people, they eventually figure out their way into the big idea.

## Where he wants more startups: energy, AI, synthetic biology

- Cheap energy: if he could snap his fingers to improve life for the poorest 3.5 billion people, it would be really cheap, clean, safe energy. He is uninterested in companies that compete with subsidies to be barely as cheap as oil or gas. Energy at "a penny a kilowatt" would be transformational, and most wars get fought over energy.
- Nuclear: he says nuclear's safety record is 1,000 to 10,000 times better than coal, yet people prefer to live next to a coal plant. His explanation is that people overweight theatrical extreme risk and underweight boring slow risk. He is convinced the 22nd century will be powered by nuclear energy of some sort, or solar and wind.
- Machine learning: it is working well and has a long way to go. YC had just agreed to fund a semi-company, semi-nonprofit doing AI safety research.
- Synthetic biology: a company he met that day is building a generic antiviral because the founders got scared of engineered viruses.
- His test for new startup areas is cost and cycle time. When both fall, a startup can exist where it couldn't before. Genomics is the example: sequencing costs fell faster than Moore's law, so a company can now diagnose infectious disease by sequencing every nucleic acid in a blood sample and matching it, which "becomes a computer science problem".

## What he looks for in founders

- Clarity of vision: founders who can say clearly in a few sentences what they do and why. He says the correlation with success is strong, probably because unclear communicators can't recruit, get press or raise money. A clear idea helps even if it doesn't sound great.
- Determination: the trait YC looks for most. Most startups fail in the first couple of years because the founders give up, so he looks for evidence they won't, from this company or other points in their lives.
- Passion over polish: a common failure case is an MBA with a polished idea and market research who picked it to make money. Better is someone like Brian Chesky who cares about the idea and is unpolished on details. Sam recalls telling Brian his first financial model was financially illiterate, and says it didn't matter.
- Intelligence: easy to judge in a 10-minute conversation. Determination is much harder to judge.
- Red flag: co-founders fighting in the room.
- His record is imperfect: "no one gets this right all the time and that's sort of why it's fun."

## Choosing between starting a company and joining one

- He uses two frameworks that can conflict: impact maximization and regret minimization.
- Regret minimization: if you'd regret for life not starting a particular company you're in love with, you might start it even if joining Airbnb as employee 30 would give more impact.
- Impact maximization: job satisfaction rises with impact on the company or world, and a huge success as an employee can beat a mild success as a founder.
- The hard part is weighing the two and getting the probabilities right.
- On picking what to work on, he says his famous post glossed over the question, and the "do what you're passionate about" advice is incomplete. His advice is to trust your own read of where the world is going in a domain you understand, shape your career toward it, and ignore what others say, which is the hardest part.
- Example: when he agreed to take over YC, people called it a joke and told him not to. He thought hard about how the world and the company would evolve, limited himself to four opportunities he understood well, and applied the two frameworks.

## Product first, then growth spend

- He argues great products win in the long run. Enduring companies win on product. Examples he gives are Apple, Google (Search, Gmail, Maps), Airbnb and Facebook.
- Weak products can survive for a while through sales and marketing or a monopoly (Microsoft for years), and you can get acquired two years in on good press. That does not create enduring value.
- He doesn't let anyone off the hook on sales and marketing: "if you build the best product in the world and don't get anyone to use it uh that still kind of sucks". Without it you don't get the flywheel going.
- This is why engineers and designers start winning companies: if you are bad at everything else but make a great product, you can still win as a startup.
- On burn: founders get in trouble by investing in growth before the product works. If users don't stick around, sales and marketing dollars are wasted.
- He says VCs push burn up too early. They earn a 2% management fee only on capital deployed, and they raise the next fund on marked-up valuations, so they want you to spend and raise again.
- His rule: "it's almost never the wrong choice to get the product great first and then ramp up the burn". Ramping first and praying works only occasionally ("sometimes lightning strikes").

## Listening to users

- Both A/B tests and one-on-one conversations have weaknesses: users are often bad at explaining what they want, and metrics can lie.
- North Star: make a product that a small number of users love rather than one that many users like (a "six out of 10"). It's much easier to go from a few users loving it to many than the reverse.
- Tactics: have lots of one-on-one conversations, still watch the metrics, identify your best users, find where more of them are, and work out how to make them like it more.
- He calls the common approach of interviewing users who churned ("why didn't you like this?") much weaker. Asking people who already use the product how to get them to use it more is "a 10x better strategy".

## Engineering culture and team size

- The thing most companies get wrong: talented engineers together easily produce bad interpersonal dynamics. Strong cultures don't let conflict fester and don't keep brilliant but toxic engineers.
- Companies pay lip service to putting people and work environment first, then drop it when the trade-off is a missed deadline versus a happy team.
- On founders asking how to make engineers work 15 hours instead of 9, he says to make them like the work and believe in the mission. Demanding more office hours is something only bad founders say.
- A good engineering culture is one where everyone believes the work will really matter and likes their environment and colleagues. Management mechanics like project sizing matter but are secondary.
- Team size: tools and languages are so powerful that work needing thousands now needs hundreds and soon dozens. Keeping the team smaller than anyone thinks possible, for a long time, makes culture better. He keeps the YC team intentionally small for this reason.
