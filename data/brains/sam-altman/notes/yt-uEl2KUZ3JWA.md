## Minimize cognitive load and check you're working on the right things

- Sam says minimizing cognitive load from distracting things that don't matter is "hard to overstate how important this is and how bad most people are at it."
- He warns that it is easy to spend a decade "incredibly busy and incredibly stressed" and feel like you're working hard, but not move forward. Common traps are urgent-but-unimportant work, office politics, and status and power games that "feel so fun and so important."
- He says he has a fixed budget of cognitive output per day. If it goes to unimportant things, he never reaches the important ones.
- Many people have told him they thought they did incredible work for 10 years but, in retrospect, it was in the wrong direction.
- His answer is to focus for a period, then step back and ask whether you're doing the right thing.
- He warns against the opposite mistake: the stereotypical 21-year-old dropout who says "I'm going to solve all the world's problems in three months" and, if it isn't done in three months, gives up and jumps to the next project. Check in that you're working on things that matter and on an appropriate time horizon.

## Annual review ritual and the explore-then-focus pattern

- Every December 31st, Sam takes a break from vacation and writes down what went well and what didn't in the past year. He then writes a to-do list for the next year. Knowing he'll do this, he stresses about it less the other 364 days.
- In downtime he asks himself whether he's being maximally useful and working on the right things. For YC, he asks whether it should do something totally different, and whether to throw everything out and rethink it.
- His core pattern:
  1. Take time to explore and try many things.
  2. Keep a beginner's mind, but trust your intuitions.
  3. Pursue many things "as cheaply and quickly as possible."
  4. Be very honest with yourself about what's working and what isn't.
  5. Cut what isn't working and focus down until you're on the one or two things that really work.
- He is skeptical of putting everything on one rubric. He follows his interests, expects most projects to fail, and doesn't care, "as long as the ones that work really work." OpenAI and YC really work. He says he has tried other things over the last five years that didn't work at all.
- He applies the same algorithm to OpenAI research. The goal is AGI, which is far off and hard, so people can work on anything that helps get there. They try lots of things, let people follow their intuitions, and put a lot more resources behind whatever starts working.

## The year off after selling his startup

- After selling his startup at about 26, Sam worked briefly at the acquirer, then took a year off. He calls it one of the two or three best career things he ever did.
- Staying unscheduled let him fly to another country for a conference on short notice.
- During the year he:
  - read many dozens of textbooks and learned about nuclear engineering, AI and synthetic biology;
  - got serious about investing (he had made about four angel investments before);
  - traveled widely and met people working on all sorts of things;
  - reconnected with friends and family;
  - helped interesting people who needed it. They taught him things or offered him the chance to invest in their startups later.
- Almost all of it didn't work out directly. Companies he invested in failed, and a few companies he got excited about starting turned out to be bad ideas. But "the seeds were planted for things that worked in deep ways later."
- It is socially hard in Silicon Valley, where status comes from your job. He calls it a privilege, but recommends it if you're between jobs.
- He says the common failure is chasing whatever is trendy. "The crowd is right sometimes," but following everyone else is usually a mistake.

## Being honest about what works, and what investing and poker teach

- Deciding what to commit to takes "brutal honesty" with yourself about which projects are working and which you'd like to convince yourself are working. Sam admits he has made this mistake many times and will again. He calls it a muscle you can build.
- It's hard because it means admitting failure. When he invests in a company he is convinced it will work, and once you've anchored on that, it's hard to say "I was so wrong."
- Investing in many companies is humbling. You learn to be majorly wrong very frequently, and that's fine "as long as the magnitude of the successes pays for all of the failures."
- The two things he says taught him most about business are poker and angel investing, and he recommends both.
- He played poker fairly seriously in college, including live games on the Peninsula. It funded his college living expenses, and he says he would have done it for free. He sees it as a way to learn about business, psychology and risk.

## Risk, ambition and who you spend time with

- Sam says things in life are rarely as risky as they seem. Most people are too risk-averse, so most advice is biased toward conservative paths.
- Real risk is looking back at the end of your career and thinking you wasted it. People are good at short-term catastrophic risk but bad at long-term chronic risk. His example: worrying about nuclear plant meltdowns but not about inhaling coal smoke.
- He spends time helping a small handful of people he thinks will do amazing things. He keeps pushing them on the same point: "you're thinking too small" and thinking about risk the wrong way.
- He says perspective shifts get harder as you get older, and keeping mental flexibility is valuable. You don't need drugs for it. You need to commit, and give yourself space, a format and people to do it with.
- He says surround yourself with people who make you more ambitious and more inquisitive. About 98% of people will pull you back, saying it's "too crazy, too out there, too ambitious," because "almost everyone wants you to be average."
- To learn about risk he recommends poker and biographies of people who did amazing things, especially big science and engineering projects (he cites the Apollo program).

## Productivity: physical foundations and simple systems

- His three pillars: get the important things done, don't waste time on stupid things, and make lots of lists. He thinks people overcomplicate productivity, and the elaborate multi-variable systems never seem to belong to people who move the world forward.
- Any system that keeps you disciplined about doing what you said you'd do is fine. Written lists matter most if you multitask. Focus and prioritization are the keys.
- Physical basics are sleep, exercise and nutrition. He aims for about eight hours of sleep and gets it most nights. He says skimping on sleep is "a terrible trade," and doubts people who claim they need four hours.
- He protects his best time, the first three hours after waking, and tries not to give it to anything other than projects he wants to work on. He can be productive for eight hours straight, but not 16, and he doesn't believe people who say they do 16.
- Accountability groups help. CEOs often form small groups of accountability buddies. They form naturally in YC through batchmates and group office hours, and when they fade, people's productivity sags and they re-form them. He cites a simple setup of four friends with weekly emails and quarterly check-ins.
- On exercise, he shifted to heavy lifting this year and says he gained 18 pounds of muscle. He says lifting gives faster visible progress than heavy cardio.

## Random meetings, "the deferred life plan," and funding ambitious work

- **Random meetings:** Sam used to spend about 10-15 hours a week on random meetings. Most were painful, but one meeting can make it all worth it. He says cutting off the flow of outside people and new ideas entirely is "disastrous." Without screeners, the tradeoff is that you must work much harder to fit it in addition to your regular work. If you won't work that hard, cut it down a lot instead.
- **Innovation:** He rejects "all the cool stuff already happened." Much recent innovation is on the internet, which he calls "a triumph of humanity" and the current frontier where the most talented people go. He says the rate of change over the last decade is "crazy."
- **Funding:** One of his big projects was how to fund work that creates value on long, uncertain timeframes without AGI's appeal. He expected to share new structures.
- **The deferred life plan:** He doesn't believe in it. The typical version is "I'll make $100 million in four years trading crypto, then build rockets." He says people rarely do either.
- Everyone can tell you aren't committed to either path. People can sense authenticity and motivation, and to succeed, people need to be rooting for you: investors, employees and others.
- Doing anything worthwhile takes a long time and a lot of emotional trauma, with people telling you you're wrong. If you're not willing to sign up for it, you won't succeed.
- His advice: pick one. If you want to build rockets, do it now. In Silicon Valley an unknown 22-year-old can raise money for a rocket company, and it has happened several times at YC. If you can't raise money, think about something else, or work at SpaceX. If you want money, say so and accept it takes a long time.

## Purpose and work in the AGI era

- Sam cares that people find purpose somewhere, whether in raising a family or running an eSports team. He says that's as valid as building AGI. What he hates is "people who are apathetic and proud of it."
- He says the human universals for meaning are human relationships, which top the list for almost everyone, feeling like you're creating value for others, and feeling respected by others.
- He expects AGI to make intelligence less of a benefit. He says 60,000 years ago physical strength and endurance mattered more than intelligence, and 60,000 years from now biological intelligence won't matter much. It just happens to be valuable in this moment.
- He notes that society rates intelligence so highly that people seem oddly disconnected from their bodies.
