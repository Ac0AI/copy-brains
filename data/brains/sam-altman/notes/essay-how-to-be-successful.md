## Compounding as the core wealth principle

Sam argues that exponential curves are the key to wealth generation. "Compounding is magic. Look for it everywhere."

- A medium-sized business that grows 50% in value every year becomes huge in a very short time.
- Few businesses have true network effects and extreme scalability, but technology will create more of them. It is worth a lot of effort to find and create them.
- The same applies to a career. Most careers progress linearly. He wants one where someone with two years of experience is not as effective as someone with twenty, so the rate of learning stays high.
- Leverage sources he lists: capital, technology, brand, network effects, and managing people. Each unit of work should produce more results over time.
- He suggests adding another zero to whatever you define as your success metric (money, status, impact).
- He will take as much time as needed between projects to find one that, if successful, "will make the rest of my career look like a footnote."
- Let small, linear opportunities go and focus on potential step changes.
- His view of the biggest competitive advantage, for a company or an individual: long-term thinking with a broad view of how different systems in the world will come together. In compound growth the furthest-out years matter most, and almost no one takes a truly long-term view, so the market "richly rewards" those who do.

## Ownership over salary

- He says the biggest economic misunderstanding of his childhood was that people get rich from high salaries. Almost no one in the history of the Forbes list got there with a salary (entertainers are an exception).
- "You get truly rich by owning things that increase rapidly in value." Examples: a piece of a business, real estate, natural resources, intellectual property.
- You need equity in something rather than just selling your time, because "time only scales linearly."
- The best way to make things that increase rapidly in value is by making things people want at scale.

## Self-belief, independent thinking and willfulness

- The most successful people he knows believe in themselves "almost to the point of delusion." Cultivate this early and trust yourself more as you collect data points that your judgment is good.
- Without self-belief it is hard to hold contrarian ideas about the future, and that is where most value is created. Most highly successful people were really right about the future at least once when others thought they were wrong. Otherwise they would have faced much more competition.
- Self-belief is needed to manage your own and your team's morale. The more ambitious you are, the more the world tries to tear you down.
- Balance it with self-awareness. He used to avoid criticism. Now he listens assuming it is true, then decides whether to act. Truth-seeking separates self-belief from self-delusion.
- Entrepreneurship is hard to teach because original thinking is, and school tends to reward the opposite. Think from first principles, swap ideas with others, then find fast, cheap ways to test them in the real world.
- "I will fail many times, and I will be really right once." Give yourself many chances to get lucky.
- You can bend the world to your will a surprising percentage of the time. Most people do not try. Self-doubt, giving up early and not pushing hard enough stop most people.
- Ask for what you want. You usually will not get it, but when it works, it works surprisingly well.
- People who say "I am going to keep going until this works" and mean it usually succeed, because they last long enough for luck to go their way.
- Airbnb is his benchmark. Examples he cites: maxed-out credit cards kept in baseball-card binder pages, dollar-store cereal for every meal, and battles with entrenched interests. He would not recommend copying these, but they survived until luck turned.
- He says he has never met a very successful pessimistic person.

## Selling, communication and being bold

- Self-belief is not enough. You must convince others. All great careers become sales jobs: you evangelize to customers, prospective employees, press and investors.
- Selling needs an inspiring vision, strong communication, some charisma, and evidence of execution.
- Invest in communication, especially written. Make your thinking clear first, then use plain, concise language.
- The best way to be good at sales is to believe in what you sell. "Trying to sell snake oil feels awful."
- Sales improves with deliberate practice. Many people treat it as unlearnable because it feels distasteful.
- Show up in person when it matters. Early on he was always willing to get on a plane. It was often unnecessary, but three times it led to career-making turning points.
- He argues it is easier to do a hard startup than an easy one, because people want to be part of something exciting that matters.
- Progress on an important problem brings a constant tailwind of people wanting to help.
- If everyone else is starting meme companies and you want to start a gene-editing company, do it without second-guessing. Follow your curiosity, because what excites you often excites others.

## Risk, focus and hard work

- Most people overestimate risk and underestimate reward. You cannot be right all the time, so try many things and adapt.
- Once basic obligations are covered, make risk-taking easy. Look for small bets where you lose 1x if wrong but make 100x if right, then make a bigger bet in that direction.
- Don't save up too long. At YC, founders who spent a long time at Google or Facebook often struggle to leave a comfortable, predictable life, and are tempted to return. People "match their lifestyle to next year's salary." Keeping life cheap and flexible lets you follow hunches, though it has tradeoffs.
- Focus is a force multiplier. Working on the right thing matters much more than working many hours, and most people waste most of their time on things that do not matter.
- Once you know what to do, be unstoppable about getting a small handful of priorities done quickly. "I have yet to meet a slow-moving person who is very successful."
- Working smart or hard gets you to about the 90th percentile. The 99th requires both.
- "Extreme people get extreme results." The trade-offs are real, and choosing not to work this way is rational. Momentum compounds and success begets success.
- Work hard early in your career. Hard work compounds like interest, and it is easier with fewer other responsibilities.
- To avoid burnout, find work you like with people you enjoy spending lots of time with.
- Work stamina is one of the biggest predictors of long-term success. He says people who claim you can be super successful without working most of the time (for some period) do a disservice.
- He notes a YC founder was much happier and more fulfilled after leaving a big company to pursue maximum impact.

## Being hard to compete with and building a network

- Companies are more valuable if hard to compete with, and so are individuals. "If what you do can be done by someone else, it eventually will be, and for less money."
- Build leverage through personal relationships, a strong personal brand, or skill at the intersection of multiple fields.
- Mimetic behavior (doing what your peers do) is usually a mistake, because it makes you easy to compete with.
- Great work requires teams, and the size of the network of talented people you know often limits what you can accomplish.
- Build the network by helping people as much as you can over a long period. This led to most of his best career opportunities and three of his four best investments. Something good often happens because of help he gave a founder ten years earlier.
- Take care of the people who work with you and be "overly generous with sharing the upside; it will come back to you 10x."
- His most important management lesson: evaluate what people are great at and put them in those roles. Push people hard enough to exceed what they thought possible, but not to burnout.
- Define yourself by your strengths. Work around weaknesses by hiring complementary team members rather than people who are good at the same things.
- Discover undiscovered talent by meeting many people and tracking who goes on to impress. Look for rate of improvement and don't overvalue experience or current accomplishments.
- His heuristic on meeting someone: "is this person a force of nature?"
- Get an eminent person to bet on you early, mainly by being helpful, and pay it forward later.
- Spend time with positive people who support your ambitions.

## Internal drive and motivation

- Most people are externally driven. Two costs: they work on consensus ideas and career tracks, which prevents interesting work (someone else would have done it anyway), and they get risk calculations wrong by obsessing over keeping up with others.
- Smart people are especially at risk. Awareness helps only a little.
- The most successful people he knows are internally driven: they do it "to impress themselves" and because they feel compelled to make something happen.
- After you have enough money and status that more stops being fun, internal drive is the only force that keeps pushing performance higher.
- Motivation is the first thing he tries to understand about a person.
- His benchmark is Jessica Livingston and Paul Graham. YC was mocked for its first few years and few expected success, but they believed it would be good for the world, loved helping people, and were convinced their model was better.
- "It is hard to be wildly successful at anything you aren't obsessed with."
