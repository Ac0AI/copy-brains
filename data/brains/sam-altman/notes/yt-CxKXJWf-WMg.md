## Why Y Combinator exists and how it works

- Sam describes YC's role as enabling as much innovation as possible, with startups as the vehicle. YC funds about 250 startups a year in its core program.
- A new "fellowship" program offers a tenth of the funding and less advice, and will be expanded.
- The goal: if a company is in YC, it gets "a hundred times" the advantage it would otherwise have, through advice, connections and access to the community.
- The most valuable thing is the alumni community, with more than 2,000 funded founders at that point. Alumni help each other raise money, hire, and buy each other's products.
- Sam sees YC as somewhere between a product company and a traditional investment firm.
- In 2005, when Sam went through YC's first batch (eight companies), investors wanted businessmen, business plans and financial models rather than engineers. YC was different: "our people", engineers who cared about the product. Founders in that batch were confident this was the future of investing.

## YC as a network-effects business

- Sam says YC is a network-effects business, which he thinks nobody knew ten years earlier.
- There were about 200 accelerators in the world. Eight companies worth over $1B had come out of any accelerator, and all eight were from YC.
- His explanation: there is a real network effect at the earliest funding stage. He doesn't think it exists as strongly at the venture stage, where five or ten great firms all do well.
- On YC's stage, the gap between first and second place is "like the difference between Facebook and my high school yearbook".
- YC is the first investor, so founder loyalty is stronger than toward later investors. Series A investors bought before success was obvious. Series C/D investors invest mostly on a financial model. Public shareholders "will sell your stock if your earnings drop a penny".
- Because of that loyalty, founders give back to the community.
- YC builds the network on purpose:
  - Takes all its founders camping once a year.
  - Runs physical spaces where founders gather.
  - Runs private online social networks for founders.
- Being a founder is "very lonely", so having peers to say "this is really hard" to matters more than it seems.
- Enterprise founders get their first customers from within the YC community, which is now standardized. Without a network like that, Sam says it is hard to do, and he no longer advises non-YC companies.

## Sam's path and the case against being a venture investor

- Sam left Stanford (computer science) in 2005 to start Loopt. He and friends were already working on it when YC was announced, and he applied the day before the deadline.
- He became a part-time YC partner in 2011, and his company was acquired in 2012.
- After the acquisition he raised a venture fund and learned he did not want to be a venture investor. He felt like a public stock picker, allocating capital where many others were also allocating, and trying to win deals with a better sales pitch.
- By contrast, in 2009 and 2010 he wrote $15K checks as an angel when founders had no other options, and that is where he felt he made a difference.
- He wanted to enable new companies, so he took the YC presidency.
- He says a great Series A board member is the most important thing to focus on, and that YC is not good at providing that. If he were a later-stage investor, he'd be a hands-off one.
- Taking over as YC's president was less jarring than most CEO transitions because he had been involved from the start. He says every successful new CEO has to "refound" the company in some way. Internally, the mission is clear and the work is the same.

## What the best founders have in common

Sam says YC tracks founder traits across about 20,000 applicants a year and develops interview questions that correlate with outcomes. In no particular order:

- **Clarity.** Can the founder explain exactly what they do and why? Without it they can't hire, sell or talk to press, and it signals unclear thinking. Airbnb knew exactly why they were staying on air beds in strangers' homes, even though it seemed odd to YC at first.
- **Determination and passion.** Some founders "bend the world to their will" and won't take no; others retreat at each small obstacle. Great companies are also mission-driven in some way.
- **Raw intelligence.**
- **Speed of execution and decision-making.** Not the same as determination, since some determined people are slow. Speed and quality of decision-making correlate almost perfectly with YC's successful founders. Founders who look great on paper but fail often lack it.
- **Problem-solving.** He recommends *The Martian* as "the best startup book" he has read in a long time. The skill is facing an unfamiliar problem and solving it quickly.

## Founding teams, cofounders and hiring

- YC prefers teams of two or three cofounders but doesn't rule out solo founders. Dropbox and Instacart had no cofounder when YC said yes, but each got one before the program or shortly after.
- Reasons against going solo: there is a lot of work, and the psychological toll is underestimated even by people who think they're strong.
- A bad cofounder is worse than none. Founders who bring a cofounder just because they think YC will like it end up heartbroken "100% of the time". Start without one if you must, but "never stop looking" for one.
- Three cofounders works well (Airbnb is the example). Four is harder, since someone usually leaves in the first two years.
- On diversity within a team: diversity of background and perspective is good. The bad kind is diversity of vision, where founders disagree on what kind of company to build (culture, decision-making, whether to sell, who to hire, how to handle disagreements).
- Conflict also arises when two founders want the same thing for themselves, like both wanting to be CEO and on a magazine cover. YC wants teams with a shared vision and varied skills.
- All cofounder pairs have big fights at some point. YC has sometimes told founders a relationship was over and one needed to move on.
- Hiring: the best YC startups hire the fewest people, and the worst rush to say "I have 10 people working for me".
  - Employees add burn, organizational inertia, internal conflict and career-path worries.
  - Before the company is ready to scale, extra hires create "potential energy" that causes problems.
  - Airbnb took about nine months to hire its first person, Stripe took about as long, and Dropbox took a very long time.
  - YC likes companies that find out how long they can wait before hiring the first non-founder.
- On firing: the biggest universal mistake great founders make is waiting too long to fire people. If someone fires easily, that is a worry. Keeping someone too long hurts them more: fired after 2-3 months, it won't go on a resume, but after a year it does.
- How Sam fires someone:
  - Treat them as a person and make it a win for them.
  - He never fired anyone without finding them a new job first.
  - Be clear the decision is firm (hesitating creates a power dynamic).
  - Offer generous severance, help finding a job, and let them stay or work from home until ready to announce their next move.
  - Don't bring a final paycheck, termination letter and legal papers to the meeting. Lawyers' advice here is bad because it makes it feel hostile.
  - Ask on what terms they want it to happen, and handle paperwork later.
  - If hiring someone was a mistake, he sees it as his own.

## Ideas, conviction, pivots and product focus

- Sam endorses Peter Thiel's line that the best ideas look bad. Good assets get competed away and bad assets are bad, so the opportunity is in great ideas that look bad.
- This is harder than it sounds because everyone is swayed by trends. Investors chased the next Facebook in 2006-2008 and again in 2009-2010, when the real opportunities were Uber and Airbnb, which looked nothing like Facebook. Investors flocked to ad tech around Google's IPO, which was the wrong bet.
- Today, he says, people are building "Uber for X" (food, dog walking). He expects the next $100B company to be something new, not a narrow vertical on Uber's model. Ask what has changed in the world that allows a new giant, then build that. It will look bad.
- Two ways to build conviction:
  - Launch fast and get users who love it. Uber was mocked but grew roughly 10x a year.
  - Be able to explain what changed in the world that others missed (e.g. everyone suddenly had an internet-connected device in their pocket).
- Whom to ignore: everyone except users. If users say the product is bad and no other users like it, listen.
- Hard tech: Sam sits on two nuclear boards. YC put hard-tech companies in the same program as web startups. Their view was that these companies failed because they weren't startup-like enough. Hard-tech companies should break their work into small, measurable internal products and find a metric to improve 10% each week. Compounded over three or four years, that makes a big company. He said they wouldn't know whether it worked for about five years.
- On pivots: Silicon Valley misunderstands pivoting and he dislikes how people brag about them. Failure is tolerable but still bad, and he thinks it is overglorified.
- The only pivots he has seen work:
  - Founders building what they wanted to build all along (Instagram: Kevin Systrom first built Bourbon, then the photo-filter idea he cared about).
  - Founders discovering it while building something that didn't work (Airbnb, which ran out of money and rented an air-mattress space to cover rent; also Slack, which came out of the Glitch game's internal tool, and Stripe, which came from Patrick Collison being unable to accept payments in an iPhone app).
- A pivot made by sitting at a whiteboard to brainstorm a new idea has never worked, in his experience. If there is no real pivot, he tells founders to shut down, return the remaining capital to investors, travel, and wait for an idea that arises naturally.
- Product focus: a startup can have many users who "like" it or a few who "love" it. Every successful company he has worked with was in the second group. The aim is to build until some users would be very sad if it disappeared. Spend nearly all time writing code and talking to users.
- Bitcoin, as an example: he said he wouldn't buy much because it had not yet found product-market fit and few people use it daily.

## Momentum, fake work, fundraising and running the company

- After Demo Day, there is often a slump. Founders then must clean up tech, organizational and cultural debt, but should spend about 10% of energy on that, not 50% or 100%, because losing growth creates more problems. He quotes a board member's line: "sales solves all problems", later amended to "growth solves all problems". The core rule of running a startup is never lose momentum.
- Employees often sense momentum changes before founders do.
- "Fake work": do the work that would be cut in a reality-show edit, like coding for 12 hours or running user interviews and sales meetings. That is boring to watch but is how you win. Fundraising feels exciting, but no great company became great because it was good at fundraising.
- Distractions: press, "30 under 30" lists, conferences, and networking events. He once got pulled into CEO events and realized he had been out of the office more than in it.
- Valuations: he thinks headline valuations are fantasies except when buying shares (as an investor, or an employee getting options). Employees shouldn't assume their equity is a percentage of the headline valuation because liquidation preferences distort it. The real question is what the company will be worth in five years and what share you own. He often tells startups not to chase the highest valuation, and sometimes not to raise.
- Career advice: if joining a company, join the most successful fast-growing one you can find, since seeing a successful company scale is the only prior training of real value. Start a startup only if you have an idea you believe matters, not for the sake of it. Joining an early company you believe in may have a higher expected value because few startups succeed.
- Hard hours, short years: when he was 23, someone told him he'd have roughly 20 years of the intensity needed to be a great startup CEO. Since the time is limited, he says to think about opportunity cost and what you most believe in.
- Location: the Bay Area is still the best place to start a startup, and cost of living is the main downside because every extra month of runway matters. Over half of YC's funded founders were not born in the US, with about 40 countries represented in the latest batch.
- Education: he sees no strong correlation between years of schooling and founder success; extremes in either direction are worse.
