## Sam's reasons for investing in startups (and what he says about angel investing)

- Sam cites energy, shaping the future, and leverage on time as the draws of startup investing. He says founders are "not burned out on the world at all" and will try things that people with more battle scars won't.
- Most investments lose money, but "occasionally you do get to make a hundred or a thousand x". He compares the appeal to a slot machine.
- Founders sometimes tell him that something he did eight or ten years earlier made or broke their career. He calls this deeply gratifying.
- He writes his confidence interval and expectations on a Post-it on the back of every stock certificate. He says this teaches you that you are usually wrong, and that the habit has helped him in everything else.
- He says you can get better at this quickly if you treat it as deliberate practice.

## The two biggest investor mistakes: copying other investors and misreading the power law

- His number one early mistake was caring too much about what other investors thought. He estimates 80% of investors outsource 80% of their decision-making to others. "The first question that most people ask... is who else is investing in your round."
- This creates a schooling effect, where a company gets hot for no discernible reason because a few people liked it.
- His second mistake was not understanding the power law. His single best investment returns more than all the rest combined, and his second best beats investments three through infinity combined.
- Most investors ask whether they can hit a bunch of singles. He says that works for stocks or bonds, but "angel investing is a business of homeruns."
- Asking about an acceptable failure rate is "totally the wrong question." You can have 95% of investments fail if one returns a billion dollars.
- When he meets a startup, the first question is "how big could this be if it works?", not what could go wrong. Asking what could go wrong first filters out the companies that could be giant.
- The best companies sit at the intersection of "sounds like a bad idea" and "is a good idea." The best investments are the easiest to talk yourself out of.
- YC numbers at the time: about 1,600-1,700 companies funded. The top five represent about two-thirds of the value created, and the top one nearly one-third.

## Finding deals: open networks, referrals, and reputation

- Many generation-defining companies come from out-of-network, unknown founders. Word of mouth from existing founders is the best way to find them. YC tries to make founders like it so much that they refer their friends.
- For new angels, he says the most successful approach he has seen is helping founders for free. Even without investing in those companies, the referrals come later.
- He argues for an open network. Many angels brag about being hard to reach. YC lets anyone apply, answers emails, and takes people with no brand, reputation or network seriously. He calls this one of the "two or three most important secrets of YC."
- He recommends staying open to random emails and odd introductions: "nine times out of ten you waste your time [and the] other time makes it totally worth it."
- There are now far more investors than good startups, so founders hold the leverage, and the asymmetry is unlikely to return. Founders talk to each other.
- Reputation is the key to winning deals. Founders do reference checks on investors, increasingly using what other founders say about them.
- He warns against fighting over a dying company for small sums (e.g. $10,000) because of the long-term reputational damage. Under the power law, those companies never mattered anyway.
- Other things that help: being fast to decide, being clear about reasoning, and being responsive and available.

## Price, terms, and what to invest in

- On bargain-hunting, he says value investing is not a winning strategy for angels. With one or two exceptions, his best investments felt the most expensive at the time, including rounds that were huge step-ups from rounds three months earlier.
- The exceptions were companies where he understood something no one else did, so there was no competition.
- Example: a company had about 10 seed investors, and all 10 asked for advisor shares, each claiming to be the only one who works hard.
- His investment filter is whether he believes it could be a $10 billion company. He has no other rules on stage, sector or business model, because the top-spot companies are so rare.
- He thinks it is easier to start a hard company than an easy one. Ambitious companies attract hires, press, advisors and help. "If you are starting the 20 million photo sharing application it's really hard; if you are starting a nuclear fusion company a lot of people want to help."
- A key filter is whether the company can recruit hundreds of talented people who could otherwise start their own companies.

## Evaluating founders

- YC mostly picks founders, because it is hard to tell at the idea stage that an idea will be huge, though you can tell when one won't. With practice you can identify founders who have a chance.
- Four traits from YC partner Paul Buchheit (the transcript renders it as "Pabu hi"): obsession, focus, formidability and love.
- Intelligence and idea generation: YC once funded 20-odd teams of strong founders who had no ideas, and all failed. Good founders have ideas all the time, so he looks for creative, independent thinking.
- Communication skills: founders are evangelists for hiring, fundraising, sales and setting direction. Founders of super successful companies tend to be great communicators.
- Execution speed: between weekly or ten-day office hours, how much did the founders try, test and learn? This relentless cadence is "incredibly predictive of success." He contrasts it with founders who look great on paper but always have reasons they didn't do things.
- Rate of improvement: don't compare a seed founder to Brian Chesky. Look at the founder's growth rate. In YC's ten weeks you can often see it. He has seen it about ten times and says he has been right every time he felt a founder would develop into a great leader.
- Motivation: starting up takes more than a decade and many days of wanting to quit. Founders who want to get rich quickly or add a resume item don't last. Every time YC thought a company would go well and it didn't, the founders lacked a deep sense of mission.
- "Scenesters" are people who like startup parties and being called founders. He treats this as a red flag, along with low integrity.
- He says he has never made a lot of money backing a founder he thought was only okay in an otherwise good business. He wants founders he'd want to work for.

## Markets, trends, and product quality

- He says investors wrongly fixate on market size today. What matters is market size in ten years. "You should prefer a small market growing super quickly to a very large market today." Facebook, Uber and others started in markets that barely existed.
- Chasing what worked last time (more social networks, more ride-sharing apps) is much harder than finding the next fast-growing market, which requires independent thought.
- Real vs fake trends: in a real trend, few people use it yet but those who do use it hourly and spontaneously tell friends. The iPhone had only a million or two sales in year one, but owners used it for hours daily and evangelised it.
- By contrast, he says VR headsets (in 2018) mostly sit on shelves. He would invest heavily in VR once users wear headsets for hours daily and tell everyone to buy one.
- He quotes Sequoia: "you cannot create a technology wave... but you can surf one if you can find the wave." He says most of the biggest tech companies form soon after a platform shift.
- He looks for good ideas that look bad. Roughly 90% of angel capital goes to bad ideas that look good, such as chasing what worked two years ago, markets with hundreds of similar companies, or founders working hard to convince you they aren't a commodity.
- Great products matter. Growth hacking and sales machines can grow a mediocre product for a while, but they don't build a Facebook-size company. His test: did a trusted friend spontaneously tell him about it? If the startup won't get there, it won't be the number one power-law return.
- Human intuition about exponential growth is terrible (e.g. 25% monthly growth for 36 months). He models it out, applies a growth decay rate, and asks how big a word-of-mouth company could be in five years.
- He wants a company that gets stronger as it grows: more pricing power, easier user acquisition, harder to compete with (network effects, moats). Almost all the value comes from earnings in years 10 and 11 onward, so be skeptical if you can't answer this.
- He asks "what do I understand that other people don't?" If he has no answer, he has no edge. An acceptable answer can be being more bullish than everyone else because of a specific insight about the market.

## Q&A: adding value, pro rata, and judgment calls

- Helping founders: the top request is hiring (finding people, helping interview, sourcing), followed by fundraising help. As an angel he made himself available for tactical advice, even for a two-minute call on a Friday night.
- Founder flaws: he separates traits that can change from those that can't. Don't compromise on integrity, but accept many gaps (like not knowing what a valuation is) if the founder is improving fast and mission-driven.
- Integrity: ask about past decisions, because founders will often tell you the unethical things they've done. YC makes its decisions in 10-minute interviews and has been fooled sometimes, but caught it hundreds of times.
- Pro rata: studies by several venture firms conclude you should always exercise pro rata if the up round is led by a top-quartile VC. Pro rata is the right to invest in future rounds to maintain ownership.
- ICOs and security tokens: he thinks they will change financings, but not as most people expect. Securities laws exist for a reason, and many ICOs fall between incompetence and scams. Better mechanical tracking of investments is plausible.
- Good ideas that look bad: Reddit (in his 2005 YC class) was dismissed by people he trusted. His own conviction vanished when they said it was a bad idea. Dropbox was dismissed because "Google, Microsoft... guaranteed to crush them."
- Uber's market size was repeatedly underestimated in articles. Look at shifts in consumer behavior: Uber replaced limo booking, then taxis, then public transit, then car ownership.
- Solo non-technical founders: he has done it and says it can work. YC strongly prefers at least one technical co-founder and at least two founders, but treats these as preferences, not absolutes.
- Self-awareness: willingness to take feedback and a drive to improve matter, as does believing you can succeed in spite of your flaws.
- Time allocation: "only fund founders that you want to allocate a lot of time to." If you don't want to help them, that is a red flag for the founder.
