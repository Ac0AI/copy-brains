## Caring about opinions: two choices, not one

- Sam rejects the question "how do I stop caring what other people think?" as the wrong one. When a founder asked him, he had no answer, and on reflection decided the framing was flawed.
- He says almost everyone cares what someone thinks, and it's probably important. "Caring too much makes you a sheep," and caring what *everyone* thinks is "definitely a mistake."
- You still need to be at least a little in tune with others to build something useful for them.
- He sees two degrees of freedom:
  - Which people's opinions you care about, and on what subjects.
  - The timescale on which you care about those opinions.
- Most people work out the first. The second "doesn't seem to get much attention."
- He cites Coco Chanel on choosing whose opinions count: "I don't care what you think about me. I don't think about you at all."

## Long time horizons: the trait of the most impressive people

- Sam observes that the most impressive people he knows care a lot about what people think, even people whose opinions they arguably shouldn't value.
- He notes that a surprising number of them keep a folder of screenshots of tweets from haters.
- What makes them unusual is the time horizon. They care about opinion on a very long timescale.
- Their attitude: as long as the history books get it right, they take some pride in letting the newspapers get it wrong.

## Trading short-term status for long-term status

- His core advice: "You should trade being short-term low-status for being long-term high-status."
- He says most people seem unwilling to make this trade.
- A common way it plays out is by eventually being right about an important but deeply non-consensus bet.
- He says there are many other ways it can happen. The key point is that it only works if you are right.

## Being misunderstood as a strength

- Sam argues that, as long as you are right, being misunderstood by most people is "a strength not a weakness."
- The reason is space: you and a small group of rebels get room to work on an important problem that might otherwise not get solved.
- When a bet is non-consensus, few people compete for it and few try to stop it. That leaves the problem to those willing to be misunderstood in the meantime.
- Practical takeaways from the essay:
  - Decide whose opinions matter and on which topics.
  - Decide the timescale you judge yourself on. Pick the long one.
  - Accept short-term low status as the price of an important non-consensus bet.
  - Being misunderstood is only a strength if you turn out to be right, so the bet has to be a good one.
