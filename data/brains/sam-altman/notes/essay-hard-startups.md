## Why hard startups are easier to make succeed

- Sam calls it "the most counterintuitive secret about startups": it is often easier to succeed with a hard startup than an easy one.
- A hard startup needs far more money, time, coordination or technological development than most. It must also be valuable if it works, since "not all hard problems are worth solving."
- Easy startups are "easy to start but hard to make successful."
- Example: the year Instagram took off, probably over 1,000 photo sharing startups were funded, while fewer than ten nuclear fusion startups existed.

## Hard startups as a recruiting advantage

- Talented people are the most precious commodity in the startup ecosystem, and they mostly want to work on something meaningful.
- The first five or ten hires are easy to get with large equity grants and areas of responsibility. Beyond that, a startup recruits on the company's mission, the likelihood of massive success, and the quality of the people there.
- He says few recruiting messages are as powerful (when true) as: "the world needs this, it won't happen any time soon if we don't do it, and we are much less likely to succeed if you don't join."
- Sam describes a derivative of the Peter Principle: a startup rises to the level where it can no longer attract enough talented people.
- The same often holds for careers. The limiting factor becomes how many talented people you know and can get to work with you.
- Alternative: build a startup that can become quite successful with fewer than ten people. As big tech compensation keeps rising, he suspects this will become a trend.

## Tailwind vs. headwind

- An easy startup is a headwind; a hard startup is a tailwind.
- If people care about your success because you seem committed to something significant, that acts as a background force helping with hiring, advice, partnerships and fundraising.

## Silicon Valley takes serious people seriously

- Part of the magic of Silicon Valley is that people default to taking you seriously if you are willing to be serious. They have learned that not doing so is a very expensive mistake in aggregate.
- Sam says founders working on better home building, gene editing, artificial general intelligence, a new education system or carbon sequestration may get funded even without a degree or much experience.

## Ambition with a reasonable next step

- Let yourself become more ambitious. Figure out the most interesting version of where your work could go.
- Talk about that big vision and work relentlessly towards it, but always have a reasonable next step.
- His warning: "You don't want step one to be incorporating the company and step two to be going to Mars."

## Think in decade-long timeframes

- Be willing to make a very long-term commitment. Most people aren't, which is part of why they pick "easy" startups.
- In a world of compounding advantages, if most people operate on a 3 year timeframe and you operate on a 10 year timeframe, you have a very large edge.
