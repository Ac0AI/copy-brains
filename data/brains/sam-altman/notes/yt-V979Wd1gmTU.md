## Starting OpenAI: lean into the improbable thing when in doubt

- Sam says OpenAI came close to never starting. Through 2015 the decision was "kind of coin flippy". AGI "sounded crazy" and "was kind of a pipe dream". DeepMind looked impossibly far ahead. Sam was running YC at the time and had plenty of other good startups to work on.
- His lesson: ambitious things have good reasons not to be done. It takes a core group of people who "sit in a room, look each other in the eye and say, 'All right, let's do this.'" "When in doubt, you should lean into them."
- Early OpenAI had no products, no revenue and no idea it would ever have revenue. It started as 8 people, then 20, writing research papers. They played video games and had a robotic hand that "could sort of barely do a Rubik's cube". ChatGPT was "completely in the realm of science fiction."
- Big companies don't start out looking big. He cites Vinod Khosla: there is a big difference between a "zero million dollar startup and a zero billion dollar startup, but they both have zero dollars of revenue."
- His advice: pick a market where some version of the future could be big if it works. After that, "one dumb foot in front of the other for a long time."

## Concentrating talent with a one-of-one mission

- Recruiting the smartest people was "easier than it sounds". Sam says "99% of the world thought we were crazy. 1% of the world really resonated with" the AGI mission. That 1% had few other places to go, so OpenAI concentrated the talent.
- His general rule from watching startups: if you do the same thing as everyone else, it is very hard to concentrate talent or get people to believe in the mission. A one-of-one thing gives you "a really nice tailwind".
- Contrarian conviction is hard. He recalls a "really mean email" from Elon Musk a few years in. Elon had just seen GPT-1 and said the project had a near-zero chance of success. Sam went home wondering "what if he's right?"
- He has no magic answer. It "gets significantly easier over time", and you "get knocked down and get back up and brush yourself off and try to keep going."

## Hiring: smart, driven people and slope over pedigree

- Sam says hiring "really smart people who are clearly really driven and really productive and can work as part of a team" gets you 90% of the way. He is surprised by how much people focus on other things.
- His checklist:
  - Really smart
  - Driven
  - Curious
  - Self-motivated
  - Hardworking
  - A good track record of accomplishment
  - Works well as part of a team
  - Aligned with the company's vision
- Early in a startup he does not believe in hiring the senior, eminent administrator as the first hire. Neither he nor YC has had much success with that. He prefers "young scrappy but clearly get stuff done" people over the "extremely polished track record". Senior people may be needed later.
- When reading YC applications he "would never look at the resume items" such as Google or a top college. He went straight to the most impressive thing the person had done. He checked the resume only as a backup if that didn't convince him.
- He looks at what people have built and coded, their velocity, and how they think about and solve problems.
- He endorses "hire for slope, not y-intercept" as "unbelievably great advice."

## Building on AI: product overhang, falling prices and defensibility

- Sam says the "product overhang" is large. What models can do far exceeds the products people have built. Even if models stopped improving, there is "a huge amount of new stuff to build."
- API prices keep falling. He says "last week o3 cost five times as much as it did this week and that's going to keep going." He expects people to be "astonished at how much the price per performance falls." He also says an open-source model is coming that will surprise people.
- Reasoning models are like "a new square on a periodic table that no one has built with yet." The interaction model differs from chat. He had only seen startups building for it in the previous month.
- On the "will OpenAI run over my startup" question, his answer is "don't build our core chat assistant." OpenAI will make ChatGPT the best super assistant it can, which is one small part of the opportunity. Other ideas he floated:
  - An app or agent store inside ChatGPT that drives traffic to new startups.
  - A "sign in with OpenAI" that lets users bring a personalized model to a startup.
- Herd behavior is a bigger problem. "People get excited about the same thing at the same time." If Gary listed the five ideas he hears most often, half the room would be working on one of them. Sam bets the person who builds something bigger than OpenAI is not working on any of the five.
- Defensibility: "the best, most enduring companies are usually not doing the same thing as everybody else." That gives you time to find the great product and build the technology before you must answer the defensibility question.
- ChatGPT's own path: "for a long time the only defensibility was we had the only product out in the market." Then came brand, then memory, connections and other features that are truly defensible. He admits "we didn't have any defensibility strategy" at first. You have a window in which to build one.
- He names the "seven powers" book as relevant to defensibility.

## Why this is the best time to start a company

- Sam says: "This is the best [expletive] time ever in the history of technology ever, period, to start a company."
- On the worry that SaaS gets replaced by just-in-time generated software, he says it will happen to everybody. "The way startups win is when they can iterate faster than big companies and they can do it at a much lower cost." Big companies have advantages but "iterate very slowly", and cheap software erodes those advantages.
- "When the clock cycle of the industry changes this much startups almost always win", and he thinks it has probably never changed this much.
- One person or a small group with a lot of agency can now get far more done. Coordination costs are huge, so empowering individuals will change what gets built, not just add a little more.
- His analogy for AI is the transistor. A scientific discovery lets society and the economy build enormous value over decades, and he thinks AI will be "even faster and steeper".

## Product direction: memory, agents, devices and a melting interface

- Memory is Sam's favorite feature launched that year. He sees ChatGPT becoming an entity that knows you, connects to all your data, runs persistently in the background, and proactively helps. It would know when to message you and when to act for you.
- He lists the ingredients: always-with-you devices, new browsers, connections to all data sources, memory and a model that runs persistently. Integrations are important, and people are starting to use ChatGPT as an operating system for their lives.
- Agents: deep research and Codex are "like a very junior employee that can work on something for a short period of time." Much of the world's computer work comes in few-hour chunks that someone then approves or rejects. He says current o3 can already support many such experiences.
- Models: the long-term aim is one integrated model that reasons when needed and generates real-time video when needed. He suggests it could write a brand-new app just in time, only for you.
- Interface: he wants the interface to "melt away". You tell the computer what you want, trust it to handle changes, and it surfaces only what matters. He compares his phone to "walking down Times Square", with notifications and flashing things.
- Hardware: there have been "two big revolutions in computer interfaces" in about 50 years (keyboard/mouse/screen, then touch). AI opens the field for a third. He is betting on Jony Ive to figure it out. He declined to describe the device but says the key to "Her" is a persistent AI companion, not a piece of hardware.
- Robots: OpenAI's strategy is to nail the model first, then connect it to a robot. He expects robots doing "super useful stuff" within a few years, though a billion robots will take a while.
- Scale: ChatGPT.com did not exist two and a half years earlier and is now the fifth biggest website in the world. He thinks OpenAI will "run the largest and most expensive piece of infrastructure in the world pretty soon". He says compute is hard to get. He would be glad to push half the ChatGPT workload onto local devices.

## Resilience, instincts and what he'd tell his 2005 self

- Sam says he wishes someone had taught him "conviction and resilience over a long period of time". It is easy for a while, but "your reserves wear down."
- His first startup (Loopt) didn't work out. He notes many people give up after one failed startup, but "startups don't work out all the time", so you have to learn to keep going.
- He advises:
  - Build trust in your own instincts and increase it as your decision-making improves.
  - Have the courage to work on things that are out of fashion but that you believe in.
- He compares entrepreneurship to having a kid: the good parts are better and the hard parts "shockingly much harder" than anyone can express.
- On being CEO of OpenAI: "I don't recommend it." No single challenge is that hard, but the volume of simultaneous work and the number of big companies gunning for OpenAI is "more context than I thought it was possible to handle at once."
- On energy and AI: he was obsessed with both for years but only saw after starting OpenAI that energy would be the fundamental limit on intelligence. He thinks AI for science is the most exciting application, because long-term economic growth comes from discovering new science plus decent governance and institutions.
