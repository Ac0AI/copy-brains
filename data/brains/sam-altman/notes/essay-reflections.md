## Building OpenAI as a product company, not just a research lab

- Sam says OpenAI started almost nine years ago because the founders believed AGI was possible and could be the most impactful technology in human history. At the time "very few people cared, and if they did, it was mostly because they thought we had no chance of success."
- He admits that "when we started we had no idea we would have to build a product company; we thought we were just going to do great research." They also did not expect to need "such a crazy amount of capital."
- His stance: "Our vision won't change; our tactics will continue to evolve." New things will need to be built that "we didn't understand a few years ago."
- He says OpenAI "cannot be a normal company" given the possibilities of its work.

## How ChatGPT came from watching developer behavior

- In 2022 OpenAI was a quiet research lab working on a demo temporarily called "Chat With GPT-3.5". Sam jokes they are "much better at research than we are at naming things."
- The idea came from watching people use the playground feature of the API. The team saw developers "really enjoying talking to the model."
- The reasoning for building a demo around that behavior: it would show people something important about the future and help make the models better and safer.
- ChatGPT launched on November 30, 2022.
- Sam says they always knew a tipping point would come but "didn't know what the moment would be. To our surprise, it turned out to be this."
- The launch started a growth curve "like nothing we have ever seen" in the company, the industry and the world.

## Growth numbers after the board crisis

- Since the crisis, OpenAI grew from about 100 million weekly active users to more than 300 million.
- He credits the focus after the crisis with "some of our best research ever."
- His stated product test: "continue to put technology out into the world that people genuinely seem to love and that solves real problems."
- His distribution philosophy: "iteratively putting great tools in the hands of people leads to great, broadly-distributed outcomes."
- He argues the safest way to build an AI system is releasing it "iteratively and gradually" into the world, so society can adapt and co-evolve with it, and guiding safety research with feedback from real-world applications.

## Building a company at extreme velocity with no handbook

- Over two years OpenAI had to build "an entire company, almost from scratch, around this new technology." Sam says nobody can train you for this except doing it, and with a brand-new category "there is no one at all who can tell you exactly how it should be done."
- He describes the process as "often two steps forward, one step back (and sometimes, one step forward and two steps back)," with mistakes corrected as you go. "There aren't really any handbooks or guideposts when you're doing original work."
- Moving fast in uncharted waters is stressful, and "conflicts and misunderstanding abound."
- His coping habit: remember the gratitude, and keep perspective "on any given Friday, when seven things go badly wrong by 1 pm."
- He calls the last two years "like a decade at a normal company."

## Turnover, competitors and being the leader

- Sam says teams tend to turn over as they scale, and OpenAI scales fast: "numbers go up by orders of magnitude every few months."
- He considers some turnover unavoidable: "startups usually see a lot of turnover at each new major level of scale."
- Some colleagues split off and became competitors. As companies grow and evolve fast, "interests naturally diverge."
- When a company in an important industry leads, "lots of people attack it for all sorts of reasons, especially when they are trying to compete with it."

## Governance lessons from being fired

- Sam was fired by surprise on a video call while in a Las Vegas hotel room, and the board published a blog post right after. He says it felt "like a dream gone wrong," and the "fog of war" was the strangest part, since nobody could get satisfactory answers.
- His verdict: "a big failure of governance by well-meaning people, myself included."
- Lessons he states:
  - Wants to be a "better, more thoughtful leader" than a year earlier.
  - A board needs diverse viewpoints and broad experience managing complex challenges.
  - "Good governance requires a lot of trust and credibility."
- OpenAI built a stronger governance system afterward to pursue its mission.

## Founder support network in a crisis

- Sam says Ron Conway and Brian Chesky were the two people who stood out. He believes OpenAI "would have fallen apart" without them, and they worked around the clock for days.
- What they did: stayed calm, kept clear strategic thinking, stopped him from several mistakes, made none themselves, and used their vast networks for everything needed.
- He says he had gotten a huge amount of help and advice from Brian over the prior couple of years, and values "being in the foxhole" to see what people can do.
- He sees this peer support as "an amazingly special thing about our industry" that does more to make it work than people realize, and says he looks forward to paying it forward.

## Where he thinks AI goes next

- Sam says OpenAI is "now confident we know how to build AGI as we have traditionally understood it."
- He predicts that in 2025 the first AI agents may "join the workforce" and materially change company output.
- The aim is moving beyond that to superintelligence: "We love our current products, but we are here for the glorious future. With superintelligence, we can do anything else."
- He expects superintelligent tools to accelerate scientific discovery and innovation and increase abundance and prosperity.
- He accepts that this sounds like science fiction: "we've been there before and we're OK with being there again."
