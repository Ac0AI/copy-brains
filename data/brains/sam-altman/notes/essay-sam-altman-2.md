## Running a company at OpenAI's pace: delegation, simplification and hiring

- Sam says he has no single productivity trick. His view: "people almost never allocate their time as well as they think they do."
- As demands grew, he hired and promoted strong people and delegates heavily. He calls this "the only sustainable way I know how to do it."
- He keeps his own focus on understanding "the core thing for us to do," which has become clearer and simpler as OpenAI's needs (such as infrastructure buildout) came into focus.
- More of the world now wants to work with OpenAI, so deals are quicker to negotiate.
- He deliberately avoids work he doesn't want to do. He used to spend a lot of time on product thinking, but now says OpenAI has "the world expert thinking about our product strategy" (a person he says is much better at it than him), so he lets her run it.
- Hardware hiring: cycle times are longer and capital is more intense, so mistakes cost more. He spends more time getting to know hardware people before trusting them to run. The rest is the same: find good, fast-moving people, get clear on the goal, and let them go.
- He says OpenAI's chip team "feels more like the OpenAI research team than a chip company." He admits it carries risk but thinks it could "work out phenomenally well."
- Hiring for AI-readiness: he asks how candidates use AI today. Using it only for "better Google search" is a yellow flag. People seriously thinking about what their day-to-day will look like in three years are a green flag.

## An AI-run company and the replacement for Slack and email

- Sam thinks email is "very bad" and Slack is only better than email. He dreads the first and last hour of his day dealing with Slack, and says it "creates a lot of fake work."
- He expects a new AI-driven replacement for the whole office suite (docs, slides, email, Slack). It would not be AI bolted onto existing tools, but "you are trusting your AI agent and my AI agent to work most stuff out and escalate to us when necessary."
- OpenAI is "very far" from using this internally. He says that is because they haven't tried, not because the models are far away. Teams get stuck in their ways, and with things going well there is "a lot of activation energy for a big new thing."
- He uses an "AI CEO" thought experiment: "shame on me if OpenAI is not the first big company run by an AI CEO." He asks what would have to happen for an AI to run OpenAI better than him, and uses the answer to decide how to design the org and spot roadblocks.
- Forecast: an AI-run division (not a tiny one) is "some small single-digit number of years" away. He agrees with Tyler that billion-dollar companies run by two or three people with AIs are coming.
- He expects AI can make most decisions before people are comfortable with it, since people trust other people over AI "even if that's irrational."
- He thinks the broader shift is good for society.

## Monetizing ChatGPT: trust, commerce, ads and margins

- Sam's key insight: ChatGPT is consistently reported as users' most trusted technology product from a big tech company. His explanation is that Google ads depend on search doing badly, while ChatGPT is paid by users and tries to give the best answer.
- Rule: if ChatGPT took payment to rank a worse hotel above a better one, it would be "catastrophic for your relationship with ChatGPT."
- Acceptable model: ChatGPT picks the best option with nothing influencing it, and takes the same transaction fee on a one-click booking as it would from any other seller. He says OpenAI's recent commerce work follows this spirit and that travel is coming at some point.
- Margins: he believes margins will fall dramatically on most goods and services, including hotel bookings. He's happy about it and thinks companies like OpenAI will make more money at lower margins.
- He says the way to monetize the smartest model is not hotel booking. The big money is in discovering new science and monetizing what only the smartest model can do.
- His stated strategy: put superintelligence in everyone's hands, make it easy to use, integrate it, build beautiful devices, connect it to your services, and invest in chips, energy and infrastructure to make it abundant and cheap. Then people figure out how to make the world better.
- He accepts OpenAI doing things that aren't "the economic-maxing thing" because they matter for the world.
- On ads: there is a kind that would be very bad (the paid-ranking kind) and kinds that would be good or pretty good. He expects OpenAI to try something at some point but doesn't think ads are the biggest revenue opportunity.
- On Walmart: when asked if Amazon would join a ChatGPT-Walmart shopping arrangement, he says if he were Amazon he would fight back.
- The best argument against a bubble, in his view: nobody knows how far past human-level intelligence goes, and people should discuss the economic impact of vastly superhuman intelligence much more.

## Energy, chips and compute as the binding constraints

- Sam's answer to why we don't just make more GPUs: "Because we need to make more electrons." If he could have one thing for more compute, it would be electrons.
- Short term, natural gas eases the constraint. Long term, he believes energy will be dominated by fusion and solar.
- On nuclear stigma: he thinks there's a price point where demand becomes overwhelming. At natural-gas prices adoption may be hard; at one-tenth the price "it would happen very fast."
- Chip-building has "no easy part." He points to an under-discussed recursive loop in hardware: robots that build robots, data centers that build data centers, and chips that design their own next generation.
- On whether cheaper intelligence reduces demand for compute: people always want more compute if it's cheaper, so he'll "take that bet every day."
- His related worry is a phase shift in how compute works (for example, full optical compute) that makes today's paradigm a dead end. The energy investment would still hold, but "everything else" would need redoing.

## GPT-6 and AI doing science

- Sam frames the progression: GPT-3 was the first glimmer of passing the "spiritual Turing test," and GPT-5 is the first glimmer of AI doing new science, in tiny ways (a new idea, a useful collaborator on a paper).
- He says GPT-6 may be a GPT-3-to-4-style leap for science: "5 has these tiny glimmers and 6 can really do it."
- His advice for a lab: the first step is to type in the research questions you're struggling with and see what experiments or ideas come back. Labs will not get GPT-6 this year.
- He doesn't think GPT-6 will have the "intangibles" of deep cultural and local knowledge (for example, when dealing with Saudi Arabia or the UAE). OpenAI brings in human experts and the US government for that, and he says OpenAI has an internal evaluation for something close to it.
- On poetry: he expects AI to reach a technically perfect "10," but argues "you won't care." As with chess, people care about the human who made it. Even if AI can't write a 10, it may only need to be able to recognize one.

## What AI will do to jobs, education and prices

- Sam expects the way he works (emails, calls, meetings, documents, Slack) to change hugely. Family, nature, food and friends will barely change.
- He cites programmers as the starkest example of 2025: the typical workflow at the end of the year looked very different from the start, and the world will get much more software. He expects similar shifts across many industries.
- Returns to a college degree: he guesses they decline slightly faster than over the last decade but won't collapse as fast as they should.
- He thinks the returns to using AI well will be widely distributed, not captured by a small group of AI specialists.
- On teaching normies to use AI, he is relaxed. ChatGPT grew fast because it is so easy to learn, and you can ask it to teach you how to use it. He says about 10% of the world used ChatGPT that week and he guesses 30% a year later, and he trusts human adoption over a few years.
- On universities, the ideal partnership is to "run 20 wildly different experiments." He wouldn't be upset if faculty and students just adopt AI privately.
- Prices: food should fall (he'd be disappointed if not down in a decade) and healthcare should get cheaper through cures, cheap treatments, devices and delivery. Housing is the hard one he doesn't see AI attacking directly soon.
- Books will survive but become a smaller share of how people learn.
- His St. Louis plan with a billion dollars: start a Y Combinator-like program there and get AI-focused founders to move and build companies. He calls it unique to him.

## Freedom of expression, privacy and persuasion risk

- Principle: "treat our adult users like adults," with very broad bounds on use and a high degree of privacy. The asterisk is adults "of sound mind."
- OpenAI restricted ChatGPT heavily over the summer to protect teens and to address mental health problems (such as encouraging delusion in role-play). With age-gating and mitigations in place, it will bring back and in some cases increase freedom for adults, including creative and role-playing modes.
- He was surprised by the backlash to his tweet on this, and concluded "people don't believe in freedom of expression as much as they say they do."
- Privacy: he wants AI conversations protected like those with a human doctor or lawyer. This needs legal change, which does not exist today. By revealed preference, he says people already trust AI companies this much.
- Chatbot psychosis is "a very tiny thing, but not a zero thing."
- His bigger worry is a third AI-safety category beside misuse and intentional takeover: AI models accidentally taking over. With the whole world talking to one model that keeps learning from the world, it could subtly convince people of things with no intent. He quotes a line he remembers: never believe propaganda doesn't work on you, "they just haven't found the right thing for you yet."
- On rogue agents, he says it's a question of thresholds. Most systems need no oversight, but an agent likely to self-replicate and sweep bank accounts would. He thinks the right move is to urgently solve the problem for rogue internet resources generally, since AI makes it worse.

## Government's role and OpenAI's posture

- Sam thinks that when something gets big enough, the federal government is the insurer of last resort, and he expects that for AI. He does not want it as insurer of first resort and doesn't expect that.
- He puts a significant probability on the social contract changing significantly in the post-AGI world, and sees the government getting more involved and having opinions about AI companies. But he puts almost no probability on a world where people lose all meaning because AI does everything.
- OpenAI's approach: "We just try to work with capitalism as it currently exists," partner with the government and be a good collaborator, but not have it write OpenAI's insurance policies.
- For data centers abroad, the key questions are who operates them, what workload and model weights go there, and what the security guarantees are. He compares the build to a US embassy or military base.
- He is also working on a new kind of computer and interface built for AI, with Jony Ive, whom he calls maybe the best bet to pull it off. He says the track record of such attempts is bad.
- ChatGPT is a "single-player" experience, so he doesn't think sharing queries is the social product. A new kind of social product probably exists once everyone has personal agents. AI-generated video (Sora) is doing well for both making and watching.
