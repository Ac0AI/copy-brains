## Operating a $1M ARR AI SaaS with an AI agent, while still paying human developers

Priyam runs an AI SaaS at about $1M ARR. He has an AI agent handling parts of the business (scheduling, monitoring, research, even writing code). If it ran on API pricing, he says it would cost roughly $1K/month. He still pays his dev team every month and says he is "not stopping anytime soon."

- His core claim: AI is "incredible at writing code" but "terrible at everything else that actually keeps a product alive."
- He frames the question as who keeps a live product running, not who can generate code.

## The "3 AM test": production incidents need tribal knowledge

Priyam's test: the production server crashes at 3 AM, customers are losing money, and every minute counts. Someone has to SSH in, read the logs, and recognize the cause.

- His example: a race condition with the payment webhook that only triggers when two users check out at the same moment on the annual plan.
- Fixes often depend on remembered history, such as a past fix that lived in a config file not in the repo.
- AI fails here not because it is dumb but because it lacks context. It doesn't know his infra, that staging runs a different Redis version than production, or that the Stripe webhook URL changed three months earlier and the docs were never updated.
- He argues this tribal knowledge lives in the developer's head and is "worth more than any AI can generate."

## Environment management is a context problem AI gets wrong

Priyam points to .env files, secrets management, staging vs production, and database migrations that work locally but fail in prod.

- He has watched AI assistants "confidently suggest fixes that would have nuked our production database."
- The code was not wrong; the context was. The assistant didn't know which environment it was looking at.
- One wrong environment variable can break the whole app, and the person who catches it is "the dev who's been burned before."

## Trust and accountability still sit with humans

He asks whether you would let an AI push to production with zero human review, and who takes responsibility when a deploy breaks and customers flood support.

- "AI doesn't have a phone number." It can't get on a Zoom call with your biggest customer, explain what went wrong, or feel the weight of a mistake and ensure it doesn't recur.
- Accountability to customers is a reason he keeps humans in the loop.

## What AI is actually doing to developers today

Priyam's view is that AI isn't replacing developers right now; it makes good ones faster.

- A dev who wrote 50 lines a day now writes 500.
- A dev who spent 3 hours debugging now finds the issue in 20 minutes.
- He is explicit that this is temporary: the "yet" in his title "is doing real work." He expects agents to improve at context, memory, and understanding systems, and says that in 2-3 years the 3 AM test might be solved.

## The real career risk: devs who use AI tools

- Developers get replaced first by other developers who learned AI tools, not by AI directly.
- His analogy: "The spreadsheet didn't kill accountants - it killed accountants who refused to use spreadsheets."
- Advice: don't panic today, but learn the tools, because the window where "human dev + AI tools" is the winning combination "won't stay open forever."
- His own stance: "I run an AI agent. I build AI products. And I'm still hiring humans. For now."
