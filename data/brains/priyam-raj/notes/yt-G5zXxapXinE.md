## How Priyam got into building: online ambition, a cheap-and-close college, and building for his own niche

- Priyam always wanted to make money online. He liked the idea of taking a laptop anywhere and earning, and as an Indian the low cost of living made it even more appealing.
- He chose a college very close to his house, which he calls "a cheat code". He didn't have to stay in a dorm, so he could keep using his PC setup and work alone in his room.
- He did not attend a fancy college (not an IIIT or NIT). He studied computer science at a small-city college and says he wasn't the best coder there.
- In second year, when everyone chased internships, he knew that wasn't what he wanted to do five years later. He had been watching indie hackers on Twitter making $5,000-$10,000 from passion projects and wanted to do the same.
- His filter for an idea: build something unique that solves his own personal problem. At the time "everyone was building a to-do list" or a mobile app, so he chose a web app instead.
- He was casually doing YouTube himself, which is how he noticed the gap: tools like VidIQ and TubeBuddy served YouTubers, but nothing used the new wave of AI.

## Morice.ai: the idea, the build, and the name

- The product was an AI idea generator for YouTube channels, riding the start of the AI boom and modeled on the VidIQ/TubeBuddy category.
- How it worked: enter a YouTube channel link, it fetches the last ~10 video titles, and predicts an 11th video idea for that channel. At the time it "felt like magic".
- He built it in his college computer science labs while friends watched.
- The domain morice.ai was originally bought for a different idea (a memorization tool). The ideal domain for that one was too expensive, so he reused this one.
- On naming, he says names don't matter much when you're just starting out: "I don't think it does so much if you're just starting out."
- Later he expanded the product with a title generator and a description generator.

## Early distribution: tweet the tool and tag creators

- His only marketing was tweeting about the product and tagging YouTubers on Instagram and Twitter, showing them using the tool.
- Takeoff came when a well-known creator (Randolph of Sideman) picked up one of his tweets and commented on it. That was when "Morice took off and it started to make money."
- Later, a popular YouTuber, Ishan Sharma, found the product, texted him on Twitter, and they did a podcast together, which exposed it to more people.
- Future Pedia, then the most popular AI-tools directory, also featured Morice.ai.
- By his account he had done "zero marketing other than all the tweets" when the first annual plan sold.

## Pricing and the first big signal

- Morice.ai launched with two plans: $29 per month and an annual plan of $261 per year.
- The first annual purchase ($261) arrived as a phone notification while he was at a wedding. He calls it a huge shock and the moment he truly believed it would take off.
- Why it mattered to him: $261 in one day is a lot for an Indian, it came with no marketing beyond tweets, and it felt "completely passive". Unlike service businesses, it was "an art you made" that people were now buying.
- He went home immediately and kept adding features.

## Scaling and moving to Bangalore

- He wanted to move to Bangalore for networking and to find a peer group, but it was hard to convince his parents. He took an internship as the excuse to relocate.
- He quit the internship within about two weeks, because Morice.ai was already making around ₹2 lakh a month and he believed he could scale it.
- By the end of that year it was making around ₹4 lakh per month.

## Platform risk and the decision to sell

- He grew afraid of platform risk: YouTube could ship the same feature inside the creator dashboard in a day and wipe out his revenue.
- He also didn't see himself being "the founder of Morice.ai" for ten years. He treated it as a step toward his next venture, so he chose to sell and take a large pile of capital.
- He listed it on Acquire.com, where many founders were publicly tweeting about selling their startups.
- Within a couple of weeks, Matt Parr reached out. Priyam sold Morice.ai for an undisclosed amount somewhere in six figures (USD), which he says was more than ₹1 crore. That was his first ₹1 crore online.
- Practical note: the money arrived as a large remittance from the USA, so the bank called about the transaction and he had to provide purpose codes and sort out paperwork. He found it overwhelming but worth it.

## Lessons and what came next

- His main lesson: it doesn't matter where you study or where you're from. He didn't think he was the best coder, and the point is to execute: "ideas are not that valued unless you execute them."
- He credits timing on both ends: building at the start of the AI wave and taking the exit at the right time.
- After the sale he partnered with Matt, who bought the startup, to build a new company, Vid.AI. They put $47,000 of their own money into buying the domain, and he says the startup is now worth $6 million (his figure).
