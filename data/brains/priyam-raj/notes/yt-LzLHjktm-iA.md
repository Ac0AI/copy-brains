## Priyam's path: from blogging to Moise.ai to Vidai

- At about 15, Priyam bought priyamraj.com for 135 rupees (his dad paid) and blogged about Avengers leaks and Clash of Clans tutorials. SEO worked and AdSense approved easily; he reached about $25 a day roughly 10 years ago. That was his first internet money.
- He also built random internet projects: a Fortnite season countdown site (timelite.com) and a T-Series vs PewDiePie tracker.
- He ran a gaming YouTube channel (reached 23K subscribers) but burned out chasing game news (e.g. rushing a video on a GTA 6 leak). He wanted something "more passive": code a product, release it, and see if the internet likes it.
- He built Moise.ai, a YouTube video-idea generator: enter a channel URL and get next video ideas. Pre-GPT, the existing tools were VidIQ and TubeBuddy. Big YouTubers retweeted his tweets, and he sold it about two years later for a six-figure dollar sum he says "changed my life".
- Now he founded Vidai, a SaaS that generates AI videos meant to go viral (e.g. "top 10 facts about London" with a script, templates, stock footage). It studies viral formats and replicates the pattern; he says these videos have done billions of views.
- His long-term ambition: "I want to create my own Facebook." He treats everything he builds as steps toward that and says he doesn't want to be "the Vidai guy" forever.
- He finds the best part of building is running a product people use and give feedback on, not the coding itself, which "can be delegated".

## Getting the first paying user on Twitter

- His first paid software sale (about $30, a Stripe screenshot) came from tweeting, not ads.
- He was active on Twitter in the "build in public" crowd, tweeting what he learned about people who grow on YouTube to build a small audience (50-60 people at first).
- He commented on tweets of bigger accounts (he mentions following Pat Flynn-style indie creators) for exposure.
- He then tweeted Moise.ai ("generate ideas like magic"); big YouTubers caught the tweet and it took off.
- He argues Twitter is the best platform for people in their 20s:
  - Fastest way to learn what's happening and the lowest-friction way to create content.
  - You can get initial exposure through replies to viral tweets, rather than being "at the mercy of the algorithm" as on YouTube or Instagram.
  - Serious builders and founders are reachable by DM, which opens jobs and networking.

## How Priyam finds product ideas and defends against AI disruption

- Build for your own problems. Moise.ai came from running out of YouTube ideas. His recent logs.so (a dashboard for server logs and generation quality) solved something he faced as a software founder, and Twitter liked it. Solving your own problem keeps you motivated.
- Experiment widely so you hit problems in your own life, rather than only researching markets.
- He accepts that many tools will be commoditized into "a chat away" (e.g. a Telegram bot saying "generate this"). His defense is to niche down and own distribution: a chef YouTuber's recipe app wins because it is theirs and they have the audience.
- He notes that AI-in-WhatsApp-type products got disrupted when ChatGPT shipped connectors, and that building speed matters as much as the idea.
- He cites the shift where Claude Code can replace paid tools like PDF editors and e-signature (a friend rebuilt a DocuSign alternative overnight), and thinks many simple SaaS products and AI photo-avatar apps are dying.

## Staying ahead technically and with AI tools

- His main skill, he says, is tracking fast-changing tech and adopting new tools early: "tomorrow something like Claude Code comes up, I get on it, I try to learn it as fast as possible so that I'm ahead of the race."
- OpenClaw (his favorite): software installed on a computer that you control through a chat app like Telegram. He named his instance "Alfred".
  - Product intel: he gave it every competitor URL and social account; it reports missing features (e.g. a competitor adopting Opus 4.7) and posts announcements to his team's Discord.
  - It has access to his Trello board for content planning, has its own Google Workspace email, and can review a contract he forwards and fill and return forms.
  - It is free; you pay for the underlying AI subscription. He uses the $100/month plan; many people use $20. Some buy a cheap Mac mini to host it.
  - He also experiments with Hermes, an alternative that does the same thing.
- Models: he preferred Claude until GPT 5.5 came out, which he now finds smarter, including for life advice (he gave it context on his whole life).
- Prompting: assign a role and give context. Example: for his US visa interview he told it to act as a strict consulate officer who rejects everyone, and it interviewed him.
- He built an iOS app entirely with Claude Code using Expo Go without touching the code, but says complex products like video generation software aren't there yet, and you still need coding basics.
- Video generation: Seedance (on Higgsfield) produces very realistic edits, but is too slow and not cost-optimized yet. He expects AI tokens to get expensive later as VCs stop subsidizing usage.
- Codex vs Claude: he finds Codex now better than Claude Code for him, more generous with credits.
- He also keeps a local Gemma 4 model on his phone via Google AI Edge Gallery for offline emergencies.

## When to quit, focus and experiment

- His advice to people just starting: experiment a lot (content, coding, editing), use the free resources online, and join communities in what you love. What helped him most was seeing others' Stripe screenshots ("wow this guy made $2,000"), which made it feel possible.
- Money motivated him early (a PC, a PS5). He keeps Stripe notifications on for motivation; he says three plans exist, the top at $137/month, and he got three of those in one hour during the recording.
- Choosing a stack: stay current but don't fear obsolescence. He relates this to coding, where he began at 15 or 16, pre-ChatGPT.

## Money, spending and investing in your 20s

- He invests in US stocks only in companies he uses and understands (e.g. Cloudflare), and ETFs in India.
- He says a creator-style income is not about flexing: he still uses an iPhone 16 with a cracked screen, and notes the excitement of a fancy car fades once you know you can buy many.
- Example: he spent money to move to Bangalore, where he met his co-founder in person by chance (the co-founder was flying through), which only happened because he made the move.

## Why Bangalore and rented-house stunt

- He built Moise.ai while living in Dhanbad/Jharkhand and took a short Bangalore internship (about 30 days) at openinapp.com mainly "to come to Bangalore". He then worked remotely for a Canadian company, Centerpoint Gaming, paying in USD, for stable income alongside Moise.
- Reasons for Bangalore: youthful median age, school and college friends, a large talent pool where you can find developers quickly, and an active Bangalore Twitter scene where he tweets "let's meet at this bar tonight". He calls it India's San Francisco.
- He considered Mumbai and Hyderabad too, but found Mumbai older and Hyderabad too hot.
- He tweeted at Vijay Mallya asking to rent the Kingfisher Towers villa for his birthday party "as an unhinged 20s stunt". It got major press and mostly supportive reactions, though Mallya didn't reply and it wasn't legally possible.
