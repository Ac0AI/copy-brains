## How Priyam started: build things, ship them publicly, monetize with ads

- Priyam has always liked building tools and watching people use them. That is where his enthusiasm comes from.
- He started in 2016 (age 14-15, ninth class, from Deoghar, Jharkhand). He bought priyamraj.com with his dad's help and wrote about whatever he was enthusiastic about (Marvel movie leaks, Clash of Clans tutorials), spamming links in Facebook groups.
- That blog made about $25 per day from ads, a big deal for a ninth-class student.
- He then learned coding, inspired partly by the movie The Social Network.
- His first viral project was a simple site showing live subscriber counters for the T-Series vs PewDiePie feud. It earned from display ads.
- He thinks the early start gave him "so much less to lose". He treats earning money as gamification: "money is like in-game currency" and the enjoyment is his main driver.
- He is wary of chasing stable monthly income, because once you have taken exits the stability stops mattering.

## First product, first revenue and the first exit

- His first real product was an AI YouTube video-idea generator (originally Morais.ai, now Tube Magic). He built it in late 2022, before ChatGPT. Users entered a channel's username and got video ideas tailored to it. It charged $29 a month.
- First revenue came from Twitter. He tweeted the product's use cases at popular YouTubers, e.g. addressing the Sidemen with "here's a new video idea for you", generated by his tool. About 99% ignored it, but one Sidemen member quoted the tweet and said it was "like magic", and the product went viral overnight.
- Because it went viral, many people wanted to acquire it. He listed it on acquire.com. The rate then was roughly 5.5x annual run rate, and the price was above ₹1 crore (about age 21).
- The buyer was creator Matt Par (about a million YouTube subscribers). Priyam stays on as a contractual CTO.
- He likes building products because there is an exit angle: when bored, you sell to a buyer, remove the product from your table and take capital without raising.
- He compares this to switching jobs for a higher CTC, "a builder's way".

## Vid.ai: partnering with a creator, and the $47,000 domain

- Vid.ai is an AI video generator. A user prompts something like "top 10 facts about London", picks short or long form and length, and pays credits. Entry price is $20 a month. It launched in August 2024.
- It makes about $70,000 a month in revenue. API costs run about $10-12K, excluding ads and team. Video is the most expensive thing to generate with AI, so pricing is deliberately not cheap. The $20 plan allows limited video volume. He says they keep roughly 60-70% margin.
- The team is 3-4 people, mostly contractors. He delegates customer support (thousands of customers), noting AI support tools exist but a few people are still worth having at scale.
- He started it because similar tools already existed and he believed he could build a better one. He prefers building something already validated, or something where he has an unfair advantage.
- His unfair advantage was the creator who bought his first product. The creator promoted Vid.ai in about 3-4 videos at launch, and the product reached about $50K MRR within a couple of days.
- He and that creator bought the vid.ai domain for $47,000 (about ₹39 lakh), split between them. His reasoning: the name could be the biggest AI video generation brand, and it makes the company look capable. They earned back the money in about a month. A domain is also a resalable asset.
- Growth now comes from sponsoring influencers, Facebook ads, and Instagram pages making videos about the product.
- He listed Vid.ai for $1.5 million. Reasons: he sees products as stairs toward a bigger thing ("I want to find my Facebook"), growth has stagnated, and a funded buyer could scale it. He says large AI video companies are interested, and the vid.ai domain makes it attractive.

## Creator partnerships as the moat against AI platforms

- His biggest lesson: partnering with creators took his solo products from about $3-4K MRR to roughly 10x that.
- Defending against OpenAI or Claude launches: "having a very specific creator under your sleeve". A recipe app can be one-shotted in Claude, but an app by a million-subscriber cooking creator still sells because "it's theirs".
- For Vid.ai, the partner is a creator whose audience wants to make money with videos, so that audience keeps paying: "the moat is him". You can "rent this sort of thing" by partnering with a creator.
- He sold his first product partly because he expected platform risk: Claude can now just give video ideas.
- Content is the best way to buy trust. An audience is an asset that cannot be taken from you (apart from platform bans), and it means he does not start from scratch after an exit. He recently hit 10K on Instagram.
- His advice: either make content (even as a bad creator you build an audience) or build good relationships with creators.

## How to pick, validate and differentiate a product

- Think about marketing before building. Build something you can talk about publicly so the target customer can see it. Early Morais.ai marketing was free: talking about how to generate YouTube ideas and mentioning the audience.
- Finding ideas: solve a problem from your own life (his own examples: idea generator because he needed Marvel video ideas; TDS/GST filing automation is on his mind now). Otherwise browse subreddits on a topic you are enthusiastic about. You need enthusiasm because you must work without money at first.
- Validate before building: post a thread in a relevant subreddit asking "would you pay for a tool like this?", then make content or tweets aimed at that segment, and build only after interest shows. Now you can one-shot a landing page and tweet it; if nobody is interested, it is a broken product.
- Anything Claude can do in a prompt is a broken product (e.g. PDF summarizer or chat-with-PDF no longer works).
- Differentiating in a crowded market: big tools like Trello or Zoho are bloated. Take one feature for one segment and make it better than the big companies do. Saturation is "not the moat anymore"; "taste is still a moat" because a lot of AI-built tools are "AI slop" with poor user experience.
- As a beginner, do not invent something new (nobody may want it). Copy a popular idea and improve it or change the angle.
- Know when to quit. If a product has no customers, pivot or shut it. He has shut many zero-revenue projects. Recent mixed results: logs.so (not great, may pivot), TypeVoice (dictation tool, not doing well; many software-engineer users said they would build it themselves), Drobe (wardrobe organizer app).
- Software engineers are a poor segment to sell software to now, since they think they can build it themselves.

## Posting revenue publicly and his one-person holding company

- He posts MRR publicly. Benefits: followers so he doesn't start from zero, easier networking and hiring (he can tweet for a video editor, though people may overcharge), and inbound acquisition interest in his DMs.
- Transparent revenue also helped when payment processors were a risk. When his Stripe account was banned after disputes (customers disputing with card companies instead of asking for refunds), his Twitter followers helped him get it resolved. He says public revenue makes you look transparent to payment companies.
- X is very important to him. The person who acquired his company did little due diligence and said he "stalked" his X and Instagram and he seemed normal. Authentic, unpolished posting signals you are a real person in the AI era. He checks developers' X accounts when hiring and prefers builders posting enthusiastic projects over generic LinkedIn content.
- He describes his structure as a one-person holding company: Vid.ai as the main earner, an ElevenLabs voice clone earning royalties, and many risky bets. Many tries let you find what you enjoy (he found content and UI/UX design fun).
- Voice side income: he trained a voice clone with ElevenLabs by reading Wikipedia articles for about 30 minutes. It is one of the most popular Indian voices on the platform and earns royalties each time used, about ₹32-33K a month now, with a peak of about ₹60,000.
- His view: with AI, ₹1 crore a year as a solo person is much more possible today. Distribution is the moat; you can build it for no money by making reels or tweeting about a problem segment.

## Advice for starters and building from India

- First step: find a problem, validate it on Reddit or similar, make content for that segment, then build.
- Mistakes to avoid:
  - Wasting time on research. "We get so much dopamine by trying to research", but progress only comes from execution. Get on Reddit or Claude and start.
  - Inventing something totally new.
  - Registering a company and chasing compliance too early. In India, Dodo Payments (a Stripe alternative) lets you start without GST registration.
- India challenges he faced: getting USD payments in an INR country, Stripe ban risk from disputes, difficulty giving equity to foreign partners because of compliance (a bigger-scale problem), and reels in an Indian accent being pushed mostly to Indian audiences. Workarounds: ElevenLabs voices, a VPN for TikTok when targeting non-Indian users.
- He rates skills over knowledge, because skills let you execute, though you need some balance.
- He sees solo billion-dollar companies as plausible in about two years, and expects less UI interaction as users connect products to Claude and prompt instead.
- He invests via a Nasdaq 100 ETF, index funds and mutual funds, and picks stocks only in the US market (companies he uses, such as Cloudflare), using Claude for advice.
