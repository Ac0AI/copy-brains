## Priyam's path: from blogging at 14 to selling a product for 1 crore at 21

- He started in 2016 (age 14-15, from Dhanbad/Jharkhand) by buying priyam.com with his dad's help and writing about whatever he was enthusiastic about (Marvel leaks, Clash of Clans tutorials). He spammed the links in Facebook groups and made about $25 a day from display ads.
- He taught himself coding and built a simple site showing live subscriber counters for the T-Series vs PewDiePie feud. It went viral and was his first viral project.
- His pattern: build things he is personally enthusiastic about, put them in public, and let distribution come from posting.
- His first real product was an idea generator for YouTubers (called moise.ai, built in late 2022, before ChatGPT). You enter a channel's username and get tailored video ideas. He charged $29 a month.
- He sold it on Acquire.com for just over 1 crore at age 21. The going rate then was about 5.5x annual run rate (he says it is different now).
- He sees exits as a core appeal of building products: "once you're bored you can exit your thing" and take capital without raising. The product later became TubeMagic, and he is still a contractual CTO there.

## How the first product got traction: tweeting at creators

- His first revenue came from Twitter. He tweeted real use cases, tagging popular YouTubers with "here's a new video idea for you", generated by his tool.
- About 99% of those tweets were ignored. One creator (from the Sideman channel) quote-tweeted him saying it was "like magic", and the product went viral overnight and started making money.
- His takeaway: show the product working on a specific person's channel, in public, and only one reply needs to land.
- On zero-budget marketing in general, he says to think about marketing before committing time to building. Build something you can talk about publicly in a way your target customer will see.

## Vid.ai: partnering with a creator for distribution

- Vid.ai (an AI video generator, launched August 2024) has an entry plan of $20 a month, with credits charged per video. It makes about $60-70k a month.
- The founding insight: he saw similar tools already validated and believed he could build a better one. "Building something that's already validated is such a better odd thing to do."
- He used an unfair advantage: the creator who had acquired his earlier product (a YouTuber with about 1M subscribers) became his partner. That creator made three or four videos about Vid.ai at launch, and the product reached about $50,000 MRR within a couple of days.
- They paid $47,000 (about 39 lakh rupees) for the vid.ai domain, split with the creator. He says it earned the money back in about a month. His reasoning: it sounds like the definitive AI video generator name, it is a resaleable asset, and a bold purchase builds a story.
- Costs: at $60-70k revenue they spend about $10-12k on API costs, excluding ads and team. Video is the most expensive thing to generate with AI, so pricing is set so the entry plan limits usage while keeping 60-70% savings.
- He sponsors influencers and runs Facebook ads, but his creator partnership means he doesn't need heavy ad spend. Instagram pages that post videos about the product are part of the setup.
- The team is three or four people, mostly contractors. He delegates customer support (thousands of customers) and wants to keep delegating it.
- He listed Vid.ai for sale at $1.5M. His reasons: growth has stagnated, it needs more scale and funds, and he treats products as "stairs" toward his bigger thing (he cites Zuckerberg building many things before Facebook). He has talked with large AI video companies and says he would likely have gotten a higher price if he had sold the year before.

## Defending a product against AI labs and commoditization

- His test: "anything that Claude can do in a prompt, I wouldn't do that." A PDF summarizer is a broken product now because people will upload to Claude or ChatGPT.
- Make the product more complex, and focus on saving someone real time.
- Take one feature of a bloated tool (he mentions Trello and Zoho) for one segment and make it better than the big companies do. If you focus on one part of an app, you can beat them there.
- "Taste is still a moat." There is lots of one-prompt AI slop, and good user experience stands out.
- The strongest defense is distribution through a creator. A recipe app built by a cooking creator with a million subscribers survives because people want that creator's app. The same applies to Vid.ai with a creator teaching how to make money with video: "the moat is him". You can rent this by partnering with a creator.
- He sold his first product partly because he knew Claude would eventually replicate idea generation.
- Audience is an asset that can't be taken from you (platforms aside). Having one means he doesn't start from scratch after an exit.

## Starting out: validate fast, copy, ship

- Step one: find a problem in your own life. He gives the example of automating GST and TDS filings. His first product came from asking what Marvel videos to make next. If you have none, browse subreddits for a topic you are enthusiastic about.
- Enthusiasm matters because "you'll have to be motivated without making money" for a while.
- Validate before building: post a thread in the relevant subreddit asking "would you pay for a tool like this", then make content about it (tweets, reels, whatever you are good at) to attract that segment. Only then build.
- Today you can one-shot a prompt for a landing page, tweet about it, and see if people are interested. If nobody is, the product is broken.
- Biggest mistake to avoid: endless research. "We feel like we're actually progressing when we're actually not. The only progress comes in with execution."
- Don't invent something new as a beginner; copy a popular idea and make it better or give it a different angle, since a never-built idea risks having no buyers.
- Skip company registration and GST early. In India, use Dodo Payments (a Stripe alternative) rather than getting stuck in compliance.
- His top lessons: start as soon as possible, build relationships with creators, make content (even if you are a bad creator you build an audience), and don't waste time researching. Creator partnerships took him from about $3-4k MRR as a solo builder to about 10x that.

## Building in public and posting revenue

- He posts revenue publicly because it gave him a following, so he doesn't start from scratch. It helps with hiring and networking (he can tweet that he needs a video editor), and it generates inbound acquisition interest.
- It's a double-edged sword (people who see your revenue may overcharge you), but he thinks the access outweighs that.
- It also makes payment processors see you as more transparent. When his Stripe account was banned after disputes, his Twitter followers helped him get it resolved. Chargebacks (customers disputing instead of asking for a refund) are a common risk in software.
- His X account was effectively the diligence for his sale: the buyer said he had just looked at his X and Instagram and decided he seemed like a normal person, and paid without verifying anything else.
- He also checks hiring candidates' X accounts and prefers developers who post enthusiastic, real projects over polished LinkedIn content.

## Operating as a one-person holding company

- He runs many bets: a main product (Vid.ai), a sold product, others that fail, and passive income such as his cloned voice on ElevenLabs. His voice is one of the more popular Indian voices on the platform and earned roughly 60,000 rupees a month at peak and about 32-33k rupees a month lately, with no ongoing work.
- He shuts down projects that get no customers, such as one called "logs", and pivots or moves to the next one. Having many bets makes failures less painful.
- Recent products include a wardrobe organizer app (Droby) and a voice dictation tool (typevoice.com), which he says has not done that well so far. People in his audience, many of them developers, said they would just build it themselves, so he notes software engineers are a poor segment to sell to.
- Trying many internet things shows what you enjoy; for him that grew to include content and UI/UX design.
- He works asynchronously by text and voice messages, with few meetings. He thinks reaching 1 crore a year as a solo person is "very very more possible today".
- He currently invests in ETFs, index funds and mutual funds, plus US stock picking in companies he uses as a customer (he names Cloudflare). He used Claude to simplify his investment decisions.
