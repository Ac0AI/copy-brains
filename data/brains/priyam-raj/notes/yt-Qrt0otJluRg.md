## Priyam's background and what a solopreneur is
- Priyam started tinkering with code at 16-17. In school he built tnit.com, a one-page site with a countdown to the next Fortnite season, and made about $1,000 a month from AdSense alone.
- He learned more coding in college (BIT Mesra) and moved to bigger products. His main product is Morise.ai (the name comes from "Memorize" with "memo" removed; he was originally building an education tool).
- He defines a solopreneur as someone who learns one building skill (iOS, web apps, etc.) and then does everything: engineering, design, marketing, getting first customers and sustaining the product alone.
- He points to Pieter Levels as the model: many products built solo, about $50,000 a month per app with simple things. He recommends Levels' book "Make", Indie Hackers and Hacker News for stories.

## Why indie building beats a job, and the lifestyle
- He says you work any hours you want, attend no meetings, and have no cap on income. He was making 3 to 4 lakhs a month at the time.
- You own every decision: if you want a feature, you ship it, and nobody has to approve it.
- You meet interesting users. He saw Morise sign-ups from Disney India, CoinDCX, MrBeast and Sidemen.
- He sees remote jobs and FAANG roles as fine in their own right; the difference is autonomy and uncapped upside.
- His motive is simply that he loves building: "For me, it was just about building something cool." He compares coding to art: people make art of their own, not only for someone else's job.

## Finding an idea and riding a wave
- Morise came from a gap he noticed: YouTube tooling was dominated by VidIQ and TubeBuddy, and he wanted to use the AI hype to build a tool that generates titles, descriptions and tags in one go, removing the friction of "what should I put here?" at upload time.
- He says being into the hype matters. Stay active on Twitter and follow whatever you want to build (AI, game dev, etc.).
- Look at small, simple paid tools for proof: a Twitter auto-retweet tool that does nothing else charges $7 a month and people pay.
- Search the #buildinpublic hashtag, and follow Pieter Levels' following list and retweets to find the community.
- He validates demand by checking whether people already pay humans to do the job. People pay on Fiverr and Upwork for titles, so he saw a market for a tool.
- Prioritise features by how much time they save the user.

## Building: stack, MVP and polish
- Use the tech stack you already know. He likes Postgres and Next.js for web apps; iOS needs Xcode and a Mac. Don't learn new tech just because it's trendy.
- Don't overcomplicate. His first mistake was avoiding Next.js, which he already knew, just because everyone used it. He cites Levels: use anything, even old PHP.
- Reuse open source instead of reinventing: for YouTube captions he searched for existing NPM libraries. "Open source was created for the very reason that people could use each other's code."
- Learn from official documentation, not courses, because courses go stale (Apple keeps changing Xcode and Swift). Beginners can use YouTube or Udemy.
- No-code can work: he has seen no-code apps at $5,000 MRR, though he is not experienced with them.
- MVP: the most basic version with a basic UI, just to show the function works. Show it to potential users and get feedback before investing more.
- He follows common design patterns so users aren't puzzled. He drafts features as bullet points in Notion and keeps the feature set small and niche (compare Sidebird, which only retweets).
- Polish after the MVP: pull from component libraries, then do backend work like error handling (e.g. if OpenAI doesn't respond, show a clear message, not a raw code error). Assume the user knows nothing.

## Getting customers and feedback
- Product Hunt: his first customers came from there. Morise ranked 4th that day. Upvotes are what count, like Reddit.
- Twitter #buildinpublic: log progress. YouTubers followed him and said they'd buy when it was done.
- Directories: AI directories such as Futurepedia and Future Tools featured Morise for free without being asked. Apple can feature apps, but you depend on them.
- SEO is his favourite channel. Publish blog posts on the product's own domain (e.g. "How to use AI to write YouTube titles") with a hook to the product. Backlinks help indexing: he launched in Feb 2023 and within two or three weeks got search traffic, mostly people searching the brand name.
- Feedback sources: for consumer apps ask friends; for niche B2B use Twitter and cold email.
- When customers arrive, reach out (not spammy) and ask about price, features and bugs. Marketing comes once the product is solid.
- Marketing channels he mentions: Facebook ads, and cold email (for YouTubers, email the address in the channel's About section). He says marketing is the part he is least experienced in.
- Second mistake: not talking to big-name users who signed up. He just posted them on stories and Twitter instead of engaging.

## Pricing, payments and recurring revenue
- In India, Stripe is the easiest way to take payments; it doesn't require a company at the start.
- Price by what the market already pays. Upwork rates suggested $30 a month was reasonable for Morise.
- B2B buyers (YouTube channels are businesses) will pay more than consumers; consumer apps like photo editors should be cheap.
- One-time products work when there are no server costs, e.g. his Safari extension Burn Feed (a feed blocker), priced at Apple's $3 tier. The downside is you can't retain customers or count on sales; a feature by Apple can bring 1,000 buyers one month and nothing the next.
- Subscriptions are a better model: "If I take a break, I can still have all my old customers" paying monthly. He took a break from marketing and travelled, and existing customers kept paying.

## Bootstrapping, costs and selling a startup
- Costs were minimal: a .ai domain at ₹12,000 for two years. A .in domain would also have worked.
- He stayed bootstrapped. Investors approached him, but he chose not to have people over his head asking for "add this, add that."
- If you lose interest, you can exit by selling. Marketplaces: Flippa and Acquire.com (which he says takes about 4% of the sale).
- Buyers may want to fold the product into their own, or have the infrastructure to build what the founder can't (he wanted a thumbnail generator that needed heavier image handling).
- Valuation: search "I sold my startup" tweets for comparables. Roughly 5-6x annual revenue on average (his example: 10 lakhs a year sells for 50-60 lakhs). Agencies sell lower because of the work involved; products sell higher; smaller startups can get higher multiples, while heavily scaled ones get lower.
- Launching on Product Hunt, Twitter and Hacker News gives a global audience who can pay more and give different feedback.
