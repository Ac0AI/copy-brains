## Prioritization means doing the unglamorous work, not your craft

Ryan argues that startups succeed or fail on what founders choose to do and not do, and that "not everything on the to do list is fun. Sometimes you have to do 'shitty' work."

- Typical startup to-do list he cites: support emails, bugs, newsletter, pitch deck, blog post, homepage design, Twitter ads experiments, new on-boarding flow, marketing events.
- Successful startups "require attention in all areas." Even without a marketing title, someone has to figure out how to acquire users.
- Prioritization is paramount because speed is a startup's most valuable asset. He quotes Paul Graham: "What's important about startups is the speed."
- Building a successful product and startup is "all about knowing what to do and what not to do."

## Founders gravitate to their craft because it is comforting

Ryan's core diagnosis: people prioritize what they like to do over what they should do.

- Coders like to code, salespeople like to sell, designers like to design.
- "We prioritize our craft because it's comforting. It's what we're best at." But the craft is not what the company needs at all times.
- Example he heard from a solo founder of an early social product: he built the product in a few weeks, but four months later still hadn't figured out getting users. His words: "I don't enjoy marketing. I just want to code."
- Ryan says he has made this mistake himself and hears this pattern often.

## Wireframes to nowhere: his own PlayHaven mistake

Early at PlayHaven, as the sole product manager, Ryan was responsible for designing new product concepts.

- He spent nights and weekends on high-fidelity wireframes, agonizing over text field alignment and copy that "didn't matter one bit at the time."
- One wireframe took days for a consumer app discovery concept the company never built. He says they should have validated market need and business feasibility instead of "pixel shifting for a product no one wanted."
- What he should have done instead:
  - Talked to customers before designing features he only thought they wanted.
  - Gotten engineering input on feasibility and tradeoffs before jumping into design.
  - Sketched first and used paper prototypes to bring ideas to life in less time.
- Many wireframed concepts were never built, or had to be adjusted after talking to engineering and customers. He concludes that doing the less fun, important things first would have saved the company time.

## Fake progress: why craft work feels like winning

Ryan calls this the "delusion of progress." Wireframing was fun and the results were rewarding, so it "felt like progress," and the wireframes became "a trophy" for late nights.

- A founder friend described the same sensation with code: "It's incredibly satisfying to build a product... Each GitHub commit feels like progress. So I keep coding even though I know I should be focusing on marketing."
- Why code is seductive: it is responsive and predictable. Write `alert('Hello World!');` and the app responds with "Hello World!" immediately.
- Why marketing and sales are less rewarding: people are "unpredictable and non-responsive," and their tastes, expectations and context keep changing. That means less certainty and delayed reward, which reduces motivation to invest in these "non-rewarding" activities, especially if you don't enjoy them.

## How to combat fake progress

Ryan's remedy is self-accountability through a daily "why" check.

- Each morning when reviewing your to-do list, ask why for every item.
- Example questions: Why should I create wireframes for a new landing page? Why should I add Facebook integration? Why should I attend the Lean Startup Conference?
- He says this simple, honest question "may have saved me a lot of time and effort."
- The goal is to spot work that feels productive but isn't what the business needs right now, and to do the shitty-but-important work instead.
