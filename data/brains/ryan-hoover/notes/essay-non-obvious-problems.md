## Framework: obvious vs. non-obvious problems

Ryan sorts startup ideas on two axes: whether the *problem* is obvious, and whether the *solution* is obvious. He borrows the logic from early-stage venture investing, where the best outcomes come from being right and non-consensus. In his words: "I've seen a ton of startups from my time building Product Hunt and now investing in early-stage opportunities at Weekend Fund." He says most founders pursue obvious problems, and he is most interested in founders who tackle non-obvious ones.

## The venture analogy behind it

- Investing in a company that is wrong loses money, whether the bet was consensus or non-consensus.
- Investing in a company that is right and consensus makes outsized returns harder, because the market already priced in the potential when the check was written.
- Investing in a company that is right and non-consensus is the most likely path to outlier returns, because the market hasn't priced in what you saw early.
- Ryan notes outliers exist, and that this is the nature of early-stage investing.

## Obvious problem, obvious solution: unlikely to succeed

- There is likely a graveyard of similar attempts that failed for legitimate reasons.
- Dozens of competitors may chase the same idea, making it hard to break through the noise and attract customers.
- Example: an app to meet up with friends (obvious problem) where the user broadcasts their location (obvious solution). Ryan says Product Hunt has dozens of products that tried this over the years.

## Obvious problem, non-obvious solution: might succeed

- The solution may only be possible now because of technology or cultural shifts. It may be the first time the problem can be solved, or a dramatically better answer to a long-standing problem.
- It can also come from an "earned secret" or insight from the founder's past experience.
- Downside: fast-followers chasing the same obvious problem. Once the solution is known, competitors can replicate it and sell into their existing, highly relevant customer base.
- Example: calendar scheduling is an obvious problem. Calendly introduced a clever UX pattern in 2013 that flipped scheduling workflows into a simple booking system, dramatically reducing friction.

## Non-obvious problem (obvious or non-obvious solution): the blue ocean

- Far less competition, and the potential to be the leader of a new category.
- Target customers are less inundated with solutions and likely more receptive.
- Non-obvious problems are often driven by a change in the world: a new problem, a problem affecting a growing market, or a problem becoming more painful.
- Example: Deel (a Weekend Fund portfolio company). In 2018 it focused on remote payroll for globally distributed teams. At the time this wasn't seen as an obvious problem and was discounted as too "niche". The market later exploded, partly because of COVID and cultural shifts in how people live and work.
- Ryan's conclusion: "Perhaps the biggest blue ocean opportunities are in non-obvious problems."

## How to find non-obvious problems

Non-obvious problems require a unique insight or rare perspective. Ryan's prompts for founders:

- What unique problems have you faced in your personal life or work?
- Which problems seem to be getting worse?
- Have you hacked together a solution to a problem few people have identified?
- Do you have a strong view of the future, and what problems would emerge if your prediction is right?
- Are you seeing recurring complaints from people in your network or communities?

He adds the caveat that startup success still depends on solving a real problem with the right solution, and that founders should place their idea along these axes.
