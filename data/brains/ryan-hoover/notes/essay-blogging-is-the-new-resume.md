## Blogging replaces the resume as proof of how you think

- Ryan argues resumes are crummy: an 8.5x11 sheet can't communicate a life's accomplishments or skill set, and he questions whether anyone reads them.
- In his view they only weed out obvious "hell no" candidates.
- Hiring managers lean on trusted recommendations and on tangible things an applicant has accomplished.
- Ryan's example of a good resume is GitHub for software developers. Public repos, breadth across languages, open source work and side projects show what someone has built, so it "shows" rather than "tells".
- For non-technical roles (product managers, UX designers and similar) his GitHub equivalent is a blog. His thesis: "Blogging is new resume."
- A blog shows expertise, personality and above all thought process. How someone thinks signals ability and culture fit, which a resume lacks entirely.

## How blogging opened doors for Ryan

- He started actively blogging about a year before, writing 1-3 essays a week.
- He wrote for fun and to learn, on startups, product design, UX and psychology. The research and reflection sharpened his expertise, and the more he wrote, the better he wrote.
- Early pieces hit the front page of Hacker News (for example "Email-First Startups" and "Stop Using NoReply"). His Twitter following grew and he gained attention in tech and blogging communities.
- His writing was featured in PandoDaily, FastCo and Forbes, and on Andrew Chen's and Nir Eyal's blogs.
- When he announced he was leaving PlayHaven with no plan, he was nervous. The network he built through blogging reached out with support, and several followers offered him jobs and introductions to founders.
- He credits his professional experience as instrumental, but says blogging "amplified peoples' interest in me and helped market my expertise."

## Blogging as an evergreen resume, even at low volume

- Ryan says not everyone needs his level of dedication. Regardless of exposure, a blog is a vehicle to share ideas, expertise and interests, "an evergreen resume."
- His example is Lenny Hu. Ryan first met him through a post, "LetsLunch Landing Page", where Lenny redesigned the homepage and explained his thought process.
- The post stayed memorable a year later because it showed proactiveness and ability.
- Lenny kept writing product deconstructions and product design thoughts. Despite a small following and infrequent posting, he built a strong, long-lasting resume.
- It paid off: Lenny messaged Ryan that he'd been hired as a short-term consultant for a stealth startup who "saw my blog."

## Blogging to build a product and seed a community

- Ryan and Nathan Bashaw launched Product Hunt, a site to share and discover products.
- They seeded the community largely through relationships formed via his writing, inviting several dozen well-respected startup founders, investors and product people to participate.
- After a private release they announced it in the press (Pando) and to his audience, gaining another 1,000 users over the next week.
- He says none of this would have been feasible 12 months earlier, before he had built a network of supporters through writing.

## Six cautions before you start blogging

- **Enjoy it.** It's hard to sustain without intrinsic motivation, and you'll quit. Frequent writing isn't mandatory, but the more you put in, the more you get out.
- **It's a long-term investment.** Your first, second, third or 50th post is unlikely to go viral. Accept that most people don't care what you think and that quantity leads to quality.
- **It's not about you.** Readers don't care how awesome you are. An occasional humblebrag is OK, but constant self-promotion about you or your startup's successes turns readers away.
- **Write about your passions, not trends.** Don't write about growth hacking because it's in vogue; write about it only if genuinely passionate. Topics without mass appeal are fine because others share the interest.
- **You don't know everything.** Have conviction but humility. When criticizing people or startups, remember you lack full context; they may have specific reasons or data outsiders aren't privy to.
- **Don't let it become a distraction.** Once you have an audience and habit it's hard to stop, but blogging shouldn't substitute for the important, less enjoyable work in your startup or life. His advice: "Do 'shitty' work."

## Using writing in place of a resume

- When startups ask about his background and expertise, Ryan replies with a brief bio and a few select articles relevant to their situation.
- His logic: if you read his writing you know how he thinks. If you agree with his analysis and recommendations, you'll trust his product decisions. If you disagree, "then we shouldn't work together anyway."
- He says resumes fail to communicate any of this, and he hopes never to touch his resume again.
