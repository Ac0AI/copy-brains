## Org structure, communication, and the founder's voice

- Ryan ran Product Hunt with a very flat structure for years, aiming to encourage autonomy and mutual respect. It backfired: the lack of structure and clear ownership produced the opposite effect, and 15+ direct reports didn't scale.
- His conclusion: "Org charts and titles aren't evil."
- He says a CEO should feel like they're repeating themselves. When a teammate asks "What are we building? Why?", it's the founder's fault for not communicating mission and strategy clearly and frequently enough to people who are heads down on one part of the business.
- A founder's word carries heavy weight, during their tenure and after they leave. The ideas a founder promotes or shoots down can shift the team's focus and motivation, for better or worse. He points to a Jason Fried post on this.

## Hiring and letting people go

- He calls "Bad hires might kill your company" a startup cliche because it's true.
- Startups inevitably hire people who aren't a good fit. Ryan has let go of a few people at Product Hunt.
- It wasn't because they were bad people. Sometimes the person simply isn't right for the product, culture, or stage of the company at that time. In most cases, he says, they know that too.

## Distributed teams

- Product Hunt has been distributed since the beginning. The team spans seven timezones: UK, France, Bulgaria, India, Canada, New Zealand, and the U.S.
- Benefits: it can hire talent from anywhere, not just the <1% who live in or can relocate to San Francisco. It also gives the team a global perspective that mirrors its global community.
- Limits: he says distributed teams "won't work for everyone," especially companies that scale to hundreds of people, although he notes a few seem to have figured it out.
- His headline view is that distributed teams are the future, but not for everyone.

## Why redesigns almost never work

- Ryan says "Redesigns" almost never work. Product Hunt wasted a lot of time redesigning its homepage.
- The trap for someone proud of the product they ship is making it look better and fiddling with pixels without questioning the impact.
- Questions he says to ask first:
  - "Is this actually going to change how people use the product?"
  - "Will it meaningfully impact our metrics?"
- His rule: don't let ego get in the way of prioritizing what's right for the business and users.

## Investors: build relationships before you need them

- Many advise founders to avoid investors until it's time to fundraise. Ryan recommends limiting the number of "coffee meetings" because they can be a major distraction.
- Even so, he stresses that investing is largely relationship-driven: the more investors trust you, the easier it is when you raise.
- Choosing the right investors matters because once they're on the cap table, it's difficult to get them off.

## Plan for acquisition and keep optionality

- Acquisitions, like fundraising, are often relationship-driven, maybe more so.
- Ryan says founders tend to pridefully claim they'll never sell without giving a single thought to who might buy the company. His advice: map out potential acquirers early and start planting seeds, because "optionality is key."
- Example: he says that if Naval hadn't invested in Product Hunt's 2014 seed round, he's not sure AngelList would have acquired Product Hunt.

## Context on the lessons

- Product Hunt started in November 2013 as an experiment announced via a tweet, and Ryan applied to Y Combinator (S14) with a video. The team was invited to join the S14 batch.
- He says much of this advice is specific to building a venture-backed business. Bootstrapped SaaS may look very different, and "there's no right way to startup."
- His broader reflection: starting a company and raising money is abnormal, and founders learn on the job. A 20-page college business plan taught him mostly what not to do.
