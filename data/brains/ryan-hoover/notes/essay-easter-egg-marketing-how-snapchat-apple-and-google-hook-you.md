## Easter eggs as a marketing channel

Ryan argues that Easter eggs (hidden features or inside jokes) are a powerful form of marketing even though they cut against the usual advice that apps should be intuitive and accessible. He calls this "the nature of Easter eggs is an oxymoron": hiding functionality violates user expectations, and that violation is what makes it work.

He credits Easter eggs with three marketing effects:
- **Get people talking.** People love knowing secrets but like sharing them even more.
- **Bring people together.** Shared secrets separate people "in the know" from outsiders, which forms a bond among holders of the secret and strengthens a community.
- **Build a brand.** Easter eggs are fun and produce a "WOW!" effect, creating a memorable connection between brand and user through a shared understanding not privy to everyone.

He recommends considering how to build Easter eggs into any product or service to increase engagement, establish a community, get people talking and build a memorable brand.

## The Snapchat black-and-white filter example

- Ryan received a black-and-white Snapchat from a friend and asked when Snapchat had added filters.
- The friend proudly explained that typing the caption "B&W…" activates a hidden black-and-white filter.
- Ryan immediately sent a dozen B&W Snapchats to friends, anticipating their curious replies "because I knew something they did not."
- The lesson: the secret spread person to person because each recipient wanted the thrill of being the one who knows. Knowing a secret creates the urge to share it and to watch the other person's reaction.
- The filter is one of many hidden Snapchat Easter eggs.

## Apple Siri: hidden answers as free marketing

- When Siri launched, Easter eggs "bombarded the internet." People couldn't get enough of Siri's unexpected, witty answers to hidden questions and phrases.
- Blogs, TV shows and social networks exploded in discussion, and websites dedicated to Siri's Easter eggs sprang up. A Google search for "siri funny questions" returned over a million results.
- Ryan argues the free marketing from these Easter eggs was "arguably more impactful than Apple's multi-million dollar advertising budget."
- Mechanism: the secretive, clever nature of the eggs pushed users to explore the product to uncover its secrets. Users who cracked one got bragging rights and shared the discovery with friends. As a result "Siri became a celebrity as people promoted her wit."

## Lyft's pink mustache: hiding the brand behind a mystery

- Ryan overheard people on Howard St. in San Francisco asking, "What are those pink mustaches for?", and saw two other groups ask the same question that week.
- Lyft decorated its fleet with furry pink mustaches instead of plastering its brand name on the side panels. The mustache acted as an Easter egg that caught attention and got people talking.
- After seeing half a dozen mustache-sporting cars, "one is bound to resolve the mystery." When people asked friends or searched online to understand it, Lyft became more memorable.
- Why it matters: in competitive markets, recall and top-of-mind awareness determine which transportation service someone turns to the moment they need a ride. Lyft stood out from competitors by hiding its brand behind an Easter egg.

## Google: playful surprises and hidden search terms

- Google replaces its logo with Google Doodles for holidays, special events and anniversaries. Examples Ryan cites include an interactive synthesizer (Robert Moog's 78th birthday) and a zamboni mini-game. Rotating doodles spur conversation and give people a reason to visit the homepage to see what others are talking about.
- Less obvious but "arguably more impactful" are hidden search terms. Typing "do a barrel roll" into google.com makes the screen rotate. Released in 2011, it was still spreading in 2013, with hundreds of tweets about it that week.
- Ryan's point: even as a multi-billion dollar corporation, Google shows playfulness and charms users with surprises. Easter eggs add no value to the core offering, but they give the brand a personality and get users to market the product by forwarding it to friends.

## Why the tactic works (summary of the mechanics)

- Hidden things reward exploration, so users engage with the product beyond its core use.
- Discovery gives users bragging rights, which turns them into distributors who share with friends.
- Spread is word-of-mouth and often self-sustaining: the barrel roll kept circulating two years after release.
- It is cheap relative to paid advertising (the Siri comparison), and it works for physical products as well as software (Lyft's mustaches).
- Ryan ends the essay with the tactic applied to the reader: "now that you know the secret, don't you want to share it?"
