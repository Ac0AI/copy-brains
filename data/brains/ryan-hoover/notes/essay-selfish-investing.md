## Why Ryan invests in products he wants as a customer

- Ryan's startup advice: build for yourself, as he did with Product Hunt. He says there is "no right way to start a startup", but solving your own problem has three advantages:
  - It is easier to empathize with the audience and know what to build when you are the customer.
  - It is more convincing to future hires and investors when you can speak firsthand about the problem.
  - It is more fun.
- He applies the same approach to investing.
- Every morning he gets a flood of startups to potentially back. At Weekend Fund they avoid imposing too much of their own opinion on what the future looks like. That leads them down many rabbit holes, from AI-powered litigation tech (Justpoint) to "boring" payroll software (Deel).
- Personally, he gets most excited talking to founders building products he wants. He says this leads to rich jam sessions and, he hopes, better input from him as the potential customer.
- He closes by inviting founders in these spaces to email him, joking that he might give them money "as an investor and customer".

## Distributed living and Edge Health: two themes drawn from his own life

**Distributed living**
- Ryan believes that despite the pendulum narrative back to in-office work, distributed work is here to stay and adoption will only increase.
- He lives in multiple cities and prefers it: he sees more friends, finds inspiration in new scenery, and expands his experiences.
- The pain points he lists:
  - Where to stay.
  - Whether to rent out his place while away.
  - Which friends are in town.
  - Whether to pause gym memberships.
  - Whether to pack four weeks of supplements.
- With kids, he notes, everything becomes dramatically more complicated.
- An EA (he mentions Athena) can handle logistics, but most people don't have one.
- Example he cites: Mozie, a private network for meeting up with friends, focused on sharing future travel dates.

**Edge health**
- In his mid-30s, five years ago, he realized he was mortal and became more proactive about health. He has experimented with peptides, regularly gets bloodwork drawn, and occasionally gets IPL/microneedle treatment.
- The problem is a dizzying number of potential solutions. Some startups treat specific things (low testosterone, hair loss), but he argues people need to evaluate their health as a whole.
- What he wants: a continuous, holistic understanding of his health in a closed-loop system that gives clear guidance, plus the agency to educate himself and pursue treatments at the forefront of science.
- Example he cites: Function Health, which tests over 120 biomarkers to identify potential issues and help optimize health. He gets his blood drawn there.

## People investing: a new asset class aligning individuals with long-term goals

- At Weekend Fund, Ryan says they invest in companies but much of the decision criteria is based on the founder(s).
- His view of venture: despite its flaws it is "a beautiful system that enables risk-taking". But it is limited:
  - Few people can access it (technology founders, typically pursuing audacious $1B+ outcomes).
  - The economics are tied to the company, which can misalign incentives. What's good for the company may not be good for the founder(s).
- The opportunity he sees is a new asset class that aligns people with their long-term goals and capitalist incentives.
- Example he cites: Humanism, which lets you invest in someone's future over multiple decades. It was started by the Liberman brothers, who have been dogfooding the model themselves.

## New work: rethinking talent matching and the economics of work

- Ryan's thesis: work used to be limited to the traditional 9-to-5. Some people prefer that, but many want more flexibility and financial and intellectual diversification.
- In knowledge work, time invested isn't always correlated with value created. This is increasingly true as AI gives significant leverage to people with good ideas.
- Fractional work (freelancing, contracting) is very inefficient. Some people spend half their time sourcing and selling clients, and hiring is laborious and drags on productivity.
- He is interested in platforms rethinking the talent-matching and economic system of work. He expects it probably doesn't involve human recruiters, lengthy interview processes, or hourly pay.
- Examples he cites:
  - Bountycaster, a crypto-native job marketplace. Each bounty has a clearly defined deliverable and reward, and in some cases no approval is needed to start and complete a task and get rewarded.
  - Assembly, an attempt at a similar mission over a decade ago.

## Weird consumer: backing novel products built for emerging behaviors

- Ryan has always been drawn to novel consumer products, especially those that build for emerging behaviors. His rule: "The more weird and counter-intuitive, the better."
- He predicts "consumer tech is about to get weird", driven by AI:
  - An explosion of new forms of UGC.
  - Shifting human-computer interaction (he expects far more talking than typing).
  - Cross-media translation (books becoming movies with one click).
  - A greater understanding of ourselves.
- Examples he cites:
  - Rosebud, a Weekend Fund portfolio company, for self-understanding.
  - Friend, a necklace pendant that records your conversations 24/7 to deliver feedback and act as a "friend". He says he loves experiments that question social norms.
