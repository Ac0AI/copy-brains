## Why Ryan left a good job at PlayHaven in August 2013

- Ryan left a full-time role at PlayHaven by choice, in San Francisco. He was paid well, the company was growing, and he worked with friends.
- His stated reason: "my #1 priority is to learn." He grew most during periods of discomfort, when he was challenged the most.
- His test for staying: if you look back at yourself six months earlier and don't feel embarrassed by your naiveté, you're not learning or growing. He credits this idea to his friend Nathan Bashaw.
- Telling his boss and friend was hard. He could barely say "I'm leaving" and cried, but he knew it was the right move for him.

## Signals it's time to move on

Ryan's signals, from his own experience:

- **Role narrowing.** As the company grew, his role became more specialized and moved away from his generalist roots. A UX designer was hired to take over many of the responsibilities and "(fun) challenges" he used to handle.
- **Lost passion for the market.** He lost passion for the industry, and a two-week vacation (his first in a long while) three months earlier did not bring it back.
- **Personal interest faded.** He grew up obsessed with games (Starcraft, Team Fortress Classic, Halo), but now rarely plays even casual iPhone games. He spends his time on reading, writing, and side projects/experiments.
- **Telling omission.** He never writes about gaming, the industry he works in. He says this "is very telling."
- **Not a builder in the space.** He is not a game developer and has no motivation to become one.

## Build products you consume

- Ryan argues people who build products for themselves have a "tremendous advantage" because they are their own user.
- They don't have to "get inside the user's head" to understand the problem.
- Their perseverance is strengthened by the motivation to solve their own problems.
- He states a strong ambition to build a product that he is the consumer of.

## Questions for evaluating the next opportunity

Ryan uses three questions to evaluate new opportunities:

1. Is this really what I want to do?
2. Am I (strongly) passionate about the product, market, and vision?
3. Will I be challenged, learn, and grow to my full potential?

## How PlayHaven started and what he learned

- He joined PlayHaven as employee #10 nearly three years earlier, drawn by Silicon Valley and the chance to work with founder Andy Yang in mobile gaming.
- Early on the product had traction, but revenue and adoption were nearly stagnant. Cash reserves dwindled and layoffs came; the company downsized and six people remained.
- The team scrapped nearly all existing code and rebuilt the product around a new vision. Ryan was the sole product manager working with five engineers. He defined the user experience and wireframed the new dashboard, learning on the job.
- They set a goal of a private launch at Casual Connect in July 2011.
- With no QA person, Ryan (a mostly non-technical person) set up a local environment of the dashboard and fixed bugs himself. The night before a demo with a large client, he tested the product until 2 A.M. He calls this "incredibly unscientific", but they had no time for "process" and "just had to Get Shit Done." The product shipped in a usable state and landed their first big client.
- By 2013 the team had grown to 85, with several thousand customers and multi-million dollar revenues.
- Lessons he lists from the experience:
  - Methodologies for discovering what people really want.
  - The importance of building the right (minimal) features.
  - Processes for bringing an idea to market.
  - How to scale a company.
  - The criticality of hiring the right people and building an aligned culture.
