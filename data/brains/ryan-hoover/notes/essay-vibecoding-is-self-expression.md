## Vibecoding as self-expression

- Ryan and his fiancée used Lovable to build an event site to collect RSVPs, even though Partiful, Paperless Post, or dozens of other tools could have done it in about 10 minutes.
- They spent a few hours prompting, tweaking, and laughing at weird outputs, and ended up with something that "felt like ours."
- The result was functionally inferior to a polished event platform, but more meaningful and more fun to make.
- His takeaway: "software creation itself is becoming the product." Vibecoding is a form of self-expression, creating software went from tedious to fun, and it is "as much entertainment as it is function."
- As a result, he argues the durability of a large class of consumer software is in question.

## Why consumer utilities are more exposed than B2B

- Ryan notes the chatter about the "death of SaaS" mostly focuses on B2B and its decaying moats, but he thinks consumer utilities may be even more exposed.
- B2B software selection is constrained by trust, security, integration complexity, and organizational inertia. Consumer is different.
- For many consumer use cases, the job to be done is no longer just functional; it is emotional and expressive. Demand shifts from utility to self-expression and entertainment.
- Software stops being purely a time-saving device and starts becoming a time-spending activity. "The value isn't only in the output. It's in the act of making it."
- This isn't entirely new. He cites people who rebuild the same habit tracker in Notion, constantly redesign link-in-bio pages, and craft hyper-personalized workout planners in Google Sheets instead of downloading a tracking app.
- What changes is that vibecoding expands software-as-self-expression to far more use cases for the masses.

## What stays defensible in consumer software

Ryan's premise: if vibecoding makes software easier to create, feature advantages collapse even faster. He lists what remains defensible in consumer utilities:

- **Social graphs.** LinkedIn is at its core a simple online resume, but the real value is the network and the assumption (correct, at least in tech) that most professionals use it to research people.
- **Distribution.** More than 12 years after its founding, Product Hunt's distribution flywheel keeps spinning. Its core product, a daily leaderboard of product launches, "could be vibecoded in a weekend but the value isn't in the code."
- **Licensing and regulations.** Spotify succeeded by securing licenses with major labels and aggregating the world's music in one place. Replicating that is incredibly difficult and time-consuming.
- **Data moats.** Waze is useful because it collects data from millions of drivers daily to estimate traffic, flag construction routes, and more.
- **Hardware.** Physical products are getting easier to build but remain relatively hard compared with pure software. "You can't (yet) vibe code an Oura Ring."

He adds that the most durable products will combine more than one of these.

## Software painting and the original Product Hunt bet

- When Ryan started Product Hunt, the bet was that software creation would become more accessible and increasingly serve as a form of self-expression. He says that has "never been more true today."
- His closing framing: "We're all software painters now. What have you made?"
