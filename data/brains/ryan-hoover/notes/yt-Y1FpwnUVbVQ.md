## Starting as a product manager without a roadmap

- Ryan's first PM role was at a PC gaming company called Interaction. He was doing marketing strategy, his boss was VP of Product, and he moved into product management "learning on the job" with no idea what he was doing.
- He then joined Playhaven, a mobile gaming tools company, where he was the first PM. He says he was only a PM for about a year before the move.
- The job came through a relationship: a former colleague (the CEO at Playhaven) knew he wanted to be a PM and gave him a shot.
- He notes that colleges rarely teach product management, since the role is vague and varies by company.
- His takeaway: opportunities come from connections, so treat people fairly and keep building your network. A large share of Playhaven's early hires came from referrals, including eight to ten people from the previous company.

## Empathy and humility as the core PM skills

- Ryan names empathy as the recurring theme for any PM: understanding what people want, how they feel, and how they react.
- It matters most when building a product for someone else. At Playhaven he built tools for mobile game developers and was not a mobile game developer himself.
- To close that gap he had to understand their day-to-day, their challenges and how they were measured. He found this out by observing behavior and talking to developers.
- He pairs empathy with humility: "your assumption is probably not correct as confident as you are." Be confident, but verify assumptions by talking to customers and looking at data.
- He also says communication is probably the most important PM skill, and that empathy for engineers and designers is what makes it work.

## Combining product data with customer conversations

Ryan's process at Playhaven combined intuition, data and interviews:

- **Intuition:** you can't talk to customers about every decision, so you use judgment on how an interface should look and work.
- **Usage data first:** the dashboard had many features, so he looked at which tools people actually used. For example, of ten tools built, why were two unused? Possible reasons: no use for it, too confusing, or output that can't be measured.
- **Form hypotheses, then validate:** he took those hypotheses into customer conversations.
- **Vary the sample:** he visited big companies like Glu Mobile and spoke with VPs and producers about the product and their personal goals. He also phoned indie developers, some of them one person working on a game alone.

Order of work: research the data internally, then talk to customers to validate and add context, so you aren't unaware of how they use the product.

## Customer interviews and usability testing

- **Don't pitch the idea.** Starting with "here's this cool idea, aren't you going to love it" is a bad way to learn what people need.
- **Explore the problem space first.** For a hypothetical feature to increase engagement, ask how they measure engagement and how users currently engage with their products, and pull out themes.
- **Introduce the idea late.** Toward the end, show wireframes, a prototype, or let them use it while you sit back and watch.
- **Usability tests are very helpful, but not too early.** Watching someone struggle with your prototype is painful but you learn a lot.

## Working with engineers and designers as a team grows

- **Role varies by team size.** At about 10 people, Ryan was the only PM with a few engineers and no designer, doing wireframes and general PM work himself. At around 100 people there were multiple PM teams, a UX designer and a visual designer.
- **Tradeoff:** the small setup moves fast with few people, but the product may be worse because a non-professional is doing the UX. The larger setup brings more resources, research and fuller features, but needs more process, planning and communication.
- **Top tip for working with engineers:** over-communicate rather than under-communicate.
- **Don't drop requests on engineers.** Asking "can you build this thing" without thinking ahead can break their train of thought and frustrate them. Give them some warning and lead time.
- He says the same applies to designers, but is "even more prevalent" with engineers because of how they like to work.

## Tools and wireframing habits

- **Tooling:** no special PM tools. Playhaven had an internal data analysis tool, used Pivotal for project management and moved to JIRA when teams grew. Otherwise Gmail, Google Docs, pen and paper, and whiteboards, which he says are often the best for collaboration.
- **Wireframing:** he likes OmniGraffle because he knows the shortcuts and has stencils. Others use Balsamiq or go straight into Sketch.
- **Force yourself to go analog.** He sometimes uses whiteboards or Post-it notes instead. Because it's constrained and can't be edited, it forces ideas to be succinct and quick.
- **Mistake to avoid:** in digital tools he becomes a perfectionist and spends time making wireframes pixel perfect. Early on that is a waste of time.

## Launching a feature or tool

- He says the answer depends on context: a new feature for an existing audience versus a brand new product are very different.
- For a new tool shipped to an existing audience at Playhaven, communicate what it is, what it does and why it matters, framed around the customer's goals and how it helps them do their job better.
- **Channels:** email, the product itself (the dashboard), and internally.
- **Internal enablement:** Playhaven had account managers and salespeople, so they needed to understand what the tool does and how to talk about it externally. Sales education on new features is part of the launch.

## Hiring PMs and breaking in through side projects

- **Best hires:** people he has already worked with or who come through referrals.
- **Evaluating strangers:** he looks at what they have actually done. He checks them online, including Twitter and whether they have a blog and what they write about.
- **Blogging:** a PM doesn't design or code, so there's no profile to point to. A blog is a good way to communicate how you think.
- **Side projects:** he encourages them, and wrote a post called "Anyone Can Ship". Examples:
  - an email newsletter (how Product Hunt started)
  - meetups in your city around a topic
  - a podcast
  - a product idea you want to try
- A side project is a way into PM when you can't get a PM job without experience. It demonstrates your process and ability to execute.
- You don't need to code. Figure out how to build something simply, measure it and get feedback on whether people care.
- **Technical depth:** it depends on the company. Some PM roles require being an engineer to communicate what's being built. Others are design-centric and need strong UX sense but little technical knowledge.

## What carries over to founding Product Hunt

- Ryan says he struggles to name specific skills. The soft skills dominate: learning to speak with designers and developers, and understanding how different people work.
- Hard skills he mentions include looking at data and deciding what to measure.
- The main thing is "learning on the job", being creative and curious, and a mindset of experimentation. Product Hunt "was not the first experiment" he did; he ran many and learned from each along the way.
- His summary: the right frame of mind is openness to experimentation and curiosity, practiced continually over time.
