## Weekend Fund's fund construction and check sizing

- Weekend Fund is on its third fund. The first fund targeted $1M and closed at $3M, helped by former Product Hunt investors who became LPs.
- Ryan raised the first fund shortly after Product Hunt sold to AngelList. He stayed on as CEO for four years afterward. The current fund was raised in 2021, which he calls a relatively easy fundraising year.
- Historically the fund wrote about a $300K average check. The goal was that any unicorn or above in the portfolio could return the fund.
- Valuations are climbing. When the fund started, median entry price across seed and pre-seed was about $6.5M post-money. Ryan says that is far lower than today.
- He keeps some price awareness. The fund passes on deals when the valuation is very high, the company is pre-launch, and there are few objective proof points. He also doesn't want to miss the best companies, so he is rethinking strategy and deployment for the next fund.
- His optimistic framing is that today's unicorn is tomorrow's decacorn. AI is also eating into services spending, so some companies that used to cap out as unicorns may grow much bigger.
- He wasn't raising at the time of the episode. He says you only learn whether LPs will invest once you actually raise, since many ask "are you raising?" but don't write checks.

## Follow-on and recycling policy

- Follow-ons and recycling are flexible at Weekend Fund. Nothing strict is written into the LPA.
- His follow-on test: "if we believe in this company, all things being equal, even if we're not an investor, would we invest in this company at this valuation today, knowing what we know now?"
- Recycling happens within a limited window. If a company shuts down or returns capital early, the fund recycles it.
- Around year five or six of a fund, the fund distributes returns instead of recycling. By then the team is usually raising or deploying a newer fund anyway.

## Taking liquidity from winners

- Weekend Fund did this once, about 18 months before the episode. It had invested in a seed round at a $10M post-money valuation in the first fund. The last round was at a $12B valuation.
- The fund sold 14% of its position, enough to return the fund on paper. About 83-84% of the position remained, so a mistake would still leave a large stake.
- The sale happened below the $12B mark because the market was in a slump at the time. Ryan says the stock would now sell for more than 2x the sale price, but he still thinks it was the right call.
- Reasons for selling: LPs would be glad to see liquidity, since almost no one was getting any. It also put 1x DPI on the board. He still counts it as only paper returns until it is true DPI.
- He remains bullish on the company, both then and now.
- General rule: early-stage funds should start thinking about liquidity after roughly 5-8 years in a position, and take 20-30% off the table even when they are confident it keeps rising.

## Why founder-reported revenue is now the status symbol

- Ryan says status signals in the startup world have shifted. Revenue is the new status symbol, ahead of raising capital. Brand-name investor backing still matters, but it is secondary.
- Public revenue numbers are also a leading indicator that gets investors interested. He admits that seeing founders post huge numbers on Twitter creates FOMO, for example companies claiming about $50M in revenue in their first 12 months.
- Press has been replaced by social reach as the way to get attention. Twitter has become a launchpad for new companies.
- Algorithm changes help unknowns. A no-name founder used to get little play from a post. The algorithm now heavily favors video, such as product demos, and surfaces them to people outside the poster's network.

## Building on improving foundation models

- Ryan contrasts the AI shift with the move to cloud computing. Cloud was close to binary: you were on it or you weren't, and it made things a bit faster or cheaper without changing the underlying product.
- With AI, foundation models keep getting better, so a product built on them improves without the founder doing the heavy lifting. For some use cases the utility hasn't yet met consumer needs, but it will as models improve.
- The implication for founders: be in position and capture users now, so that when models become good enough to deliver what the product promises, the users are already there.
- He says building on top of these models is quite different from earlier tech waves because the underlying technology itself upgrades the product.
- On the number of builders: tools are getting better, so people like an accountant in the Midwest who understands a niche problem can ship at least an MVP. He acknowledges today's vibe-coded tools aren't production-ready, citing security problems at one product. He is confident the number of builders will rise sharply, though not all will be venture-backable.

## Revenue quality for fast-growing AI companies

- On brittle versus durable revenue for AI tools, Ryan says churn and retention matter, and points back to improving foundation models rather than a metric-specific test.
- He sees the Lovable check as a case for thinking about this.
- He cites a team member who built a portal for the syndicate by vibe coding in Lovable, as a sign of how non-engineers can now produce software.

## Founder gatherings and portfolio support

- Weekend Fund used to run small Zoom hangs for its portfolio, capped at roughly five to seven people. Founders talk about what they're working on and what challenges they have.
- Ryan argues the format works because there is more trust among peers. In front of a VC or their team, a founder has to look confident and can't be honest, and the same person acts differently. He calls it an environmental effect.

## Portfolio companies he highlighted

- **Atlas**: Weekend Fund invested in the seed round, and the company has since raised a Series A and B. It helps Indian travelers apply for visas inside a mobile app, with tracking built in, instead of using clunky government websites. Ryan calls it a massive, overlooked market.
- At the start, founder Mohawk effectively built APIs on top of government websites through web scraping. Ryan says this was a clever "wrapper" around the whole experience, and he points out it began just before COVID hit.
- He also names the CEO, Mohawk, as a reason he likes the company, and says making travel more flexible and convenient is a net positive.
