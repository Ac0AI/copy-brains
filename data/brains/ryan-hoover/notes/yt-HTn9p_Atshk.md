## How Ryan and Nathan shipped the first Product Hunt in about five days

- Ryan describes himself as non-technical: he can do front-end work but can't build a Ruby on Rails app.
- Product Hunt began as an email list of roughly 20-30 founders and investors who each contributed cool products they'd found, with a daily digest emailed to the group.
- Subscribers grew and several people told him they looked forward to the emails, so he decided to turn it into a real product.
- He asked Nathan Bashaw (a "unicorn": designer, developer and product-minded) what to build it in. Nathan offered to build it over Thanksgiving break, and he took about four or five days.
- The goal was to build something quickly and get it to 20-30 early adopters for feedback as fast as possible. That meant being strict about cutting features to the bare minimum.
- v1 scope:
  - Post a product (link, name, short tagline).
  - Upvote.
  - Flat comment feed.
  - Twitter for authentication, so no auth system had to be built.
- Things deliberately missing at launch, such as the ability to hide posts. Inappropriate posts were hidden by editing the database directly.
- The model is "a Hacker News style" product, and Ryan says the first version "doesn't look much different than it does today."

## Seeding both sides of a marketplace with an existing audience

- The initial email list was not marketed. Ryan posted about it on Twitter and Quibb, where he had built a following of entrepreneurs and founders.
- That produced about 150 subscribers and about 20-30 contributors. Some contributors came from direct outreach to people in their network.
- The list was then opened to more people, and there was more marketing at launch.
- He says that launching six or nine months earlier "wouldn't have been where it is today." Its success rests on the following, audience and trust he and Nathan built over the previous year or so, largely through blogging and connecting with others.
- He argues many products take long to grow because their founders haven't built that following first.
- Post-launch, the focus was engagement and retention rather than acquisition. Acquisition came mostly from word of mouth and press.

## Earning press through relationships and free content

- Ryan got press from Pando Daily, including an interview with Carmel when Product Hunt was publicly announced. He also wrote three or four guest posts for Pando Daily or Fast Company on learnings from building Product Hunt.
- He says the interview worked because he had built relationships first: roughly four or five essays contributed to Pando, offered free, which earned him respect and a willingness to listen.
- He met Carmel once in person before she took the call, and notes that for reporters "it's all about timing too," so some luck is involved.
- Press that came from other writers covering Product Hunt added to this.

## Cold-emailing a publication for a guest post

- His first Pando relationship started with a cold email to Sarah Lacy. He asked a friend who had guest posted there how he got in, and the friend said he cold emailed Sarah.
- The essay was "What product designers can learn from Phil's Coffee."
- His tactics:
  - Attach the full final draft. Pitching only a topic puts the publication at risk because they can't commit to something they haven't seen.
  - Add a short description of why it's important and relevant to their audience.
  - Make it specific to them. His subject line was "Love Pando Monthly," and the body praised the interview series, which he really did enjoy, and mentioned an upcoming interview.
  - Keep it short and genuine. He says "cold emails work" and that most people are too afraid to send them, though a warm introduction is usually most effective if available.
- Sarah forwarded it to Adam Penenberg, who published it and invited more essays in that domain.

## Distributing writing across multiple platforms

- Ryan posts on his own blog first (hosted on Tumblr at the time). The next day or so he cross-posts to Medium, Svbtle and sometimes Quora.
- The purpose is distribution. He says it's easy to copy and paste, and "if it's relevant to the community... then why not post it everywhere."
- Some topics do better on certain platforms. An essay can flop on his own blog and take off on Medium; Medium suits topics like writing.
- On the SEO risk of Medium outranking his own site, he posts to his own domain first so Google tends to treat it as canonical. He hasn't analyzed it closely and judges that the upside of more distribution outweighs the risk.
- He broadcasts each post on Twitter and Quibb, but hasn't pushed people from those channels into Quora.
- Even on platforms that don't allow custom pop-ups, he ends essays with calls to action: his Twitter handle (spelled out) and a link to subscribe to his blog or email list. He gets few subscribers this way, but it is "always worth doing."

## Planned improvements and why community engagement mattered

- Next priorities were monitoring engagement and adding missing features:
  - Comment upvoting, because he wanted to give someone a pat on the back for a good comment without posting "awesome post."
  - Basic comment threading.
  - Search.
  - A better, more scalable home page.
- Founder AMAs (Q&As with the makers of products) were already running, and the flat comment feed made them hard to follow, so upvoting and threading were meant to make discussion richer.
- Over the next few months the plan was to build culture in the community and improve the product based on feedback.
- Makers join in because "people like to talk about themselves." Ryan reaches out to makers when their product is posted, and others find it via Google alerts or Twitter mentions.
- He flags founders bringing in dozens of people to upvote as gaming of the site to address later, calling it "a high class problem to have."

## Why founders value Product Hunt traffic

- Ryan's example: the founder of Appcues had his brand-new product posted by someone else and it topped the leaderboard that day. The product had little prior feedback.
- He jumped into the comments, got over 100 signups that day, and engaged with the audience.
- The next day he created a special landing page and sign-up for "Product Hunters," giving them priority access to the beta. Ryan praised this as capitalizing on the traffic and giving back to the community.
- Ryan describes the audience as people who like talking about products, who readily give feedback and who want to help founders, with a "pay it forward" mentality common in startups.

## What makes blogging worthwhile to Ryan

- He has no binary success metric; you never know which post will do well. He admits to liking seeing many people read and share his work.
- He also values a single reader whose perspective or actions change.
- Example: he wrote a post about leaving PlayHaven, a company he worked at. It did well on page views and shares and drew a lot of direct emails, about 70 by his count, from people in the same position.
- Even one email like that makes several hours of writing worthwhile; the human connection is the value.
- He worries about giving bad advice, so he has written about reading everything with context: advice may not apply to you.
