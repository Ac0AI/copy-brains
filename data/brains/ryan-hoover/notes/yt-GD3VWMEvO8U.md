## How Ryan validated Product Hunt before building it

- Ryan loves discussing products and how people build them. Offline, conversations with entrepreneurs always came back to "what cool apps are you finding" and "what's on your home screen."
- He wanted to move that conversation online. He found no community or destination dedicated to products.
- He validated with no code. He used Linky Dink, a simple collaborative link-sharing tool (he calls it "a collaborative email list"), created a group, and named it "Product Hunt," the first name that came to mind.
- He invited 20-30 entrepreneurs, founders and investors to submit products they found. A nightly digest was emailed to everyone.
- He posted once on Quibb and tweeted once. About 170 people subscribed in the first week without real marketing.
- The stronger signal was unprompted feedback. People emailed him or told him in person that they looked forward to the nightly emails. That convinced him others wanted it too, and that there might be an actual product.
- Linky Dink had no feedback mechanism for surfacing the best items. He wanted a Hacker News/Reddit-style community for products.

## Building the first version in four days

- Ryan is not a technical founder. He can do some front-end work but has no backend experience, so he asked technical friends what to build it on. He considered Ruby on Rails and Telescope (Sacha Greif's app for building Hacker News/Reddit-style sites).
- Nathan Bashaw, a friend of about a year, offered to build it over Thanksgiving. He built the first version in roughly four to five days using Ruby on Rails. Ryan says Nathan had built similar social community products before (including General Assembly's Dash), so he wasn't doing anything new.
- They collaborated by phone and IM while both were at their parents' homes. Each sketched their own wireframes, then they aligned on a deliberately minimal V1 scope.
- Because the product was so simple, little back and forth was needed after the initial design. Ryan reviewed the first version (posts, no comments) and gave feedback.
- Their approach to speed: "making a decision quickly is better than stagnating." Ryan says it's better to ship something imperfect that people can touch and feel than to spend more time talking to users and refining the design.
- He agrees that the longer you delay putting something in front of real users, the more you're guessing. He reminds himself that his roadmap ideas, though he is confident in them, may not be what the community actually wants.
- On the partnership, there was no signed agreement and costs were informally split. Ryan relies on trust built over a year and says the two complement each other's skills. He admits it's "probably not the best way" and expects to sort out details later.

## Seeding the community with a private beta

- For the first week, they re-invited the same contributors who had used Linky Dink, about 30-40 people. Nathan and Ryan emailed founders and product-minded contacts, asked for feedback, and asked them not to share it publicly. The group grew to around 50-60 by the end of the week.
- They showed these early members wireframes and upcoming features and asked for feedback. This served two purposes: feedback before building, and making them feel part of the process, "and realistically really they were part of the process."
- A public launch came after that first week.
- Ryan says Product Hunt would not have worked as well nine months earlier. It was feasible because of the network he built over the prior year through blogging and projects like Startup Edition (a newsletter).

## Growth tactics: personal email, early-member loops, and press

- Ryan's marketing strategy was email. His outbound email rose roughly 5-10x over three months. He did not use a mass-outreach tool (he mentions Tout App) because he prefers the personal touch.
- Each email used a base template describing Product Hunt, plus something relevant to the recipient, such as the last interaction.
- The goal was first to reach product-minded people likely to be good contributors, then get them to invite friends. Communities are more fun with friends.
- To make referrals easy, he included a ready-to-paste two-sentence intro so people didn't have to write the introduction themselves.
- He emailed the audience of Startup Edition (about 3,500 subscribers) only in week three or four, and it didn't drive much user acquisition.
- Results at the time of the interview: almost 5,000 subscribers, growing 80-150 new subscribers per weekday.
- Current focus was retention and engagement (people sticking around and coming back), with some user growth to keep momentum.

## The launch-day trick: tell early members first

- Ryan had written for PandoDaily a handful of times and emailed reporter Carmel, whom he'd met once. They did an interview and she wrote about the project.
- The post drove only about 1,000-1,500 unique visitors. Its purpose was not user acquisition. It was to show the community that Product Hunt is real and get them excited.
- The night before, he and Nathan emailed about 80-100 early contributors saying the public announcement would run the next day, and followed up on that email thread with the link once it was live.
- People like being part of the behind-the-scenes process and wanted to read the article. They shared it, and per Carmel it became the second or third most popular PandoDaily post that day. Ryan credits that largely to emailing the contributors.
- He frames the strategy as making people feel included and giving them ownership.

## Word of mouth and the founder-engagement loop

- Growth came from direct email recruiting, press, and word of mouth. He hasn't measured the split but sees dozens of tweets every week saying things like "I use this every single day" and "this is replacing Hacker News."
- Founders are a built-in distribution channel. When a product is posted, founders often notice via mentions or by monitoring their product name, join the thread, and bring in their own audiences to upvote.
- Upvoting requires logging in with an email address, so founder-driven traffic converts into sign-ups. Founders want to be at the top of the leaderboard, which grows more important as the community grows.
- The best discussions are when founders join to do Q&A or an AMA. Ryan says they encouraged this and that he personally likes talking directly with founders about product decisions.

## Background and path into product

- He has been entrepreneurial from childhood (selling joke books at his dad's video game store, running a gumball machine).
- He started as an unpaid marketing intern at a video game company in Eugene and got hired after college. He migrated into product because he kept suggesting product features, and his VP of product made him a product manager. He says he didn't know what a product manager was six months before becoming one and was "a terrible product manager" in hindsight.
- After that company failed, he moved to San Francisco and ran product at PlayHaven for over three years, as the only product manager for the first year and a half.
