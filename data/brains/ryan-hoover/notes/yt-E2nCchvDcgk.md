## Ryan's path from side project to Product Hunt: start with a cheap MVP

- Ryan started Product Hunt in late 2013 as an email list. He and his friends kept sharing new apps and sites in messaging apps and on Twitter, but there was no single place listing "new and cool today."
- He is not an engineer, so he had to find the fastest way to test whether people cared as much as he did. An email list needed zero engineering and took "an hour or half an hour" to set up.
- Several hundred people subscribed in the first week or so. He then watched whether they kept opening and engaging with the emails.
- He argues many startups begin as a simple email list. Six months earlier he had written a post called "Email for Startups." Its examples were AngelList, Sunrise (the calendar app) and Timehop, all of which started as emails.
- There was no single tipping point. It was a series of signals: more subscribers, unprompted emails saying "I like this email, it's really useful," and a steady pull from the startup community once the website launched.
- He ran it as a side project for roughly four to five months (early to mid 2014) before deciding whether to build a team.

## Deciding to turn the side project into a company

- By early 2014 more founders and startups were launching on Product Hunt. The job board was already earning $4,000 a month (the transcript text reads "$44,000," which looks like a transcription error, so treat the figure as unclear). It paid his rent and he believed it could reach 10K to 20K fairly easily.
- He framed the choice as two paths: keep it a side project, or build a team and make it something much bigger.
- He got advice from Y Combinator partners (Gary Tan introduced him to Kevin Hale, Kat Manalac and Alexis Ohanian) before deciding whether YC was the right path.
- He made the call once he could answer two questions: could he see a path to it becoming a big and important thing, and would it be fun to work on for a decade? YC came out as the best route, and he went through it.
- He raised money leading into YC.

## Seeding a community: manual invites, a common bond, then productize

- Ryan knew that opening the site to everyone would flood it with self-promotion, especially after the first press announcement. Early on they had little ability to detect fraud, and the feed can only hold so many items.
- In the beginning it was friends. Before any invite system existed, he emailed highly engaged people, asked if they knew a couple of others who would want to contribute, had them CC that person, and onboarded everyone by hand.
- The goal was people who would genuinely want to participate, not influencers or people over a follower count. Influence "wasn't even part of the criteria."
- On early community building, he wants "some sort of common bond." Not everyone must be friends, but it should feel like a cool house party rather than "a random mix of people all over the place that don't know each other."
- Once the manual process worked, they built an invite system so the community could invite others without him editing the database.
- Access later expanded: if you make a product that is on Product Hunt, you get access, because you need to answer questions and join the conversation.
- Opening the gates fully waited until certain product work was done.

## Why launching on a community works: Reset Plug and "unsearchable" products

- Ryan says founders get unexpected results from Product Hunt in traffic, downloads, signups, sales, press and funding. Some investors found companies there and started a dialogue.
- Press uses the site too and sometimes writes about what it finds. "Ship Your Enemies Glitter" got extra press beyond Product Hunt, went viral, and the founder got more orders than he could fulfill because he was assembling the packages by hand.
- His favorite recent example is Reset Plug. You plug your router into it and it detects when the router has gone down and power-cycles it for you. Someone in the community posted it, and it got a lot of upvotes.
- Two days later the founder emailed Ryan: he had sold about 500 units in a couple of days, versus almost no website visitors before. The founder had not posted it himself, so it came as a surprise.
- The lesson Ryan draws: when you show a product to a community, people convert and buy. This is a product you would never search for, because you don't know it exists, even though everyone has had the problem.

## Why product management prepares you to be a CEO

- Ryan says he could not have built Product Hunt successfully five years earlier, without his years of startup experience. He worked professionally in startups for about six years first, and he stresses there is no universal answer on whether everyone should do the same.
- He "accidentally fell into" product management at his first startup, loved it, and became Playhaven's first product manager in 2010.
- He calls it a unique role. You communicate across engineering (leading indirectly), design, sales and customer support, and you message both up to the CEO and down the org.
- Those communication skills transfer directly to being a CEO, at larger scale. The same people skills apply to sales, and to recruiting, where you are asking someone to quit their job and trust you and the company with their livelihood.

## Solo founder, and building a brand through personal social media

- On being a solo founder, Ryan says there is no magic formula: solo, two or three founders can each work. He stresses he is just one piece of the product and that many teammates past and present got the company where it is.
- Product Hunt began differently from most startups. It was a side project, not meant to be a startup at first, and friends helped bootstrap it with enough data to prove people wanted it.
- He has no regrets about not having a co-founder and may or may not have one for whatever comes after.
- He was active on social media long before Product Hunt. His writing and Twitter connections were effectively "the MVP for Product Hunt": building an audience, trust and a brand that seeded the first community.
- He posts partly for professional reasons but mostly because he enjoys it (mystery package unboxings on Facebook Live, Snapchat). For the company, he says it matters that the brand feels authentic and human.
- He tries new social apps early and engages with the community there. He says he prefers Snapchat because it opens straight to the camera, and he won't post the same content to both Snapchat and Instagram Stories.

## Customer support: use Twitter, automate the obvious, keep it human

- Ryan says Twitter is often faster and more responsive than a generic support email, and it feels more human than an anonymous inbox. He doesn't like being a jerk in public, so he often tweets a suggestion rather than a complaint.
- Example: he tweeted a suggestion to Uber about being charged $5 to cancel after a driver sat idle for 10 minutes. They responded, moved it to DM and gave him $10 in ride credit.
- He hates phone calls and avoids calling businesses. He would rather chat or solve the problem without a call.
- On bots, he says the aim is to automate obvious questions (for example "how late are you open today?") that a human shouldn't have to answer. People still want a human for big or complicated issues like banking.
- Product Hunt's support is run by community members, and the team takes pride in how fast they respond and in surprising and delighting people. He says the best outcome is someone arriving with a problem and leaving happy or having learned something.
- Because launches are a stressful and exciting time for founders, he thinks helping at that moment is what makes people feel passionately enough to send thank-you notes, stickers and gifts.

## Brand lessons from LaCroix and the Planet of the Apps opportunity

- Ryan notes LaCroix, an old brand, took off in recent years. He doesn't claim to know if it was intentional, but points to what he thinks helped: many flavors, each person has a favorite, which creates personalization and competitiveness. He tweeted that he is "team coconut" and people argued back with their own flavors, which drives word of mouth and brand affinity.
- He also cites the retro, "normcore" look and rising health consciousness as factors.
- He joined Apple's show Planet of the Apps (via Gary Vaynerchuk's intro) partly for selfish reasons, to learn how the entertainment industry works behind the scenes.
- He sees a huge opportunity in mainstream interest in technology, and potentially large distribution for founders who appear on the show in a world where breaking out and getting attention is increasingly difficult.
