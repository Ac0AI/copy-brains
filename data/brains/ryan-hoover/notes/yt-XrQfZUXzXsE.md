## Start with an experiment, not a business plan

- Ryan frames Product Hunt as an escalation ladder: experiment, then side project, then startup. It began as an experiment to see whether people liked the idea.
- At the side-project stage he worked on it about 30-40 hours a week while still employed part-time elsewhere.
- The seed was his own interest: he downloads many apps just to explore how others build, "almost the same way that like maybe an art connoisseur loves to look at paintings."
- His friends already shared apps in private group chats. He asked why there wasn't a single email list of cool new products, and that email list was the first version of Product Hunt.
- It grew into a website and a community of makers, founders and early adopters.
- He warns against overthinking: "You can't write a 20-page business plan and think your way into a company. You really just need to build and create."
- He advises building something useful and fun for yourself, because you will be more motivated to push through when it gets difficult.
- He tinkered with other projects too, and many went nowhere. He found the process itself fun.

## Building in public

- Ryan credits Joel Gascoigne of Buffer as the influence. Buffer shared even sensitive data such as team salaries and revenue numbers.
- He sees two benefits. It is strong content marketing, because people thirst for authenticity. It also builds a connection between customers and the team, so they feel part of the journey.
- Product Hunt adopted the same approach early: share what you're working on, which creates buy-in and gets feedback before anything is built.
- Example: the team put feature mock-ups on InVision, a tool meant for internal design feedback, and shared them publicly with the community by email. Within the first day there were hundreds of annotated comments.
- The comments ranged from tactical confusion ("I don't really understand what this thing does here") to new feature ideas. All of it arrived before any code was written.
- The team also hosted a podcast early on. Ryan thinks hearing someone's voice is a more intimate experience.
- The format was a conversation about technology rather than a typical interview, as if the community were "a fly on the wall" chatting with well-known innovators.

## Prioritizing feedback and features

- A product community gives easy feedback because it is full of makers with opinions. The downside is that everyone has an opinion, so you have to filter.
- He argues you need your own vision and direction. If you rely 100% on user input you only iterate on features and never reach the next slope of growth.
- For social and community products he thinks in systems: how each feature or change affects the rest of the experience. He admits this is hard to put in a catchy one-liner.
- For each feature, identify the one metric it is primarily meant to move. Most features move several, but focus on one.
- Estimate the impact by working backwards from the metric. For revenue, back into an estimated number. It will never be exact, but it lets you cross items off the list.
- Example: if a feature will raise revenue about 5% "if we're lucky," it is probably not worth investing in.
- In early-stage startups, he says not to invest in marginal 5% improvements unless they take about five seconds. Work on things that probably won't work but might have a 2x, 3x or 5x impact.
- He says deciding what to prioritize is the hardest part, because there are a million things you could do.

## Closing skill gaps: hire, and ask for help

- His biggest answer to skill gaps is hiring. He says there are other ways, but building a team is the biggest leverage.
- Early on the team was generalist, and it is still only about 20 people. Over time you should hire specialists in areas where you are not an expert.
- He says the real purpose of raising venture capital was to pay salaries, since he had little money in the bank. There were other benefits, but that was the main one.
- He is not an engineer, so engineering was the first need. Andres joined as CTO and built the engineering org.
- Rebecca Wilson (her name is garbled in the transcript, so omit it here) is not named; the point is that a CEO with over a decade at Microsoft brought operations, team building and finance experience he lacked.
- He also hired a community lead because Product Hunt was so community-driven.
- Method: ask which holes the business has, then find the best people who share your values and want to hustle hard.
- Looking back, he thinks he could have done better on personal growth. He learned everything on the job as problems arrived, rather than proactively reading or seeking guidance.
- He wishes he had used his investors more. Steve Newcomb-style advice is not claimed; he says board member Steve Anderson (name unclear in the transcript) came from Microsoft and had seen companies scale, and he still looks up to him.
- His conclusion: "I don't think I asked for enough help," and he says that is a common pattern among founders. Asking someone who knows can beat searching for the answer in a book.

## What Ryan looks for in founders (Weekend Fund)

- Weekend Fund invests very early, sometimes pre-product, so there is little data to go on.
- He looks for founder-market fit: is this the best team to solve this particular problem? That may come from personal experience or past work that gave them an insight few others have.
- He looks for companies built on a consumer behavior shift or a technology shift that makes something newly possible. His reasoning is that so many people build companies that something must have changed, or the founders must see something no one else sees.
- His favorite diligence question: "You could be building a million different things, why are you building this?"
- He listens for whether they just want to build a company or are authentically passionate about the problem. Founders in the first group worry him, because they may give up when things get hard.
- For hiring, he looks for the highest-skilled person for the specific role who also shows the qualities and culture you want.

## Advice for starting out in a career

- Step one: create something on the internet, such as a podcast, website or newsletter. Pick whatever makes you curious, don't overthink it, and keep shipping.
- Step two: join online communities such as Product Hunt, Twitter, Reddit or Indie Hackers.
- He hates traditional, transactional networking. Participating in communities creates serendipity that may pay off only in year three or four, for example by introducing a future co-founder, investor or insight.
- Step three, if you want to join a company: decide between big and small. Small companies give you many hats and exposure to different roles. Big companies make you a specialist but give you a bigger network and a view of building at scale.
- For a big company, aim for one on a rocket-ship trajectory. He got this advice from Josh Elman around 2012, when Ryan was leaving PlayHaven: join Pinterest or something similar.
- Elman's own path went through Facebook, LinkedIn, Twitter, Robinhood and Apple.
- He calls creating things online and participating in communities "table stakes" if you want to grow and eventually become a founder.

## Skeptical of college, bullish on real-world experience

- Ryan is "pretty bearish on college." He has a business degree with an entrepreneurship focus and says it was irrelevant and even negative for his founder journey.
- He says following that playbook (a 20-page business plan, talking to many customers) would have meant Product Hunt never existed, because the real first step was an email list that took about 20 minutes.
- He finds four years very inefficient and the cost too high. He feels sad for graduates with $100,000-$200,000 in debt and few marketable skills.
- He says college has value for relationships and personal growth, and he is not totally against it.
- He prefers hands-on experience with people who do the job today, rather than textbooks and professors who haven't built companies. This is the appeal of Acadium, in which he is an investor.
- He says the missing piece when learning online is community and network, which is why he recommends joining communities as a supplement.

## Remote work, onboarding and the startup "levels"

- Product Hunt was distributed from the start. It once had a San Francisco office with about a third of the team, and now the team is spread across places like India, Canada, France and Bulgaria.
- Benefits he cites: you can hire anyone in the world, and there is no "facetime culture." Output matters more than hours, and he says he doesn't care how many hours you work if performance is good.
- Hiring only locally limits you to a tiny slice of the talent pool. He expects more companies to go remote because talent demands it.
- He cites a friend at a big, well-known company who said he would take a third of his salary to stay in his state instead of moving to California.
- Remote doesn't mean never meeting. Product Hunt met in person twice a year, summer and winter, for example in LA, SF and Tahoe. He values dinners and shared activities for getting to know each other.
- Onboarding tactic: in the Monday all-hands call, a new teammate introduces themselves with role and location, and shares "a product that they use and love that very few people know about." He calls it basic but an effective icebreaker.
- Other onboarding tactic: connect new hires one-on-one with the right people, in some cases four to six people while they get to know the team.
- Startups are like a video game with levels. Things go great, then you hit bumps where what worked before stops scaling.
- He thinks no company has had one playbook that scales from zero to a billion, so you must keep reinventing.
- The easy part is the admin that paralyzes young founders: legal setup, taxes, recruiting. He says it is well documented, and a mentor can connect you with the right lawyer and accountant. It "shouldn't stop you from starting."
