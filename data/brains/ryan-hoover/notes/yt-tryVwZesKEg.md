## Ryan's fund size and check-size strategy

- Weekend Fund is on its third fund, $21 million, kept small by design. Ryan says the next fund won't scale much.
- Average check is about $350K, for roughly 2-3% ownership. He calls that small but meaningful because the target is decacorn outcomes.
- Plan is about 50 portfolio companies per fund, which he says is "very manageable" because the fund is not a lead investor and is not on boards.
- Ryan avoids being a lead and avoids the middle ground of being a non-lead who still has to write a $750K check to get meaningful economics. That is "really tough."
- A small check helps win deals. Even if a founder's allocation is tight, they can make room.
- Historically the fund avoided very small checks where a $10 million outcome doesn't return the fund. Small checks can also conflict the fund out of a better future investment. He is rethinking this as companies grow faster and more of them appear.
- Construction has to be realistic, so he works backwards: how many checks can be deployed, how many people he must meet, how many companies can be supported. He doesn't expect a 10% conversion from meetings to checks.
- Example outcome: a seed investment in Deel at a $10M cap, now a roughly $12B company, "almost a 400x outcome." Most investments won't be that, but one can return a 5x or 10x fund.
- He argues yesterday's unicorns are becoming tomorrow's decacorns because revenue growth is scaling fast and AI will eat more of the services side of GDP.

## Raising a fund from 360 LPs

- The fund raised most of its capital from existing fund one and two LPs, some product investors from the Product Hunt days and some institutions. The rest was raised publicly.
- The strategy was to bring on "an army of operators and founders," from salespeople and designers to AI researchers. The aim was an extended family helping with diligence, investor intros and deal flow, not the money. The fund was already near its target.
- The fund was announced publicly under a 506(c) structure so it could legally take applications from LPs. Many LPs wrote very small checks.
- Applications included questions testing whether LPs understood early-stage investing. Ryan didn't want someone expecting their money back in two years.
- Results across 360+ LPs were mostly good. One LP asked for money back about two years in because they were becoming a monk, and it was a small amount that was resolved. One or two LPs had capital call issues.
- Hindsight: some LPs don't grasp that a commitment means future capital calls are not optional. The LPA lacked protection if an LP stops funding after capital was reserved for them, and he plans to add it in the next fund, "nothing aggressive."
- Small checks also meant no LP relied on the money for things like tuition, which reduces future headaches.
- Another fund's approach: put family offices and high-net-worth LPs into a more liquid six-year strategy before a 10-year fund. (This was the host's example; Ryan called it smart.)

## Productizing LP intros and an LP newsletter

- Ryan runs a basic newsletter that reaches about 150 LPs who deploy roughly $50 million a year in total. Some are small $10K LPs who add value in other ways. Some are institutions writing $2.5M+ checks.
- The newsletter just shares what he is investing in. He has invested personally in over 30 funds, mostly small checks. Using his own capital is meant to be a signal.
- He started investing in funds about seven years ago. He doesn't mainly invest for returns. Motives: support fellow GPs, read their updates to see how they view the market, and occasionally get deal flow.
- Most of the funds he backs are under $30M. They are either narrow specialists or generalists who are highly connected, since generalists need to see a lot and be in the right places to spot spaces before any VC blog post exists.
- Fundraising "sucks" and many friends are doing it, which is why he helps with LP intros.

## Rolodexer: using a social following as a distribution tool

- Weekend Fund built Rolodexer, a tool that searches Ryan's roughly 300,000 Twitter followers (he estimates about half are active, mostly tech).
- Use case: a company wants intros to early design partners or customers. Ryan asks for their target company list, say Ramp. Rolodexer finds followers who work there, and Ryan DMs them an intro.
- Conversion: "30 to 50% of people accept an introduction" from these outbound requests, much higher than email because the DM comes from someone they follow.
- Every portfolio founder gets access, which supports them "without requiring any of our time." Founders with their own following can search their followers too.
- It also helps win deals. Ryan will offer "tell us your B2B target customer list, let's go make some introductions," usually once a founder says it's a priority, and mostly after securing the allocation rather than before.

## Winning deals and being a non-annoying investor

- Ryan lists the ingredients: access to see the company, a meeting with the founder, a small check that fits tight allocations, tools like Rolodexer, and references. His edge on access and meetings is Product Hunt (nearly 12 years old, roughly 100,000 makers launching per year) plus having "chewed glass" as a founder. He says there is no cookie-cutter template and it's customized per founder.
- Founders usually don't ask for references, but they may be doing them quietly, so reputation compounds.
- His approach: "We don't disappear and we don't bug them." Respond to every email, be proactive, and don't distract.
- "The worst thing you could do as a founder is bring on an investor who is net negative. And word gets around, reputation matters."
- When sharing useful info, he sometimes writes "no response needed" so the founder can archive it instantly.
- He describes a former Product Hunt COO's "piggy bank" idea: keep depositing small value into relationships, not as a transaction. It applies in venture because "everybody kind of knows each other."
- On SPVs and doubling down: LPs get first dibs, and they bring in strategic angels. Later-stage SPVs have been opportunistic. The game is early stage, and for competitive follow-on rounds it's relationship-driven: "have they been helpful? Do I feel like I owe them?"

## Scaling a small fund: scouts, tools and a two-person team

- Ryan is an introvert who prefers working behind a computer and scaling through products. He does many quick Zoom calls (15-20 minutes for first meetings). His partner Jedica meets founders in person in San Francisco, so the two divide and conquer.
- The "weekend partners" scout program is non-exclusive. Scouts may scout for other firms, which suits the fund because it's small and early-stage, and Series A+ scouts can deploy earlier or gain access.
- Scouts get 25% of the carry on their deals, less than a16z or Sequoia scouts may get. Ryan says they are not doing it mainly for economics but to be part of the ecosystem. One scout has made 150 investments.
- Why scouts: tech changes fast and Ryan is a "product person, community person." He wants builders actively working in new areas, like a scout who is a founder in an upcoming YC batch and runs a hacker house in San Francisco.
- Transparency rule: scouts share deals in a shared Slack channel, not DMs, with the company, why they're excited, how they know the founder and other thoughts. Others can add views or founder connections. It creates more signal, and Ryan wants to see what everyone is looking at.
- He is building an investor workflow tool using LLMs and third-party data enrichment, "fun and frustrating," and says building it taught him what LLMs can do and where the holes are.

## Finding alpha: founder secrets, weird bets and lived experience

- "A lot of what we're looking for is secrets." He backs a founder with a secret or with traction. Pre-launch: what do they see that others don't? Post-launch: if there's traction he doesn't understand, he digs into what he is missing.
- His view of the best alpha is lived experience with the problem a founder is solving. Example: he invested in Deel in 2018 because Product Hunt was fully distributed. He had seen the pros and cons and strongly believed remote hiring would grow, since recruiting in San Francisco was expensive and retaining talent was hard. This was before COVID.
- Invest in what you understand, though volume means many deals are outside his domain.
- He likes weird companies, especially weird consumer, though the fund invests in little consumer because it's very hard. Hot deals are often good, but only chasing them means playing the consensus game and missing weird companies. Backing a founder no one else would gives status and is "meaningful and important." Zeros are the nature of the game.
- The fund invests broadly, from "boring SMB tech to weird consumer stuff."
- Info diet: Twitter ("a fire hose," serendipitous) and conversations with founders and smart people. Conversations are "maybe the ultimate alpha" because they cover what people can't say publicly or haven't written. He used to blog from such conversations.

## Experiments, failures and early lessons

- Weekend Build: an 8-week mini accelerator with no equity, run in Slack with weekly syncs, for people with side projects at big tech companies. Almost 1,000 applied and 10 teams were chosen. It failed because the thesis was finding venture-scale founders to back so they could quit their jobs, and none emerged.
- Why it failed: selection bias. People who had worked on a side project for over a year without leaving probably lacked traction, not just conviction or support. It would also need massive scale (about 100 teams, not 10) and is hard to productize. He says "most of them are complete failures."
- Advice to his younger self: he made zero investments before raising the first $3M fund in 2017, since investing as a CEO wasn't socially acceptable. He used to get too excited about the product and impart his own vision. He had to learn to ask what the founder's plan and the business are, a different muscle from "this is a cool product."
- Founder dynamic: he wants conversation, not lecture. If a founder agrees with everything he says, he's concerned.
- Introductions: he is strict about connecting people, and the host's double opt-in point was echoed only as a shared practice.
