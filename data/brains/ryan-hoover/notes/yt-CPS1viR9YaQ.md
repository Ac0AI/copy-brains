## Ryan's investing thesis for the Weekend Fund

- The Weekend Fund is a $3M fund, about two years old at this taping, with 37 investments made. Checks run $50k-$100k, with a median of $75k.
- It invests in the first round of funding, sometimes before a product exists. Ryan buys roughly 1% at a pre-money of about $6.5M.
- He calls himself a generalist, but he focuses on product, marketing and community, where he thinks he adds help.
- His core thesis is backing "people who are building for platform shifts and consumer behavior shifts": someone building something nascent today for a future they can articulate.
- He is not strict about the thesis. Some investments don't fit it.
- Voice and audio is a category he is excited about.
  - About 70 million US homes have a smart speaker, roughly a quarter of the US population. Amazon bundles Echo Dots for $20-30.
  - He compares it to early mobile, which had no app store and no consumer understanding of how to use the device. People are still working out what to use voice for.

## Investing in community-driven companies

- Ryan says community is trendy and not every business should have one. A community can add defensibility and strength on top of a core product.
- Voiceflow is a no-code tool for building voice apps, which he describes as "Squarespace for voice or Wix for voice." He built a joke app in about ten minutes.
  - The CEO runs a Facebook group, where he posts authentic vlogs. He thanks members for suggestions and for submitting apps to the marketplace.
  - Members show off what they are building, which gives a new, underserved group of voice-app builders a place to gather.
- Girlboss (Sophia Amoruso) is another community bet.
  - Ryan says Sophia is a natural community builder, and that is the "X factor" that got him to participate.
  - Girlboss runs community-driven live events. They have discussed a possible LinkedIn for women.
  - Her draw is authenticity and personal style. He says "her superpower is people want to hang out with her."
- Breaker is a podcast app, an investment from YC 2017.
  - Product Hunt once ran podcast discovery, with the This Week in Startups and Kara Swisher shows among the few podcasts posted. It failed for several reasons.
  - The need stuck with him: podcasts lack discovery and community. They share traits with apps, since both need users, feedback and listeners.
  - Ryan was drawn to the market need and to how Breaker supports creators.

## Where his community instincts come from

- Ryan traces it to gaming. Starcraft led him to Blizzard's Battle.net, clans and meeting people under pseudonyms. That led to IRC.
- He describes the IRC "warez" scene as a reputation economy.
  - Access came through friends. You earned your keep by uploading movies and games for credits and a leaderboard rank.
  - People worked together mostly for status inside the group, not money.
- He also says much of what he knows came from doing things and following his interests.

## Monetizing Product Hunt

- Product Hunt only started prioritizing revenue in early 2018. Ryan says revenue was not the priority before, and in hindsight it perhaps should have been.
- In 2018 the goal was profitability. At the time of this taping the team was making more than it spent, and the model worked every month.
- Revenue comes from product promotion, job promotion, event promotion and a few other products.
  - Founder Kit is a bundle of deals on tools and services.
  - The simplest and best performer is the promoted post: one slot, fourth down on the homepage, sold to one company at a flat fee.
  - The price is roughly $4,000-$6,000 per day.
- To avoid community backlash about pay-to-play, the promoted product must have been posted to Product Hunt before. You can't launch a new product and pay for the slot.
  - So whatever is promoted has already been validated by the community.
  - Ryan frames it as selling the brand and unique real estate in front of early adopters, investors and reporters.
- He estimates introducing it sooner would have covered a third to a half of the burn.
- Ryan says he has no regrets, and that advice always depends on the founder's context. A community like Girlboss differs from Product Hunt.

## Why he stayed independent and how the fund works

- Product Hunt was acquired by AngelList. Ryan says he has "unfinished business" there.
  - He has teammates of over four years who are taking on more responsibility, which frees his time for strategy and for investing.
  - He turned down offers to join other firms. He wants to build his own brand and the Weekend Fund long term, though he might join a firm someday.
- Fund economics: he takes carry only and no management fee on Fund I. AngelList takes part, and he says the setup cost is a small percentage of the fund.
  - He doesn't expect to see carry until about year six.
- For the next fund he plans a management fee of roughly 2% per year, part of which goes to AngelList. On a $10M fund, he says, his share could be around $100k a year.
  - That would cover a chief of staff's cash pay and a small working budget.
- He hired a part-time chief of staff on carry.
  - Duties are evaluating investments, market research, diligence and sourcing.
  - The job listing drew Twitter backlash for sounding like unpaid labor. Ryan says his wording was poor.
  - He now offers options: more carry and less cash, or less carry and more cash. He wants the hire to feel well compensated.

## How he evaluates deals

- Founder-market fit: he doesn't mean the founder came from that industry. He means the person has "some unique quality that's competitive in the market to solve this problem," such as extreme passion or domain expertise.
- A great founder has a strong perspective on the future and knows what they are building toward, without needing every answer. Greatness depends on context and what they are building.
- Authenticity matters to him. As an example, he notes KC (Casey Neistat) drove more traffic on a Product Hunt live chat than Snoop Dogg.
- Valuation matters because he is not leading rounds. He has passed on exceptional founders at $35M pre-launch valuations.
  - At a $50k check, a $35M pre-launch company needs a $3-4B exit to move the fund.
  - He compares putting the check in three companies instead of one. A $12M-15M cap beats the $5M-6M average only if the company is exceptional.
- On portfolio size, he believes odds improve with scale. At five companies you probably won't hit a unicorn.
  - Around 30-40 companies is where outcomes start to distribute, so he planned about 40 for Fund I.
  - You can only lose 1x on a check but can win 100x or more.
- He asks for pro rata in nearly every deal. For follow-ons he uses AngelList SPVs to raise allocations from his LPs, since a $3M fund can't write million-dollar checks.
- Process:
  - He receives hundreds of emails a month.
  - He estimates about ten meetings per investment, which he calls a low guess.
  - He takes meetings over Zoom or Hangouts, which he says is about 95% as effective as in person and gives him access to companies worldwide.
  - His portfolio is roughly 30-35% San Francisco-based, with the rest spread from Canada to London to New Orleans.
- On TAM, mature markets suit TAM math. For nascent categories like voice, it's "fuzzy math." You can't quantify what mobile would enable, so you ask whether enough people will pay.

## Where founders should build

- Ryan moved to San Francisco in 2010, and Product Hunt wouldn't exist if he hadn't. Being there let him build relationships and absorb ideas he can't fully articulate.
- Location depends on what you're building. If it's for the tech world, be in the Bay Area. A media or music company might belong in LA.
- Teams can be remote. Product Hunt's team is spread around the world, and he'd build remotely if starting another company.
