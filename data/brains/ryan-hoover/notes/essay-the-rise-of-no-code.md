## Why "no code" is inevitable: Ryan's case for the rise of no-code tools

Ryan's core claim is that "no code" tools are collapsing the time and skill needed to turn an idea into something people can use, and that this will widen who gets to build. He ties it to his own start: he built his first website in Dreamweaver as a 15-year-old with only basic HTML and CSS. It was ugly and didn't take off, but he calls it "a gateway drug to creating things on the internet."

- Earlier WYSIWYG tools (Dreamweaver, FrontPage, Flash) cut the barrier to creation sharply, and he says their impact on today's tech ecosystem is understated.
- He sees a new wave of tools that make creation more accessible and change how things get built for the internet.
- Once, only a small group of engineers (self-described "webmasters") built on the internet, often after months of learning to code.
- In December 2018 he tweeted that powerful "no code" tools are entering the market, that he was speaking with 3 startups raising in voice, mobile and web app creation, and that "the time and cost to build software products will drop by another 10x." He called this part of Product Hunt's thesis.

## The no-code stack Ryan lists (as of January 2019)

He says that in under an hour, anyone can now build:

- A CMS-driven responsive site with Webflow.
- An ecommerce shop with Shopify.
- A Facebook Messenger bot for a shop with Octane AI.
- A web app with Bubble.
- A mobile app with Thunkable.
- A voice app with Voiceflow. He built a joke bot in 10 minutes and discloses he invested in Voiceflow.
- A complex web app combining Zapier and Airtable.
- A single-page site with Carrd.
- A paid newsletter with Substack.
- An AR/VR/3D browser experience with Scapic.
- An online magazine with Readymag.
- A website from a Google Sheet with Sheet2Site.
- Internal dashboards and tools with Retool.

He also points to no-code resources: Zeroqode, NoCode and MakerPad.

## Everyone becomes a maker

- Building is no longer limited to the "<1% of engineers that can code," which he expects to cause "an explosion of ideas from all kinds of people."
- You no longer need to become a programmer to build on the internet, which brings in makers from different backgrounds and perspectives.
- He says Product Hunt sees no-code projects often, including a Golden Kitty award winner.
- Making things accessible was part of Product Hunt's thesis when it started five years earlier.

## Answering the critics

- Ryan expects people to criticize and judge no-code users. He grants there are tradeoffs, but says it is "inevitable" that more products will be built, or at least MVP'd, without code, including by programmers who can code.
- Example: a VP at a Series A startup told him they use Salesforce and Zapier for sourcing B2B leads. The company has an engineering team and could build anything, but chose no-code because it was faster to build and easier to maintain.
- His diagnosis: "We often perceive things that are hard to do as better." Sometimes that's true, but as no-code tools advance, "it will be silly to do it the old way."

## The hosting analogy

- Ryan compares no-code to hosting. Putting a site online once meant buying expensive physical servers and heavy dev ops work, and a feature on Yahoo's homepage could crash the site.
- Now AWS, Heroku and Google Cloud make this easy, and even the most experienced dev ops engineers use them to start and scale.
- He says it would be foolish for them to spend time racking their own servers, and he expects the same shift for building software.

## Where opportunity follows: maker communities and tools

- As more people become makers, there is "an even greater thirst for community" to give feedback and support along the way.
- He predicts maker communities will become increasingly popular as everyone becomes a maker.
- He also expects growth in tools that help makers monetize, find side-project buddies, and get the word out, and says those tools might themselves be built without code.
- He says he is very interested in tools for makers and creators and is looking to invest in the space. He notes Product Hunt was building something new for makers, freelancers, remote workers and side-project builders.
