## Why Ryan started Product Hunt as an email list

- Ryan likes talking about products. His conversations with friends often start with "what apps are you playing with" or "did you see that cool new app yesterday."
- He wanted a place to curate and share cool products with friends, so it began as an email list (a little over a year before this 2015 interview).
- The list was a true mailing list. He invited entrepreneurs and investors he knew, and anyone who found a cool product submitted it, which then emailed the whole group each day. He was not curating it himself at first.
- The list gained traction, and he saw that people love sharing technology products.
- A friend, Nathan Basha, built the first version over Thanksgiving in about five days on Ruby on Rails. It was quick and dirty.
- Ryan did not invent new mechanics. He took inspiration from Digg, Reddit and Hacker News, because tech people already understand upvoting and curation and don't have to learn something new.

## Why the daily email and daily rhythm drive retention

- The email is the core experience. Each day it lists the previous day's top 10 products by upvotes, so it is an easy way to catch up on what's hot.
- The site is split by day on purpose, to encourage a daily habit.
- It goes out at 7:30 a.m. Ryan ran it himself every morning at the time of this interview.
- The email also carries editorial content and featured collections, such as on-demand delivery services, emoji products and gift apps.
- Its open and click-through rates were 2 to 3x the industry average, and the rates held up over time.
- Ryan says becoming part of someone's morning routine is the key to stickiness.
- He cites Nir Eyal (whom he helped with the book "Hooked") on triggers. Internal triggers are FOMO ("what's new, what's hot") and "I need a product that does X." External triggers are the daily email.
- He says many companies are afraid to spam users, and some do abuse email and push notifications. But "without reminding people you exist even if your product is awesome... people just don't remember to come back," so he thinks most should be more aggressive.
- The reminders must provide value. He gives the example of "Merry Christmas" and "happy New Year" emails with no value: "an excuse for me to unsubscribe."
- Marketing and sales only feel negative if you forget to put yourself in the consumer's shoes. Ask what information they would want and how you can deliver value that brings them back.

## How he got traction and approached Y Combinator

- Ryan met Alexis Ohanian, Kevin Hale and other YC partners before he applied.
- He believes YC accepted Product Hunt because of the traction it already had. "Upvote cool products" is not an idea that obviously works on paper.
- Most YC companies already have something working when they join.
- YC lasted three months. He says the relationships, access to partners and office hours, and the alumni network continue afterward.
- Funding: Kevin Rose and Google Ventures invested in the seed round, and Alexis invested in the Series A. The total was $7.1M across the seed and Series A.
- Ryan used Alexis, Reddit and Hacker News moderators for advice on keeping the community healthy.
- Early on, it was an experiment and a side project. He debated bootstrapping it into a profitable business before taking money.
- He took the Series A to get a long runway. That lets the team focus on the product instead of short-term gains and fundraising, which he says is a huge distraction even when it's relatively easy.
- Funding does not change the team's intensity, but it adds pressure to succeed and return investors' money.
- Growth: Product Hunt grew well over 10x in monthly visitors in 2015 and the email list grew significantly. The team was 14 people.
- The site had no advertising, and Ryan did not see any coming for a while. Growth was viral.

## Community is the product, and the main risk

- Ryan answers the question "how is this even a business" (a top Hacker News comment on the YC announcement) by saying the value is the community and the people who use it.
- Other "Product Hunt for X" sites appeared (health foods, niche categories). Most don't succeed. They build a working product but not the community, and the community is what makes it work.
- He says "we will either succeed or fail based on the community. It's not going to be a technical challenge."
- He watched Digg grow and implode, and keeps that in mind.
- Anti-gaming measures: voting-ring detection, spam-bot detection, sandboxing, and manual review. He says gaming is a sign of success and will be a constant fight.
- He also puts unusual focus on the internal team, finding community leaders who understand psychology and can build communities.
- Posts by makers promoting their own product are fine, as are posts by people who found something cool. The goal is to surface things people find interesting or useful, not who posted them.
- Makers often join the comment threads. Ryan says that direct dialogue between consumers and the people who made the product is what sets it apart from tech blogs.
- He worries every morning that he doesn't know what will be on the site or whether people will keep coming. During the 2013 holidays he feared users would not return, but they did.

## Metrics, data and platform strategy

- Ryan's single core metric is how much traffic Product Hunt drives to the products listed. It shows the impact on the ecosystem. Founders also write blog posts about their own traffic from the site.
- Audience: about 4% is in the Bay Area and about half is outside the US, so it is not just a Silicon Valley bubble.
- The API was built after he saw spikes in real-time analytics from people scraping the site. He was nervous about opening it but decided it was the right direction.
- People built dozens of apps on it, including Android apps (Product Hunt had none of its own then), Alfred and Mac menu-bar tools, and Map Hunt, which maps products by maker location. He sees community-built hacks as a way to be led by what people actually want.
- He sees a longer-term platform and data play. "Related products" shows similar products when one launches, and the site gets more valuable as more products and makers join.
- Collections began as team-curated and became creatable by anyone about three to four months before this interview. Examples included emoji apps, Valentine's gift apps, helping hands, and a 2014 music collection that was an experiment launched that day.
- Long-term he has ambitions to expand beyond tech into other verticals ("there's no ceilings").

## Focus and product decisions

- He started on the web, not mobile, for two reasons: limited resources (two part-time people) and content that suits desktop, since many listed products are landing pages built for full screens.
- He does not focus much on mobile yet, but sees mobile as the primary interface long term as they expand to different verticals.
- The site gets 200+ submissions a day, and not all reach the homepage. Even if all are amazing it would be overwhelming, so they are looking for the right balance.
- Voting helps users cut through a crowded space. In the GTD to-do collection, upvotes pushed a little-known app to the top.
- On risk: he is somewhat risk averse, yet loves entrepreneurship. He feels that no matter what happens he can't really lose, because he is learning and doing something he believes in.
