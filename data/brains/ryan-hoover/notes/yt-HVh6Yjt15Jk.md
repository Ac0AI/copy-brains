## Ryan's route into tech: entrepreneurial childhood, games, and product management

- Ryan grew up in Oregon with an entrepreneur father. At 11-12 he ran gumball machines at his dad's video game store, collecting quarters and reinvesting them in candy.
- He disliked hourly jobs: "you could do really well or really poorly and you [get] paid the same amount." Getting paid for output rather than time appealed to him.
- Side hustles: buying and selling on eBay in high school and college, and modding Xboxes. He tried a joke-site idea after a friend reportedly made $100,000 at 14 and made $70.
- He studied business with a computer information tech minor, then took an internship at a video game company. That led to marketing and then product management, a role he says he hadn't heard of before becoming one.
- In 2010 he moved to San Francisco and joined a mobile gaming tools company (Play Haven) at about employee number 10. It grew to roughly 100 people over about three and a half years. He left when he felt he "wasn't learning as much."
- His rule for early careers: "if you're not learning, you need to figure something out" and do something different.
- He says he never had a clear game plan, and Product Hunt wasn't intended to be a company at first.

## What a product manager does

- A PM works with internal and external stakeholders (customers, engineering, design) to decide what gets built, and recommends options aligned with company strategy.
- The work can include wireframes and scoping projects. Smart people generate endless feature ideas, so a good PM narrows to the core necessities, ships, then learns and iterates.
- Ryan loves product because "the product is the thing" delivered to customers.
- He thinks PMs should explore what others are creating: "I would just play with products... not to use them... just because I was curious." He saw this as a way to learn.

## How Product Hunt started and the MVP approach

- It began as an email list in late 2013: Ryan and a couple dozen friends sharing cool new products daily.
- It got traction quickly. He saw a gap: no simple daily list of what's new and cool, and no place for the global tech community to discuss new products.
- He and Nathan Basha built a simple website over about five days during Thanksgiving break, scoping an MVP by asking what was the minimum to bring to market.
- They modeled the UI and UX on Reddit, with comments, discussion and upvotes as the curation mechanism. Ryan's reasoning: "you don't need to reinvent the wheel" in every part of a product; the innovation was in "the content [and] the community."
- It stayed an unincorporated side project for about five or six months. After conversations with Garry Tan and other YC partners they went through Y Combinator and raised a round.
- A Series A from Andreessen Horowitz followed in early 2014, within roughly the first nine months.
- AngelList acquired Product Hunt at the end of 2016. It kept its own codebase, brand and mission inside AngelList's mission of supporting entrepreneurs and startups.
- Naval was a seed investor. Ryan later started a fund (Weekend Fund), led Product Hunt as CEO, then stepped down with Josh Buckley taking over.

## Why game builders make good product people

- He has a bias toward people who worked in games, calling them "some of the smartest people."
- Games demand deep understanding of human psychology and behavior, since the job is creating fun, which is hard.
- He cites Super Mario World's onboarding: players are taught new skills almost invisibly as they progress.
- Games were early adopters of data-driven optimization over roughly the last 10 years; reading and interpreting that data is a real superpower elsewhere.
- Games often sit on the frontier of new tech (VR, AR, AI).
- Games also taught him about virtual economies, which he sees now being applied to crypto and community tokens.
- Competitive play (Starcraft, soccer) taught strategy, collaboration and pushing through difficulty.

## Engagement, habits and the Hook model

- Ryan avoids the word "addiction." He worked with Nir Eyal on the book Hooked and frames the goal as building engaging, habit-forming products that are net positive.
- His litmus test: do you use it yourself and believe it helps? A crack dealer who doesn't use his own product fails this test.
- Example: Strava is designed for engagement, and that engagement makes users healthier.
- He is skeptical of The Social Dilemma, saying it ignores user agency and the value products like Facebook create, and he favors equipping users with understanding and tools (Nir's book Indistractable) over blaming products.
- The Hook model has four steps: Trigger, Action, Variable reward, Investment. Using early Twitter over SMS:
  - Trigger: a tweet arrives as an SMS notification.
  - Action: you open it. The lower the friction, the better.
  - Variable reward: you don't know what you'll get, like a slot machine.
  - Investment: you contribute back (for example, replying to a friend), which triggers the other person and pulls you back in.
- Early on, products need external triggers. Over time the product should rely on internal triggers, the user's own thoughts. Ryan has only Twitter DM notifications on, yet checks Twitter dozens of times a day.
- Products have several loops. Twitter DMs have their own, and Product Hunt has its homepage loop and a separate collections loop.
- For designing a loop, start with the user's goal and what behavior they're already trying to hack around.
- He sees Twitter's value as the best place to see what's happening right now, plus community and learning.

## Distribution, audience and community

- Ryan says the hardest part of a startup is increasingly not building but "can they get users and can they get attention." As competition rises, distribution matters more.
- He wrote about "audience-first" startups: people who build an audience before they have a product. Example: Casey Neistat spent years vlogging, so his later product, Beam, had guaranteed distribution from day one. Beam ultimately didn't take off.
- Audience is one-to-many. Community is many-to-many and more powerful and scalable. He blogged enough to launch Product Hunt with users from day one, and now the community creates the content, so he can go on vacation and it keeps running.
- Writing helps you learn because you must understand something to write about it. It builds relatability and authority, and trust can convert into product users.
- An audience is evergreen: if one product fails, the same audience carries to the next.

## Investing criteria and trends at Weekend Fund

- There is no formula. They invest very early, sometimes pre-product, and look for teams capitalizing on a consumer behavior shift or a technology shift.
  - Behavior shift: what does the world want now that it didn't before? Example: remote work, which also changes education and travel.
  - Technology shift: what's possible now that wasn't yesterday, such as audio/voice or crypto.
  - Then why this team, with what unique insight or experience.
- Areas of interest: crypto (social and community tokens, with Trevor's FWB tokenized Discord as an example), the creator economy, audio and voice (about half of US homes have a smart speaker; earbuds reduce friction), and distributed and remote work (a thesis since Weekend Fund began in 2017).
- On hiring, he says resumes (school, past company) are insufficient proxies. Better: look at what people have built (code for engineers) and use references, which are powerful but hard to get honestly. His head of engineer at Product Hunt, in Sofia, Bulgaria, is one of the best he's worked with, and Ryan doesn't know where he went to school.
- On India: he cited 12 new unicorns in India in 2021 versus 9 in 2020 and 8 in 2019 (he flagged the figures as unverified). Weekend Fund had invested in six or seven Indian companies.
- He said roughly 50 of about 300 companies in a recent YC batch were from India.
- As companies scale, alumni spin off new startups, a flywheel like Silicon Valley's earlier. The old worry about follow-on funding is, in his view, largely gone.
- His critique of Indian products: design execution often lags, which creates an opening for startups that make design a strength. He noted France as a design strength and said each ecosystem has its own strengths and weaknesses.
- He is a strong optimist on tech and entrepreneurship, and encourages people to build even if the first version is poor: "go build."
