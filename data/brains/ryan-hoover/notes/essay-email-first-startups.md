## Why Ryan favors email-first startups

Ryan describes the "email-first startup" as a less common launch strategy, chosen after weighing product, market, team, timeline and competition. His claim is that email, as the starting medium, lets a startup validate a business and gain traction "with relatively minimal investment."

His six reasons:
- **Validate ideas quickly.** An email-first product is generally faster to build than a full website or mobile app. The sooner it reaches customers, the sooner you learn what does and doesn't stick.
- **Fake it 'til you make it.** Email is largely asynchronous, so manual processes can stand in for missing functionality (Wizard of Oz experiments).
- **Force focus.** Email "doesn't afford feature-creep." Email-first products are limited, which forces a succinct focus on one use case.
- **Create habits.** People check email several times a day, so a well-done email product can become part of a daily routine.
- **Be ubiquitous.** Everyone uses email, and it doesn't discriminate against devices or operating systems.
- **Transfer to other mediums.** Up-selling or cross-selling to web or mobile products is easier when a reliable communication channel already exists.

## Examples of startups that began as email products

Ryan lists these as having started email-first:
- **Timehop** began as 4SquareAnd7YearsAgo, which emailed Foursquare check-ins from exactly one year earlier.
- **iDoneThis** was created over a weekend. Users email the tasks they accomplished that day, and the next morning an email goes out to their team.
- **Thrillist** was a hand-made email newsletter on men's lifestyle.
- **AngelList** narrowly focused on a curated list of angel investors, delivered by email weekly.
- **Sunrise** sent a morning email with the day's schedule: "Simple. Useful."

He notes that all of them have since expanded into web and/or mobile applications. Email is where they were born, and it let them validate their business cheaply.

## When to choose an email-first strategy

- His rule: "If your product and market fits the medium, consider an email-first strategy."
- Good fits, from his examples, are products that deliver one narrow, useful piece of information on a schedule (a daily digest, a weekly curated list, a recap of past activity).
- He treats email as a starting channel, not the end state. The plan is to validate with email, then move users to web or mobile once there is traction.

## Using email to run lean experiments

- Because email is asynchronous, a founder can do the work by hand behind the scenes (curating, compiling, sending) before building automation.
- This lowers the cost of testing demand. The Thrillist example, a hand-made newsletter, and the iDoneThis example, built over a weekend, both reflect it.
- The goal is learning what sticks with customers early, before investing in a full product build.
