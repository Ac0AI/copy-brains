## Product is a solution to a real need, and positioning matters as much as the build

- Ryan grounds product thinking in his dad's phrase: "find a need and fill it." At its core a product solves a real problem for a very specific customer or user.
- Building the best product is not enough. If nobody knows about it, or it doesn't resonate, it goes nowhere.
- He wants a holistic view: how the product positions in the user's mind, what people do today to solve the problem, and how you insert yourself into that decision or workflow. This needs marketing and writing perspective, not just building skill.
- Products often stay close to their MVP. Twitter's core is still text broadcasting with a follower model, and more features don't automatically make a product better. Some products (he cites Airtable) really do need years of depth, so he avoids absolutes.

## How Product Hunt started and grew from a newsletter to a funded company

- It began as a newsletter of products Ryan and friends found. He was scratching his own itch: he wanted one daily list of cool new things. He was not thinking about makers or founders, and admits he never thought of it as a distribution channel for them.
- The newsletter lasted only about a month to six weeks. Ryan and Nathan Bashaw then built the site over Thanksgiving break in roughly five days, from mock-ups to an MVP.
- The site MVP was a daily list of posts with upvotes and comments. It later added product pages, mentions, a follower graph and galleries, but the core loop stayed the same.
- It evolved into a launch pad because makers and founders showed strong demand for getting new products out.
- Ryan worked on it on the side for about four to five months before going all in. The site had been live three to four months before he raised money.
- The first round was a $5M cap with strong traction. He says that would be roughly a $20-25M cap today. Early growth was very high and consistent for about six months, and he recalls something like 50% week over week or month over month (he's unsure which).
- He was not trying to build a startup at first. He just liked building and community, and the idea was thrown on the internet to see what happened.

## Raising the first round: traction plus a vision that could expand

- Investors understood Product Hunt easily because they were effectively the customer, since it served the tech community.
- Some doubted there would be enough products launching every day. Ryan believed there were plenty and that the supply would grow.
- He communicated two things: the short term (here's what it is today and the traction) and the long term (here's what it could become). The pitch was starting with tech, then expanding to music, movies, fashion, beauty, games and more, in the way Pinterest expanded across user-generated content categories.
- He says the pitch later changed from the original vision, and that adapting to the market is fine.
- He didn't want to take money until he was convinced it was the right path, even with lots of investor interest.

## Seeding a community: design it like a good house party

- Ryan says community design matters enormously: the same product with two different communities can have very different outcomes.
- His metaphor: a house party where you know nobody feels unwelcoming, while a party where a friend introduces you to people you respect feels like "here are my people."
- He replicated that early on by inviting designers, founders and early adopters, many with small to medium Twitter followings, so newcomers recognized and respected who was posting.
- Tactics he used:
  - Personally emailed a large share of signups to welcome them and give posting access.
  - Asked already-engaged users to CC one or two people they'd invite, like a friend bringing a friend to the party.
  - Sent lots of personal emails and tweets to build the initial strong cohort.
- The takeaway: people join products because of people, and the personal touch from a named founder goes a long way.

## What Weekend Fund looks for: insight, a why-now, and the right team

- Weekend Fund invests across B2B and consumer. It looks for the technology shift or consumer behavior shift (emergent behaviors, generational shifts) that is new today, and asks what insight the founder has about it.
- A core question is "why now." Product Hunt's own thesis was that building would get easier, so more people would start companies and distribution would get harder. He says no-code and AI generation will magnify that, lowering the bar further.
- He assumes others have tried the idea before, so something must have changed to enable it today.
- Insight is the big signal: after a call he wants to come away thinking "I didn't know that" or "that's counterintuitive." He shouldn't know more than a founder about their space.
- Product quality: he's fine with products being ugly or broken early. He cares more about the decisions behind the product (why this onboarding flow, why this feature priority, how people use it).
- He identifies the biggest risks and checks the team can address them. Examples:
  - Strong insights but zero design or product skill is an orange flag.
  - A strong product and design team building something technically hard, like AI tooling, makes him cautious without strong technical talent.
- Traction helps. For pre-product or pre-traction deals, founder insight and ability to execute matter even more. Once there is consistent, non-spiky traction, the market is saying something and that weighs more.
- Different risks call for different lenses: a known problem in a mature market leans toward product execution, while a new idea leans toward whether a market exists.

## Pitching Ryan: be succinct, test for clarity, make it authentically personal

- Easiest route is email (Ryan at weekend.fund).
- Be very clear and succinct. He sometimes reads a paragraph or two and still doesn't know what the company builds.
- Test the email on friends, and better, a stranger, and have them explain back in their own words what you build. Founders forget others have no context.
- Personalization should be authentic, not fake ("I see you invest in consumer apps"). Real effort creates reciprocity, so he's more likely to pay attention and respond.

## Deal structure, check size and how Weekend Fund supports founders

- Weekend Fund is a small check writer that doesn't lead rounds. It usually targets about 2-3% ownership in the first check, enough skin in the game that each company could theoretically return the fund multiple times.
- Roughly 10-15% of the portfolio is outside the US (India, Europe, Brazil, Canada). Metrics are mostly evaluated the same way, but revenue potential differs by geography. For example, Indian consumer apps can grow fast, but average revenue per user is dramatically lower, so user counts aren't comparable to US apps.
- Terms: sometimes he helps set the cap early in a conversation; sometimes terms are already set and he takes them. He doesn't try to demand a better deal for coming late. Valuation is not a precise science.
- He also asks how much money the founder needs for 24 months or more, then works backward. Example: raising $2M at a $5M cap would be too much dilution and cause future problems, so a higher cap may be reasonable.
- A risk of writing the first check is that the founder can't raise enough, leaving only about six months of runway. He hasn't often run into that, but notes he's been in a bull market and considers holding a check until the round is large enough.
- Support model, positioned between two bad extremes (the investor who disappears and the naggy one who is extractive):
  - Read investor updates and take catch-up calls.
  - Use that context to be tactical: intros via the LP network, Twitter and LinkedIn; jamming on product for an hour.
  - Help close rounds, intro other investors, and avoid signaling risk since he isn't the lead. If Weekend Fund doesn't follow on in the Series A, it isn't a problem.

## Consumer social, lean teams, and where new opportunities may come from

- Ryan jokes "don't invest in consumer social," but still can't help backing some. Conviction comes from the founder's insights and ability to navigate the nuances (he cites backing Alex Ma on TTYL, an early audio social network around AirPods, which led to Paparazzi).
- For consumer social, he wants very small, tight teams of generalists with strong product and consumer thinking and a low burn. A V1 needing 10 people is concerning; hiring the fifth or sixth person should come after some product-market fit.
- B2B usually has a clearer problem and market need. Consumer social is fuzzier, with "do people want this thing" unclear and more iterations needed. Post-traction, the hard question is whether metrics are sustainable, since consumers are flaky and churn around month six.
- On the future of social: it's hard to pull people from existing networks, so it needs a shift. Snapchat capitalized on a generational and behavior shift (ephemeral photos for younger users who didn't want permanent posts), not a technology shift. He thinks pure UX innovation on mobile is now much harder.
- He's more interested in new platforms or technology unlocks. He doesn't see any hardware (VR, AR glasses) reaching mainstream scale for a while, and sees AI creative tools as a possible source of new interaction modes, networks and identities, though he doesn't know the product.
- He likes founders who are scrappy and can get revenue early. Example: a thermodynamic computing company he backed already had a $2M LOI before building, and had a V1 that could make money soon alongside a long-term vision. He says revenue also shapes the product, since you build for one customer's specific workflow instead of a theoretical problem. He doesn't treat early revenue as a requirement, though, since over-prioritizing it has risks.

## Ryan's motivations and other asset classes he's exploring

- The through line in his career is that he gets energy from creating, from childhood projects (a website, a Shreveport build) to Product Hunt. He's trying to scratch that itch at Weekend Fund with content and internal tooling, and is learning music production with a coach.
- He says having a teacher cut the friction of going from zero to one, and thinks adults should have more teachers and coaches.
- He's interested in alternative investing models beyond the traditional 2-and-20 VC fund: SMB investing, creators, buying small businesses, and micro-PE roll-ups. He isn't actively working on any.
- He's invested in some small funds as an LP (around $10K checks) for the experience, and in SMBs through revenue share, such as a hair salon or matcha shop. It won't produce a 100x return, but he's unlikely to lose his money.
