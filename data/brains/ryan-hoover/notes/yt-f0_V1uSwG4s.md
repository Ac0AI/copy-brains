## Why Ryan started Product Hunt as a newsletter, not a grand plan

- Ryan did not set out to build a company. He liked finding new products and wondered why people had to "hunt for the link inside the TechCrunch articles" instead of having a simple daily list of what launched.
- He browsed the App Store in different countries and AngelList's new companies just to see what people were building.
- The first step was a newsletter, because it was easy to start. It proved that other people, not just him, wanted this content.
- A website with comments and curation followed, since the newsletter had no place for discussion or curation at scale.
- For the first 12-18 months, the roadmap "just fell out" of what people were doing and the feedback received. It was close to obvious.
- The first site worked like Reddit: posts, comments, upvotes. He chose to "not reinvent the wheel" and keep it simple.

## Over-engineering and being "too clever" with product systems

- Ryan thinks in systems and flywheels, but warns that people like him (designers, product thinkers) can become paralyzed and over-engineer.
- He made this mistake inside Product Hunt: he built new products that were "way too complicated" because he thought he was "so clever", adding every feature idea. V1 took far longer than needed.
- His rule: at the end of the day you have to prove out the core dynamic and core value prop early.
- Systems thinking is still valuable to dissect and understand. In his experience it works better emerging organically than being designed from day zero.
- He describes early-stage consumer social building as intuition-led and organic.

## How the Product Hunt roadmap grew into engagement loops

Ryan's example of a feature that unlocked a system:
1. Makers kept showing up in comments saying "I made this thing, ask me questions."
2. Product Hunt productized it by clearly labeling who made the product.
3. That let community members follow makers and see what friends or people they follow launched.
4. This unlocked email notifications when someone you follow launches, which had very high open and engagement rates.
5. Profiles followed, letting makers show off what they have built. Indie makers and young entrepreneurs were especially proud of this.

His point: a small, seemingly trivial feature (identifying makers) unlocked a whole engagement loop, and it came from watching user behavior.

## Starting with a tight-knit community and doing things that don't scale

- Before Product Hunt, Ryan hosted brunches and small meetups with founders to build relationships and learn.
- After reaching some product-market fit, meeting in person felt obvious: the community had been talking about tech online, so why not meet up.
- Community members then asked to run their own meetups (e.g. Bangalore, Berlin). There have been maybe over a thousand community-hosted meetups worldwide. Product Hunt supports them and members run them.
- This acts as an IRL version of what happens on Product Hunt: people want to connect and geek out on technology.
- He ties it to "do things that don't scale": for social and community products, "you don't need everybody on there. You actually probably don't want everybody on there." Start with a tight-knit group excited about what you built, and "that kernel can grow into something much bigger."

## Finding contrarian insights and a weird niche for consumer products

- Ask "what's changed?" Is there a regulatory shift, a consumer behavior shift, or a technology shift that enables the new thing? He uses this lens as an investor and when talking with founders.
- Snapchat example: kids on Facebook did not want parents and teachers seeing their posts, and cancel culture was emerging, so ephemerality ran counter to older generations who want to save everything.
- To build something huge, the initial insight has to be contrarian: "no one really agrees with you at the time and then everyone agrees with you later." If everyone agreed, someone would already have built it.
- After product-market fit, customers lead you into next steps that feel obvious, so the roadmap gets informed by them.
- Generational shifts matter in consumer: people who grew up with smartphones have different expectations and ways of speaking than users of today's giants.
- Pick a niche and serve it very well. Snapchat began as a product for Los Angeles high school students and was the best product in the world for them for a period, then expanded to hundreds of millions of daily users.
- He cites Nikita Bier's TBH as playing to a natural human instinct (high schoolers gossiping). One small insight about a human desire can be a powerful building insight, and one small insight can change a maker's life.

## Podcasting: Product Hunt podcast and why growth is the hard part

- The Product Hunt podcast brought on one or two guests (investors, founders, people in tech) to talk about products they love, what's on their home screen, and what they were excited about.
- He stopped doing it because it was not the highest-ROI use of time.
- They "never really cracked the growth thing": the most engaged Product Hunt users listened, but it never reached hundreds of thousands or millions.
- He sees podcast growth as the hardest part, like Twitter but harder: both take years to build a sizable audience, and podcasts lack built-in social dynamics and feedback loops.
- He has considered a new podcast but is hesitant to commit right now.

## Ryan's investing approach and the Operator LPs experiment

- Ryan invests full-time, early stage (pre-seed and seed) across weird consumer, web3 and "boring SaaS". The through-line is what has changed: a consumer behavior shift or a technology shift.
- Private-market startups are not 100% efficient but are getting more efficient as more people build, so he stresses the "why now" question.
- He splits the fund's work in two: business as usual (meeting founders, evaluating, due diligence, supporting founders) and ongoing experiments.
- He believes investing is increasingly customer-, community- and network-driven. The fund raised from 370 LPs in its latest fund, including many operators: designers, data scientists, engineers, salespeople.
- Operator LPs experiment:
  - A "GP demo day" connecting operator LPs (people who might write $10K-$100K checks into funds) with mostly small funds (sub-$20M).
  - They do not charge or take economics. The goal is to build a stronger network with LPs and GPs and to push the industry toward more collaborative, community investing.
  - Two demo days so far, three GPs each: the first generated about $1.1 million in interest, the second about $1.5 million.
  - Next step is a V2 that expands to more GPs and LPs and reduces fundraising friction.

## Advice for breaking into investing

- Write "fantasy memos". His partner Vedika, then a product manager, wrote memos on companies (such as Retool) as if proposing an investment, without sharing them publicly. Anyone can do this with no money or connections.
- It refines your thinking and creates a paper trail. When he interviewed her, she sent him the memos and he could see her work and how she thinks. His view: "if you don't want to do that, then maybe you shouldn't be investing."
- Become the XYZ person (e.g. "the community person" or "the product person"). Develop a niche and people start coming to you, becoming a center of gravity for your vertical.
- Caveat on picking a niche: choose something you have extremely high conviction in, that is authentic and sustainable. If you brand yourself around a hot trend like chatbots and it cools, you start behind the starting line and must rebrand. He notes his own firm does not follow the one-niche advice: "do as I say, not as I do."
- Creating language is a powerful signal. He points to Andreessen Horowitz and Chris Dixon coining terms (e.g. "skeuomorphism" applied to web3 social) that move thought and an industry forward. It must not feel cheesy or forced, and it must be valuable and not selfish.
- Founders should weigh check size against helpfulness. He endorses Pipe's Harry Hurst's "check size to helpfulness ratio": a $10K angel can be more helpful than a $5M lead who takes far more equity. This is why he supports small funds and angels, and he asks how founders can bring on many helpful people without taking the entire round.

## Ideas Ryan floated: an influencer-sports league and "Weekend Cafe"

**Influencer sports events (Ryan's view as audience member)**
- He attended a TikTok vs YouTube influencer boxing event, where the energy was "insane" and it was more interesting than a professional match, because influencers are entertainers who know how to work a crowd.
- His open question was whether it would get stale as a recurring league versus a one-off. He likened it to reality TV, which made regular people feel relatable and famous.
- He noted pay-per-view software exists, and the math can add up (he cited 50,000-100,000 buyers at $30-$50), though he was unsure it is venture-scalable. Someone could start small locally by reaching out to a few influencers and prove out the model.
- The concept could apply to other communities, like tech founders and investors.

**Weekend Cafe (Ryan's own idea)**
- An intimate co-working space with good music, a comfortable temperature, and first- or second-degree connections. He finds WeWork valuable but cold and not his vibe.
- The idea includes a bunch of phone booths for calls as more people work remotely.
- Possible business-model twist: 100 membership slots as utility or membership NFTs. Members can resell on a secondary market and the operator takes about 10% of secondary transactions, giving better price discovery (e.g. a $100 membership becoming $10,000).
- He had thought about it for years without acting because he is "scared of commitments."
- His concern with token-gated clubs generally: how to avoid wealthy buyers diluting the curation. Soho House was cool when curated toward artists and designers and became more corporate and finance-heavy. (He raised this as a pushback on others' versions of the idea, applying it to this one too.)

## Remote work, Miami, and where founders actually are

- Ryan grew up in Oregon, then lived in San Francisco for 10 years, LA for two, and Miami for a year at that point. He is glad he moved.
- He has always worked remotely or distributed, so Miami is one of many places he spends time (New York, LA, Tulum in coming weeks).
- Pros: no West Coast morning rush, nightlife, warm weather. Cons: summer hurricanes and heavy rain.
- His observation: more investors than founders seem to be in Miami, and he does not see a substantial number of tech founders there, though he meets founders over video anyway.
- He sees a missing shelling point for founders to meet and co-work, and is open to small events of 10-20 builders rather than large ones.
