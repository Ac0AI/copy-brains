## Fund 1 construction: small, diversified, non-lead

- Ryan's first check was a fund investment, not an angel check. He avoided angel investing while running Product Hunt (a signaling risk as CEO, plus a loose "teammates shouldn't invest" policy). After the AngelList acquisition he raised Weekend Fund in 2017, as AngelList was rolling out its funds product.
- He targeted $1M and raised $3M from product investors he had known for a long time. At that size (even sub-$10M), check size doesn't make deals harder to get into.
- His early decisions: a more diversified fund, no leading deals, and not 100 companies in two years, since he was still CEO and part-time on investing.
- Why non-lead: he treats the fund like a product sold to founders. Checks are meaningful to the fund but leave room for co-investors. Not elbowing others out of rounds attracts more deal flow, because investors bring deals to each other.
- Why diversified: "it's power law," so a fund needs enough shots to find the one or two companies that drive returns. In fund one, a $75K check at YC/seed represents well over 90% of portfolio value (he's unsure of the exact figure, 90 or 95%).

## Evolution of fund size and strategy

- Fund sizes: $3M, then $10M, then $21M for the latest. He may never raise a larger fund for first checks.
- Reason: once checks pass roughly $500K, you need to be in the conversation earlier. If a founder is 90% committed, fitting a $500K check means "cramming people down," which he doesn't want to do.
- Leading is a different game: one player competes for the lead slot, and not taking pro rata in the next round can signal badly.
- Scrapped idea: about 2.5 years ago, while planning fund three, he modeled a separate pool for small checks (e.g. $100K) into pre-founders. The model assumed higher failure rates, so it needed low entry prices and high volume.
- Why it died: the market got frothy and entry valuations over about $2M broke the model.

## How he raised LPs: three stages

1. **Own network.** Build a spreadsheet from LinkedIn, Twitter lists and email; brain-dump every possible LP (high net worth individuals, funds, family offices). Early commitments from reputable people create social proof, since raising your first dollar is hardest. Most people can't raise a full fund from first-degree contacts.
2. **Referrals from existing LPs.** Ask specifically: "can you introduce me to one or two people who are like XYZ," and supply a ready-to-forward blurb to cut friction. Existing LPs are the best referrers. Most introduced one to three more LPs, and well over 50% of those meetings converted. He jokes about an "LP K-factor."
3. **Community round (Fund 3 only).** With about $18-19M of a $20M target raised, they structured it as a 506(c) so they could discuss it publicly. They took applications for $2,000 to $10,000 checks (capped at $10K). About 700 accredited investors applied; they accepted 180, bringing total LPs to about 370. The goal was not money but a network of operators and founders for deal flow, diligence and portfolio help.

## Pitch deck and positioning to LPs

- Basics: who you are, your focus, your fund construction strategy, and your track record (angel investments and their results).
- Different LPs weigh different things. Institutional LPs ask about portfolio construction, mechanics, and whether you're building a multi-decade franchise. High net worth individuals care more about your niche and how you win great deals.
- Through-line for everyone: how you get access to the best deals and how you judge them, because there are so many funds.
- Positioning: a founder sells equity, so the investor should deliver more value than the equity taken (e.g. "if you're selling 10% of the company you better deliver 10% or more equity value"). Weekend Fund pairs small checks with outsized value from many co-investors.
- Their own strengths are product and community building. Outside that (enterprise sales, data science) they route founders to LPs, which scales beyond their own skills and time. LPs have a financial and emotional interest in helping.
- A known founder helps: the first conversation is warm.

## When to close and start investing

- No fixed rule. He suggests not deploying until you've raised at least 25% of the target.
- Risk: raising 10% (e.g. $1M of $10M) and writing $150K checks leaves about six companies if the rest never arrives, versus a model of 35-45.
- Smaller early checks distort position sizes versus the final construction.
- He is "a pro small funds" person. Raising $50M as a first fund (a friend's plan) means needing deep-pocketed LPs or institutions, which take long and often want your second or third fund. Better to raise small, deploy for a couple of years, then raise a larger fund. "We're not starting a fund to raise money from LPs. It's a means to an end."

## LP follow-up and timing the next raise

- Institutional LPs who pass on fund one: add them to your LP updates. It costs nothing, gives them data points on how you operate and communicate, and keeps the relationship warm. Some also like a catch-up every 6 to 12 months. He admits Weekend Fund hasn't prioritized this because it isn't aiming for a massive fund. Early touchpoints still matter because institutions rarely invest before seeing you operate for months or years.
- Next-raise timing: depends on how fast you can raise. Public raises can lock commitments within a month, with about two more months for wires, and they create urgency. His own rule: start conversations about six months before needing money to deploy, not a year ahead.

## Follow-on and reserves

- He says he hasn't figured out when to double down, calling it one of the most heavily debated decisions.
- Spectrum: some funds hold zero reserves and write the biggest check upfront. Weekend Fund leans that way, sizing initial checks assuming they may never get to invest again.
- Non-lead downside: even with legal pro rata, a big incoming lead may pressure the founder to cut others' allocation, and they don't want to be a difficult investor.
- They passed on some pro ratas a few years ago when companies were marked up 3-5x in under six months with similar risk. Test: "would we invest in this company if we weren't investors today, at that price?"
- Hindsight caveat: some passed Series A rounds would have returned many millions more to LPs, but "hindsight will kill you."
