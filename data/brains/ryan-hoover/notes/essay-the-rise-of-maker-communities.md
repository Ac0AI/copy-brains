## Why maker communities are emerging now

Ryan argues that building has become radically cheaper and more accessible, which creates a new problem: getting feedback and standing out. Maker communities are the response.

- Spinning up a simple web app "used to require months of development, a team of people, and tons of capital for (physical!) servers."
- Today "a single person can build more than a team of ten in a weekend." He says he sees this daily on Product Hunt.
- "No code" tools and decades of progress have made turning an idea into a product easier than ever.
- People now use technology to build and express themselves "the same way a musician dabbles on the drums."
- Side effect: it is harder to get feedback and to break out from the noise.
- He sees "increasing demand" to connect, learn, and collaborate with other makers, and new spaces keep appearing.

## How Ryan defined and mapped the maker community landscape

In November 2018 he asked Twitter which communities to include in a map of the maker ecosystem, then compiled the results with further research.

Inclusion criteria:
- The primary interaction is user-to-user or UGC: users share, connect, or collaborate with each other.
- The focus is on making something tangible in tech (an app, logo, DIY project, podcast, etc.), directly or indirectly.
- Broad social platforms like Twitter and Reddit are excluded. They serve makers but it isn't their primary purpose.
- Hundreds of small maker Slack teams and Facebook groups are skipped in favor of larger communities.

## A four-part taxonomy for maker communities

Each community is categorized by its area of focus:

1. **Vertical:** a specific industry or craft (crypto, DIY hardware, bootstrappers).
2. **Role:** a job function or skill (design, engineering, writing).
3. **Demographic:** a gender, location, age, or identifying trait (women, POC, high school students).
4. **General:** broadly serving makers of all kinds.

He notes the categories aren't perfect, since some communities span several dimensions.

Examples he lists:
- General: Product Hunt, Pioneer, Buy Me a Coffee, Patreon, YC Bookface (private to YC alumni), Prototypr.
- Vertical: Kickstarter, Stripe Atlas, Indiegogo, Substack, Indie Hackers, Nomad List, MegaMaker, Instructables, Make.
- Demographic: Girlboss, Tech Ladies, The Wing, Hack Club, Women Make, Afrotech, Elpha.
- Role: Hacker News, GitHub, Glitch, Dev, Codepen, Makerlog, Stack Overflow, freeCodeCamp, Dribbble, Behance, Designer News, MakerPad, Product Manager HQ, CMX, and others.

## Ryan's observations on gaps and opportunities in maker communities

- **Many audiences are underserved.** Engineering-focused communities are relatively abundant, which he says fits how the internet evolved. That leaves room for others to serve different types of makers.
- **Most communities are young**, which he reads as a growing desire to build community for every niche.
- **Most are web and desktop focused.** That makes sense for people working at a desk, but he suggests there may be a bigger opportunity for mobile.

## Product Hunt's tie to the maker community movement

- He frames the trend as something Product Hunt sees firsthand, through the volume of products makers launch there.
- Product Hunt is listed in the General category, and he discloses he is building "something new" for makers at Product Hunt, inviting readers to sign up for early access.
- He discloses he is an investor in Girlboss, and invites readers to suggest communities he missed via comments or Twitter.
