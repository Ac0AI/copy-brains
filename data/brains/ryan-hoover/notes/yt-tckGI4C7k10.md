## Starting without engineering: validate with non-code tools first

- Ryan applied to YC as a non-technical solo founder, which he assumed YC disliked. His answer was traction: Product Hunt was growing about 50% month over month in top-line user visits, consistently for the first 2-4 months.
- His view: "if you can demonstrate data like that it's hard to argue." Proof that people use the product is the ultimate proof, whether or not you can code.
- Product Hunt began as an email list that took about 20 minutes to set up and reached a few hundred subscribers initially. Not being an engineer forced him to avoid spending weeks building something people might not want.
- Email suits an audience that uses it daily, and it is easy to iterate on. Each day's send can change the copy or the format, whereas code is much harder to change.
- Ryan wrote an early Hacker News article, "email first startups", noting that AngelList's MVP was also an email digest.
- Non-code stack suggestion: Typeform plus Stripe lets you collect money without coding, and getting people to pay is "the ultimate test."
- He says even technical founders should often start with non-technical tools so something is out there sooner.

## Ship: tool for building demand and talking to users before launch

- Ship is a toolkit for makers and startups. It announces a new product, collects emails and gets feedback from those users.
- Product Hunt ran this workflow by hand for years using MailChimp and Typeform, then productized it after makers kept asking how to get initial users and feedback before launch.
- Makers use it to capture interest in upcoming apps and to seed beta users for feedback.
- Ryan's belief: the more you communicate with your audience, make them feel involved and get their feedback, the higher the chance you build something people want.
- Product Hunt had never charged for anything except job postings long ago, which it dropped to focus on the community. Ship was the first direct charge.
- A Slack bot notifies the team of each payment, including a $249/month subscription on launch day.
- Q4 strategy: build things people like enough to pay for. A second planned stream is connecting companies with talent, using Product Hunt's engineers, designers and marketers and AngelList's talent platform, without heavy-handed pop-ups.

## Launching on Product Hunt: build an audience and engage it before launch

- Ryan says upvote counts don't predict success. Launching is one moment, while startups are multi-year journeys, and many succeed only in year five to seven.
- Product Hunt doesn't cover everyone either. A product for an audience that doesn't use it shouldn't be judged on upvotes.
- Common mistake: building a product you think the world will love without getting community feedback or building an audience first.
- He cites Casey Winters as an example. Before building, he built an audience, so whatever he ships gets at least some attention.
- His tips: build an audience of the people you're targeting, engage them and get early feedback, and use Ship to do both.
- Without his first few hundred sign-ups, built from years of audience-building, Ryan doesn't think Product Hunt would exist today.
- Customer service is an engagement opportunity: "people care enough to talk to you." He runs TweetDeck with a column for every Product Hunt mention and replies to issues.

## Growing the early community

- First few hundred users: people who had followed his writing for years. He wrote about products and behavior, wrote with Nir Eyal on *Hooked*, and had relationships with founders and investors who were comfortable joining.
- Press was a strong early growth driver. He wrote a Fast Company guest piece on how they got their first 2,000 users, aimed at getting another thousand.
- Maker outreach: when someone's product was posted, Ryan searched Twitter each morning for their handle and invited them to answer questions. About 80% said yes. He spent the first hour of each day on this, which led to several thousand early users.
- Productized it: the community tags makers, and a bot notifies them they were added to a product with a link. Makers get upset if they are never notified.
- Audience is international from early on (no 80% US skew), tracking startup hubs such as Paris and Berlin.
- Product Hunt reflects trends: anonymous social apps around Secret, then blockchain/crypto and AI.

## Category expansion and staying focused

- Product Hunt added games, books and podcasts, and Ryan admits mistakes in that execution.
- Podcasts were quietly shut down: discovering podcasts differs from discovering apps, and new episodes appear weekly or faster, which didn't fit the existing community dynamics.
- Conclusion: "podcast is not our business." Product Hunt focuses on product discovery.
- He still thinks a community place to discover and discuss podcasts will exist, and names Breaker (YC) as executing on it.
- Why not a syndicate or fund for top products: it would shift priorities toward building an investor platform, a different product from product discovery for the world. They hold lightweight founder and investor dinners and meetups with AngelList instead.

## YC lessons and interview advice

- YC's main value for him was accountability: weekly group office hours where partners (Kevin Hale and Kat Manalac) asked "did you do that thing we talked about last week?" He calls this uncomfortable but the best way to get off the ground.
- Interview approach: he sold the vision, a place to discover the products being built. He projected confidence, and expected interruptions. He prepared to move on when cut off and tried to be succinct.
- YC instilled a data-driven, talk-to-your-customers culture that he thinks Product Hunt already had.
- Process keeps changing as the team grows: Product Hunt raised a Series A right after YC and hired, and he expects to revise process again each quarter based on what went poorly.

## Building a remote team and managing as a founder

- Product Hunt had 17 people across eight or nine time zones, about two-thirds outside SF. The first hire, Ricardo, was in Italy and paid out of pocket. Being distributed began as necessity, not strategy.
- Advantages: hire anyone in the world, lower cost than SF, avoid competing with Google/Facebook pay and perks for seed-stage hires, and a global product perspective (over half the product and community is outside the US).
- Challenges: communication and limited overlap, sometimes about four hours of shared time. He calls them workable. Tools: Slack and Zoom.
- Early PM mistake: he rewrote a UX designer's work over a weekend to save time, which caused frustration, and he's not a UX designer.
- Lesson: as a team grows, founders must give up work, hire the right people and give them autonomy. It feels less productive short-term but is better long-term.

## Weekend Fund and early-stage investing

- Raised a $3M fund via AngelList's Angel Funds, a "VC in a box": they handle legal setup, banks and LP communication for about $12,000 plus a share of carry, versus roughly $100,000 normally.
- Checks: $50,000 to $200,000 in early-stage companies.
- Thesis is intentionally open, favoring companies where he can be helpful, such as community-based products. Interested areas: remote and distributed team tools and voice.
- Early investments leaned community-focused. The name "Weekend Fund" nods to Product Hunt starting as a side project and to humble weekend projects growing big.
