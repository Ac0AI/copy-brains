## Ryan's early product-management mistakes and what he does differently

- Ryan became a product manager at about 22, coming from a marketing internship. Looking back, he calls his early work embarrassing.
- He spent weekends alone writing specs and wireframes, believing he was helping the company by working hard.
- He now sees that as a huge mistake. He was "living in my own box", not talking to customers or the team.
- Handing a team a finished sheet of "here are the things to build" doesn't build buy-in. People don't invest in a product they had no hand in shaping.
- His fixes: talk to customers, get the team's input early, and build the product together.

## Using the community in the product-building process

- Product Hunt's success depends entirely on its community: without people posting products, voting and commenting, it has nothing. Ryan says involving the community in building works as a tactic even for products that aren't community-based.
- Tactic: share mockups before building. The team sends an InVision mockup to the community with context and asks for feedback and ideas.
- InVision lets people annotate anywhere on the mockup with red dots. A few hours after sending one out, the page was covered in dots, and the team had feedback before building anything.
- Contributors feel more involved because they are shaping the product, and they are.
- The team doesn't share everything it designs, only some things, and does it early and often.
- Don't build everything people suggest. Work out why they suggest it: the idea may be the wrong solution, but the problem or goal behind it is worth understanding.

## Using metrics, KPIs and fake-door tests

- Every product and initiative at Product Hunt should move, directly or indirectly, three core KPIs: general user growth, revenue, and community contributions (a measure of community health).
- Example of a cheap test: login was only via Twitter and Facebook. The hypothesis was that some people, especially those logging in for business, didn't want to use social profiles.
- The team put a Google login button on the login page without building the login and counted clicks. This was a "Wizard of Oz" test.
- About 40-45% of clicks went to Google login, which justified building it.
- Ryan says data suits optimizations like this. For something brand new there's often no data, so you rely on gut and knowledge of the audience.
- Product Hunt has also surveyed its community about motivations and goals to inform product decisions.

## Flywheels as a growth framework

- Ryan recommends dissecting a product as a flywheel. Product Hunt's flywheel wasn't designed up front; it emerged organically.
- The loop: someone finds a product and shares it on Product Hunt, which introduces it to the maker. The maker shares it with their audience, which includes other makers, so more posts lead to more makers, more people and more products.
- The less friction between each step, and the more people who do each action, the better the system works and the more users arrive.
- Method: map your product's steps (step 1, 2, 3...) and find the one with heavy friction or failure. Fixing it can complete the flywheel and drive growth.
- Example: early on there was no system to tag makers or notify them. Makers only noticed when they saw odd traffic in Google Analytics.
- Product Hunt then let the community tag makers. If you know a maker's username, tagging notifies them on Twitter or by email if the team has their address.
- Ryan's next step is automating this so every maker gets tagged efficiently. He says it is hard but worth solving.
- He cites the AngelList talent platform as another flywheel: free job postings attract candidates, which produce matches, which attract more companies.

## Trends: use them without forcing them

- Ryan has watched trends on Product Hunt: anonymous apps (when Secret was popular), on-demand startups for everything, and now blockchain/crypto and AI. Some "AI" products just sprinkle it on for marketing.
- Trends can be a trap. People force blockchain or AI onto a problem that isn't native to them.
- He stresses founder-market fit: the best founders solve a problem for themselves or have a unique advantage. His own example of a poor fit: he would be the worst person to build an automated laundry service.
- Trends are still valuable as a new opportunity. Ask what blockchain or AI makes possible today that wasn't possible yesterday.
- Two framings he likes beyond trends: platform shifts (smartphones and mobile enabled Instagram and Snapchat) and behavior shifts (e.g., a younger generation not wanting to share on Facebook with family, which Snapchat used).
- Timing example: Dailybooth was a roughly decade-old desktop product for a daily webcam selfie, reaching millions of users but ultimately failing partly because the platform was wrong. Launched five years later and focused on mobile, it might have worked.
- Incumbents like Facebook and WhatsApp are susceptible to platform and behavior shifts, which are holes in their armor that startups can exploit. Ryan says timing is hard to predict, and being too early is also a risk.

## Voice, audio and blockchain social networks

- Ryan is most interested in voice and audio. People are shifting from tapping screens to audio interfaces and forming new habits, such as saying "OK Google, play..." out loud.
- He cites 40-60 million US homes with an Alexa or Google Home device.
- Exercise for finding opportunities: list behaviors people repeat daily that suit voice better than typing. His example is asking for the weather while getting dressed.
- He also suggests B2B voice use cases, since B2B more often has a direct revenue or efficiency impact and is a better business than hard-to-win consumer.
- He has invested in a couple of audio companies, calling it an early bet on an audio-based future.
- He also finds blockchain-based social networks fascinating. Traditional social networks are centralized, and the people who create the value earn none of the revenue. A token model could change that, which is why Facebook built a team to explore it.

## Team structure: hierarchy and titles help

- His most counterintuitive lesson, learned recently: startups are often told to be flat, with no titles and everyone wearing many hats. Ryan found a lack of titles and structure can be bad.
- Product Hunt moved to giving people domain ownership and creating team leads. Before, everyone reported to him, which doesn't scale.
- Results: people are more motivated and clearer on what they are responsible for. Hierarchy and titles exist in most companies for a reason.
- The team is distributed: about 3 people in San Francisco and about 14 elsewhere (Bulgaria, Paris, Toronto, New Zealand, India). Over half the community is outside the US and less than 2% is in Silicon Valley.
- He rejected splitting Product Hunt into regional versions (Europe, India, US), valuing the diversity of what people build worldwide. He notes products from Paris are consistently beautifully designed.

## Launching advice

- There is no single launch. You are always shipping and launching, whether it's a first version, a second version or a new feature.
- Common mistake: selling the dream (the vision and five-year plan) instead of the product you built today.
- Describe what the product does and how it's useful. For a video conferencing app, "the future of remote working" tells people nothing, while something like "super fast way to video chat with your co-workers" says what it does. Visitors lack your context and don't care about your vision.
- Test messaging by showing your landing page or tagline to people who aren't your friends and know nothing about the product. Ask for impressions, and if they don't get it, rework it.
- Talk to people, but have a perspective. Customers inform your decisions about what to build; they shouldn't tell you what to build.
- Brand note: the Product Hunt cat with Google Glass came from an illustrator named Jesse who offered free t-shirt and flag art early on. Ryan liked it because it felt "friendly and welcoming and a little bit silly", and it now reflects the culture. He says it wasn't strategic or data-driven.
