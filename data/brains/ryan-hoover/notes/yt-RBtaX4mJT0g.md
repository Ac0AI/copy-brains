## Early life and the entrepreneurial lens

- Ryan grew up in Eugene, Oregon, in a middle-class family. His parents were entrepreneurs who worked together on "normal" small and medium businesses, not tech-enabled or VC-backed ones. His mom handled back-office accounting and operations; his dad was a problem solver and salesperson.
- His parents owned a video game store. At 14 he worked there for about $4 an hour, talking to customers and wiping down the glass counters.
- His dad's saying was "find the need and fill it". Ryan says this shaped how he sees the world: he can't help spotting business opportunities and problems solvable through entrepreneurship.
- As a kid he bought and sold on eBay, did some gray-hat projects (such as bonding Xboxes in high school), and built a website. These small projects scratched the entrepreneurial itch.
- He hates hourly pay. The incentive is misaligned: he could do the best or worst job and get paid the same. He prefers being paid on impact or output. He held hourly jobs in college (a call center, a pool store).

## How Product Hunt started: a side project, not a company plan

- Ryan never woke up and decided to start a company. After studying at the University of Oregon, he interned at a video game company in Eugene, then moved to San Francisco in 2010 to join an early mobile gaming startup, PlayHaven (about employee number six). He did product there for about three and a half years.
- He followed his curiosity. He blogged about products (Snapchat's prompt design, Tinder's card stack that cues you to swipe more) and used writing "as a vehicle to express myself and learn".
- Product Hunt began as a newsletter aggregating products launched each day, sent to a few friends. He had little business thinking behind it; he just wanted to see if people liked it.
- Subscribers grew organically, which gave him confidence. He then turned it into a simple web app with his friend Nathan Bashaw in early 2014, built on the side over about four months.
- It grew quickly with no outside capital and no company. He paid a Heroku bill of roughly $500 a month and ran some advertising to cover server costs. Ryan Delk, then at Gumroad, Venmo'd him the first $5,000 the project made.

## Raising money for Product Hunt

- Raising was "very, very easy" back then. The market was good (valuations lower than today), and Product Hunt served the tech community, so it already had investors' attention. VCs were courting him for coffee months before he decided to raise.
- He was cautious and delayed the decision for many weeks until he had conviction. His reasoning:
  - You can't just give the money back if you change your mind.
  - Raising VC capital means you have to go big; you can't aim for a $10 million exit.
- He says many founders get infatuated with the fundraising process without asking what they need the money for. His answer was simple: hire a team, since he had no money to build one.

## Product Hunt's mission and community

- Over roughly seven and a half years, Product Hunt saw something like 200,000 to 250,000 products launched.
- Ryan says his motivation was not only VC-backed companies. It was encouraging people to build and giving them first users and self-confidence, such as a 14-year-old in the middle of nowhere launching a first app, Chrome extension or website.
- Users range from VC-backed startups to teenagers. Ryan says the global community helps people reach at least their first hundred, maybe thousand or tens of thousands, of users.
- Ryan stepped down as CEO in October 2020. Josh Buckley took over and the team has grown about 50% in recent months. Product Hunt was sold to AngelList; Ryan stayed about four years.
- On the cost of community: it is partly a support role, fielding issues every morning, and it is draining. Some long-tenured people stay (several have been there five to six years), but some community staff have burned out.

## Becoming an investor and starting Weekend Fund

- He first considered venture in 2013, before Product Hunt. The only path then was an associate role, and as an introvert who didn't want to do a "bunch of coffee meetings", he passed.
- In 2017, AngelList launched venture funds, its first all-in-one fund management product after mostly doing syndicates/SPVs. On a walk-and-talk, Ryan asked Naval for advice on starting to invest. Naval suggested he start a fund.
- Ryan thought a fund was daunting and something he hadn't "earned the right" to do. He planned to raise $1 million and ended up raising $3 million for the first fund.
- Two motivations:
  - He was already doing similar work at Product Hunt (finding companies, building founder relationships).
  - He wanted to find out whether he was good at investing and start building that muscle as soon as possible.
- On rolling funds: the emerging-manager problem is that it's hard to predict how much you can raise. He aimed for $1M and raised $3M. The flexibility of not fixing fund size upfront lets you test the market.

## Investing philosophy and filters

- He plays where he has an edge: private, early-stage technology companies. He has never bought an individual public stock and doesn't follow macro markets. His filter is "good product, good team".
- His advice to emerging managers is to be an expert in something and go deep. He admits he doesn't follow it: Weekend Fund invests broadly across consumer and B2B, avoiding hard tech and hardware, because it is introduced to many interesting emerging spaces.
- Deal flow comes from co-investors and his network, but also cold emails and proactive outreach after spotting something (for example on Product Hunt).
- On the biggest lesson: there is no formula for early-stage investing. Weekend Fund has passed on deals that failed its check boxes and that became billion-dollar companies a year or two later.
- On consensus: "where there's smoke there might be fire", so check out what everyone is talking about, but don't chase the herd.
- Crypto: he is not a token or NFT day trader because he lacks an edge. Weekend Fund invests in companies building things of real utility (DAOs, social tokens, tools that help communities organize and align incentives), not token projects as such. He believes this is where the internet is going.
- Examples he cited:
  - Deel: pitched as "Gusto for remote workers". Weekend Fund was a seed investor, and Deel later raised a Series C at a $1.25B valuation. The thesis in 2017 was that distributed and remote work would be the future, partly because Product Hunt itself was fully distributed.
  - Magic: a passwordless authentication platform. Weekend Fund did a $2M SPV. He likes it because it serves Web2 customers and revenue today while building for a Web3 future.
  - MiamiCoin (City Coins): he follows it as an opt-in, tax-equity-style idea in which 30% of purchases goes to a city-controlled wallet and the coin is programmable.

## Building a community around the fund

- Ryan loves building and says community is often misused as a word. At Weekend Fund it means activating the 350+ LPs (operators and founders) who are excited to support early-stage companies and giving them a system to help founders directly, not only through the fund managers.
- The reasoning: founders have limited equity to give out, and each percentage point should buy high return. Founders benefit from as many capable, financially invested people around the table as possible.
- About 40 to 50% of people who invested through the community raise had never invested in a fund before. Some checks were as small as $2,000.
- Constraints: accreditation laws limit who can invest. He hopes laws change and has floated a future equity crowdfund, which has its own restrictions. If the community system scales, he says LPs could be far more helpful than the fund managers alone.
