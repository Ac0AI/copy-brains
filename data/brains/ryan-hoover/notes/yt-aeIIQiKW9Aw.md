## Why Ryan started Product Hunt as an email list and kept it a side project

- Product Hunt began as a community-curated email digest of new apps, sites and products. Ryan was, in his words, a "manifestation of all the things I love": playing with new products and services.
- He built it with Linky Dink (by Makeshift), a service where invited people email in a link plus a sentence and it auto-sends a digest each weekday at about 5pm. He had no approval control over submissions.
- It started with about 20-30 contributors and 200-300 subscribers in the first week or so. It had about 400 subscribers when work on the site began, roughly three weeks in, with no marketing beyond tweets and a few posts.
- Validation was unprompted emails from readers saying they liked the list.
- Ryan is not an engineer. He asked a friend whether to learn Ruby on Rails or use Telescope. The friend, Nathan Basha (designer, product manager and engineer), offered to build it together over Thanksgiving.
- The first site cost no money, only time. It was a passion project with no business cards and no plan to start a company: "I just want to find cool new products and share them with friends."
- At the time he worked part-time (about 20 hours a week at reduced salary) while leaving a previous startup. That gave him time to experiment.

## Seeding a community with a small, connected group and doing things that don't scale

- Early contributors were product enthusiasts in the tech scene (friends, investors, reporters), not "anybody in the world submitting anything." Ryan says this made it useful from day one.
- The first ~50 people were loosely connected, many knew each other or knew of each other. People he respected using it made others feel welcome to contribute.
- He grew it by email, one person at a time: ask engaged members "do you know two more people that you think would like Product Hunt and want to contribute?" He spidered out through degrees of separation.
- It was manual: many personal emails and lots of tweeting. He says people recognize a real human behind an email, unlike automated drip campaigns.
- He doesn't do this today because it doesn't scale, but "in the early days when you're just starting things up you have to often times do those things that are super laborious... seem really inefficient."
- He pushes for exclusivity early. Small invite-only groups are more intimate, and a shared bond (location, industry, loose network) gives a community its starting culture.

## Product design decisions that kept the community healthy

- Ryan worried that opening up to everyone from day one would bring marketers and trolls, and he was the only community manager, with unmanned hours while he slept.
- Invite-only: the community invited others, so the system wasn't flooded with products.
- Twitter authentication: no email-only signups and no anonymous accounts. Companies still can't sign up under a brand name. The goal is to see the person's face and name for "real kind of direct interaction" and empathy. Identity curbs trolling, since "it's a lot easier to hate on someone when you're hiding behind a fake name." He notes pseudonyms work well for Reddit, so identity isn't right for every platform.
- Ongoing temptation: opening posting to everyone would likely give about 10x the products on the homepage and more engagement. But with 1,000 products, a product at 500th place gets lost. The goal is surfacing great products, so he manages volume against quality as the community scales.
- Culture is set by early members and is hard to change later. This is true of Reddit, 4chan and Hacker News too. Tone set in the beginning influences long-term direction dramatically.

## Earlier community experiments that informed Product Hunt

- Ryan's background was product management, with no formal community-building job. Earlier side projects worked as retroactive practice.
- Startup Edition (with Nathan): a weekly email of blog posts. They emailed founders and CEOs a writing prompt (e.g. "how I built my team," "how I raised capital"). About 40-50 participants blogged on it, and the pair assembled the posts into a digest for subscribers. They also ran brunches of 8-10 people for in-person community.
- The model: give people a reason and platform to write, and curate the output for a broader audience.
- Pre-Product Hunt relationships mattered later. By the time he raised money, investors already knew him, and reporters he pitched had existing relationships with him. He didn't build them with the intent of extracting value, but they paid off.
- He built the network through curiosity: Twitter ("lightweight" and accessible for tech people), blog posts and writing, sharing his takes on products.

## Delegation, hiring and getting advice as a founder

- A mistake he names: holding onto tasks. When it was just him he did everything (emailing, product wireframes, marketing). As the team grew he had to trust people to run with the job they were hired for.
- His reasoning: "you should be hiring people who are way better at all these things than you anyway," and his time and energy won't scale. He also doesn't have the best ideas, and teammates will make mistakes just as he does.
- Excessive control drives away the best talent.
- Two keys to giving people room: hire the right people, which is hard if you haven't worked with them, and make sure you're aligned on where you're going. Otherwise someone builds outside the vision and the founder takes the work back. He calls communication among teammates one of the most important things.
- Moving to mission-based initiatives rather than task or project assignment was a leadership process change prompted by board member Steven Sinofsky's input.
- Ryan leaned on experienced people: Josh Elman (Greylock) introduced the lawyer Josh Cook, who has handled incorporation and legal work since. Steven Sinofsky (board, a16z) advised him through the first time he had to fire someone.
- Fundraising is something most founders do rarely, so he talked to mentors and former founders. Lesson: "being okay with asking people for help."
- He values boards despite criticism of them: they keep a founder in check and flag pitfalls (legal, product direction, process) the founder doesn't know about.
- Backstory: early on, Naval (AngelList) was a seed investor. In 2017 Product Hunt joined AngelList, which focuses on fundraising, recruiting and now getting users. Both started as email lists (AngelList's was highly qualified investors receiving forwarded hot deals).

## Early tinkering, entrepreneurial roots and why passion makes it easier

- At about 14, Ryan built a site after a friend made $100,000 from one. Operation Laugh (around 2002-2003) hosted jokes and comics and ran banner ads. He made about $70 and hosting cost about $60, so it was technically profitable after dozens of hours. He says the value was learning that making money online is hard and learning HTML and CSS.
- He hacked his Xbox as a kid (soldering in a modchip) partly to avoid paying $60 for games, and broke one doing it.
- His parents owned a video game store in the late '90s. He worked there at 13-14 and ran gumball machines, restocking and recording expenses and profits.
- He liked entrepreneurship because pay depends on output, not hours. Money is "a measurement of impact or value that you create."
- He used to dread public speaking but found pitching investors and giving talks easy once it was about something he was passionate about and knew well.
- Product Hunt's team hires for curiosity: curious people are open-minded, willing to experiment, and drawn to discovering new products daily.

## Ryan's stance: embrace tinkering and creation, and stop judging side projects

- Ryan encourages tinkering and curiosity and loves seeing side projects launched on Product Hunt.
- He finds the startup ecosystem's negativity frustrating ("does the world need another gift app?").
- His argument: not every technology product is a company. Building an app, Chrome extension or website is increasingly "a creative expression," like learning guitar or drums. Nobody criticizes a kid whose first song is awful because he's learning.
- Conclusion: "we should embrace more creation... be less judgmental about people's products," because the kid tinkering could become "the next Zuckerberg" and build something meaningful.
- Product Hunt itself was an experiment, not a planned company.
