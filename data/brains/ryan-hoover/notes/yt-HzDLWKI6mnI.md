## Launching: decide the goal first, then write like a human

- Ryan's default answer on launching is "it depends." He starts by asking the same question he asks about fundraising: what is the goal?
- Many founders default to customer acquisition, but launching can serve other goals:
  - Recruiting: sharing the story gets people excited about the company, which can help land engineers and designers.
  - Fundraising: more public attention creates momentum and heat around a deal.
  - Feedback from users, and serendipitous partnerships.
  - SEO: positive articles and a product launch page help.
  - Team morale: a launch lets the team celebrate what it built, for example sharing a TechCrunch article with a parent.
- He warns against launching only to chase likes, tweets and votes, but sees morale as an overlooked benefit.
- He has seen tens of thousands of launches on Product Hunt.

## Launch copy and Product Hunt page tactics

- Founders tend to "write like a PR person and not like a human." Buzzwords and vague language don't click.
- Tagline test: write how your customers describe the product to other people, or how you'd describe it to friends when hanging out.
- Keep the maker introduction short. Most browsers are skimming many ideas looking for something that sparks curiosity, and they won't read an essay.
- The gallery is often the first thing people see. Some of the best launches use it like a slideshow that tells a story, such as a before/after with the product or the evolution of what it can do. Keep it clean and visually interesting.
- Product Hunt has links to guides, FAQs and a post-launch checklist.
- Before the launch, understand who you're building for and how to communicate the value prop, and know where the product fits in the user's mind.
- Results vary widely. B2B products probably do better there than B2C, boring-seeming products that tap into a real need can reach #1, and silly apps do too. Success is partly driven by the zeitgeist of what the early-adopter tech community finds interesting.

## Whether and how to raise money

- Ryan challenges founders on why they need to raise. Tech culture treats raising as the default first step after an idea, but it's a huge decision and the answer should be clear.
- Product Hunt began as a side project and newsletter. About four to five months passed before incorporating, during which he considered whether to raise, whether he wanted to work on it for years, and whether it was venture-backable. Investors were courting him at the time (late 2013 to early 2014).
- He raised because he needed to hire and had no personal capital. An open-source build was the alternative, but he doesn't think a company should innovate on too many things, including how it builds a product and team.
- Today he thinks he could fund about a year of a very small team himself, so he might not raise venture capital. Product Hunt had no direct competitors that forced a war chest, unlike an Uber and Lyft situation.
- Raise from people aligned with you. Series A, B and C bring different expectations than pre-seed or seed, and founders can't easily quit: "you can't get off."
- Rule of thumb on starting: do you see yourself working on this for a decade? It probably won't last that long, but it's a litmus test of whether you care.
- Call early projects "experiments." The goal is to learn whether people want the thing, not "success." This is partly why it's named Weekend Fund.
- For his next build, he'd like to own 100% for now: "you can always raise venture later, it's a one-way door."

## Regrets building Product Hunt

- Expanding horizontally was a mistake. Product Hunt tried podcasts, video games and books. He severely underestimated two things: the product experience for discovering games or fashion differs from tech products, and translating a community into other communities is very hard (Reddit is one of the few that did it). He says they should have focused vertically on the tech community.
- Monetize sooner. They raised a seed and a Series A but didn't try to make money until after AngelList acquired them. Once they started generating revenue, they reached cash-flow break-even in about 12 months. Had they started in 2014, they could have been break-even around 2015 or 2016, with flexibility and ownership of their destiny. He says he should have put roughly 10% of focus on revenue.
- Delegate more. Until shortly before leaving he was editing the newsletter every morning around 5 to 5:30 a.m. The team was great; the issue was his own trust. Caring about details helps, but he can't scale himself and isn't the best person at everything.

## Finding consumer startup ideas

- Consumer is hard for several reasons:
  - Monetization is harder than B2B, and is often advertising-driven, which requires massive scale first.
  - You compete for attention with everything, such as Netflix, and against incumbents' built-in habits.
  - Eventually you need an internal trigger in the user's mind, though you start with external triggers.
  - Problems are fuzzier. Twitter's use comes down to innate desires like staying informed or feeling less lonely, whereas DocuSign solves a clear problem (signing legal docs online).
- It's very hard, maybe impossible, to build a consumer company without a unique insight.
- Idea sources he uses:
  - A "problem journal": write down annoyances without trying to solve them (for example, no laundry in many San Francisco units).
  - Immerse in niche or long-tail communities (subreddits, a robotics hobbyist community) and watch for repeated problem statements.
  - Observe consumer behavior shifts. In 2017, Weekend Fund had distributed work as a theme. After COVID, Airbnb reshaped its home page toward exploring places at different times of year.
  - Observe technology shifts: what can be built today that couldn't be yesterday (mobile in the early 2010s, web3, AI).
- Because more people build, the market for problem discovery is more efficient, so you need a stronger "why now" and a reason it doesn't exist already.

## Start narrow

- The narrower the audience, the easier it is to build a great solution, with tighter product experience, messaging and homepage value prop aimed at exactly those people.
- He cautions this isn't universal, but if the goal is a massive company, don't worry too much about the niche being too small. Expansion opportunities appear over time.
- Example: Airbnb started with couches and extra rooms, which most people wouldn't do, and expanded to housing.

## Angel investing and running a fund

- A product management background helps in early-stage investing: it gives perspective on ideas and, more importantly, on how the founder thinks and reached their product insights.
- Weekend Fund has invested in over 110 companies. The third fund is $21 million and about 30 to 35% deployed. He prefers staying small so he can write smaller checks, fit into competitive deals and be collaborative, and may keep the next fund around this size.
- Operational "hygiene":
  - Always respond to a founder after a meeting, with context, not just "pass".
  - Always follow up with the person who made the introduction to close the loop.
- Biggest surprise: companies he mentally wrote off (product not working after many months) later pivoted or got a major partnership or M&A event. His lesson: "never count a portfolio company out until it's over."
- He evaluates holistically (team, market, product), judging the team indirectly through the product thinking and traction. He makes pure founder bets only on people he knows well, for example a talented designer-engineer who may launch ten apps before the eleventh succeeds.
- Ways to get into investing:
  - Angel investing: maximum flexibility but needs disposable income you can lose, and takes about 10 years to pay off.
  - Scout programs: easier to start, but less autonomy.
  - SPVs: deal-by-deal carry and you can bring in strategic people, but it's a lot of work to fundraise for each deal.
  - Raising a fund (his 2017 choice): the most work and responsibility, but the most flexibility to write many checks without much personal capital.
  - No capital: write memos on companies you'd back (his colleague Vetica did this before joining) to build a track record.

## Momentum and founder mindset

- Momentum matters hugely in early-stage startups and is reflexive: high momentum creates more of it, and slow shipping drags others' motivation down.
- Being a CEO means wearing a confident "mask" for the team without subjecting them to all of your anxiety. He values authenticity but can't share everything.
- When founders DM him for free advice, many are vulnerable, such as having three months of runway left. He answers by DM, then tweets anonymized Q&As about every three or four days as "micro blog posts" that refine his own thinking.
- He deprioritizes dark mode before product-market fit: building small things before the product is useful is a red flag, with the exception of audiences like developers who may need it.
