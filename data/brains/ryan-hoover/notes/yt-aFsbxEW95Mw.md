## Launch as a continuous process, not a one-time event

- Ryan says launching is never a single moment: "you're always launching."
- Product Hunt launched an email list, then a beta, then the website, then many features.
- Because of that, he stays in constant contact with users. Product Hunt runs feature flags and beta users, and tests both qualitatively and quantitatively.
- Example: one weekend he planned an MVP of a new idea using only a Typeform survey and a Quip document, with no software, to see whether people would use it.
- He applies the same logic to any creative work (photo series, paintings, mini-documentaries): everything you make is a chance to get feedback and earn some respect.
- Going into a bunker and coming out with a "tada" is, in his words, fiction.

## Build an audience before you build the product

- Ryan says the overlooked part of launching is building an audience or community in your space before the product exists.
- Product Hunt would not exist without his earlier writing, which gave him people who loved tech.
- He wrote about 150 essays in 2013, some very short, on technology, user psychology and marketing. Over about two years that produced a small following on Twitter and a personal email list.
- That gave him an audience to post the launch to, and a community of tech people.
- You don't need thousands of people on day one. You need about 100 who love what you're building.
- This is not transactional self-promotion. Create something of value for a community: a podcast, a video show, writing or meetups.
- That audience becomes your first feedback, first users, first customers and possibly hires. Andreas, Product Hunt's CTO, was an early prototype user.
- Old model: build in the basement, ship, and hope people find it. His view: "if no one finds out about it doesn't matter." Distribution and discovery are now the hard part because building is easier and more people are building.

## How Product Hunt went from idea to company

- Ryan quit his product job gradually. He gave two months' notice, then went part-time at roughly half salary (20 hours a week) while exploring ideas. He says early on you need time and space more than money.
- It started as an email list, set up in about half an hour or less. He sent daily new products to people following him on Twitter.
- After 2 to 4 weeks, people kept signing up and replying that they liked it.
- Around Thanksgiving, four years before this interview, he and Nathan Bashaw (who built it, as Ryan is not an engineer) made the website in about 5 days. It was a simple Ruby on Rails app with posts and a comment feed.
- Beta ran about 5 to 10 days with roughly 100 people. He emailed each one individually and involved them in the process, then launched in December.
- It kept growing for 3 to 4 months as a side project, not even incorporated.
- The next decision was whether to raise money, build a team, or stay a side project monetized with job postings. He talked to Gary Tan, Kevin Hale, Kat and Alexis Ohanian before applying to Y Combinator, mainly for feedback, since he could not undo raising money.
- Several YC companies already used Product Hunt, so it circulated there. He applied, interviewed and got in.

## Why mentorship and Twitter relationships work

- Ryan has no formal mentors, and thinks "can you be my mentor?" emails from strangers are a hard ask. His approach: identify who you can learn from and create value for them, or keep it casual.
- He met Nir Eyal by emailing him after admiring his writing for 3 to 4 years, which led to him helping write Nir's book.
- Twitter is the main tool for building relationships, because it is passive and light touch:
  - Follow people and engage over about 12 months, adding value to conversations.
  - Then reach out ("it's me from Twitter") so the ice is already broken.
  - When he read tech blogs, he always tagged the author. It cost 5 to 10 seconds, gave them credit, and built recognition and relationships over years.
- Early advisors included Josh Elman (Greylock) and lawyer Josh Cook.
- He now mentors in return, since people helped him at that stage.

## Does location matter?

- Product Hunt would not exist if Ryan weren't in San Francisco. The serendipity of the city led to connections such as Josh Elman and future investors.
- He says there is no single right way to build a startup. Identify your biggest current challenge and solve for it:
  - Hiring: distributed teams work (Product Hunt had people in about nine time zones). Local teams need a talent hub, which Eugene, Oregon is not.
  - Fundraising: it is easier in San Francisco, though you can travel.
  - Media: Los Angeles has advantages. Finance: New York.
- Over half of Product Hunt's makers are outside the US, so it is global.
- He prefers digital communication for reach, since one post or tweet reaches thousands, then doubles down on in-person time with the people he most wants to know. He avoids most conferences as costly in time and less intimate.
- He also cites founder-market fit (Chris Dixon's idea): whether you are the right person to build for that market.

## Common launch mistakes and what good looks like

- **Most common mistake: marketing-speak.** Founders lose touch with the real value proposition, use buzzwords, and describe what the product might be in five years instead of what it does today. The result is disingenuous and confusing.
- **Fix:** use simple, clear language, and get people who don't know you to review the landing page, onboarding and copy.
- **The two-second test:** visitors judge "is this for me?" and "does this look useful or interesting?" If you miss either, they won't sign up.
- **Good example: Superhuman**, the email client from Rahul Vohra (founder of Rapportive):
  - It onboards users manually, one by one, in a long private beta.
  - A 20 to 30 question survey asks how much users care about speed, snippets, read replies and so on.
  - It makes sure the early product serves the needs of its audience (for example, someone who must have labels).
  - It positions around speed and saving time ("give you superpowers"), not "a better email client."
- **Underlying needs people buy for:** saving time, saving money, solving loneliness (a reason people use Facebook and Twitter), or just having fun.
- **Examples:** Instacart implies saving time without saying so. CryptoKitties has a fun, collectible value prop plus an implied crypto and blockchain angle.

## Share openly; don't hide your idea

- Ryan says being secretive about an idea is not your real risk: "you have a lot more problems other than someone's stealing your idea."
- Many people have asked him to sign an NDA before giving feedback. He thinks people are too busy to care.
- The odds that someone steals your idea and out-executes you are "somewhere around zero."
- Sharing and getting feedback is more advantageous than secrecy.
- For creative people nervous about publishing: haters usually mean people are paying attention, and the worst case is that nobody notices.
- His brother posted dozens or hundreds of videos on YouTube, Facebook and Instagram, built a portfolio, and recently got a videography job.
- Exception: a small number of companies should be stealth, but the majority should not.

## Trends he was watching (December 2017)

- **Voice:** about 11 million Alexa devices in US homes by mid-2017 (he was unsure of the exact figure), plus Google Home. He cites a claim that half of teenagers' searches are by voice. The opportunity is to build for a future where voice replaces some tap-based computing, such as a voice social network or voice platform tools.
- **Avatars:** face filters and emoji show avatar communication. He thinks it can give a veil of privacy that reduces social-network hate without losing emotional expression. He called the thinking "raw."
- **Blockchain and crypto:** he compares it to TCP/IP, which no one knew would enable Twitter. He said it was full of hype and questionable ICOs but also real innovation.
- **Backlash against Silicon Valley:** problems like harassment, distrust of venture capital, "raise money" culture treated as success, and automation displacing jobs. He is an optimist who thinks tech should push forward responsibly.
- **Timing caveat:** trends like VR were predicted years early, so timing is the hard part.

## What's next, and what drives him

- **Monetization:** Product Hunt started collecting money about two months before the interview, with hundreds of makers and companies paying monthly. The business goal for the next year was to break even.
- **Mainstream discovery:** use the curated database and community so people can browse for gifts (smart home gadgets for mom) or the best Android photography apps, since more people have that problem than want to discover what's new in tech.
- **Investing:** a small fund raised about 6 or 7 months earlier, investing in early-stage companies. He likes it as an excuse to learn new industries, such as shipping.
- **Motivation:** the satisfaction of helping people. A 21-year-old founder asked why Ryan was helping him; the answer was that people helped Ryan at that stage. He wants a legacy of helping people build valuable products and companies.
