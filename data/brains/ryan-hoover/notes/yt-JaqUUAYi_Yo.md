## Starting Product Hunt as a side project and deciding to raise money

- Ryan started Product Hunt as an email list while between startups. It got traction, became a website modeled on Reddit and Hacker News, and was still an unincorporated side project about four months in. He woke up excited about it and thought about it constantly, but did not know what it should become.
- Nicholas from Algolia (previous YC batch) DMed him that many YC founders were launching on Product Hunt and that Gary Tan wanted to meet.
- Ryan's open question for Gary was whether he wanted VC funding at all, and whether doing YC put him on that path. He had little savings and is not an engineer, so he needed money to pay people to build the roadmap.
- Gary's advice: "when you strike lightning in a bottle that doesn't happen very often." Ryan says very few times in life do you build something showing real traction, and that advice stuck. Gary never pressured him either way.
- Ryan weighed bootstrapping (pocketing something like $20,000 a month at 100% ownership) against raising, and cites bootstrapped examples like Wirecutter and Techmeme.

## The manual growth flywheel before the YC interview

- Growth going into the YC interview was roughly 50% month over month over a few months, from a small base.
- The tactic was manual: Ryan emailed and tweeted at makers and founders. When he messaged them, they joined the conversation and shared it, which drove more traffic. Ryan says this flywheel still exists today.
- After discovering it, he did it as often and as fast as he could before the interview so he could honestly cite the growth numbers. Real metrics gave him confidence without needing to "pump his chest."

## Raising money: seed and a preemptive Series A

- Product Hunt raised about $1M before demo day, targeting about $400k. Investors included GV, Greylock and SV Angel (first check), plus strategics.
- Strategy: because the product served the tech community, having notable tech people involved was beneficial. Ryan admits they probably gave up more equity than needed.
- Mark Andreessen's firm reached out mid-batch, then again weeks later before demo day, and ended up doing a preemptive Series A. Ryan says they were very early for that kind of round.
- His reasoning: metrics were great and the opportunity massive, so it was better to raise more capital now with a great firm than risk raising in six months with a weaker firm and weaker numbers. The money funded several years of hiring and building.

## Mistakes: revenue, premature vertical expansion, and redesigns

- **Revenue:** Ryan says they should have focused on revenue earlier. Before funding he paid the Heroku bill (a couple thousand dollars a month at one point) from his own credit card and added job ads, which made about $4,000 a month (transcript says "$44,000", which is ambiguous). He notes the community could easily have supported more. The tradeoff: consumer startups that make money get judged by VCs on that revenue.
- Product Hunt made covering costs the number one goal last year, which took a lot of experimentation, and became profitable.
- **Expanding verticals too fast:** The demo day vision was to replicate the community across categories (gaming, music, books, podcasts). Ryan was "a little bit naive" thinking communities could be replicated quickly. They launched gaming, then books, then podcasts, but the community stayed tech-focused and the side communities saw little engagement and never became standalone.
- Game discovery works differently than tech discovery (more visual, video, different focus). Ryan thinks a separate domain and experience would have been needed. He thinks someone should still build it.
- He rejected a "Product Hunt fund" suggested by investors, believing it would have changed the business into something like AngelList.
- **Redesigns:** He prioritized redesign work partly because he wanted it himself. Redesigns are often a trap: users care about the content and conversation, not whether the upvote button looks shinier. Communities also resist change; some changes were reverted, others users got used to.
- Treat funding as if you don't have infinite runway.

## Product Hunt's current strategy: go deep, three KPIs, and what is next

- Ryan's strategy shifted from going wide to going deep on technology, his "bread and butter," about three years before this talk.
- Three KPIs last year: revenue (number one, to build a scalable model covering expenses; they hit profitability), user growth, and community contributions (upvotes, comments, as a health measure).
- This year the priority flips to user growth, with revenue no longer number one.
- Themes: help people find the best products for a need (e.g. best analytics tool for a given budget), building a "product graph." Product Hunt has been about new products, but more people want the best product for a specific job.
- Social and maker side: virtual co-working for side-project builders, freelancers and remote workers who want to meet others, get help across skill sets, and escape loneliness. Most people lack a large following, and friends give overly positive feedback, so there is a need for a support network.
- Build process: usually one engineer starts a project, working with a designer. This lets them run many projects at once.
- Ryan sees maker communities (Product Hunt, Indie Hackers, Dribbble, Stack Overflow, Lpath) as a growing ecosystem, since more products will be built as it gets easier.

## Launch advice and what makes launches stick

- Launch basics: make the imagery clear, make the tagline clear, bounce it off people who don't know you, and "speak like a human not like a marketer."
- Feedback loops matter: building something and getting no use or feedback is hard. Product Hunt gives makers feedback; one maker of the year learned to code, launched on Product Hunt, and went on to ship many products.
- Meerkat example: a clear tagline ("broadcast live video to your Twitter followers"), a design leveraging the Twitter graph, and it blew up. But live video had poor retention (hard to create, must be watched live).
- Founders noticed people hacking it to hang out with friends, which inspired Houseparty. Ryan's takeaway: a launch spike is not a business, and the team had to be honest with themselves and change rather than persist.
- Ryan advises starting by thinking "side project" or experiment rather than market: ask what you would want to build or use. Product Hunt was "me in many ways," which is why it worked. He named his fund Weekend Fund because much can be built on a weekend.
- Ryan values progress: he has invested in companies after following them for just two or three weeks based on what they ship and how they interact with users.
