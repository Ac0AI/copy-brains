## Ryan's lens on the biggest opportunity: vertical job marketplaces

- Ryan wants more people to find work that fits their skills and that they enjoy. He says "too many people hate their job."
- The gig economy already gave millions more flexibility. He cites Uber as having employed millions of people with more freedom than a typical 9-5.
- He sees vertical job marketplaces as the next big shift.
- General-purpose job boards will stay valuable. But a company built around one specific vertical can serve both talent and employers better than a horizontal board.
- He finds the category impactful for people and also potentially very lucrative.

## Pattern: a vertical marketplace for hourly workers

- Ryan is an investor in Pattern. He first met the founder, Wilson, when Wilson asked him for feedback on his YC application.
- Pattern is a marketplace for hourly workers. It started with hospitality.
- Example he gives: an event needs two bouncers, waitstaff and a few cooks, all arriving at the same time, with the right skills, and reliable. Assembling that crew is a hard logistics problem.
- Pattern uses software to assemble the people, qualify them, and give a signal of quality. The alternative he names is hiring off Craigslist.
- He says revenue has grown significantly over the past year or so, but he doesn't give numbers.
- The industry gets little attention from tech. Hourly work covers warehousing, hospitality and cleaning, and is less top of mind for most entrepreneurs.
- On market size, he says hourly hiring is massive: people are always hiring, and these industries have a lot of turnover. He says it could be a multi-billion dollar company, though not "tomorrow or next year."
- He has also spoken to truck-driving staffing marketplaces, which he calls massive too.

## How Ryan sizes a new market

- He looks at who else is building in the space or has tried it. Comps with real scale are some evidence that a market exists.
- Early first-to-market players often create markets or prove they exist.
- He also looks at adjacent old-school industries (for example, staffing) and does the math on the number of people employed and the value of contracts.
- For brand-new categories this is hard. His example is Clubhouse, which he sees as talk radio plus podcasting plus a bit of Twitter, a massive market, but one it is partly defining as it goes.

## Finding the key risk in a company

- Ryan asks what the big risk in a company is.
- He assumes everyone has already tried everything. So something must have changed, such as a consumer behavior shift or a technology shift, and the team needs a unique ability to solve the biggest risk.
- For Product Hunt, the big risk was the community, not the tech. He says "it's not hard to build Product Hunt from a technical perspective."
- Once you build the community, establish the brand and get the flywheel going, the product can work and grow.

## Marketplace strategy: solve the hard side first

- A marketplace has two parties: people who hire and people who want to be hired. He tries to work out which side is harder and solve for that first.
- His warning: if you focus on the easy side, you never get the hard side, and you are "almost like delaying failure."
- He gave Pattern as an example. Without detailing how, he says it found a balance of high-quality talent and demand, then scaled both.
- In freelance marketplaces like Marketer Hire and Advisable, he assumes talent is the harder side. His reasoning: with high-quality talent, companies will come, especially for distribution help, which is hard right now.
- He prefers companies that aren't reliant on paid acquisition. If you pay for talent, you run into hard CAC vs LTV problems, which get thrown off if Facebook changes its algorithm or a funded competitor enters.
- Example: AngelList has essentially free talent. People sign up and create profiles, which is unusual for job marketplaces.

## Challenges and upside of freelance marketing marketplaces

- Marketer Hire, Indispensable-style and Advisable-style marketplaces are hard businesses. He names two problems:
  - Keeping people on-platform. Clients may take a marketer off platform and hire them full time.
  - Retention. Project work means churn after 3 to 6 months.
- He is still attracted to the opportunity of overcoming them.
- Why he likes hiring marketers by contract or project at early-stage companies:
  - There are many channels (TikTok, SEO, Facebook ads, Instagram ads) and a small team can't be expert in all of them.
  - An expert can both do the work and teach the company.
  - A generalist hire is probably not as good, and may not fit once you know which channel to focus on.
- If he were building a new company and needed marketing help, he would look to these platforms first.

## What makes a vertical marketplace attractive

- Industry shifts: pick the industry going through the biggest shifts, or the one that would benefit most from technology and software. Many haven't been reshaped by software yet.
- Low trust: he likes markets where there is no good way to verify a person's abilities today. A marketplace can build trust through verification.
- That trust also helps retention. If talent builds identity and reputation on your marketplace, which helps them get more jobs or money, they stay.
- He said he hadn't thought deeply enough to name a specific vertical.

## Community: how Ryan defines it and why it matters

- He recommends community-driven strategies. Starting with a community, even before you know exactly what the product is, gives you an audience for launch and insight into what to build for those people.
- His three-part definition of community:
  - People in it interact with each other. By definition, it isn't a community unless they do.
  - A sense of belonging. His examples are church and religion, and swag such as Hustle or Trends merch that ties a person's identity to the brand.
  - Utility. This is not strictly required, but he thinks it's important: members must get real value, such as learning what's next.
- He rejects treating a Twitter following as a community.
- Why communities are powerful: they scale and let you reach people you couldn't create content for personally. An audience stops growing when you stop posting. A community doesn't.
- Product Hunt example: he barely posts to Product Hunt himself and intentionally does so rarely, and it still runs.
- He says he never read about community building, so for him it was intuitive and hard to teach. He adds you can teach anyone anything, but some people pick it up more naturally.
