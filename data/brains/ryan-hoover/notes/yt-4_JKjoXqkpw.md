## Why Ryan helps founders and how he does it

- Ryan says helping founders "gives me purpose" and feels good, especially because founders are told no constantly and fundraising is hard for most.
- He has a bias toward supporting very young or inexperienced builders who lack support. Example: a 10-year-old DMed him before a Product Hunt launch. Ryan first suspected an adult posing as a kid, then confirmed it via a video.
- He argues even a single tweet or like can be meaningful: it nudges people past the fear of "what if people don't like it?". The default outcome of posting is that nobody cares, but the upside is nearly unbounded.
- When advising portfolio founders, he shares experience, frameworks and ways of thinking rather than answers, since founders have far more data on their own company.
- He only advises where he has expertise (product, community building, marketing). For data science or enterprise sales questions, he connects founders to the fund's LPs. Weekend Fund raised from over 350 LPs, mostly operators and founders.
- He increasingly prefers building software that scales help beyond his own time, since software "can exist while you sleep."
- A pivotal nudge in his own life: a college friend (Luan) told him to run for president of the investment club, which made him realize he could lead.

## How Product Hunt started and was built

- 2013: he left a job of about three years (the company grew from 6 to ~100 people) by going part-time at half salary and 20 hours a week to tinker.
- He applied to Medium, MessageMe and Bibo, and was rejected or didn't pursue them. He then built Product Hunt as a side project because he wanted a list of cool new apps (mobile was taking off).
- Version one was an email list on a tool called Linky Inc., where friends collaboratively submitted links that were sent out as a daily email. It took 20-30 minutes to set up and was shared on Twitter.
- It had fewer than 1,000 subscribers, but "nudges of confidence" came from organic growth and random people saying they read it every morning.
- He then pinged Nathan Bashaw (a product designer/engineer hybrid he'd worked with on Startup Edition). Over a Thanksgiving week they built the website MVP.
- The site replicated the email as a daily leaderboard, adding comments and upvotes as the curation mechanism.

## Product design principle: reuse familiar patterns

- Ryan borrowed from Reddit and avoided inventing new language: "upvotes" were already understood by people in tech, so they didn't invent a new verb.
- He warns people sometimes get "too cute" with new terminology or UX, which can hurt understanding.
- Naming example: they chose "makers" rather than "founders" so anyone involved in a product (CEO or customer support) is celebrated. It is deliberately flat, with no hierarchy of founders and VPs.

## The Product Hunt growth flywheel and tactics

- Flywheel: a maker launches and shares with their audience, some of those people sign up, some are makers too, so they launch and share with their own audience. More people engage, which drives more engagement back to the site.
- He thinks about social product design as building flywheels, then asking what speeds the spin or raises conversion.
- Twitter-only sign-in at the start let them replicate the Twitter graph. When someone you follow launched, you got an email. This was one of their highest open and click-through emails, since people genuinely care what people they know are building.
- Outreach to top launchers: they contacted the top 10 by upvotes each day and asked them to write about their launch experience, promising cross-promotion on the newsletter and social.
- Many wrote posts (often 1-3 hours of work) on their own blogs or Medium. This gave Product Hunt authentic content not hosted on its own blog, plus backlinks that improved SEO. Hundreds of people wrote these posts.
- Ryan describes it as "giving them permission to promote themselves to promote us," a win-win.
- Scale: Ryan believes over 250,000 makers launch per year (rough napkin math, possibly off by 2x). Early skeptics said there weren't enough products to launch daily; he disagreed.
- Defensibility: Product Hunt's flywheel, brand and network mean clones haven't worked, and it would keep spinning even if no features were added. He argues products with a people or network component are far more defensible than purely software-driven ones.

## Building communities

- Audience vs community: "There's a big difference between an audience and a community." Twitter followers are usually an audience. A community means members connect with and help each other.
- The leader is like a good party host who sets the environment, rather than the one talking to everyone.
- Benefits: more word of mouth, higher engagement and retention, and more enterprise value, all else equal (his thought experiment: Figma invested heavily in community and events versus a comparable rival).
- Not every SaaS company needs one, and nobody wakes up wanting to join a community, so it needs utility.
- How to start: find the member's motivation. On Product Hunt, people come to get users, feedback and status. That hook pulls them into higher engagement and events.
- Hundreds of Product Hunt community events were largely community-led. Ryan attended only about four or five. Early ones had lines wrapping two blocks.
- Observed shift: communities have moved toward small private group chats. He is in a ~30-40 person health group and another group of LPs. He cites Greg Eisenberg's Islands as a company built on that thesis.

## Product building in the age of AI

- Human psychology stays constant while technology changes. He worked with Nir Eyal on Hooked and has long studied what makes products compelling. Status, human connection and status within a community will still matter.
- AI collapses idea-to-software: far fewer people are needed, and non-engineers (marketers, PMs, designers, accountants) can build their own ideas, removing translation loss. Today it suits MVPs and testing more than production, but he expects it to improve.
- Example workflow: a PM spends an afternoon building a working prototype instead of a clumsy Figma clickthrough.
- He mentions personalized, remixable software and notes Weekend Fund invested in Adaptive in that category, though he isn't sure where he stands on the thesis.
- He compares AI today to mobile in 2011-2013: a new mature-enough platform with lots of opportunity (he cites subscriptions in app stores as an unlock). "We're still in the fog of war."
- Cost advice for founders: bet on where the technology is heading. Example: Rosebud (journaling app) charged around $5 a month while power users cost more via API calls; the bet was that costs would keep falling.
- Practice: retry old AI tasks periodically. A year ago OpenAI deep research missed companies in his diligence; he expects it to become far better.
- Rule of thumb: people overpredict one year and underpredict ten.
- Being positioned for what AI won't change, such as in-person events, is a barbell strategy.

## Starting Weekend Fund and his investing approach

- After Angelist acquired Product Hunt (end of 2016), he started Weekend Fund in mid-2017 while still running Product Hunt. He aimed to raise $1M, raised $3M (3x oversubscribed) and deployed it over two years into 40-something companies.
- He took no management fees at the time (on $3M fees are a rounding error) and prefers that money go into companies.
- Product Hunt rule: if you hold a stake in a company, you cannot hunt it.
- The first fund's results: one position (Deel) is roughly a 10x fund returner on paper, and he sold about 15% of that position around 24 months earlier to return the whole fund to LPs. In hindsight he'd have made more by holding, but he valued getting LPs distributions and reaching carry.
- Fund size: he keeps it small on purpose. Larger funds mean leading deals, sharp elbows, more flying and schmoozing; he dislikes transactional conversations and rejecting founders.
- His view: a first-round founder is better served with a cap table of 20-40 investors (angels and small funds) than one lead investor taking a majority of the round, and then putting them to work. Unless the lead is exceptional in an area the founder desperately needs and trusted, he would not take a majority lead check himself.
- He stays generalist because trends change, and asks what a thematic fund does when its theme falls out of favor. He oscillates on valuation sensitivity (passing at a $30M post pre-launch company that later became multi-billion) and on whether to make more, wider bets in a fast, noisy market.
- Leverage via products: Rolodexer (searches his ~300k Twitter followers, half perhaps fake) helps portfolio founders find intros and helps diligence, e.g. finding accountants who follow him. He argues CRM should be "who knows you, not who do you know." He values tools that "barely work" but are extremely valuable.
- Sourcing: he asks founders "what do you see that other people don't?" He would raise venture only with a secret or traction. He cautions founders against raising when they lack either, since they may regret it after a year or two.

## Investing in consumer health and other categories

- About 80% of Weekend Fund's investments are B2B or B2B-to-C, since that has been a clearer path to revenue.
- He is excited about consumer health: GLP-1s opened people's eyes to non-traditional health approaches, people are acclimating to self-injection, and AI can interpret blood work (he uses Function Health). Health is huge ("if you're not healthy nothing else matters") and has a very high share of wallet.
- Examples he cites: Mindbloom (at-home ketamine; injectables preferred over lozenges by many users), and TrueMed (use HSA/FSA funds for products like Peloton and AG1).
- Personal angle: peptides (BPC-157, melanotan) after knee issues. He admits he is not a scientist and is partly investing in what he finds fun and could be a customer of.
- He is open to unglamorous categories such as CPG: valuations are more reasonable, and incumbent competitors may be poorly incentivized. He cites a friend's $1M post CPG investment that became his best.

## Status in Silicon Valley, LP investing and founder realities

- Status has shifted: raising money was once a primary signal; today revenue and shipping matter more, along with credentials. Twitter has become the launchpad (algorithmic reach, video demos, tech town-hall mindshare), replacing press such as TechCrunch.
- Product Hunt's original premise: press can't promote every company, so a community-led launchpad was needed.
- On status-seeking: avoid inauthentic routes or chasing fads. They may work short term, but "people are going to see through it" and the trend may fade.
- LP investing: he has invested in about 30 funds, starting organically (e.g. Andreas Klinger's early fund) to support friends. Checks are small (around $10K), and the motivation is partly fun, partly updates and some deal flow.
- How LPs help GPs: number one is simply honoring capital calls (one LP refused a small call; the commitment was reduced). Beyond that, introductions to other LPs are the most valuable help, since LPs are hidden and fundraising is hard.
- Writing: he published over 100 posts early on and still uses it to test whether he understands an idea. "If I can't write about it... maybe I don't understand it enough."
- Founder hardship: once you raise, you can't give money back, you must project confidence to the team, and can't be fully honest with the board or team. He cried inside while smiling during hard Product Hunt days.
- Advice: lean on close friends, and remember the worst case is usually not that bad since most founders can get a job.
- Reward: a portfolio company acquisition returned cash to LPs; emailing LPs "here's money back" is the best feeling.
