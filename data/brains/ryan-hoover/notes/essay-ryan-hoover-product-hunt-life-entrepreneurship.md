## Starting with a hole to fill

- Ryan grew up in Eugene, Oregon, with entrepreneurial parents who owned a video game store. As a kid he bought and sold on eBay and made video game handbooks to sell in that store.
- His dad's advice: "Find a hole and fill it." Ryan uses it to pick projects that solve problems he has or sees.
- Product Hunt began as one of many experiments, started because he wanted the thing to exist. He had no plan to make money and no plan to turn it into a business.

## Where Product Hunt came from

- Ryan and his friends were already swapping cool products over SMS and group chats ("What's on your home screen?", "What are you playing with?"). He argues people like to talk about products the way they talk about movies or music.
- No place on the internet was dedicated to that conversation. Twitter and Facebook are general-purpose networks and aren't built for discovering new products.
- There was also no single list of new products launching each day. Tech publications mix products with other news and have no community.
- The first version was a simple email list of the most interesting new products of the day, launched in November 2013.
- Writing fed into it. In 2013 he published 150 blog posts, nearly one essay every two days. He used writing to learn about products, such as teardowns of early Tinder that asked why it was compelling and which cognitive biases and design cues it used.
- He hated writing in school because the topics were assigned. He took to it once he wrote about things he cared about.

## Build a following before you build the product

- Ryan's top advice for some entrepreneurs is to build a following. He means an audience in a similar interest space, not Twitter vanity.
- Product Hunt started with about 300 subscribers on day one. That was possible only because of years of blogging and relationships with notable people who became core early community members.
- He says "The hardest thing is distribution for any kind of product, and even just getting beta users can be really difficult." An audience that cares about your work gives you people to launch to and support you immediately.
- He says Product Hunt wouldn't have worked without at least some following.

## How Product Hunt grew in year one

- Ryan says two mechanisms drove growth in the first year.
- **Press:** Product Hunt was a new source for finding products and the people behind them, so reporters used it. TechCrunch sometimes found a product there and linked to Product Hunt. That sent relevant readers to a small user base, and the people who clicked through found value.
- Ryan calls this a rare case where early press worked as an acquisition strategy. For most companies, he says press misses the real target audience and is more of a vanity metric.
- **Sharing and word of mouth:** People like Ryan shared finds on Twitter, Facebook and elsewhere. Makers also shared their product pages, and discussion happened on those pages.

## Team, momentum and morale

- The hardest personal challenge is letting a teammate go, especially when teammates become friends who made sacrifices to join. He says "If you're not letting someone go, and you haven't ever fired anyone, something's probably wrong."
- On the business side, he says every company must keep up momentum. Early excitement fades, every company hits some stagnation, and every company tries things that don't work.
- The harder part is keeping the team pumped and working hard when things aren't going great.

## The AngelList acquisition

- Product Hunt was acquired by AngelList in 2016. It's the only acquisition Ryan has been through.
- From the start, his aim was to keep autonomy, brand and voice. The team and community stayed relatively intact.
- He says acquirers often kill a product or merge teams and integrate products. He wanted to keep ProductHunt.com and the kitty mascot, and AngelList agreed, understanding the importance of the community and brand.

## Moving to profitability and what he was building (2018)

- Ryan says Product Hunt intentionally avoided focusing on revenue from the start. Experiments began around September and October of the prior year.
- Goal for the year: reach profitability. Last month, the company covered more than half its expenses, up from $0 six months earlier. He didn't share specific numbers.
- **Ship:** a toolkit for startups and makers to communicate with users, acquire beta testers and understand customers. It pulls together tools people already use, such as surveys, landing pages and email marketing.
- **User growth:** SEO was producing strong results. He was also trying a new place for makers to share what they're working on and get help from others, and early feedback from that small community was good.

## What motivates him and how he works

- Motivation: thank-you notes from makers, and the drive to make a bigger impact and move a number (revenue or users) from x to y. He likens building products to a giant strategy game.
- His favorite part of the role is building new products and experiences, even though not all of them work.
- Time management: he says he has no great process. He jumps between product, marketing and team work, which he enjoys but which makes long focus blocks hard.
- What helps most lately: at the start of each week, he writes down three things that must get done. If only those happen, the week is still a win. This keeps lesser tasks from eating the week.
- He is routine-driven. He spends at least a couple of hours each morning at Philz Coffee, where Product Hunt itself was started.
