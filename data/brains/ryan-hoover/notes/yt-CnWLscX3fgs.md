## Ryan's path from email-list experiment to Product Hunt startup

- Product Hunt began as a simple email list built with Linky Dink, a tool for collaborative email lists. Ryan is not an engineer, so he did not try to build a full app to test the idea.
- He invited a few dozen people (investors, founders, friends) to submit cool new products and get a daily email of them.
- He had run many similar experiments before, such as an "Uber for laundry" landing page test. None had stuck. He treated Product Hunt as just another cheap, fun experiment.
- The email list had only a couple hundred subscribers. What convinced him to continue was qualitative feedback: people went out of their way to email or tell him in person that they read it every day.
- Stages as he describes them: experiment (email list), then side project (a website Nathan Bashaw built over Thanksgiving break, worked on together for about 3-4 months), then startup. He was still at PlayHaven, so there was no pressure to commit early.
- He considered a bootstrapped path (for example a $20,000-a-month business he owned fully) but chose to raise money and go bigger. He says self-funding is the right path for many people and most businesses.
- By the time of the interview, Product Hunt had over 200,000 monthly uniques and about 70% month-over-month user growth since January.

## Build an audience before the startup to de-risk distribution

- Ryan argues the hardest part of a startup is not building the product. It is (1) figuring out what people want and (2) breaking through the noise to get attention.
- Building an audience first de-risks the second part before you have an idea, rather than starting from zero once you have one.
- His own audience came from about two years of writing on startups, growth, marketing and personal growth. He also helped Nir Eyal write *Hooked*. He calls it "not a huge audience" but enough credibility to run experiments to.
- Writing is not the only route. Podcasting and organizing events (conferences, brunches) work too. The point is to "provide value to some community of people."
- Advice for someone starting out:
  - Be curious and experimental. If writing doesn't go anywhere after a year, try something else like podcasting.
  - Pick something you will use and care about for years. He would not build a women's-fashion community because he isn't the right person for it.
  - Use your own product all the time, because the founder's behavior shapes the community culture.
- His motivation for writing was enjoyment and recognition, not a master plan. He admits the dots only connected in hindsight.

## Designing Product Hunt for habit and daily return

- Email is central. About 50,000 people subscribed to the daily email at the time, and sending it makes site traffic spike. Featured products get thousands of extra visitors.
- Ryan's point: even if people like a site, they usually need an external trigger to return. Email is that trigger, and it also delivers value as a quick digest.
- For mobile apps, he says personal notifications and the app icon serve the same trigger role.
- Social re-engagement pulls people back too: when someone is mentioned or tagged, they get an email.
- "Day parting": the leaderboard resets each day. Unlike Hacker News or Reddit, which feel like an endlessly changing board of links and overwhelm visitors, a fresh daily list reinforces a daily routine.
- Login uses Twitter, for two reasons:
  - It was quick to build (no custom authentication).
  - It gives a consistent social graph (who follows whom), real names and handles, and a way to verify identity (for example a real Ashton Kutcher). It also gives another channel to talk to users. The Product Hunt Twitter account had about 20,000 followers.

## Incentives, sharing and community rules

- Ryan avoids heavy sharing calls to action. There is only a tweet button, with no sharing walls. People already want to share cool new finds, and early adopters like looking ahead of the curve.
- Incentives are aligned: founders want upvotes and to reach the top of the leaderboard, so they share with friends and users. Some products got over 10,000 uniques in a day or two.
- Every participant must be a real person (no company Twitter accounts), so conversations feel human.
- Posting and commenting were limited to a whitelisted group at first. Reasons he gives:
  - Over 100 submissions per day never reached the home page (off-topic, duplicates, low quality).
  - Opening to everyone would bring self-promoters, and 60 products a day is already a lot.
  - Trolls and spam are hard to moderate with a tiny team.
  - Limiting early membership lets them shape the culture. He jokes that an influx of Justin Bieber fans would be "a different audience."
- Later, they began a recommendation system where members invite people they'd like to add. Early tests showed people like inviting friends, "like giving their friends a gift."

## Growth channels: word of mouth, press, user content, no paid

- Word of mouth is the main driver. Everyone registers via Twitter and shares there.
- Press works unusually well for Product Hunt: TechCrunch, Next Web and VentureBeat readers are the same people who want to discover new products. Reporters use Product Hunt to find stories, so the relationships go both ways.
- Content marketing is done by the community. They ask the top 10 products' founders each day to write about their experience, then share it. Both sides benefit, and some of those posts did better than the original Product Hunt listing.
- Investors also discover companies on the platform, and a few products got funded, which completes the cycle of aligned incentives.
- No paid acquisition. He says paid is expensive, and he doubts it brings the best users, who are friends of existing community members.
- Custom boards work well: a board of every company in 500 Startups' batch 9 on demo day was a strong acquisition source. Other "collections" included GIF apps, a YC hackathon, and a nostalgia Flashback Friday.
- AMAs with well-known founders (Dennis Crowley, Mark Cuban, Jason Calacanis) bring in influential users because people ask them questions about their products.
- Tactic: check who is signing up. In the early days he put signup emails into Rapportive, spotted investors and reporters, and personally thanked them to start relationships. He calls it "a missed opportunity" not to do this, especially with reporters.

## Twitter and relationship tactics

- Twitter is "arguably the most important product in my life" outside email. He used it to distribute writing and get feedback.
- Reply to everyone who shares your work, using their first name ("thanks Kyle"), because people appreciate that you typed it.
- He shared other people's blog posts via Buffer and always tagged the author. Over time, authors remember you, which leads to relationships (coffee, hiring, early users, investors, celebrity users).
- Advice: don't treat things as transactional, act like a human, and have fun. Individual interactions seem small but add up. There's a balance between being genuinely appreciative and "kissing ass."
- For email at volume: use Streak shortcuts and hotkeys for repeated answers, but stay human and add a relevant personal note (for example about a recent tweet). He once ranked first among about 150 startup people on an inbox-checkup app for emails sent.

## Building in public, podcasting and live events

- Ryan shared wireframes and mockups with the first couple dozen users by email and asked specific questions. Later he put a site redesign on InVision and emailed it to the community, getting 100-200 replies in a morning. They changed things before building.
- His principle: transparency makes people feel part of the product, and "it's more important than anything for people to feel like they have a say."
- He cites Buffer as the most transparent company he knows (public metrics, investor updates, replying to everyone on Twitter).
- Podcasting: he started it partly because he enjoyed it, and also because it gives access to admired people and shows the human side. Hearing someone's voice builds more connection than Twitter or email. It's another engagement channel (iTunes subscribers).
- Live events:
  - Happy hours are easy: email a group with an event page. A New York one drew about 500 RSVPs.
  - Brunches of about 25 people are more intimate, and he enjoys them more.
  - Meeting in person builds more authentic ties, and people who meet this way become big supporters. Members have asked to run their own meetups elsewhere.
- Challenge he flags: keeping a high-touch support style while volume grows.

## Roadmap and team-size views

- The iOS app (designed by David McKinney) was close to beta, with launch expected within a month. The mobile website was only semi-responsive.
- He expects the team to be the "face" of Product Hunt for the short-to-medium term, which won't scale long term.
- On company growth: PlayHaven went from 10 to about 110 people. He learned that the people who get you there early aren't always the right ones later, and process needs rise with size. He concluded he works best in smaller companies, and left to build a consumer product with a small team.
