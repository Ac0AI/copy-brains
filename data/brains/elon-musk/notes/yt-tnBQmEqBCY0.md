## Usefulness as the test for what to work on

- Elon says work does not need to "change the world" to be good. Even a small game or a photo-sharing improvement is fine if it is useful to people.
- His optimization at the time was "what can I do that would actually be useful".
- He estimates usefulness as the utility delta versus the current state of the art, times the number of people affected.
- A big improvement for a small to moderate number of people and a small improvement for a vast number of people have roughly the same "area under the curve".
- He says most people should not get a PhD. A doctorate can be earned on many things that "do not have a practical bearing on the world". Some should, but mostly not.
- In the mid-1990s he put off grad study at Stanford on ultracapacitors and energy storage for electric cars. He started an internet company in 1995 because a technology at a steep point of its inflection curve is a good time to act. He was also not certain the technology he would study would succeed.

## The problems he thinks matter most for humanity

- At 22 today, he would put AI first. He calls it the single biggest near-term item affecting humanity and says it must arrive "in a good way".
- Genetics comes second. Examples are solving genetic diseases and preventing dementia or Alzheimer's through genetic reprogramming.
- Third is a high-bandwidth interface to the brain. People are already effectively superhuman through phones, computers and email, a "digital tertiary self". The constraint is bandwidth between the cortex and that digital self.
- His college-era list was five items, from about 25 years before this talk:
  - making life multiplanetary
  - accelerating the transition to sustainable energy
  - the internet
  - genetics
  - AI
- He says he did not expect to be involved in all of them.

## Doing "crazy" things: fear, fatalism and odds

- Elon says he feels fear strongly. He does not lack it. He acts anyway when something is important enough and he believes in it.
- He says feeling fear is normal and "you'd have to have something mentally wrong" not to feel it.
- Fatalism helps: accept the probabilities and the fear diminishes.
- He put SpaceX's odds of success at under 10% and expected to lose everything. His fallback was that the company might move the ball forward, and another company could pick up the baton.
- He thought a car company's odds of success (Tesla) were also extremely low.
- On SpaceX, he agrees it was crazy if the goal was the best risk-adjusted return. That was not his goal.
- He had concluded that without improved rocket technology humanity would be stuck on Earth forever. The big aerospace companies had no interest in radical innovation and just tried to make old technology slightly better each year.

## Technology does not improve by itself

- Elon argues technology gets better only if smart people "work like crazy" to improve it. Left alone, it can decline.
- His rocket example: the Saturn V took people to the moon in 1969. The Space Shuttle could only reach low-Earth orbit, then retired, and the trend tends toward zero.
- His civilization examples: ancient Egypt built pyramids and later forgot how, and even forgot how to read hieroglyphics. Rome forgot how to build its roadways, aqueducts and indoor plumbing.
- His summary: "entropy is not on your side".

## Mars timeline and AI safety through democratization

- Elon is now certain a self-sustaining Mars colony is possible. Until a few years earlier he was not sure success was even one of the possible outcomes.
- He thinks a meaningful number of people could go to Mars in about 10 years, maybe 9. It depends on SpaceX surviving and on someone continuing if he dies. The first launch would be robotic.
- On AI, he says his view is predictive, not prescriptive. His best available alternative is democratization of AI technology, so no single company or small group controls advanced AI.
- His reasoning is that concentrated AI could be misused by its owners or stolen by a bad actor, such as a dictator's intelligence agency. He says the main risk is people misusing it, not AI developing a will of its own right away.
- That is the reason OpenAI was created. It is structured as a 501(c)(3) nonprofit, and he says its people believe in the mission of minimizing existential harm. It was six months old at the time, and he calls the team really talented.
- He pairs democratization with a neural link between the cortex and the digital extension of the self, which he says humans are too slow to use today. He compares it to the cortex and limbic system working together.
- If widespread, he says humans effectively become an "AI human symbiote". This solves the control problem because "we are the AI collectively".

## How he spends his time as CEO: engineering first

- About 80% of Elon's time goes to engineering and design, mainly developing next-generation products. He says many people wrongly assume he spends it on media or business matters.
- At SpaceX, Gwynne Shotwell, as chief operating officer, manages legal, finance, sales and general business. Elon works with the engineering team on:
  - Falcon 9
  - the Dragon spacecraft
  - the Mars colonial architecture
- At Tesla, he works on the Model 3. He spends part of each week in the design studio on aesthetics and look and feel. Most of the week goes to engineering the car and the factory.
- He spends about half a day most weeks at OpenAI, with some more interleaved during the week.

## The factory is the product: "the machine that builds the machine"

- Elon calls his biggest epiphany of that year that what really matters is "the machine that builds the machine", meaning the factory. He says it is at least as hard as the vehicle itself, and his phrasing puts it as harder.
- The Model S/X line then ran at about 5 centimeters per second, which he calls incredibly slow. It had a relatively low level of automation compared with what the Gigafactory and Model 3 would have.
- His target is at least 1 meter per second, a 20-fold increase.
- For scale, 1 m/s is a slow to medium walk. A fast walk is about 1.5 m/s and the fastest humans run over 10 m/s. At 1 m/s you can still walk faster than the production line.
