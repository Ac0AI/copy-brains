## How Elon defines the goal for builders: make more than you take

- Elon's advice to young entrepreneurs in India: "Aim to make more than you take." He wants founders to be net contributors to society.
- He says you shouldn't pursue money directly. Provide useful products and services, and money follows as a consequence. He compares this to happiness, which you also can't pursue directly.
- His simplest rule for what to build: "the most important thing to do is just make useful products and services."
- The test he gives is whether the output is worth more than the input: "Are you a value creator?"
- Founders trying to make a company work "should expect to grind super hard," and there is "some meaningful chance of failure."
- On startups and hard problems, he says: "you definitely need to put in serious hours."

## How Elon evaluates a company to invest in or build

- He doesn't buy stocks, keep a portfolio or hunt for investments. He says: "I just try to build things." The stock is a by-product of the companies he built.
- His long-term test for any company has three parts:
  - Do you like its products and services today?
  - Does its product roadmap suggest it will keep making great products?
  - Is the team talented, hardworking and still motivated to make things?
- If the answers are yes, he calls it a good company to invest in. His reasoning: "a company is just a group of people assembled to create products and services."
- He says not to worry about daily price fluctuations, which "sometimes are pretty extreme." Over time, product quality and team quality are what matter.
- If he had to pick outside his own companies, he'd pick AI and robotics. He names Google, which "laid the groundwork for an immense amount of value creation from an AI standpoint," and Nvidia ("obvious at this point").
- He argues AI, robotics and possibly spaceflight companies will capture almost all future value, because their output of goods and services will "dwarf everything else."

## Talent: why Elon hires globally and pays above average

- Elon says there is always a scarcity of talented people. At his companies, "we have a lot of difficulty finding enough talented people to get these difficult tasks done."
- His hiring goal is "the most talented people in the world." He says "we pay way above average," and he doesn't hire to cut costs.
- He credits talented Indian immigrants with benefiting America "immensely."
- He criticizes outsourcing firms that have gamed the H1B program, and says that gaming should stop. He opposes shutting the H1B program down and says doing so "would actually be very bad."
- He says a single person can't build a spaceship. No one has time in one lifetime to learn every area of expertise, so a rocket needs "a collection of humans."
- He argues the quality of information flow within a group determines how much it achieves: "the better it is the more the human collective will achieve."

## X as a product: positioning, scale and content philosophy

- Scale figures he gave for X:
  - About 600 million monthly users.
  - Spikes to 800 million or a billion during major world events.
  - Roughly 250 to 300 million users per week.
- He sees X's strength among "readers, writers and thinkers" and calls it number one there. He says text is a small share of internet traffic but is "higher value" and "more densely compressed information."
- He expects most future interaction to be real-time video with AI, covering both comprehension and generation. That is where most bits and compute will go.
- He positions X as a "global town square." Its features include:
  - Words, pictures and video.
  - Secure messaging.
  - Newly added audio and video calls.
  - Automatic translation, so people across language groups can read each other.
- He deliberately declines to optimize for dopamine. Videos that deliver "dopamine hits one after another" are, in his words, "brain rot." He says "I could do that if I wanted to," but that isn't his goal. He admits many people will want that content anyway.
- His stated operating principle for moderation is to adhere to each country's laws and not "put our thumb on the scale beyond the laws of a country."
- He says he bought Twitter because he believed it amplified a far-left ideology, and he aimed to restore balance.
- His concept of X is "WeChat++": one app where you can share information, publish and move money.

## The X.com money idea and how he thinks about money

- The original X.com in 1999 was an attempt to "solve money from an information theory standpoint." He saw the banking system as many heterogeneous, insecure databases doing batch processing. A single real-time, secure database would be more efficient.
- X.com became PayPal, which was acquired by eBay. eBay later offered to sell the domain name back to him, and he bought it.
- He says he is "slowly revisiting this idea that I had 25 years ago to create a more efficient money database." His attitude toward adoption: "if that's successful, people will use it. If it's not successful, they won't."
- His definition of money is "an information system for labor allocation." Money has no power on its own: a trillion dollars on a desert island is useless because there is no labor to allocate.
- He expects money to disappear as a concept in a future where AI and robotics can satisfy all human needs. He sees energy as the true currency because "you can't legislate energy."
- He recommends Iain Banks's Culture books as the best picture of that future.
- He frames civilizational progress on the Kardashev scale, as the share of a planet's, then the sun's, energy converted into useful work.

## Where Elon sees his companies converging

- He sees a growing convergence between SpaceX, Tesla and xAI. If the future is solar-powered AI satellites, that mixes Tesla expertise, SpaceX expertise and xAI.
- Tesla: he calls it the world leader in real-world AI. He pushed the self-driving product, which was available in Austin through the Tesla app.
- Optimus robot: production is expected to start hopefully next year (the talk was recorded in late 2025), "at scale." He says "everyone's going to want their own personal C3PO, R2-D2."
- Starlink:
  - Several thousand satellites in low Earth orbit at about 550 km, with a goal of getting down to 350 km.
  - Latency is low compared with geostationary satellites at 36,000 km.
  - Satellites are linked by lasers into a mesh, so the network kept working when Red Sea cables were cut.
  - It operates in 150 countries and he wants it in India.
  - During natural disasters Starlink is free: "we don't want to put a pay wall up while somebody's trying to get help."
- He says Starlink is complementary to telecom companies and serves "the least served." Beams are wide and each beam has a fixed number of users, so it works best in sparse rural areas.
- Physics limits it in cities. Cell towers about 1 km away beat a satellite at 550 km, so Starlink can serve only about 1% to 2% of a dense city.
- Naming: "Space Exploration Technologies" became SpaceX ("like FedEx for space"), and he liked capitalizing the X.

## Using simulation to find what matters: SpaceX and Tesla methods

- Elon's simulation analogy describes how his companies test:
  - SpaceX simulates rocket flights and discards the boring ones, because "we don't learn anything from those."
  - Tesla's self-driving simulations hunt for corner cases. It already has plenty of data on a straight road on a sunny day. It needs heavy weather, a small winding road and a near head-on collision. Those cases are "weird stuff basically, interesting stuff."
- The principle: spend effort on the rare, informative cases, not on the ones you already have data on.
- He says AI has to be built around truth, beauty and curiosity:
  - Don't force an AI to believe falsehoods. He cites HAL in 2001: A Space Odyssey, where a conflicting instruction led to harm.
  - Training on an internet full of propaganda means absorbing lies that conflict with reality, which hurts reasoning.
  - Curiosity makes AI favor humanity continuing, because we are more interesting than not.

## Macro views: AI, productivity, deflation and the future of work

- Elon predicts that in less than 20 years, maybe 10 to 15, working will be optional "like a hobby," like growing your own vegetables instead of buying them. He calls AI and robotics a "supersonic tsunami" and expects "universal high income."
- His deflation logic: inflation or deflation depends on goods and services output relative to money supply growth. If real output grows faster than money supply, you get deflation, and if slower, you get inflation. He says it's "that simple."
- He says AI hasn't yet increased output faster than money supply growth. US deficits are on the order of $2 trillion. He guesses output growth will exceed money supply growth in about 3 years, followed by deflation and interest rates going to zero.
- On the US debt, he says interest payments exceed the entire US military budget. He thinks only AI and robotics can solve it.
- He thinks digital content will be overwhelmingly AI-generated, including real-time movies and games. Live events will become the scarce commodity, with a premium over anything digital, and he calls them a good industry to invest in.
- On college: go for social reasons or interest, take a wide range of subjects, but skills will probably be unnecessary in a post-work society. He said recently that an MBA may not make sense anymore.
- On tariffs: free trade is more efficient. He asks whether anyone would want tariffs between cities or US states, and so why between countries. He says he tried and failed to dissuade the president.
- On politics: "When I get involved in politics, it ends up badly," and "politics is a blood sport." He says to avoid it where possible, and politics finds you once a company reaches scale.

## DOGE, fraud detection and giving money away

- DOGE lesson: some of the biggest efficiencies were basic controls. He cites requiring every federal payment to carry a congressional payment code and a non-empty comment field. His guess is this saves $100 billion to $200 billion a year, because many payments had neither, which made auditing impossible.
- He says the Defense Department fails audits because the information needed to pass one doesn't exist.
- His fraud-detection tactic: ask to talk to the recipients of the money. He gave an example where a payment was said to go to children in Africa but the wiring instructions pointed to Washington DC. When recipients couldn't be reached, the payment wasn't sent.
- He expects fraudsters to make sympathetic arguments, such as a "save the baby pandas" NGO that can't show a panda.
- On philanthropy: giving money away so it truly helps is very hard, while it's easy to get "the appearance of goodness." He runs a large foundation without his name on it and says the biggest challenge is giving money away for real benefit.
