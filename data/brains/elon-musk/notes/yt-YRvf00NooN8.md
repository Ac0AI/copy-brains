## Elon's view on how to set internal timelines and why his forecasts run late

- Elon says internally he wants "the most aggressive timelines" possible. He compares a schedule to gas expansion: whatever timeline is set, the work expands to fill it.
- He says the media reports his wrong predictions and ignores the right ones. On long careers across many industries, he says "the longer you do something, the more mistakes you make."
- Example of a hit: he predicted Tesla would reach 50% of a production growth target and it reached 80%.
- Example of a miss: he told TED five years earlier that a car would drive from LA to New York without intervention that year. He admits "I'm not always right."
- He says his predictions lean optimistic but not all are optimistic. Some arrive later than stated, but they arrive. On radical technology bets, "it doesn't matter if they're late, but the fact that it happens."
- Chris Anderson recalled that in 2014, when Tesla sold 60,000 cars, Elon said Tesla would make about half a million a year by 2020. Elon confirms Tesla built about that many.
- He declines to guess how much of the world's battery need Tesla will cover. He says guesses get turned into "Elon said this would happen and it didn't. He's a liar and a fool" articles.

## Why full self-driving slipped: AI in the real world follows a logarithmic curve

- Elon says self-driving timelines slipped because progress looks like a series of logarithmic curves. Progress rises, flattens, then rises again. He calls the flat points "local maxima" where "you don't realize how dumb you've been."
- His reframing: the real problem is real-world AI, not self-driving alone. Roads were built for biological neural nets (brains) and eyes. A car therefore needs cameras and silicon neural nets, so "the only way to solve self-driving is advanced AI and vision."
- Training data approach he describes:
  - Single-image recognition was ambiguous. A few seconds of video resolves the ambiguity.
  - Tesla stitched eight cameras together into one synchronized view, labeled by a single person. This avoids different people labeling the same scene differently.
  - Time was added as a dimension, so labeling happens on video clips.
  - Labeling a 10-second clip took many hours at first, which doesn't scale. Tesla wrote auto-labeling software.
  - Auto-labels are corrected by human editors, and the corrections feed back into the labeler. This creates a flywheel where the labeler handles far more video at high accuracy and labels cars, lane lines and drivable space.
- The car also needs spatial and temporal memory. Memory can't be infinite because it uses compute. Example: a pedestrian walks behind a truck, and the car should infer they will emerge on the other side and slow down.
- Status he gave: his own car drives him around Austin without intervention, and there were more than 100,000 people in the Full Self-Driving beta program. He says it is still beta. He was confident of a solution "this year" but admits the topic could come up again next year.
- He says some places are harder than others for autonomy.

## Optimus: why Tesla can build a humanoid robot

- Elon says it took him time to realize "instead of developing self-driving, we should develop AI in the real world." A car is a four-wheeled robot. Once that works, "you can do it for a robot with legs."
- He argues others have already shown sensors and actuators for humanoid robots (he cites Boston Dynamics for hardware). What's missing is "enough intelligence for the robot to navigate the real world and do useful things without being explicitly instructed," plus mass production.
- He says Tesla is good at both missing pieces. Tesla still has to design specialized actuators and sensors.
- He says "people have no idea, this will be bigger than the car."
- Launch plan:
  - First units target dangerous, boring, repetitive work people don't want to do.
  - He expected an "interesting prototype" this year, something useful the following year, and "within 2 years" to be safer.
  - He expected rapid year-over-year gains in usefulness, falling cost and rising volume.
  - Consumer availability is "less than 10 years" away.
- Pricing logic: he expects it to cost less than a car, since a car is more complex. It starts expensive as a low-volume new technology and eventually costs about as much as a cheap car.
- Safety design: a ROM chip in the robot so it cannot receive commands over the air, and a stop command that works. He also supports an AI regulatory agency. He says he has said this for years, "I don't like being regulated" but considers it important for public safety.
- On jobs, he does not expect unemployment. He expects a labor shortage and "an age of abundance" where goods and services become very cheap. "The only scarcity will be the things we create as humans."

## Starship economics: full, rapid reuse and scale as a cost lever

- Elon says Starship matters because "the holy grail" for rockets is full and rapid reuse, which has never been achieved.
- Falcon 9 reuses the first stage, which he says is roughly 60% to 70% of the cost of a launch. SpaceX has done it about 100 times.
- Falcon 9 still needs refurbishment of the first stage and nose cone. Starship aims for immediate reflight: "you put in propellant and it goes again."
- Cost claim: Starship putting 100 tons into orbit costs much less than the small Falcon 1 putting its payload into orbit. He compares it to flying a 747 around the world costing less than a small airplane that's thrown away. "Big things cost less than small things."
- Propellant choice: methane and oxygen (about 77-78% oxygen by weight). Mars has a CO2 atmosphere and water ice, so SpaceX can make CH4 and O2 there. Propulsive landing with no parachutes means nothing is thrown away.
- Production plan he stated: about one booster and one ship every two months at first, hoped to be every month by year-end. He wants "a giant rocket, and many of them."
- Orbital test: he expected a first orbital attempt within a few months pending regulatory approval. He jokes "excitement is guaranteed, success is not."
- Rockets have exploded many times, but not with people aboard, which he says is the key difference.
- NASA picked Starship to return humans to the Moon. He sees it as a general solution for the solar system but not for other star systems.

## Pricing and scaling a Mars city

- Elon says a self-sustaining city on Mars needs "thousands of ships" and a "Helen of Sparta" on Mars, a "planet that launched a thousand ships."
- Chris Anderson described the vision as about 1,000 Starships launching every two years, with 100 or more people each.
- Elon says there is a group of people who want to go. He thinks a small percentage will, perhaps about a million people.
- Pricing target: he says a ticket around $100,000 would let "anyone" work and save to go. "We want to make it available to anyone."
- Pitch he would use: "It's dangerous, it's uncomfortable. You might not come back. It's a hard job." He compares it to Shackleton's Antarctic voyage and says it will be glorious.
- The critical threshold is whether the city survives if ships from Earth stop coming.
- Reason he gives: to extend the life of consciousness. Multi-planetary status is one filter to pass. He compares consciousness to "a tiny candle in a vast darkness."
- For governance he would favor direct democracy over representative democracy, and short, comprehensible laws. He says laws are "much harder to create than to get rid of."

## How Elon runs several companies and why SpaceX stays private

- Headcount he cited: Tesla about 110,000 people, SpaceX about 12,000, and the Boring Company and Neuralink about 200 each.
- He says the companies are not meant to be merged. Their investor bases differ and Tesla is public. He expects some synergies.
- On taking SpaceX public: "Multi-planetary" is "off the radar" for Wall Street analysts. Public company costs are high, including constant lawsuits and the time and effort they consume. He would like to take it public later but sees the burden.
- Board time: he says he is not on the Neuralink or Boring Company boards and does not attend SpaceX meetings. SpaceX holds two a year and he speaks for about an hour.
- Where his time goes: he says any hour spent thinking wisely about Tesla can be worth millions of dollars. He cited Tesla making about $2 billion a week in revenue, roughly $300 million a day. He says in one half-hour meeting he improved the company's economics by $100 million.
- Personal spending: he says he owns no house and stays with friends. He has no yacht and takes no vacations. The one exception is his plane, because it saves time for work.
- His framing of the companies:
  - Tesla supports sustainable energy.
  - SpaceX seeks long-term survival through multi-planetary life.
  - Neuralink treats brain and spine injuries and addresses AI risk.
  - Boring Company works to fix traffic.
- He considers all of them "philanthropy" in the sense of love for humanity.

## Sustainable energy build-out and the battery bottleneck

- Elon says a sustainable energy economy needs three pieces:
  - Solar and wind as the primary generation sources, with hydro, geothermal and nuclear acceptable.
  - Stationary battery storage, since the sun and wind aren't constant.
  - Electric transport: cars, planes and ships. Rockets can use propellant made from the same energy.
- Moving electricity, heating and transport onto sustainable energy triples the electricity needed. He puts the required installed battery capacity at about 300 terawatt-hours. He calls the numbers approximate and invites others to check.
- He says the limiting factor is battery cell production, the whole lithium-ion cell supply chain from mining through processing to cell and pack making.
- Texas Gigafactory target: 100 gigawatt-hours per year, which he says is 0.1 terawatt-hours, "within a few years." He said Tesla might do more.
- He thinks Tesla could supply about 10% of the world's need, roughly.
- Warning against complacency: he says "as long as we're not complacent," a sustainable economy is achievable.
- He adds that abundant sustainable energy makes carbon capture and desalination possible at low cost.
- Elon says the way to a bright future is to "build it. Take action to make it happen."
