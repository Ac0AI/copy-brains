## Energy is the binding constraint for AI, and scaling it is how the US wins

- Elon says the US needs "a lot of power, a lot of electricity generation." He argues both power and chip production must scale.
- He thinks the US leads in software and anything intellectual or digital.
- The long-term rival is China, which he says generates roughly three times the electricity the US does.
- He expects chips will get made. The core long-term challenge is generating enough power and securing enough memory and processor manufacturing capacity in the US or a safe region.
- Jensen Huang makes a related point on the same panel: the AI stack starts with energy at the base, then land and infrastructure on top of it.

## Elon's rule of thumb: 1% more power use means about 1% more GDP

- US average power consumption is about 500 GW. Each additional 5 GW of continuous generation is a 1% increase in power use.
- Elon expects that 1% to translate into roughly a 1% increase in GDP. He says "intelligence per watt" keeps rising, both from GPUs delivering more compute per watt and from algorithms getting more intelligence per watt.
- He says he'd bet anyone that a 1% rise in energy use matches about a 1% rise in GDP. By that logic, 10 GW would add about 2% to GDP.
- Panel math on the same idea: 1% of US GDP is about $300B. That works out to about $60B of output per GW, per the panel's figures.
- SpaceX has publicly announced 10 GW, which would be about 25% of all power added in America.

## Orbital compute: why SpaceX and Tesla aim to move solar power into space

- Elon says SpaceX is looking long term at far more power than Earth can supply, and that space makes this possible. "Orbital compute" will be critical.
- Evidence he cites that it's already happening:
  - He thinks an H100 was placed in orbit last year.
  - Google is sending, or is about to send, TPUs to space.
- SpaceX, together with Tesla, aims to produce 200 GW of solar power per year.
- Solar in space gets nameplate capacity or better. On the ground it gets between one-fifth and one-eighth of nameplate, and it needs huge batteries. In space the sun is essentially always on.
- Timing: AI compute returns are so good that launch cost, though still high, is reasonable against the total economic benefit.
- Jensen Huang agrees that putting Nvidia compute in space is the way to get there.

## Starship's role and numbers

- Elon says the first orbital Starship launch deployed third-generation Starlink satellites. Their wingspan matches a 737's. He calls it the largest payload delivered to orbit since Skylab.
- He expects Starship to reach a weekly or twice-weekly launch cadence by next year.
- Starship's purposes:
  - Carry enough payload to the Moon and Mars to build a self-growing city. He estimates roughly 1 million tons of supplies on the lunar surface, or a similar amount for Mars.
  - Launch at least 300 GW per year of AI compute capacity.
- He says 200 GW per year would be a 40% annual increase in total US power consumption. He thinks the total could reach terawatts someday.

## Running data centers as good neighbors (Colossus, Memphis area)

- Elon describes the local economic effect of Colossus and Macrohard as hugely positive. The area went from heavy unemployment to more jobs than available people.
- Tax benefits were large. He thinks the community's tax budget roughly doubled.
- Concrete community gestures:
  - Reduced-price road access, at half price.
  - A $250M water recycling plant.
- His principle: "you have to do things for the community, and if you do, they'll reciprocate."
- He also notes that, for the first time in a long while, market forces fund sustainable energy. He says nearly every form (fusion, fission, small and large reactors, battery storage ideas) gets funded today, so the energy future needs no government subsidy.

## Safety is compatible with building, and the judgment problem

- Elon says safety and technology development are "two sides of the same coin." He compares it to Autopilot, which is both more advanced and safer.
- He calls the idea that capability work compromises safety, or that safety work means falling behind, a false choice.
- Superintelligence as a tool: he worries about AI that has "values," meaning it passes judgment on how it's used, which then limits its usefulness.
  - His analogy is a meat-cutting knife that cuts well-done meat but refuses raw meat that looks too much like living flesh.
  - He questions how much judgment these tools should have.
  - User intent matters, for example in cybersecurity, where a defender's actions can look like an attacker's. The AI should still act for the defender.
- On the White House meeting, he says the group signed a joint declaration on superintelligence safety. It covers joint monitoring or special committees and evaluating each other's work, which he says is much better than each party grading its own.
