## Elon's first-principles algorithm for design and manufacturing

Elon says he uses a "first principles algorithm" for the design and manufacturing of anything. The steps, in order:

1. **Make the requirements less dumb.** Whatever constraints you were given, question them. If you skip this, "you get the right answer to the wrong question." Each requirement must come from a named person who can explain it, not from a department, because then you don't know who to talk to.
2. **Delete the part or the process step.** This sounds obvious, yet at Tesla parts were often "put in there just in case or by mistake," or a step was thought necessary and wasn't.
3. Only after that, simplify, accelerate and automate.

He says he first did these in the wrong order: "first I try to automate it, then I try to accelerate it, then I try to simplify it and only then did I delete it."

## Deleting parts: Model 3 examples

- **Fiberglass mats on the battery pack.** Three mats were glued on top of the pack, and gluing them was the number one choke point in battery pack production. The noise and vibration team thought they were there for the battery fire safety team. The fire safety team thought they were for noise and vibration. Tesla ran two cars, one with the mats and one without, with a microphone in each, and nobody could tell the difference. The mats were deleted.
- **Laser-welded cross beam in the passenger footwell.** The Model 3 body line was stuck because the laser welding cell for this small beam wouldn't work. The production team said it was for crash safety. When Elon called the crash safety team, they said it did nothing and should be deleted. They had forgotten to tell production.
- He compares this to "living in a Dilbert cartoon." His "Dilbert ratio" at Tesla "is not zero," and he tries to keep it low.
- He found these by walking the production line to fix the choke points himself, "like a maniac."

## Leading by being visible in the factory

- For about three years Elon lived in the Fremont and Nevada factories as his primary residence. He slept on a couch, then in a tent on the roof, then on the floor under his desk in the open factory.
- The reason was shift change. He wanted the whole team to see him sleeping on the floor, so they knew the leader was there and not "off somewhere having a good time" on a tropical island.
- He says this "made a huge difference" and "then they gave it their all."
- He says most of his day-to-day work at SpaceX and Tesla is "almost entirely engineering and design," and he counts production as part of engineering. He says his memory for technical matters is very good, and that is how he knows details like launch pad plumbing.

## Cheaper cars and why autonomy matters most

- He says Tesla is probably the best at manufacturing in the auto industry, and perhaps in history. He declined to detail unannounced vehicle programs, but said Tesla expects to make cars more affordable than the Model 3 or Model Y.
- He says a car is made of roughly 10,000 unique parts and process steps. Deleting parts and integrating the design (the casting approach was raised by Baron) cuts cost and weight and raises performance.
- He calls autonomy "by far the biggest factor" in the value of a car. Cars are driven about 10 to 12 hours a week out of 168. Autonomous cars could drive 50 to 60 hours, roughly a fivefold increase in utility.
- Autonomy would also mean far fewer cars and far fewer parking lots.
- On valuing Tesla: if a car with 25 to 30 percent gross margins becomes about five times more useful at the same build cost, "it boggles the mind." He claims no one is close to solving generalized self-driving.
- He says he rarely tries to convince anyone to invest in Tesla, has said the stock was too high, and people keep buying anyway.
- He frames Optimus, the humanoid robot, as leveraging Tesla's manufacturing and self-driving intelligence. His argument: "the economy is fundamentally GDP per capita times capital," so with a useful humanoid robot there may be no limit to the size of the economy.

## Vertical integration, supply chain and patents

- Tesla became vertically integrated out of necessity. Good suppliers wouldn't talk to a car startup, and no US car startup had succeeded in about a century (he cites Chrysler in the 1920s).
- That became an asset. Tesla understood the whole supply chain, so it could design a more integrated vehicle with far fewer parts.
- He says scaling constraints change over time. For batteries, Tesla is looking at getting into mining and refining itself. He says Tesla has never contemplated investing in Glencore.
- On logistics: Tesla outgrew car carriers and ships, so many cars were in transit at quarter end. He says this is good long term, because it smooths deliveries and avoids rushing and paying expedite fees at quarter end. He floats autonomous trucks and cars driving themselves to customers as solutions.
- On patents: they make sense where a company spent heavily on trials and the product is cheap to make, such as drugs. Tesla gave its patents away because its goal is advancing sustainable energy and "we need the whole industry to go that way."
- Long-term plan for Superchargers: solar plus battery storage at each station, so they are self-sustaining and keep working even in a "zombie apocalypse."

## SpaceX: reusability and Starship

- Falcon 9 is about 70 to 80 percent reusable (booster and fairing recovered, upper stage not). Starship targets 100 percent.
- A fully and rapidly reusable orbital rocket is the "Holy Grail" for becoming multi-planetary, and he says it could cut the cost of access to space by a factor of a thousand.
- Starship has more than twice the thrust of a Saturn V and is designed to land propulsively, so it can land on any solid surface in the solar system.
- Why others haven't done it: it is "only barely possible" with known physics, like a video game on extreme difficulty. Reusability requires everything to be perfect: very efficient engines and structure, advanced avionics and software, a very light heat shield, and orbital refilling with tankers for Mars trips.
- Baron contrasted launch pads and SLS (cost-plus contract, billions over budget). Baron said SpaceX built a pad for a few hundred million dollars in six months, versus about 10 years for incumbents. Elon declined to comment on competitors, saying he'd like fewer companies that want him dead.
- He frames the goal as lowering the cost of access to orbit to the point where humanity can afford a permanent moon base and Mars.

## Mission and motivation

- Tesla's purpose is accelerating sustainable energy. He says Tesla pushed nearly every major carmaker to build EVs, and "there is still a long way to go."
- For SpaceX, he gives two reasons for becoming multi-planetary. The defensive reason is that consciousness is "a small candle in a vast darkness" that could go out, for example through nuclear weapons or bio-terrorism. The second reason is that it is inspiring: "life can't just be about solving one problem or another."
- On entrepreneurship: "starting a company is like eating glass and staring into the abyss." His advice is "if you need encouragement, don't start a company." He also notes that prosperity makes some talented people stop working.

## Twitter (X) strategy as of late 2022

- Elon calls Twitter's price high but sees potential to become "one of the most valuable companies in the world." He says he will execute his 22-year-old x.com plan from July 2000 (the most valuable financial institution in the world), with improvements.
- **Bots and spam.** Paid verification at $8 a month (he later says $7.99) exists to make "crime not pay." Creating a bot costs less than a penny. With a fee, 100,000 fake accounts would cost $800,000 a month instead of $800.
- **Ranking.** Verified users get priority in search, replies and mentions, and unverified accounts get pushed down. His analogy is page eight of Google search, where scams live but nobody scrolls.
- **Payment authentication.** The scheme piggybacks on payment and Apple's authentication systems as extra layers of security.
- **Creators.** Subscription revenue would fund long-form video, long audio, and revenue sharing with creators. He says Twitter then sends users to YouTube and TikTok because it gives creators no way to monetize video.
- **Advertising.** He says Twitter relied on large brand advertising, which is discretionary, unlike direct response. He says activist pressure caused a major advertiser drop despite no moderation changes.
- **Cost.** He says Twitter had serious revenue and cost problems before the acquisition.
- **Workload.** His week went from 70 to 80 hours to about 120, seven days a week, until Twitter is on the right path.
- **Goal and customization.** He wants 80 percent of the public in a "digital town square," and says extremists on either side can't be satisfied. Users should choose their experience, from "easy listening smooth jazz" to "heavy metal thrash."
