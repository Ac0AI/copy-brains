## Tunnel economics: how Elon plans to cut cost per mile to a tenth

- Elon's starting point is that tunneling is too expensive today. He cites the LA tunnel extension: about 2.5 miles for $2 billion, or roughly $1 billion per mile.
- His target is at least a 10x cut in cost per mile, and he thinks it can go further.
- He says two changes get most of the way there:
  - **Shrink the diameter.** Regulations require a single-lane road tunnel to be about 26-28 feet across, to allow for accidents, emergency vehicles and ventilation for combustion engines. An electric-vehicle platform needs only about 12 feet. Halving the diameter cuts the cross-section area to a quarter. Cost tracks cross-section area, so this alone brings cost down to about a fifth.
  - **Dig and reinforce at the same time.** Current boring machines run about half the time and spend the rest adding reinforcement to the tunnel wall. A machine built to do both continuously halves the cost again, which gets to about an eighth.
- He adds a third lever. Current machines run far below their power and thermal limits, so machine power can be raised a lot. He thinks that cuts cost by at least half, and maybe to a quarter or a fifth, on top of the rest.
- His conclusion: "a number of fairly straightforward steps" get to under a tenth of the cost per mile.
- He uses a pet snail, Gary, as a benchmark. Gary is about 14 times faster than a tunnel boring machine, and the goal is to beat Gary.
- The Boring Company is a side project. It takes about 2-3% of his time. Interns and part-time people mostly run it, and the team bought used machines to start.

## Why tunnels in 3D beat flying cars for congestion

- Elon's aim is a 3D network of tunnels under cities to relieve traffic, which he calls one of the most frustrating things in life.
- Surface roads cannot be fixed by adding one tunnel layer. One layer fills up with demand and traffic returns. Tunnels can have any number of levels, and mines go much deeper than skyscrapers are tall, so congestion can be reduced to any level.
- Design for the first system:
  - A car-sized platform on an elevator carries the car, so entrances and exits take only two parking spaces on the surface.
  - There is no top speed limit. He designs for about 200 km/h (about 130 mph). He says a trip from Westwood to LAX could take 5-6 minutes.
  - At first it might work like a road with toll points. He thinks it would also ease surface-road congestion.
- He prefers tunnels to flying cars. He says flying cars would be noisy and create high wind loads. He also thinks people would not like cars flying overhead and wondering whether the wheel covers were maintained.
- He argues that autonomous, shared, electric cars make traffic worse. Once a ride costs less than a bus ticket, people will drive more, so demand for road space rises.
- Tunnels under homes are not a nuisance. If a tunnel is deeper than 3-4 tunnel diameters below a house, you cannot detect the boring from above. He says that if you could detect it, the Israeli military and US Customs and Border Protection would pay a lot for the device, because they try to find smuggling tunnels.

## Tunnels and the Hyperloop

- SpaceX built a test Hyperloop track next to its site for a student competition, to encourage new ideas in transport. He says it became the second-largest vacuum chamber in the world after the Large Hadron Collider. He calls it a hobby.
- SpaceX also built a small pusher car for the student pods. The team wants to see how fast the pusher can go with nothing to push. They feel cautiously optimistic that it can beat the fastest bullet train even within 0.8 miles.
- A tunnel built to resist groundwater must already withstand about 5-6 atmospheres of pressure. Vacuum is only 1 atmosphere of pressure difference, so a good tunnel can hold a vacuum by default.
- That makes Hyperloop inside a tunnel workable over long distances. He sees no limit on length. For a route like Washington to New York, he would prefer the whole route underground, because the area is dense and a deep tunnel passes under buildings without being detected.

## Autonomy: vision-only approach and the timeline

- Elon says Tesla's Autopilot demo used only cameras and GPS, with no lidar or radar. He compares this to the passive optical network humans use.
- His logic: once you can process camera data, autonomy is possible, and if you can't process vision, it isn't. Tesla's focus is therefore a highly efficient neural net for vision under road conditions.
- He says many others are going to lidar. He argues that cameras (and radar) are all you need, and that cameras alone can be "superhuman", perhaps 10 times better than a human.
- Cars on sale at the time had 8 cameras. He says they could not yet do what the demo showed.
- Timeline claims:
  - He says Tesla was on track for a fully autonomous cross-country drive, LA to New York, by the end of 2017. It would run from a parking lot in California to a parking lot in New York with no controls touched in the trip. He gave "November or December".
  - He expects it to work across the highway network, not just one route, so the destination can change (for example Seattle to Florida or LA to Toronto).
  - On a person sleeping in the car, he says that would be possible in about two years, setting regulations aside.
- On safety, he says the hard part is not 99.9% reliability. Safety is probabilistic and no system is perfect. The question is how much better than a human the system must be before people rely on it. He thinks autonomy will likely reduce collisions except in rare conditions.
- On shared fleets, he says an autonomous shared fleet will happen "100%". An owner could let the car be used only by themselves, by friends and family, or by 5-star riders. A car could earn money in a ride-sharing service when not in use and cover its purchase cost.

## Tesla products: Semi, solar roof and Gigafactory

- **Tesla Semi.**
  - Elon describes it as a heavy-duty, long-range truck with the highest load capacity and a long range.
  - It aims to change the belief that electric trucks lack power or range. He says an electric truck can produce more torque than any diesel semi, and that in a tug of war it would pull a diesel truck up a hill.
  - He explains that an electric motor has a flat torque curve versus RPM, while diesel and internal combustion engines have a hill-shaped curve. There is no gearbox and it is a single speed.
  - He says it drives like a sports car. Early on it would be driven by drivers, not autonomous.
  - He described driving the first prototype around the parking lot.
- **Solar roof.**
  - Elon describes roofs made of real solar glass tiles. The pattern and color can be tuned very finely. Tiny louvers in the glass make tiles look the same from street level whether or not a solar cell sits under them. He says cells go in the tiles that get the most sun, which keeps cost down.
  - Tesla was confident that the cost of the roof plus the electricity would be lower than a normal roof plus the cost of electricity. He says it is toughened glass, and thought about an infinite warranty.
  - The plan was to start releasing two of four roof types, with the other two early the next year.
  - Roofs are replaced about every 20-25 years. He says the shift will take 40-50 years, but if it is accelerated it could be about 15 years before a roof without solar cells is unusual.
  - He argues most US homes have enough roof area to meet nearly all their energy needs.
  - The vision pairs the roof with 3 wall batteries and an electric car in the driveway.
- **Gigafactory.**
  - Falling lithium-ion battery cost is the base of the economics. Tesla built the Gigafactory to double global lithium-ion output.
  - At full build it would produce about 100 GWh of batteries per year, maybe more. Cells are already being produced. At full speed the cell line is a blur.
  - He estimates the world needs about 100 Gigafactories to end fossil fuel use, "not 10, not 1,000".
  - The first cost $5 billion, and he expects the next ones at $5-10 billion.
  - He expected to announce 2-4 sites for new Gigafactories later in the year.

## Reusable rockets and the Mars plan

- Elon says reuse only matters if it is fast and complete, "like an aircraft or a car". You don't send a plane back to Boeing between flights.
- SpaceX had landed boosters 8 or 9 times by then. It then relaunched a landed booster, the first second flight of an orbital-class booster, with the two flights back to back.
- The Mars vehicle in its design has about 4 times the thrust of Saturn V. He compares it to 120 747s with all engines running. He says it can carry a fully loaded 747, with passengers and cargo, as cargo.
- He hoped people would be going into the rocket in 8 to 10 years. He admits SpaceX's timelines are optimistic ("our self-ambitions are exaggerated").
- He expects future spaceships to be so large that this one will look like "a rowboat".

## Why he builds Tesla and SpaceX: probabilities and inevitability

- Elon sees the future as a branching stream of probabilities. His actions can speed up or delay particular outcomes.
- **Sustainable energy is inevitable.** If Tesla had never existed, it would happen out of necessity. Unsustainable energy runs out, and the economics push civilization to sustainable energy.
- Tesla's core value is how much it speeds that up. If it brings it forward by a decade or more, that is the benefit he wants.
- **A multi-planet civilization is not inevitable.**
  - Technology does not improve on its own. It improves only if many people work hard on it, and it can regress.
  - He points to 1969 (moon landing) followed by the Space Shuttle, which only reached low Earth orbit, and then the retirement of the Shuttle, after which the US could not send anyone to orbit. He says the trend was heading toward nothing.
  - He points to Egypt forgetting how to build pyramids and Rome forgetting its aqueducts.
- His motivation for Mars is that the future should be inspiring: "there have to be reasons that you get up in the morning and want to live." He says being among the stars is the future he wants.
- He values beauty and inspiration and says they are underrated.
- He says he is not trying to be anyone's savior. He is trying to "think about the future and not be sad".
- On advisory councils, he says he is on two, and used the meetings to argue for immigration and climate action.
