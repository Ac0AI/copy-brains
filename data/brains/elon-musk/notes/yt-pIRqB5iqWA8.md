## Build a product customers love, and the business follows

- Elon's core advice to founders is to "focus on making a product that your customers love."
- He says it is rare to buy a product and love it right away, and very few things fall in that category. If you create one, "your business will definitely be successful."
- At the end he repeats it. Stay focused on the best product or service, one that delights the end customer. "If you can get your customers to want you to succeed," you will probably succeed.
- Earning customers' love raises the odds of success a lot.
- On Tesla, he wants a car people want to own and drive, one that happens to be good for the climate. The environmental benefit is secondary to desirability.

## Staying competitive when oil prices fall

- Elon says falling oil prices make the shift to sustainable energy harder. Interest in electric cars in general will fall.
- Tesla's answer is to make the car so compelling that people still want to buy it when gasoline is cheap.
- He says this is the only lever Tesla really has: "You have to make it attractive."
- This is the logic behind Tesla's whole approach. Don't sell guilt or subsidy; sell a better car.

## Model 3 pricing and strategy

- Elon gave no design details. He described the Model 3 as a somewhat smaller, mini version of the Model S with fewer extra features.
- The goal is to cut the price roughly in half for a smaller car.
- He expects it to be Tesla's most important car, because it will be a compelling car at an affordable price.
- With the Model 3, he says electric cars can become fully mainstream.
- Tesla welcomes competitors such as GM's Bolt. Its goal from the start was to accelerate the transition to sustainable transport.
- To that end, Tesla open-sourced all its patents the previous year, so any company in China or elsewhere can use them to build electric cars.
- Tesla also did partnerships with Mercedes and Toyota to the same end.

## Ignore competitors, focus on the product

- Elon says Tesla doesn't think much about what competitors are doing. It is more important to focus on making the best products possible.
- He compares it to a race: don't worry about the other runners, just run.
- Asked about Chinese electric carmakers, he declined to single any out.
- He tells his engineering and design team to aim for the "perfect platonic ideal of a car." He admits nobody knows what that looks like.
- The method is to make every element of the car as flawless as possible. Some imperfection always remains, but you work to minimize it and build a car that is delightful in every respect. "If you do that, the rest will take care of itself."

## User-focused design and customer feedback: Model X Falcon Wing doors

- The doors have a purpose. They make the third row far easier to reach, even with child seats in the second row.
- A parent can step into the car and place a child in the seat. They don't have to lift the child through a narrow opening over armrests.
- Only two ways give this level of access: a minivan sliding door or Falcon Wing doors.
- He rejected the sliding door for two reasons. It constrains the exterior styling, and it needs three support bars that hurt the look. He says this is why minivans all look similar.
- Falcon Wing doors have a double hinge, so they open in less space than a conventional door. If you can walk between your car and the Model X, you can get in.
- He takes feedback from customers. Hong Kong buyers asked for a partial-open mode, so the doors can act like an umbrella in heavy rain, at about 50 to 60 percent open.
- It was already in progress and ships as "just a software update."

## Autonomy, market structure and fleet ownership

- Elon predicts that in 10 years, and no more than 15, almost all new cars produced will be self-driving.
- This does not mean all cars on the road. There are about 2 billion cars and trucks, and fewer than 100 million are produced each year, so annual production is only about 5% of the fleet.
- He thinks most cars will eventually have no steering wheel. Any wheel might be an extra that appears only when manual control is needed. He compares it to elevators, which once had manual operators.
- He frames this as a forecast, not an endorsement: "Predictions are not endorsements."
- Ownership: roughly 60 to 70% of people will want to own their car, or about two-thirds own and one-third share. He calls it a guess in the dark.
- Owners may add their cars to a shared fleet and pull them out when they like. He doesn't see this as a threat to Tesla's model, as long as Tesla makes great autonomous cars. It is just more options for the consumer.
- Long term, a car without autonomy will be like owning a horse, kept for emotional reasons rather than transport.
- Current Autopilot works well in two cases: highways with clear lines and no traffic, and heavy traffic. He doesn't recommend reading or emailing in traffic, though people say they do.

## Charging, cities and local partnerships

- Hong Kong has the most Teslas per capita of any city he knows of. He thinks it will eventually have the highest share of electric vehicles in the world, so it could be a model for other dense cities.
- Range isn't an issue in Hong Kong. The challenge is where to charge as electric cars multiply.
- The ideal place to charge is home or the office, "the same place you charge your phone." Many apartment buildings can't supply that power in the garage, and parking spots are sometimes not fixed.
- Superchargers matter, but they are for long trips and top-ups away from home or work.
- Tesla is working closely with the Hong Kong government on home and office charging.
- In China, Tesla has a Supercharger network covering nearly everywhere, plus third-party destination chargers. Some drivers have driven Model S from Beijing to Tibet.
- For China, Tesla needs a local partnership and is working out how to do it.
- His view on traffic: cities are built in 3D but roads are 2D. Tunnels add a third dimension, with many crossing tunnels a short vertical distance apart. He calls tunnels "underrated."
- He is skeptical of flying cars. They are noisy and kick up wind, and the chance of something falling on your head is higher.

## Startup survival and where he sees opportunity

- A friend's line he quotes: starting a company is "like eating glass and staring into the abyss."
- The first few months are exciting. Then reality sets in: things don't go to plan, customers don't sign up, and the technology or product doesn't work as well as you thought.
- A recession can make it worse. It can be very painful for several years.
- His advice is to have a high pain threshold and to expect a long period of extreme difficulty. He knows very few companies that avoided it.
- Innovation comes from questioning how things were done before, and asking "is there a better way?" An education that discourages this hurts entrepreneurship. He doesn't fully accept that China can't produce an Elon Musk, and points to Jack Ma and Pony Ma.
- Ideas he would like others to take up:
  - Electric transport, including electric aircraft.
  - Genetics, for solving some intractable diseases.
  - A neuron-level brain-computer interface, which he describes as augmenting intelligence rather than AI. He thinks it will probably narrow gaps between people.
- Solar: rooftop solar alone can't power dense cities. Ground-based solar plants connected to existing power lines can supply them. He says China has ample sparsely populated land and could be powered by solar "easily."
