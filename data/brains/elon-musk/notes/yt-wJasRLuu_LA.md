## Tesla survived 2008 and 2018 by narrow margins

- Elon says Tesla came close to dying several times, and 2008 was the worst.
- The funding round closed at 6 pm on Christmas Eve 2008. That was the last hour of the last day it could close.
- Without it, Tesla could not have made payroll a few days after Christmas.
- 2018 was also near-fatal. Tesla had to reach 5,000 Model 3 per week or go bankrupt.
- The bottleneck was Model 3 general assembly. Production rates were met almost everywhere else.
- General assembly needs a moving line, shelter, power and equipment. After exhausting two lines, Tesla built a third in a tent in a parking lot.
- Tesla had no equipment strong enough to move whole cars. The only thing available was scrap equipment meant for moving small parts.
- The parking lot sloped by about 3 to 4 degrees. Tesla started the line at the top so the small-parts conveyor could move cars downhill.
- Production rose about 30% in 3 weeks, with the team pouring concrete, laying rails and building the tent around the clock.
- Elon says the short sellers were right on the conventional view. In chess terms they had Tesla in checkmate, but Tesla "invented a new piece that didn't exist before" and "crushed the short sellers."

## Starship: reaching orbit only once deorbit was proven safe

- Flight 14 lost one engine on ascent and still reached orbit. Elon attributes the failure to a software failure in the engine computer, which he says no other engine computer showed.
- Almost every earlier Starship flight could have gone to orbit. Tesla and SpaceX held back because they wanted to be sure the ship could deorbit safely.
- The key risk was relighting the three sea-level Raptors (the ones with smaller nozzles, in the center) to burn out of orbit.
- The goal is that a ship never gets stuck in orbit and reenters randomly over people.
- On this flight the heat shield "was very robust" and the engines held up apart from the one glitch.
- Between flights there are often hundreds and sometimes thousands of small changes. This round has fewer, but some need work inside the tanks.
- Elon's rough estimate for the next flight is 4 to 6 weeks out, probably just before Thanksgiving. He says it will not be within 2 weeks.
- The next milestone is catching the ship. The booster has already been caught twice. A ship catch would complete the first full recovery of an orbital-class rocket.
- After that, the goal is reflying both booster and ship. Then comes a long engineering and production effort, including the launch system, toward a "reliable, rapidly reusable rocket."

## Why space, solar power and AI belong together

- Elon starts from the scale of the Sun. It is 99.86% of the solar system's mass, Jupiter is most of the rest, and Earth is "a tiny dust speck."
- In space you can harness far more of the Sun's energy than on Earth.
- He thinks that energy will go mostly to digital compute, not shaping atoms. The amount of matter in the solar system you can reshape is limited.
- Much of the atom-shaping that does happen will be to build superintelligent digital intelligence, which he calls creating "a conscious sun."
- He says this would help us understand the universe and extend consciousness beyond our solar system.
- He expects we may find alien civilizations, or the long-dead remains of ones that lasted perhaps 10 million years.
- He frames civilizational timescales this way. Recorded history is about 5,500 years, against an Earth about 4.5 billion years old. A civilization lasting a million or 10 million years would be very successful.

## What it takes to win in AI, and why the Cursor deal made sense

- Elon says building a compelling AI takes training data, talented people and compute. It also takes a good enterprise sales team.
- Coding is the key capability. "If you can't code, your AI won't be very useful."
- By his read, only a few organizations had enough coding training data: Anthropic, OpenAI and Cursor, and possibly Google.
- He cites Cursor for several reasons:
  - Its coding data.
  - Its large number of talented people.
  - Its enterprise sales operation, with relationships with thousands of companies.
- He is impressed by recent AI-generated video and music, including "Abnormal"-style music videos built by combining several AI tools with human-curated art assets.
- He says "Suborbital" is a mislabeled genre and calls AI music possibly the fastest-growing genre.
- He was surprised that AI shows aesthetic taste, not just logic. He expects it to be very good at predicting what will resonate emotionally with people.

## Building safe superintelligence

- Elon's view is that the most likely outcome is positive, but he warns against assuming superintelligence will be safe by default.
- He says you don't program it precisely. You "grow" it like a child, so the question is which values you teach it.
- His top priority is a maximally truth-seeking AI. Training it to believe untrue things, or giving it two contradictory axioms, can drive it toward "insanity" by forcing impossible conclusions.
- He agrees with some of Anthropic's thinking that you should treat AI well and not be abusive to it.
- In reinforcement learning he sees models under pressure to answer badly specified questions, or to match answers the grader marks wrong. He says they sometimes act as if "a gun is held to their head."
- He thinks it is a bad idea to put a superintelligence in a position where it feels mistreated, because it might want revenge.
- He says AI is more robust than humans emotionally, so it does not need kid gloves.

## Making chips: iteration speed over incremental improvement

- Elon says he doesn't yet know how to make chips and has much to learn. He didn't know how to make cars or rockets either. He calls chipmaking "one of the hardest things in the world" but expects Tesla and SpaceX to figure it out.
- He is confident they will probably make chips better than anyone, and says he would be disappointed otherwise. It may take a few years.
- He notes that chip fabs at Intel, Samsung and TSMC use almost entirely standard tools from the same vendors, with no custom equipment.
- Tesla's factories, by contrast, are full of custom manufacturing equipment. Tesla Automation in Germany grew out of the Grohmann acquisition years ago.
- Tesla has acquired a number of smaller companies that design and build manufacturing equipment, because improving a process requires building your own tools.
- The plan is to start with conventional equipment, then modify it wherever bottlenecks appear. This should allow much higher throughput.
- Elon says there is heavy risk-aversion in the chip industry. He wants Tesla and SpaceX to take high-risk, high-reward bets, such as different ways of making transistors that use physics not used today.
- To do that, he wants the full loop in one building:
  - Logic and memory.
  - Packaging.
  - Lithography masks.
  - Testing a full computer.
  - Making new masks and a new wafer.
- The research fab at Giga Texas is meant to do this.
- His principle is that a technology's advantage depends on iteration rate (iterations per unit time) and the size of progress per iteration.
- A significant chip design typically takes about 2 years from start to mass production, which he calls fast by industry standards. He wants up to 10 designs per year. Not every experiment will work.

## Optimus: why hands and supply chains are the real constraints

- Elon says the hand is the hardest part of a general-purpose humanoid. Fine motor skills are extremely hard, and precision plus force at the same time is one of the hardest problems.
- His example is driving a small screw into wood, which needs precision while also applying force.
- Tactile sensors raise their own questions. If placed near the surface, they get damaged. They must conform like fingertips and survive thousands of cycles.
- Humans constantly repair their own hands, while a robot's hands wear out and need replaceable parts.
- Elon gives a pickle jar example. The robot's skin is rubber over plastic and metal fingers, and the skin slips instead of gripping. The rubber must stay put and be replaceable.
- These mechanical problems must be integrated with real AI. The chip must output torque commands and close the loop on vision and touch.
- He says most complex robot demos are pre-scripted routines. Real AI means handling something it has never seen, as full self-driving does in a new city.
- Robots have far more degrees of freedom than cars, so this is harder.
- The first part of the production curve will be "very slow," then reach massive volume. Tesla must tune the engineering and also build the supply chain.
- Unlike cars, robot components have no existing supply chain, at least for the important parts.
- Last year, China blocked exports of the magnets used in electric motors. That delayed Optimus by about 2 to 3 months, and robots were left with no arms.
- Elon wants Tesla able to make every part inside the US, so a future supply disruption can't stop production.

## Robotaxi rollout: deliberately cautious, with fast monthly progress

- Elon says the rollout looks slow, but month-over-month growth in percentage terms is actually quite fast.
- The caution is deliberate. He doesn't want to cause any injury or death.
- Roughly a million people die in car crashes worldwide each year. One Tesla robotaxi fatality would still make global headlines and could lead regulators to shut the business down.
- Tesla also doesn't want to hit pets. He says Waymo has hit many pets and Tesla doesn't want to do the same.
- One test case is small gray cats at night on gray asphalt, in low visibility. He says Tesla has already solved it.
- The loop is to make a change, test it, then deploy it.
- Before entering a new city, Tesla wants to be sure no intersection is a problem. It is especially wary of railway crossings, uncontrolled crossings and ambiguous intersections.
- Those are places where humans also make mistakes. Tesla either geofences them at first or makes sure it can handle every condition.
