## Elon on why he communicates directly and on Twitter

- Elon held one of the very first Twitter accounts (under 10,000 users). He deleted it because people tweeted what coffee they had. Someone else took the handle and tweeted in his name. Friends including Jason Calacanis said he should use Twitter to get his message out, and he got the account back.
- He started tweeting for fun. He says his early tweets were "quite crazy" and "the arc of insanity is short" because it started insane and hasn't changed much.
- He uses Twitter as his one main channel. Facebook and Instagram don't suit his style, and Instagram is hard for conveying intellectual arguments. He doesn't spend much time on social media: "if you're wondering what I'm saying then they can just sort of go to Twitter."
- He sees crypto scammers impersonating accounts as a problem. He thinks a few people at Twitter could block them easily.
- He values customer feedback as a way to find problems: "a lot of times I just don't know if something's wrong... It has to have to learn it somehow." Scaling service effectively is "very difficult" and "very important."

## Elon on wealth, capital allocation and regulatory capture

- Elon says he is not an investor. The only publicly traded stock he holds is Tesla, plus privately held SpaceX. He has no diversification.
- For liquidity he takes out loans against Tesla and SpaceX stock, mostly to reinvest in SpaceX and Tesla, and sometimes to fund projects like Neuralink and The Boring Company. He cites about a billion dollars of debt against that stock.
- On capitalism vs. communism, he says what matters is "the responsiveness of the feedback loops" to maximizing the happiness of the population. Resources controlled by monopolies, oligopolies, or government have poor feedback loops. He says the issue is the operating system, not that people in government are bad.
- He wants truly competitive markets with correctly set rules, and warns about regulatory capture: "the player should not control the referees."
- His example is California's zero-emission vehicle mandate. He says car companies tricked regulators into easing EV requirements by pointing to fuel cells, which he says are far off. GM then recalled and crushed the EV1.
- He flags oligopolies (candy, dog food) as showing price-fixing and cut R&D, with little innovation. He says the car industry is competitive, which is good, because a better product wins in the marketplace.
- He argues for a carbon tax: "it's a no-brainer." The market works well with the right prices, and 90%+ of economists agree. Raising the price on carbon would also encourage sequestration innovation.
- On law, he wants laws short and simple, with sunset periods so they don't accumulate. He favors direct democracy as less susceptible to corruption. He suggests making it easier to remove laws than add them, for example 60% to pass and 40% to repeal. He says industry groups write laws that entrench incumbents.

## Elon's reasoning for his mission and why he works on sustainable energy, space and more

- In high school he expected to do physics at a particle accelerator. The canceled Superconducting Super Collider pushed him away from that.
- He says Douglas Adams's Hitchhiker's Guide shaped his view: the hard part is knowing what questions to ask. So we should expand the scope and scale of consciousness, keep human civilization going as long as possible, and become multi-planetary.
- Five things he thought in college would change the future: the internet (like a nervous system giving everyone instant access to information), sustainable energy, making life multiplanetary, changing human genetics (first by removing disease propensity, e.g. with CRISPR), and AI.
- His initial motivation for electric vehicles was resource necessity: you can't keep mining and burning finite hydrocarbons. The environmental urgency came later.
- He says he is "not trying to be some Savior": maximizing happiness and understanding reality "just seems like the obvious thing to do."
- He says he left South Africa at 17 to be where new technology was being created. He arrived in Montreal with about $2,000 Canadian. He worked on a wheat farm in Saskatchewan, then as a lumberjack and in a lumber mill boiler room (the highest-paying job, $18/hr vs. under $8). He studied at Queens, then Penn on a scholarship.
- He chose the internet over a PhD because "the Internet is not gonna wait," while electric cars could wait.

## Elon's early companies: Zip2 and X.com/PayPal

- Zip2 started with maps, directions, and Yellow Pages-style listings. He believes it was the first maps and directions on the internet. Elon wrote the initial code personally. He and his brother had a few thousand dollars, and they lived in the office and showered at the YMCA.
- Cheap-living tactics: pasta, vegetables, and beans were cheap. They ate at Jack in the Box when they could afford it.
- Mapping tech: they used vector-based maps through a Java applet, sending vector data instead of bitmaps, which was much faster on modems. Map data came from NAVTEQ, which had spent a large sum building it, for free in exchange for a share if they ever made money.
- Most VCs had never used the internet. After the Netscape IPO, interest changed. Mohr Davidow invested about $3 million for about 60% of the company, which seemed "crazy" at the time.
- Yellow Pages companies were dismissive. A Yellow Pages executive held up the book and asked if the internet would replace it. Zip2 pivoted to newspapers (Knight-Ridder, New York Times, Hearst), which were better partners because Craigslist was eroding classifieds.
- Elon's problem was that media companies held board seats and voting control and kept pushing the company in senseless directions. The tech was not deployed properly. Compaq acquired Zip2 in 1999; Elon received about $20 million.
- X.com thesis: money is an information system for labor allocation. Banks are "a high latency, low security collection of databases," so a real-time secure single database should be better.
- X.com merged with Confinity (Max Levchin, Peter Thiel and others). They raised $100 million in three weeks in March 2000, just before the crash. They were adding about 100,000 users a month.
- His view: PayPal should have kept the broader X.com plan, offering every financial service so users never needed to take money out. He disagreed with the PayPal brand as too narrow, plus proposed risky technical and branding moves that the team found too scary during the dot-com crash. He says PayPal executing his plan might have become the most valuable company in the world, but notes the PayPal alumni then built YouTube, Yelp, Palantir and LinkedIn.
- Payments lesson: instant payment raised transaction velocity "by a factor of maybe three to five" compared to mailing checks. X.com dropped the banking license plan to focus on payments (a state money-transmitter license cost "fifty bucks or something").
- On Bitcoin he is "neither here nor there." He calls crypto a possible bridge between legal and illegal transactions, a replacement for cash, not the primary database.

## Elon on starting SpaceX and why reusability is the key

- After PayPal, Elon went looking for NASA's Mars plans and found none. His original idea, Mars Oasis, was to land a plant greenhouse on Mars to excite the public and raise NASA's budget.
- He went to Russia in 2001 to buy ICBMs and said he could pay under $9 million, not $20 million. The Russians kept raising the price, so he concluded he'd get "shafted" afterward as well.
- He found that rocket raw materials are a small share of cost. He concluded the real fix was reusability and lower launch costs: "if it's not reusable it's extremely expensive." A single-use car would double the cost of a round trip.
- SpaceX started in early 2002 and moved to LA for aerospace talent. He gave it about a 10% chance of success.
- Recruiting was hard: he had built no hardware and was called "Internet guy." No good people would join, so he became chief engineer.
- Falcon 1 was the simplest liquid-fuel rocket and left room for reusability. The first three launches failed; the fourth reached orbit in 2008. The first failure was a fuel-line fitting that cracked, causing an engine fire.
- 2008 was brutal: third failure, Tesla financing collapsing, and a divorce.
- He says startups have a "chasm of doom" after an optimistic first year, then years of grief.
- On Rocket Lab's helicopter recovery, he thinks it will be harder than it seems because of lift limits, weather constraints, and danger. He's impressed with Rocket Lab overall.
- On city-to-city Starship travel, he says it can be done and is the fastest way to travel based on known physics. The economics could be competitive with international air travel. Sonic booms mean it must be offshore.
- On other competitors copying reusability: "it's actually surprising how little innovation has been" despite SpaceX landing and reflying boosters. Innovation tends to come from new entrants.

## Elon on the early days of Tesla and the Roadster

- In 2003 JB Straubel and others introduced him to AC Propulsion's tzero. He knew lithium-ion gave about 4x the energy density of lead-acid. He tried to get AC Propulsion to commercialize it and offered to fund a tenth of an electric Scion that he thought would fail at $70,000.
- Tesla was formed with a small team. The first plan was to put AC Propulsion's powertrain and battery in a Lotus Elise. This was a mistake: the car ended up about 60% heavier, and only about 6-7% of the parts were common with the Elise. Crash tests, airbags, and HVAC all needed redoing.
- AC Propulsion tech couldn't be industrialized (handcrafted electronics, analog motor controller, hand-wound motors). He says Tesla ended up using none of it. "The real test of any given startup is how well does it respond to adversity and adapt."
- In 2007 an investor audit showed the Roadster cost was more than double what they'd been told, requiring a price near a quarter million dollars to break even. Elon fired the CEO (Martin Eberhard) and says he found the problem was an orchestrated deception. He used interim CEO Michael Marks (Flextronics) as a manufacturing-savvy bridge.
- On the CEO role: running a startup is about 80 hours a week; being CEO of two is physically impossible, which is why he tried not to be CEO of Tesla.
- Tesla name: they had to buy the trademark; he mentions Faraday as the fallback name. The Tesla.com domain took years and about $10 million.
- Roadster final assembly happened in a leased former Ford dealership in Menlo Park at about 50 cents a square foot, with battery production in San Carlos. Early Roadsters were driven about 50 miles before delivery, since many failures happen early.
- He moved battery assembly from Thailand to San Carlos because moving a prototype to production needs a fast engineering feedback loop, and five months of waiting for cell feedback meant scrap inventory.

## Elon on manufacturing, vertical integration and why car startups fail

- Manufacturing is "insanely difficult" and "under appreciated." Prototyping is easy; "building a production system is a hundred times harder."
- Suppliers gave Tesla their worst teams, since big customers get the top ones. So it was "vertically integrated or die."
- Model S was a huge leap: a full sedan against Mercedes, BMW and Audi. NUMMI gave them a building, but the only equipment GM left was what couldn't be used elsewhere, and the body line had to be built new.
- Organizational structure shows in the product: a "box in a box" results from separate teams each wanting an enclosure. The Model 3 pack had a top enclosure and the car had an underbody. Modules in packs are "vestigial": they were meant to be swappable like server racks. He wants cells straight into the pack.
- Battery swap (Model S/X): they demonstrated swapping two packs faster than gas fills, but faster charging and more range made it unnecessary.
- Model 3 production hell: he pulled nearly everyone onto it, including the Semi and Roadster work. "Either you go to volume or there won't be any Tesla." Chicken-and-egg: affordability needs volume, volume needs affordability. He compares it to "a boulder chasing you."
- Why car startups fail: legacy automakers make most of their profit (he says sometimes more than 100%) from spare parts and service on their existing fleet, so they can sell new cars at roughly zero margin or at a loss. Like razors and blades, or consoles and games. A new company has no fleet to subsidize new cars. About 80% of legacy fleets are out of warranty, versus under 20% of Tesla's.
- His conclusion: a new car company has to win on two technology discontinuities, electrification and autonomy, and be compelling enough that people pay a premium.
- Dealerships profit from service and aren't aligned with customers. Tesla's goal is to minimize service costs and use over-the-air updates.
- Money isn't the constraint on R&D: "it's not like if you just had more money you could spend it effectively." Exceptional engineers are scarce. He says prioritization was "usually out of desperation, not choice."
- On electric aircraft or VTOL, he says each such effort takes a massive amount of effort, so Tesla can't do everything. Aircraft regulation is a separate regime.

## Elon on Gigafactory expansion, cash flow and local production

- Reasons for Giga Shanghai: China is the biggest car market, tariff uncertainty could have blocked or priced out sales, and there's talent and drive. He says Tesla pushed for a wholly-owned factory, arguing American companies should be able to own factories in China as Chinese firms (e.g. Faraday Future) do in the US. China changed the law over time.
- Shanghai was cheaper and faster because "we are way less dumb than we were." They designed out foolishness from Fremont and Nevada, and the production line is simpler. Suppliers in China were often more efficient.
- Fremont's Model 3 body line, designed for 5,000 cars a week, was pushed to 7,000 by removing unnecessary steps, about 40% more output, which also lowered marginal cost and improved quality.
- He wants production on each continent. Shipping from California means finished-goods inventory on the water with high capital carrying cost, transport costs, tariffs, damage, right-hand drive, and many languages' stickers. It also creates a quarterly wave: Europe/Asia first, then East Coast, then West Coast, so deliveries spike at quarter end.
- Local production breaks the wave. Examples of logistics pain: wrong-headlight shipments swapped at port, port strikes, wrong stickers.
- Cash flow: when customers pay before you pay suppliers, growth helps cash. When you pay suppliers first and get paid after shipping, "the faster you grow the faster your cash position" worsens. Local factories fix this.
- Berlin Brandenburg was chosen for speed: BMW had planned a plant on the site, so much of the environmental paperwork was done. The local government was supportive, and it has talent and affordable housing nearby.
- Cybertruck: he calls it Tesla's best product. It uses an exoskeleton architecture, with the shell as the load-bearing structure. The inspiration was Blade Runner, Mad Max, and Aliens. The base price was $39,900. He says he told the team nobody might buy it, but they could always make one like other trucks. "You want to have something different." He says reported pre-orders were over 400,000 (his words at the time were uncertain).
- On Autopilot, he says vision with deep learning is the path. A rewrite combines perception and planning, with 3D labeling cutting labeling effort by two to three orders of magnitude. Dojo is meant to speed neural net training.
