## Elon's approach to cutting waste: look at the computers, not the org chart

- Elon says the federal government is "run by computers", with "several hundred computers" effectively running it. An executive order passes through many people until a computer implements it.
- He argues the only way to find waste and fraud is to analyze the databases directly. Asking a human leads to another human, then a contractor, then another contractor running a query, so answers come many layers removed.
- That is why, before the 2024 election, he said he wanted "the login for every computer" rather than a Washington job. He calls this "reprogramming The Matrix": reconcile the databases and follow the electronic money flows.
- He says money moves by wire and Swift, so the flows are traceable if you look at the payment systems.
- He found what he calls "magic money computers": systems that issue payments with no coherence across agencies. He says they number 14, mostly at Treasury, plus some at HHS, State and DOD.
- Government numbers are "probably off by 5% or 10% in some cases".

## Finding waste is basic: audit licenses, cards and payment codes

- Elon says it takes no genius. "You don't need to be Sherlock Holmes."
- In every department, with no exception found, there were more software licenses and media subscriptions than people. Many had zero logins, yet the government kept paying.
- He found twice as many government credit cards as humans, at $10,000 limits, with no good explanation.
- Treasury payments went out with no payment code and no explanation. Tracing them showed contracts that were supposed to be shut off but never were, so the company kept receiving money.
- He says the federal government does not code what money is spent on, unlike Tesla, where every expenditure is coded. He argues a public company keeping books that way "would be delisted immediately" and its officers imprisoned.
- He puts the split at roughly 80% waste and incompetence to 20% fraud or malice.
- Grants to nonprofits have few controls and no later auditing. Recipients can pay themselves lavish salaries and buy jets and homes.
- He cites $1.9 billion going to a nonprofit linked to Stacey Abrams, for environmentally friendly appliances, with about 100 appliances delivered.

## Transparency and targets for DOGE

- Elon says he is "reasonably confident" of removing a trillion dollars of waste and fraud, producing net savings in FY26 (which starts in October). This is conditional on being allowed to continue without impediment.
- His tactic is extreme transparency. Every action, small or large, goes on the Doge.gov website and on the X handle. He says "I don't think anything's been this transparent ever."
- When people complain about cost-cutting, he says they are free to point out which cuts they object to, since everything is done "in the daylight".
- He estimates entitlement fraud nationwide at "well north of 100 billion, maybe 200 billion".
- He says the biggest thing opponents fear is DOGE turning off fraudulent entitlement payments. In his words, "we're clearly over the target."

## Execution: first principles and in-sourcing manufacturing

- Elon says he takes a "post physics, first principles approach to everything".
- He did not want to in-source manufacturing; he "was unable to outsource it effectively". Tesla's original plan was to outsource almost all manufacturing, but there were no good companies to outsource to. Over time they in-sourced almost everything, and SpaceX did the same.
- He argues outsourcing manufacturing "is actually the exception of the rule", and that he became very good at manufacturing "because I had to".
- He claims he may know more about manufacturing than any human ever, given how many different things he has manufactured in so many arenas.
- On ideas versus execution: "The ideas are easy... the execution is the hard part." He cites "1% inspiration" and "99% perspiration".

## Intellectual property: open patents, with a narrow exception

- Elon assumes everyone will copy, but says that by the time they do, he is five generations ahead.
- Tesla open-sourced its patents so anyone can use them free. It files patents only to head off patent trolls, then makes them free.
- He argues patents are meant to maximize innovation, but "most patents inhibit innovation, they do not help it". He wants ideas to flow so people get there faster.
- The exception is pharmaceuticals. A Phase 3 trial can cost about a billion dollars, while the drug is cheap to make. Without protection, nobody would pay for the trial, which is a free-rider problem. Otherwise he says there should be no patents.

## How he started and reframed SpaceX's problem

- Early history:
  - He arrived in North America with $2,500, a bag of books and a bag of clothes, and graduated with about $100,000 in student debt.
  - He gave up a Stanford PhD (ultracapacitors) to build an internet company, because he "couldn't bear the thought of just watching the internet happen".
  - He wrote the first version of the software himself in 1995. The company, Zip2 (the transcript calls it "sub2"), did maps, directions, Yellow Pages and White Pages online.
  - Compaq bought it for about $300 million in cash about four years after he graduated. His share was $21 million.
- Early frugality:
  - With about $55,000 between him and his brother, they rented a leaky attic office for $500 a month, since the cheapest apartment was $800.
  - They slept on couches that converted to beds, showered at the YMCA, and reset the couches before anyone arrived. This lasted several months.
- He founded X.com, which merged with Confinity (Peter Thiel and Max Levchin) and became PayPal. He recalls about $180 million after tax from PayPal.
- SpaceX began as a plan to spend part of that on a philanthropic Mars mission. The idea was a greenhouse on Mars with seeds in dehydrated nutrient gel, for the "money shot" of green plants on a red background, to raise NASA's budget.
- He tried to buy refurbished Russian ICBMs in two trips to Russia. The price went from $4 million to $8 million to higher before any contract was signed, so he walked away. A US rocket with the extra stage would have cost about $80 million, and he wanted two in case one failed.
- Lesson: he concluded America had plenty of will to go to Mars but lacked an affordable way there, so he built the rocket himself.
- Context on the problem: Russia was charging NASA up to about $90 million a seat for a seat that cost about $10 million. He called this a fleecing.

## Work ethic and mission

- Elon sleeps about 6 hours a night and works "almost every waking hour". He eats lunch and dinner during meetings and skips social dinners.
- His motivation rests on a "philosophical foundation": expanding the scope and scale of consciousness. He argues that a multi-planet civilization hedges against risks such as nuclear war or a meteor.
- Mars goals:
  - Earliest human landing: 2029, with the best case "two to four years" beyond that if it slips.
  - A self-sustaining city is the key threshold: one that survives even if supply ships from Earth stop.
  - He thinks it takes about 20 years and on the order of a million people and a million tons of cargo.
  - It requires rebuilding Earth's entire industrial base, starting from mining and refinement.
- His AI predictions:
  - In 10 years AI is smarter than the smartest human.
  - Billions of humanoid robots will exist.
  - About 90% of miles driven will be autonomous (50% in 5 years).
  - Goods and services become close to free, and the challenge is fulfillment and meaning.
  - He gives a 10-20% chance of an apocalyptic outcome, but about 80% likelihood of extreme prosperity.
- On the AI race: he thinks the US is likely to win in the next few years. After that it depends on who controls AI chip fabs, and today 100% of advanced AI chips come from Taiwan. He calls this a national security weakness.
