## Twitter's financial position at takeover and the cost-cutting logic

- Elon says Twitter was "tracking to lose over 3 billion dollars a year" when the deal closed, with about $1 billion in the bank. He calls that "four months to death."
- In a normal year he describes Twitter as roughly $4.5 billion revenue and $4.5 billion costs, "really kind of like a nonprofit" running at break-even.
- Two things changed that picture:
  - Revenue fell by over a third, from $4.5 billion to about $3 billion. He attributes this partly to the economic cycle and partly to political concerns. He says Facebook and Google also saw ad declines.
  - Expenses rose from $4.5 billion to about $6 billion once about $1.5 billion of debt servicing is added.
- The result was a roughly $3 billion negative cash flow. His conclusion is that without drastic, immediate cost cuts the company would go bankrupt.
- Headcount went from just under 8,000 to about 1,500.
- On criticism that the layoffs felt abrupt: "this is not a caring uncaring situation... if the whole ship sinks nobody's going to drop." He says people got 3 months of severance, and more in some cases.
- He says he couldn't simply sell Tesla stock to fund Twitter. He can only sell during certain windows, and he did not want to sell it.
- He says Twitter was roughly break-even at the time of the interview. He thought it could be cash-flow positive "this quarter" if trends held, and "literally in a matter of months."

## Running Twitter lean: infrastructure, usage and product shipping

- Elon points to usage as proof the cuts worked: all-time highs, passing 8 billion user minutes per day.
- He says the site runs on two data centers instead of three, so about two-thirds of the prior compute capability. He says improvements to the core algorithm, in some cases 80%, sharply cut CPU usage.
- He claims the site is faster and more responsive than before the takeover, despite record usage.
- He lists what shipped with the smaller team:
  - Long-form tweets.
  - Video uploads of up to two hours, with videos of any length coming soon.
  - A subscriber program so creators can put content behind a paywall and "make a living on Twitter."
  - An open-sourced recommendation algorithm.
- On open-sourcing, he argues users should trust "the thing that you can actually see and understand" rather than a black-box algorithm. He says Twitter took feedback from people reviewing the code and improved the algorithm further.
- He says predictions that Twitter would stop functioning didn't come true, and quotes Mark Twain: "rumors of our death are greatly exaggerated."
- A mistake he admits: shutting down one data center was "quite catastrophic." He had thought the centers were redundant, but many things were hardcoded to that one. Functionality was lost around late December and early January, and the team had to rush to restore it. He calls it the biggest crisis of the six months.

## Why Elon tried to leave the Twitter deal: the warehouse analogy

- Elon says the only reason he tried to pull out was that publicly stated user numbers exceeded real user numbers. He ties this to bots.
- His analogy: you buy a warehouse of goods after being told less than 5% is broken. You inspect it and find 25% is broken. You might still want the warehouse, "but probably at a lower price."
- He says he went through with the purchase because he thought a court would force him to. This followed legal advice.
- He claims earlier management ignored the bot problem because "their bonuses were tied to user growth." His point is that if pay is tied to user growth, you won't look closely at the users.
- He says removing bots reduced scams, spam and misinformation.

## Paid verification as a bot and disinformation defense

- Elon says the paid verification program is not mainly about revenue. His example: a million subscribers at $100 a year is $100 million, "a fairly small revenue stream relative to advertising."
- The real goal is to "massively raise the cost of disinformation and bots in general."
- His prediction: "any social media company that does not insist on paid verification will simply be overwhelmed by advanced AI bots." He describes such bots as "resilient instances of ChatGPT."
- He says legacy blue ticks would be removed the following week, and he expects the paid system to "swim just fine."
- He refuses to give legacy media special treatment, including the New York Times: "we're not going to say that there's some anointed class of journalists." Everyone pays the same small amount.
- He wants the public, not only media, to shape the narrative and add corrections to stories.
- His standard for success: "if Twitter is the best source of truth it will succeed and if we are not the best source of truth we will fail."

## Moderation approach: bots, Community Notes and neutrality

- Elon says Twitter should be a "digital town square" serving all political persuasions, provided speech is legal. He argues it shouldn't amplify a narrow regional politics worldwide.
- He believes speech limits should come from law: "if the people of a given country are against a certain type of speech they should talk to their elected representatives and pass a law."
- On country rules, he says Twitter will comply with local laws, such as India's strict rules on social media content, rather than put its people at risk of prison. He says it can't go beyond a country's laws.
- He claims misinformation is lower now. His reasons:
  - Millions of bots are removed daily.
  - Community Notes, which he calls "extremely powerful" with a "batting average" he finds "extremely high."
  - Contract moderators still exist.
- He admits Community Notes won't be perfect and says "no system is going to be perfect." His aim is to be "the least inaccurate."
- He describes the old trust-and-safety function as "putting a thumb on the political scales in the far left" and says it was let go. He argues for equal treatment across the political spectrum.
- His stated metric is "unregretted user minutes," in contrast with apps where users regret time spent.
- He is generally against banning things, including TikTok. He concedes a ban would help Twitter's usage.

## Advertisers, feedback and personal operating rules

- Elon says most advertisers have returned or said they will, and he knows of none who said definitively they won't. His signal is that "if Disney feels comfortable advertising children's movies and Apple feels comfortable advertising iPhones," Twitter is a good place to advertise.
- On negative feedback: "if you do lose your feedback loop that's actually not good." He says he doesn't turn replies off and removed his entire block list, so he gets a lot of negative feedback and thinks that is good.
- Tweeting rules he describes:
  - Don't tweet after 3 a.m.
  - Don't tweet when drunk or really upset.
  - Save a controversial tweet as a draft and re-read it the next day. This came from a friend, and he calls it a good rule of thumb.
- He says he sometimes sleeps in the office library, about three days a week.
- On his view of journalism: experts and people who were there often know more than journalists, because most articles are an "approximation" of events.
- On the role of money: he says he doesn't care about money, and would hand off Twitter only to someone who would "rigorously pursue the truth."
- His overall verdict on buying Twitter: the pain level was "extremely high," and there were money mistakes along the way. He still thinks "it should have been done."
