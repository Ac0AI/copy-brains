## How Tibo approaches the first conversation with a founder

- He starts by reaching out to the maker of a product he likes and giving a genuine compliment. He wants the maker to feel unthreatened: "You are not trying to bring the product down or behaving like a shark."
- The aim of the opening is to build trust and a relationship, not to pitch.
- He then asks questions about the product's history, achievements and challenges. He notes that "Founders generally love to talk about what they created."
- His tip is to start with easy, low-effort questions and not to ask for full stats or analysis right away.
- Once the tone is set, he states clearly that he wants to buy. He believes transparency builds trust and sets expectations.

## Handling "No" and timing the approach

- He treats an initial "No" as normal, because makers love what they built from scratch. He respects the decision and stays friendly to keep the door open.
- Timing matters. Founders of newly launched or very successful products often refuse to sell. When things start to go south, they become more willing to talk.
- He follows up periodically without being pushy. Owners remember interested buyers and often come back when they decide to sell.
- He watches industry news and the business's progress to spot challenges or turning points, and times his follow-ups around them.
- He does not rely on one seller. While discussing the acquisition of Typeframes, he was also talking to another video-editing SaaS, but no deal came of that one.
- His principle: "building relationships is the key. It's not just about the offer; it's about creating a long-term mutually beneficial partnership."

## Pricing and deal structure

- He starts due diligence while terms are still being negotiated.
- He suggests looking at startups above your initial price limit. Buyers should also seek concessions through earn-outs, seller financing or loans.
- For small acquisitions, he starts due diligence with a simple letter of intent (LOI) sent over email.

## Technical due diligence

- If you can't judge code quality yourself, he strongly recommends getting someone who can. Code quality and maintainability "will drastically change your acquisition experience."
- A premium SaaS should have clean, somewhat documented, annotated code. If it is a ticking time bomb, that should be part of the deal and lower the valuation. Bad code "could set you back a full year."
- Technical debt: understand its state and how hard the code will be to update. Ask for the ugliest and the most complex parts of the codebase.
- Infrastructure: ask about uptime over the last several months and the procedure for pushing code to production.
- Data security: understand how user data is protected and what authentication is in place.
- Integrations: they must fit business needs and future growth. Ask for all services, APIs and dependencies the product relies on.
- He quotes John Rush on uptime: most SaaS tools don't know their uptime stats, so ask them to set up monitoring and watch it for a month.

## Financial due diligence

- Review the P&L and cross-check it against bank and credit card statements so income and expenses match.
- Getting view-only access to the seller's Stripe account is a deal-breaker for him if refused.
- Manually check the billing history of a few randomly chosen customers for anything odd.
- For consequential deal sizes, or without a finance background, hire an accountant or expert. A good broker like Acquire may have done preliminary due diligence.
- He suggests building your own P&L and comparing it with the founder's to find discrepancies: "it is worth the cost."
- A comprehensive report covers the P&L, balance sheet and cash flow, historical revenue and profit trends, and quality of earnings and net debt.
- Put churn "under a microscope": high churn means losing customers fast and paying for marketing.
- Check a sample of customers' demographics against what the seller claimed.
- Look at outliers such as spikes in new subscribers or churn, and ask why.

## Legal and marketing due diligence

- Legal: get all running contracts, make sure the domain transfer is in the deal, and confirm the seller owns the property rights, trademarks and copyrights.
- Marketing: find out whether the seller pays to acquire customers and understand their CAC. Check where customers come from and whether that channel will stay active after the handover.
- Understand market size, the competitive landscape and trends such as shifts in demand, technology and regulation.
- Check whether growth is artificial because of heavy ad spend.
- He sees fewer explored acquisition channels as a plus, because that leaves more growth opportunities for the buyer.
