## Building products for himself first

- Tibo argues that building for your own problems is a big advantage: "you are such an expert at understanding your own problems that it makes the building so easier."
- He built Tweet Hunter for himself because he struggled to grow an audience on Twitter. He used the product daily to post and find inspiration.
- Every small annoyance sent him back to the code. That "never ending loop of fixing stuff" is why the product reached "super super high quality."
- He says his failed ideas were not for him. One example was a search engine for communities where founders could find places to promote their product. He now sees it as a bad idea for two reasons:
  - Users would launch once, so there is no recurring use.
  - Dropping a product into a community and leaving doesn't work. People ban you, or you get no results.
- He still says he had to build it to learn why it wouldn't work.

## Shipping a product a week to find one that works

- After two startups that each took about two years to fail, Tibo and co-founder Tom decided to succeed or fail fast.
- They shipped a new product every week. If it brought no revenue within a few weeks, they moved on.
- They shipped roughly 10 products over four months, building a small audience and learning what people want along the way.
- Tweet Hunter was the 8th product, and it took off from the start.

## How Tweet Hunter went viral

- It launched around the GPT-3 rush. Most people used GPT-3 to generate tweets, but it wasn't good enough for that.
- Tibo and Tom used it to analyze, cluster and categorize old viral tweets instead. This became the "inspiration wall," Tweet Hunter's core feature: evergreen viral tweets users could draw ideas from.
- Early promotion was Twitter posts and a few Reddit posts.
- Even after they stopped talking about it, new subscribers kept arriving, which told them they had a viral product.
- Tibo's view: a product aimed at people active on social media is organic by nature. If users succeed with it, they talk about it. So "work more on the product and a little bit less on the distribution."

## Equity partnerships with creators

- Tibo cold-reached big Twitter accounts. One, with about 40K followers who was growing fast and talked about the same topic as Tweet Hunter, reacted enthusiastically and wanted to become a shareholder.
- They gave him 25% of Tweet Hunter, and it "worked so well."
- For Tapio (the LinkedIn product), they spent about six months looking for the right LinkedIn influencer. This was the longest part. The product was nearly ready before they found him. They partnered with Alex Burman.
- They targeted LinkedIn because its users have more money, are more willing to buy, and include salespeople who buy quickly.
- Tibo thinks builders and makers "do not partner enough with influencers and creators." Many big creators don't make much money and would happily partner with talented builders.
- With one distribution person and one builder, he says, that is "way enough to build the business."

## Mini free tools as an SEO acquisition channel

- Tapio's biggest acquisition channel became free mini tools that rank on Google. Each is a landing page with one specific goal, such as downloading a video from a LinkedIn post.
- The tool does the job, then suggests using Tapio (for example, to schedule that video).
- They shipped about one new mini tool every month or two weeks. Today, he says, one can be built in a day or two.
- The video downloader began as a developer's hiring test. It was good enough that they hired the developer and published the tool.
- Social media was the first channel at the start. Mini tools later took over because their traffic compounds. Each new tool doesn't hurt the others, whereas a creator partnership or viral post "is a spike and then it goes to zero."
- Tapio combined both: being active themselves, sponsoring many creators, and building searchable tools.
- He thinks people are leaving SEO "way too soon." It still feeds most of his projects, so he invests in SEO tools.

## The earn-out acquisition and growth after

- At sale, the company was about a year old, with two products (Tweet Center for Twitter and Tapio for LinkedIn). Elon Musk was taking over Twitter, and Tapio wasn't fully compliant with LinkedIn, so it was a risky deal.
- The deal was structured with about 80% as earn-outs tied to growth milestones and 20% upfront, which de-risked the sale for them.
- The original plan was to build, reach 10K monthly revenue, sell for 1 million and start over.
- Early buyers in India and the US wanted them to stay two years, which they feared. They sold to a former middle-school classmate who founded Lamb List and Lempire. He had been Tapio's first big client, buying 17 licenses for his team.
- Sale in summer 2022: annual revenue was a little under 1.5 million, about 75% Tweet Hunter and 25% Tapio.
- By April 2024, annual revenue was 8 million, with Tapio now about two thirds and Tweet Hunter one third.
- They hit five of six earn-out milestones and received 8 million of a possible 10 million.
- Enterprise sales were a gap. He and Tom dislike calls and custom onboarding, and limited support, so they only scratched the surface of B2B selling. He sees room in turning LinkedIn followers into clients and using LinkedIn data as purchase intent.

## The LinkedIn ban

- Tapio's analytics and a large database of LinkedIn posts for the inspiration feature relied on a Chrome extension using LinkedIn's internal API.
- That broke LinkedIn's rules. It was safe for users but not for Tapio, and LinkedIn suspended his account about two years after Tapio's creation. By then he had been out of the business for about six months.
- The ban was about the extension, not AI content creation itself.

## His current challenge

- After leaving in April 2024, Tibo returned to builder mode with a challenge: build five products to 100K monthly revenue each.
- The most successful so far is Revid AI, which turns text into video, aimed at TikTokers and YouTubers. He made a 5-minute explainer for his daughter on how a camera works.
- He shares wins and failures weekly at tmaker.io.
