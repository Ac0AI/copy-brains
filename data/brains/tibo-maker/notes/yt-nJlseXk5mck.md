## Building a lot of cheap products to find what sticks

- Tibo says the most common first-time founder mistake is sinking years into one idea that isn't worth it. He did this twice, spending about two years on each, with basically no revenue, which he counts as roughly four years lost (though he learned skills that later made him a CTO and then an entrepreneur again).
- He blames wanting to "sell at all cost" and refusing to hear feedback that says the idea is bad. He also rejects the engineering-school advice to chase one billion-dollar "next Facebook" idea.
- His process on returning to startups was to ship one new product every week until something stuck. Over about four months he shipped more than 10 products. Some were tiny, some got a few hundred dollars in revenue, some got zero, and some were info-product landing pages (a lead model).
- Building more shows you what the market wants. You see quickly when a launch goes "to the void" and when a tweet about a product takes off, which you can't see by working on a single product.
- If you have no idea what to build, he says to pick the very first thing and start. It will probably fail, but it leads to hundreds of better ideas.

## Build products you use yourself, then look for others like you

- His edge is building products where he is the user. He used each product daily, fixed friction and bugs right away, and collected feedback through Twitter DMs in a never-ending loop.
- Without this, you either spend huge effort understanding the end user's core need or end up with a surface-level view of the problem and fail.
- If a workflow is weirdly personal, he suggests recording everything you do and explaining why. The internet is large, so other people almost certainly share your habits.
- Big companies must go broad, so they skip niche workflows. His example is mind maps: loved by some, disliked by 80-90% of people, so big software ignores them. That leaves room for focused products that do just that very well.

## Ideas are not the edge: be 10x better at one thing

- Tibo says there is nothing valuable in the idea itself. Tweet Hunter, Taplio, Revid and Outrank were all unoriginal ideas. His edge is being a "product person" who builds something at least 10 times better than competitors at one specific thing.
- "You have to do one thing extremely well", and then you can be average or good at everything else, as long as that one thing is very valuable to users.
- Taplio's one thing was actionable analytics. You could see the top-performing content of any LinkedIn account, find the best-performing formats, and repurpose a viral post into your own niche.
- Outrank's one thing: most SEO time goes into keyword research, not writing. It found high-volume, low-difficulty keyword opportunities, put them in a calendar, and each day generated a post for that keyword.
- What made Outrank take off was a backlink exchange. Because it had a large user base, it clustered users with similar content and created backlinks between their blog posts, raising domain authority. Users saw it as the cheapest and most efficient way to get backlinks, since AI means everyone can now generate a good post.

## How small the first version should be, and how to talk to users

- The first Tweet Hunter was one feature: an input field where you typed a topic like "cold email" and got a wall of viral tweets on it. That alone got traction and justified building the rest.
- He notes that with AI everywhere, expectations are higher, so it is now hard to ship something that looks very rough.
- For a new product idea (a "backup" high-quality podcast recorder, discussed with the host), he warns that trust is the hurdle: losing a one-hour recording to a bug would be fatal, so early users need strong reliability signals. He advises being flexible and following what users actually care about rather than your original vision.
- On Tweet Hunter and other early products, the support button linked to his Twitter DMs. It opened a conversation (why did you do that, how would you push this further) instead of a bug email. "The forms are the worst. I would definitely not do that."
- His advice to newcomers: build real things and expose the work publicly, and have real conversations with people, which is the hardest part but where the knowledge about direction and opportunities comes from.

## Forking, pivoting and acquiring: the Revid story

- Revid was originally Timeframe, a product video maker (script and music in, text animations synced to the beat). Tibo acquired it with a fixed $15k plus revenue share up to a total of $50k, which he paid off fully within 6 months. It was generating $3,000 per month at the time.
- The issue was that product videos are only needed once or twice, so he pivoted to social media and AI videos. At the time AI video was very glitchy, so value came from sourcing the best models and many hacks to fix generations.
- He did not kill Timeframe. He copy-pasted the codebase into a new repository, called it Revid, and ran both in parallel. When Revid took off he killed Timeframe, redirected its traffic, and merged them. Revid is his best product so far.
- He did the same earlier: Taplio was a fork of Tweet Hunter dedicated to LinkedIn. "Every product is an experiment", and he will kill, merge or fork as needed.

## Validating without an audience, and launching

- He says the audience came from the successful products, not the other way around. When he started Tweet Hunter and Taplio he had about 900 followers on Twitter, which he says were 99% bots or dormant accounts.
- The audience formed from sharing the process: building a product a week, documenting wins and fails, and doing more SEO than other founders.
- You don't need thousands of followers to validate: DM a few relevant people and look for three or four replies on a tweet. Platforms are shifting from followers to reach and engagement, so good content can reach anyone.
- Product Hunt (he was Product Hunt maker of the year in 2022) is no longer a big traffic spike, but he still launches there for two reasons: a high-DR backlink (plus further backlinks from sites connected to Product Hunt) and an excuse to make noise to his audience.
- Founder Circle, his community, went from zero to $15K in about 36 hours with about 150 members. He says that was 100% his five years of audience and authority, and that managing a community is a lot of work and he's unsure he'll keep it alive because he prefers building products.

## Pricing: one offer, aimed at serious customers

- Across his products the standard is a single price, often $99 per month (he mentions $49 to $99 as his range). Testing showed that more choices make it harder for users to convert, so he uses either one offer or a no-brainer option where the choice is obvious.
- Price is not set from cost: "the product and the cost is based on the price, not the other way around." He can deliver better or worse results by using better or worse AI models.
- Price selects your customer. Going from $9 to $49 to $99 brings in very different people: the higher the price, the more serious and successful they are, and they are more loyal if served well. People who want the best product don't mind paying a bit more for a 10% quality gain.

## Running several products and affiliates

- He doesn't run multiple products alone: each has a different co-maker who can handle things when he focuses elsewhere.
- All his products target the same niche, small-team founders who want more sales and traffic. So one distribution action (his audience, newsletter, SEO, socials, ads) benefits every product.
- Affiliates: he uses an affiliate platform, tries to turn users into affiliates, and keeps them engaged weekly or monthly with content on what works and how top affiliates operate. One post mentioned affiliates making roughly $30,000 in a month (confirmed in the question, not stated by him).
- A tactic: find competitors' affiliates through tags in their links (such as "via" or "ref") using Ahrefs or Semrush.
- Affiliates accelerate growth but don't create it. People risk their reputation, so they only promote a high-quality product that already has proven growth and conversion.
- He remains bullish on software despite AI: developer job openings are rising again, one engineer now produces about 10x more, and he cites Stripe's reported 70% revenue growth. He expects a rotation in which software wins, not the end of the industry.
