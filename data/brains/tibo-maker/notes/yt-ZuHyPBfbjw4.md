## Why the failures stopped: build for revenue, not for the family dinner

- Tibo failed for about four years. His diagnosis: he was "doing everything I could to just feel good at a family dinner to boost my ego" (managing 20 people, raising VC money), with no clients and no real traction behind it.
- The shift came in 2021: no employees, no VC, just him and his co-founder, aiming for a decent revenue and a good living rather than "the next Facebook".
- He frames it as building for yourself (to make money and be free) rather than to prove something to others.
- He and his co-founder (a marketing person he met in business school) set a challenge to create and ship one new product per week until one stuck. Eight or nine went nowhere; Tweet Hunter was the 11th and took off fast.
- His build habit: when he has an idea he doesn't discuss it, he builds a prototype in 6 to 8 hours and sends it to his co-founder. If the co-founder doesn't see it at first, he iterates a few days and shows it again.

## Tweet Hunter: scratch your own itch, ship one feature

- Twitter had been the acquisition channel for his first user on nearly every product, so he wanted to grow an audience there himself.
- He downloaded viral tweets, used early GPT-3 to categorize and cluster them (e.g. startup-related or not), and browsed them in a CLI to spark his own content. Posting from that worked, so he turned it into a product.
- The MVP was one feature: a search bar returning viral tweets on a topic (e.g. "marketing") for inspiration. Behind it sat the AI clustering algorithm. It launched on Twitter and Reddit like that.
- His lesson: "by doing something for myself first, I was able to build something useful which then became a product."
- Later he built a Twitter scheduler out of the inspiration feature.

## The GK Moreno partnership: giving equity to a distribution partner

- At about $1-2K MRR, he reached out to big influencers somewhat randomly. GK Moreno replied "okay I want in": the product matched the methodology of his Twitter course, and he wanted to be a co-founder, not just promote it.
- Deal: GK Moreno got up to 25% of the product. Tibo calls 25% of a small team "quite big", but says it worked incredibly well. Complication: he already had a marketing co-founder, so they worked out an agreement.
- They worked in "staff mode" for about two months, improving the product.
- GK's own launch in September 2021 took them from $3K to $20K MRR in about two weeks.
- He expected heavy churn after the launch; it didn't happen. They then grew about 20% month over month, from roughly $20K to $200K/month over a year and a half.
- Tibo later replicated the influencer-maker model on every new product, but now he is the creator and partners with a developer.

## The influencer flywheel and why he dropped it

- For Taplio they replicated the approach: a LinkedIn influencer partner, plus a group of about 15 Twitter influencers each getting 0.1% of profits and 0.1% of the exit.
- To make "yes" easy, there was no obligation: they'd love shares and feedback, but the contract required nothing. About 80% of those approached said yes, even though Tweet Hunter was only around $30K/month then.
- Each launch or feature announcement then got amplified by 15 high-caliber influencers.
- He hasn't repeated the group: profit payouts and exits are hard to organize and sustain, and he says crypto/NFT tooling that could have handled it now "screams scam".

## Marketing: sell the outcome, not the feature

- Tweet Hunter's first positioning was feature-level: inspiration to write better tweets. He moved up the chain: better content, more followers, a better personal brand, a more successful business, more money.
- Changing the marketing to that outcome "worked like 10 times more". He applies it to all new products.

## Co-maker model: take over stalled products and fix retention

- How he finds partners: posting on X, and since the exits, inbound DMs from builders. If a product is well built, he proposes working together.
- His framework: find someone with an interesting idea and decent execution, stuck at $200-300/month without knowing how to escape. Revamp core things, work with 10 to 20 users for weeks or months to learn what makes it sticky and distinctive, then go broad using his audience, newsletter, SEO and ads knowledge.
- Why it beats hiring a developer for your own idea: on their own project the dev stays highly motivated.
- The big lever is talking to users. Until $10K MRR, the in-app support button on his products goes to his X DMs. The goal is to understand the user's core workflow and why the product does or doesn't fit it, not to fix color requests.
- Hundreds of tiny things (design, conversion rates, onboarding) also matter, and he says the bar rises because AI makes products better: "you have to raise your standards."

## Outrank: forcing stickiness with auto-publishing

- Outrank began as a blog post generator (input a topic, edit, publish). It was well built but gave users no reason to return, since people lack the discipline to come back daily.
- Fix: make the software do the work automatically every day. It understands the product, researches competitors, generates high-volume low-difficulty keywords, and publishes one post per day to the user's CMS. Users can review beforehand, but "if you don't, it's going to be published anyway", which forces them to return and cut churn.
- It publishes daily on all his own five projects.
- Network effect: Outrank cross-links its users' sites to build authority. SaaS sites suit this; for niches like a bakery it's hard to find related users to link between.
- Outrank has crossed $200K/month. The Revid product earns slightly more; he doesn't share its numbers because the space is competitive.
- He attributes his recent success to combining dev skills, SEO and social distribution. He uses every product himself and sends the team constant feedback.

## Audience building, working style, and exit lessons

- On X: consistency over talent. "I'm not the best writer", but he shows up daily, is nice to people and shares work. Even past 100K followers, some threads got two likes.
- Routine: works 9am to 5pm at his desk, takes no calls, keeps team communication written.
- Exit: Tweet Hunter and Taplio sold for $8M total, mostly earnout-based (about 25% upfront, 75% via revenue milestones over two years). Because they insisted on keeping control, he felt little buyer help on promotion and put heavy pressure on himself.
- His new rule: no exits. If he ever sells again, he'd insist on no earnout and a transition of 3 months max.
- Twitter API: after Musk's takeover the free API became $42K/month. They could afford it but were cut off for 48 hours while arranging payment.
- He started new products before his earnout ended, so he never had a break.
- Money: Peter Levels told him a steady revenue stream is healthier than one lump sum. Tibo spends only about what he earns monthly.
- Product advice: when you have an idea, build it in two days with AI and put it in front of relevant users rather than showing a piece of paper.
