## Why Tibo builds relentlessly for one audience and solves real problems

- After two failed VC-backed startups, Tibo and his co-founder decided not to raise money and not to hire (they later had to change the hiring part).
- He says a big team without product-market fit is a nightmare to pivot: the team thinks you have no vision and loses motivation. He needed "two big failures" to learn this.
- At university in France, people tell you raising money and being "important" is the path. He did exactly that, right after graduating.
- New rule: "build relentlessly for the same people trying to solve the same problem." Everything built was meant to solve real problems, which he calls "10 times more sustainable."
- Ideas that sound good but come from problems you haven't worked on are usually bad businesses, because you lack history and core skills. You have to "get your hands dirty" to understand the user's core problem.

## Build for your own problem

- Working on your own problems is "a huge competitive advantage."
- His first startup, an app to motivate kids to do chores, failed partly because he wasn't yet a parent and didn't understand the guilt and alone-time struggle.
- Tweet Hunter came from his own problem: he was trying to grow his Twitter audience, because Twitter was the best growth driver for all their products.
- If you build for yourself, you are the first customer, likely many others share the problem, and you know what is real value versus a useless nice-to-have.
- Whatever you do, you must do it for a long time. If it isn't a problem you care about, you'll give up after three months, which is too short to learn an industry's core problems.

## Shipping one product a week to find the winner

- Previously they spent two years on one idea that got invalidated. This time they built roughly one new product per week and validated as fast as possible, moving on if it made no revenue.
- Over about three to four months this produced 11 products. Most made no revenue; per the transcript, some had users or modest cash flow, but nothing proved validation.
- Examples: Twitter analytics delivered by email, a community database and search engine for promoting products across Slack groups and Twitter accounts, and a GPT-3 tweet generator.
- The 11th was Tweet Hunter. From the first day of onboarding users, growth happened even without pushing it on Twitter.
- Original MVP: a huge categorized database of viral tweets. AI analyzed your account and surfaced viral tweets on your topic and in your style, shown as a wall of inspiration. The MVP was essentially one input field (e.g. "marketing").
- How to know when to stop: when it's not working you never know, but with true product-market fit "you know." He cites a YC-style idea that it is painful because users want it so badly it crashes your servers and overwhelms you.
- He thought in early 2021 they were too late for GPT-3 (a rival headline tool sold for $1M). He says that was wrong and that AI still has many opportunities.

## Support via Twitter DMs as a growth and product loop

- The support button in Tweet Hunter pointed to his Twitter DMs. He kept Twitter and his code editor open all day, fixing things in real time.
- Because they used very flexible serverless tooling, a fix could ship about 5 to 10 minutes after the DM, and he'd reply "it's now fixed, you can see it in the app right now."
- Users tweeted about how fast the team was, which was a strong early marketing boost. It faded as the team grew and processes were added.
- Direct feedback loops beat quarterly NPS-style cycles at deciding what to build.

## Growth levers: influencer co-founder, tool credits, build in public, free tools

- **Influencer CMO with equity:** a Twitter copywriter (around 4K followers then) asked for equity because Tweet Hunter's "wall of viral tweets" matched the copywriting method in his course. Tibo didn't want to give equity for a few promo posts, so the contract made him a real CMO who also had to run affiliates, SEO and ads, not just post.
- Goal then was only 10K MRR by end of 2021. They hit about 5x that in the first year.
- He ran a Twitter launch in September 2021 that took them from 3K to 20K MRR in three weeks.
- **Attribution by default:** early on, tweets showed the tool used to post them. Because the product was premium and attracted big viral accounts, "sent with Tweet Hunter" spread. Twitter later removed that.
- **Build in public:** sharing steps and learnings helped growth.
- **Free mini tools:** they built 20 to 30 free tools, each serving one need (e.g. analyzing your audience to find the best time to tweet). They are real apps with personalized answers, rank on Google for queries like "best time to tweet," and bring growing traffic.
- He argues Twitter and LinkedIn now drive B2B SaaS buying: people choose tools based on who they follow, and indie makers building in public gain an emotional audience relationship.

## Platform risk and moats on Twitter and LinkedIn

- When Twitter moved API access from free to enterprise, the tier price they heard was $42,000 per month. Access was cut about four days after the announcement, so users couldn't post and other things broke.
- They worked frantically, built "crappy things" to bypass blockages, and coordinated with other Twitter-tool founders. After 48 hours, one of about 10 contacts replied and helped move them to the enterprise tier, which they now pay for.
- That contact came from a competitor. He distinguishes competitors who share contacts and help each other from those who tried to steal customers while they were nearly offline.
- On LinkedIn, the official API can't retrieve your posts, so no analytics. Taplio's workaround is a Chrome extension using LinkedIn's internal API, which is harder technically and explains why LinkedIn has far less competition than Twitter had.
- Taplio use case: export people who commented on your LinkedIn posts, find their emails and send them to lemlist, which is "almost warm emailing."
- On AI content, he thinks OpenAI's rules on social media use initially blocked this use case. His view is that people don't care if a tweet is AI-generated if it's good, and the best output is AI fine-tuned by humans. Good results need huge prompts that capture the user's tone and topics, which is the value the product adds.

## The exit: earn-out structure, timing and pick-the-right-industry advice

- They sold with revenue a little below $2M annually. The deal could exceed $10M if revenue milestones are hit, structured with a large earn-out and six revenue milestones, so the upfront amount was "quite small." The goal is reaching $10M annual revenue.
- Earn-outs of around 50% over two years are common; one tied to massive growth is less so. The structure reflected how risky and platform-dependent the business was.
- Original plan was to sell at 10K MRR for a 10x multiple. He later realized going from 10K to 20K MRR is much easier than starting over, and valuation multiples improve at larger scale (about 100K MRR or more).
- Selling was hard: growth was strong and staying could have paid more, but the risk for two people was high.
- Advice: pick the right, fast-growing industry (they chose social media/creator tools plus AI) so you don't need crazy tech to grow. Don't hire early, build yourself, and build for yourself.
- He uses GPT-4 for most of his code: reusable prompts describing the project, stack and file hierarchy, then specific questions. He also argues serverless lets one person do what needed 10 to 20 people ten years ago.
