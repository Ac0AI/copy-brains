## Retention is a system you build once; acquisition is a sprint you repeat forever

- Tibo calls acquisition "a sprint you repeat forever": find someone, convince them, close them, repeat next month. It is expensive and the lowest-margin part of the business.
- Retention is "a system you build once". It is high margin, compounds instead of resetting every month, and is what stacks MRR and builds enterprise value.
- Founders over-invest in acquisition because it is visible and urgent. Retention gets ignored because it is quiet.
- He admits retention isn't glamorous (no screenshots to post on X), but says "this will make you rich."
- He lists five retention levers: filter requests, build systems, raise pricing, make customization a moat, tie customer success to the product.

## Stop complying with every request: filter feedback instead

- Tibo says building exactly what every complaining user asks for is the mistake "almost everyone makes", and AI making building easier is making it worse.
- Adding every request makes the app do too many things, and new users get confused about the core function.
- His rule: the founder's job "isn't to comply. It's to filter." Get the feedback, then check whether the request aligns with what you're building.
- Two tests before saying yes:
  1. Protect margin: some value should stay reserved for higher tiers.
  2. Protect differentiation: if the workflow was built to differ from what already exists and the request erases that difference, don't build it.

## Revid.ai example: saying no to manual control is the product

- Most AI video tools give users micro control. Revid.ai bet differently: AI models keep improving, and people want to save time, not micromanage a timeline.
- Instead of manual scene-by-scene editing, Revid tries to generate a full 2-minute video out of the box that does what the user actually wanted.
- Revid gets many requests for more manual control. Tibo says saying yes to all of them would make it "exactly like every other AI video tool out there."
- His conclusion: "Saying no to that request is the reason people choose Revid."

## Build systems, not patches

- When solving a user's problem, ask whether you patched one instance or built something that prevents the whole category of problems.
- "Systems compound. And your revenue compounds with it."

## Raise prices to reduce churn (Tweet Hunter: $9 to $49/month)

- Tweet Hunter started at $9/month, was raised over time, and ended at $49/month.
- The expected outcome was more churn. The actual outcome was the opposite: "Higher prices meant lower churn."
- At $9/month, people sign up on a whim and cancel the moment something feels slightly off, because there is no real commitment.
- At $49/month, people are already convinced it needs to work for them, have skin in the game, and try to make it work.
- Takeaway: "Higher pricing doesn't just increase revenue per customer. It changes who your customer is."

## Customization as a moat

- The more a user configures a product around their exact workflow, the harder it is to leave.
- Tibo treats customization as more than a feature: it is a retention mechanism.

## Tie customer success to the product (Outrank.so example)

- When a user's own success is built on top of what you made, they are far more likely to stay.
- Example: every great SEO case study for Outrank.so used it extensively, with 30 articles a month, backlink matching with a partner website, and a DR increase.
- If those customers wanted to churn, they would have to do all of that, and more, themselves. They are already getting results, so they stay.
- Tibo ends by asking readers how many months their average customer stays, as a way to make retention a number founders track.
