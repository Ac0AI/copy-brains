## Tibo's bar for validation: charge from day one, judge by revenue and churn

- Tibo says the most common founder mistake is wanting to "not be wrong" more than wanting to succeed. People then force-sell instead of validating, and stop listening to signals pointing at a bigger opportunity.
- He charges from day one. His reasoning: "if there is no revenue, there's no stickiness in the revenue, it's going to be very hard to build a successful business." Tiny validation moments make it easy to lie to yourself.
- A decade ago, after college, he cared more about sounding important ("managing a team of 10", "raised 200k") than about a foundation of real revenue. He now relies on revenue only, to avoid spending 2 years on a product that ends with nothing.
- Churn bar: monthly churn better than 20% is worth pursuing. At 40%, a subscriber stays about two months, and the product is not sticky.
- He cites "max MRR": churn and acquisition together cap your revenue on an S-curve, and a high-churn product needs unsustainable acquisition to grow. He prefers to pivot and iterate until churn is under 20% before thinking about acquisition.
- He picks 20% because AI users hop between products; he says five years ago the bar would have been under 5%.

## Pivoting Typeframe into Rabbit (AI shorts)

- Tibo acquired Typeframe (product videos) at about $1-2k/month. Making product videos is something people rarely do, so churn was high.
- He talked to users constantly and saw they were using it for something else. His rule: "When you have a product that is made for one thing and people are actually twisting it to turn into this other thing, that's usually a very strong signal that you have to follow."
- In 2024, AI video models only produced 5-second clips. Rabbit splits a long video into scenes and stitches many 5-second clips together, keeping characters and environments consistent. That stitching is still the core engine.
- He pivoted completely: rebranded, bought a new domain, relaunched.
- Rabbit now offers more than 100 tools, including music videos (drop a Spotify link or audio file, choose a character), YouTube-to-shorts, avatars, and an auto mode that turns new blog posts or channel content into videos automatically.
- It does a bit over $600k/month with a team of about four. Each new AI video model release made it take off again.
- He thinks the product video idea alone could have reached roughly $10k/month, but pivoting gave him another shot at something bigger. Pivoting several times gave him multiple shots at winning.
- When he pivots, he refunds existing customers, locks them out, and does not let them block the decision.

## Keeping a direct feedback line with users

- Tibo says the one thing needed to understand a product's core problem is constant communication with users.
- With Tweet Hunter, his first successful product, the in-app support button sent users to his Twitter DMs. It was overwhelming, but as the builder he heard every bug, pain point and feature request.
- He did the same with Typeframe.
- Today Rabbit and Outrank are big enough that he has hired customer support. He still gets DMs and likes them because they keep him "inside the loop".

## Ideas, quantity over quality, and shipping fast

- Idea source: look at products you already pay for and build a more specific version that fits your own, narrower use case. Other people likely share your odd situation.
- He suggests copying an existing (often expensive) SaaS and niching down. He thinks $10k/month is already a huge success and enough to be "completely free, no boss", and says hitting $1M/month is very hard and he never expected it.
- Before his earlier project was acquired, he spent about four months shipping one new product per week: build a landing page, tweet it, check traction. If none, move on; if traction, build the product and onboard sign-ups. "Nine products failed... and the 10th one just took off."
- He now believes quantity leads to quality. The best product in a series of many tends to be better than one built with sole focus on quality. He recalls a Harvard-style experiment where a class split into one-essay and ten-essay groups produced the best essays from the quantity group.
- He says he cannot predict his winners; his successful products surprised him. He treats every release as an experiment that can fail.
- He builds many small single-page tools (e.g. an AI Reddit humanizer, a tweet rewrite and validation tool) to test whether people use them, to start ranking, and to start user conversations.
- An audience gives a shot but does not make an unsuccessful product succeed. You can hack your way to $5-10k/month, but going beyond needs a sustainable channel.

## SEO playbook: domain rating plus tools

- SEO is his favorite channel and the number one acquisition channel for Rabbit, because it gives a recurring flow of interested users. He says "tons of people are calling SEO to be totally dead. It's really not the case for me."
- Two components:
  - Domain rating: it is very hard to rank with a new domain under about 20 DR. Outrank's backlink exchange marketplace helps raise it cheaply. Ranking in search also matters for being mentioned by ChatGPT.
  - Tools: Google's AI snippets make blog posts harder to rank, but cannot replace tools. Rabbit has a dedicated page per tool (e.g. audio to video).
- Keyword research: type phrases like "AI video tools for" into Google and treat every suggestion as a candidate landing page. He gives Peter Levels' Photo AI as an example of a famous founder still building many specific landing pages for SEO.
- Domain relevance matters. Competitors with "AI video" domains rank more easily, so he gave up on "AI video" and targets niche keywords.
- AI makes spinning up many landing pages easy, which makes differentiation harder. He says ranking now needs strong content, instant load speed and backlinks.
- Tool: Ahrefs. He says it is expensive but worth it.

## Outrank and the portfolio

- Outrank started as a tool that publishes blog posts daily and evolved into a full SEO solution with the backlink exchange marketplace. He says it might reach $1M/month on its own.
- His five products are all AI-related. Super X crossed $30k/month. He keeps small projects because they could outgrow the biggest ones, as has happened before. He compares it to a diversified portfolio.
- He doesn't see himself going from five projects to 20, and is trying to remove himself as a bottleneck.

## Landing pages, pricing and running lean with AI

- Landing page purpose: move visitors from a visit to a sign-up. Social proof is the most important element given trust issues in AI: testimonials, user counts, numbers. For Outrank he built success stories from real users with large business impact.
- He says most people jump to the product too soon. Describing the problem and why it is hard to solve is forgotten but important.
- Pricing: he picks ideas where he can charge about $50-100/month and adapts the product and AI usage to fit. At that range people don't ask for quotes before subscribing, and customers are higher quality with lower churn. At $20 or $9 you get churn, refund requests and the position of a cheap product; he wants to be the premium product.
- Team: he uses contractors and keeps the team very lean. He is "convinced right now that just one person can do the job of 20 compared to like 5 years ago." In the past he had a 20-person team for one product and says convincing each person of a new direction when pivoting is exhausting.
- Tooling: Cursor, with GPT 5.4 for complex non-UI work and Gemini 3.1 Pro for UI. He says the model mix changed within two weeks (Opus was his favorite before). One prompt in Cursor with Gemini 3.1 Pro produced a landing page animation.
- Spec workflow: he talks with the AI in Cursor to generate a spec, discards about 50% of them and restarts rather than editing, and repeats until he'd use the feature. To visualize a spec, he asks the AI for an image prompt and runs it in Google's Nano Banana 2 to get a dashboard mockup.
- Building is no longer the bottleneck: his backlog is empty and small items take about 10 minutes. The hard parts are good ideas and earning trust and promotion.
- Support automation: if he does something twice, he automates it. He uses text shortcuts (e.g. a refund macro that forwards to the right teammate) and has hired support for the larger products.
