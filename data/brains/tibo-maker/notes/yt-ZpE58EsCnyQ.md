## Validation means revenue, not usage

- Tibo lost two years each on a 2015 project and a 2017 project that were never validated. After the second, he and his co-founder set an absolute rule: "never ever again" work multiple years on a product that isn't validated.
- Number one mistake he sees: treating free users or usage as validation. His kids' programming game reached about 15k monthly users, but almost no paying users.
- Revenue (willingness to pay) is the signal. In 2021 the plan became shipping one new product a week until something stuck.
- Most of those products made roughly $200-300 a month. Tweet Hunter, the 11th product, hit $1k MRR in its first 2 weeks because "people were willing to pay just for the promise of what it was doing."
- Free users dominate feedback and skew the roadmap. In Outrank and Revid he has only paid users, so all feedback comes from people who pay, which he says makes implementation easier and higher quality.

## Going deep on one platform to beat saturated markets

- Tweet Hunter entered a crowded scheduler market (Buffer, Hootsuite, Later), whose tools targeted enterprises and supported every social platform shallowly.
- Tibo integrated only Twitter, but at full depth: every Twitter feature, plus automations and integrations only possible with that focus.
- He targeted individual creators (the creator economy was starting in 2020-2021) rather than companies. An individual can realistically perform on one platform, so the depth-first tool became the obvious choice.
- He replicated this for LinkedIn with Taplio, as a separate product instead of folding it into Tweet Hunter. Reasons: supporting a platform deeply is hard across multiple channels, and the distribution model was tied to one platform's audience.

## Distribution share for influencers

- On Tweet Hunter he gave 25% of the product to a Twitter influencer who ran all distribution. This was a product-level contract, not company equity: 25% of monthly profits plus 25% of any exit.
- The influencer's audience and methodology matched the product, so alignment was strong. He repeated it with a LinkedIn influencer for Taplio.
- He also created a group of up to 17 influential Twitter people, each owning 0.1% of the product, called the "creator investors."
- The contract placed no obligation to promote; they kept their share regardless. A few never promoted, but about 80% did, retweeting and sharing product updates.
- Tactic: treat them as investors, not affiliates. He sent monthly financial investor updates with key details, which was unusual for them and worked.

## Exits and earnouts

- Tweet Hunter and Taplio sold together for $8 million (per the intro). Tibo says the churn, stakes and platform dependency (Twitter chaos in 2022, LinkedIn cracking down on non-compliant API use) forced a roughly 2-year stay and about 75% of the price as earnout.
- Earnout milestones turned the mood from gaining with each milestone to fearing losses if missed. He didn't enjoy it, so his new rule is "never sell ever again."

## Building the "indie hacker stack" portfolio

- He runs five public products (plus a few in stealth), with a goal of a bootstrapped portfolio; he says they recently crossed $1M per month in payouts across the five.
- Strategy: pick a target persona (indie hackers, solo founders, tiny teams) and build everything they need to run a business, instead of picking an industry like video editing and building every tool for it.
- Portfolio is opportunity-driven, not a master plan. Outrank came from a founder whose product Tibo loved but saw big gaps in. After sharing lots of feedback they partnered as equals, with Tibo providing distribution.
- His best and worst performing products were both acquisitions, so he stays undecided on buy versus build.
- Each product has a different co-founder, and separate brands keep that fair. A personal brand across all of them would benefit him but not the partners.
- He considers product-led growth the only model: a product that isn't product-led "feels like a scam" because the product isn't good enough to drive growth.

## Shipping often and talking to users by DM

- He expects a 5-10% success rate on new products (citing Peter Levels) and says more shipping is the way to learn what people want.
- Five years of daily posting on X toughened him to rejection, so "your product sucks" no longer hurts.
- He shifted from launch announcements to DM conversations with people actually trying the product: onboarding them, asking about pain, value and workflow. New products now go more into stealth with real users.
- He keeps a strict no-call policy to protect coding and family time; this podcast was his only call that week.

## AI-run operations and daily workflow

- With AI he codes 0% himself and acts as QA. His backlog of ideas is now empty and he struggles to feed it, and the risk is feature creep, so he tries to build the right things but not too many.
- He keeps the stack he's most comfortable with; he says stack isn't a differentiator.
- Each morning he opens about 15 dashboards (Stripe, PostHog or Amplitude, ads, newsletter, social performance from his own tools Postsyncer and SuperX) in random order. Whatever grabs his attention (a spike, a dip, a viral tweet) sets that day's work: a campaign, an email, or a free lead-magnet product.
- He is moving off Claude Cowork-style chat to scheduled systems on Mission Control HQ (an OpenClaw wrapper). Example: a weekly job reads the last 7 days of Revid commits and emails affiliates a "what's new" digest so they promote more.
- Goal now: build systems so the portfolio scales beyond five products without him operating each one.

## Advice for product founders

- Time AI spends working should go to customers, not TikTok or X. Spend it DMing people and understanding core needs.
- His method for resisting distraction: feeling guilty about it.
