## Pivoting from VC-style startups to indie hacking

- Tibo spent about 10 years launching startups and had several failures before Tweet Hunter and Taplio.
- His first two startups lasted about 2 years each. He raised money, hired big teams and chased user counts.
- He says he was not revenue-driven: "you're not revenue-driven". Users who didn't pay still reassured him, so he kept going.
- Raising funds and hiring a large team "completely stops you from pivoting quickly". Asking 3 of 15 employees to explore a new idea every week would burn them out.
- He argues the indie hacker or solopreneur advantage is waking up with an idea and testing it fast.
- A change of life (a child, travelling as a digital nomad, the start of COVID, no office) pushed him to work alone and without a team.
- He admits the solopreneur model is hard to make aspirational compared to Facebook or WeWork stories. Earning 6-7k per month does not make people dream.

## Launching a project every week to compress 2 years into 1 week

- His goal was to compress the 2 years it took to learn a startup was failing into about 1 week.
- The target was a new project per week. In practice projects took up to 3 weeks, and some ran in parallel.
- He and Thomas launched about 10 projects in roughly 4 months, and the 11th was Tweet Hunter.
- Examples that didn't work:
  - A geolocated lead and email scraper for local businesses. It made about 2-3k€ from one big contract, then was dropped.
  - A tweet generator built on GPT-3, right after GPT-3 launched.
  - A Twitter analyzer using GPT-3. It reached about $300 MRR at $9/month (about 30 payers) but had high churn, so they dropped it.
  - A community search engine telling you where to promote a project. He says it fails because you can't drop a link in a community and expect sales.
- They asked for payment very early on every product, to avoid repeating the old mistake of measuring users instead of revenue.
- They reused the same payment system, accounts and backend on each project, which made each new launch fast. He compares this to Marc Lou's ShipFast boilerplate.

## How Tweet Hunter emerged

- Twitter was becoming the main acquisition source of every project. Tibo and Thomas then wanted a bigger Twitter presence themselves and hit the problem of writing viral tweets.
- They built a search engine for viral tweets: the tool works out what you tweet about and shows high-reach tweets on similar topics. The first version was just a wall of tweets that refreshed.
- Tibo used it himself, and the wall sparked plenty of ideas for his own tweets.
- With only this feature, they reached $200, $300, $800 and even $1,000 MRR.
- He started with 1,500 followers, about 950 of them dormant. He says he was "nobody".
- The timing helped: 2021 was the creator-economy boom, so they shipped as people got interested in creating content on Twitter and LinkedIn.
- He frames building for a problem you have yourself as a solopreneur edge. The feedback loop is fast: use the product, see an issue, change a few lines, ship.
- Example: a user reported a bug in a DM, Tibo fixed it in about 5 minutes and replied that it was fixed. He says such users become your best advertising.

## Distribution: giving equity to creators and building in public

- They sent Tweet Hunter to several big Twitter influencers. One loved it and joined the project, and the joint launch happened 3-4 months after the first version. In 2 weeks it took MRR from 3k to 18k.
- They then kept about 20% monthly growth for the following 6 months.
- That influencer got about 25% of the product's profit and 25% of the sale price of that product only. It was a distribution-style contract, not company equity, since the company held other projects.
- This worked well when they later built Taplio, a LinkedIn version of Tweet Hunter. Taplio now generates about twice what Tweet Hunter does.
- In January 2022, with Tweet Hunter at roughly 35-40k MRR, they onboarded 17 large Twitter creators and gave each 0.1% of Tweet Hunter.
  - The creators had "no concrete obligation". They were just asked for feedback, answers to questions and retweets of launches.
  - He says dropping the obligation unblocked everyone, and in practice they all did what was asked.
- He says this kind of move is easy when you're small, while a big company might take 18 months and many legal reviews.
- He says affiliate and referral programs are hard to launch before you are successful, because creators have hundreds of tools to promote and pick known ones.
- They built in public and shared numbers and weekly updates showing a healthy, steady growth curve. He says this built trust and made creators want to join.

## Growth channels: SEO via mini tools and social

- Tweet Hunter and Taplio have two main acquisition channels: SEO and social.
- The SEO channel is the mini-tool strategy. They extracted features as free, single-page, SEO-optimized tools.
  - Example: a LinkedIn stats checker, where searching a person's LinkedIn stats can land on their tool, which then pushes Taplio.
  - The LinkedIn video downloader gets about 1 million visits per month, as he recalls.
- They have about 15 mini tools on Taplio and about 15 on Tweet Hunter, built at a pace of roughly one every two weeks. Traffic accumulates as Google surfaces them.
- The social channel is people talking about them on LinkedIn and Twitter. Often there are no links, so many people search the brand name on Google, and he can't always tell where users came from.
- Context he gave: ARR reached 3 million in March and 5 million in June of a given year (recalled from his 2023 review); he said he didn't remember exactly what drove it.

## Team split and partnership

- Tibo and Thomas split equity 50/50.
- Thomas is a pure product marketer. He handled acquisition (ads, SEO, landing pages), copy, brand text, product direction, design and wireframes.
- Tibo handled product, mockups and implementation, plus distribution, since he had the Twitter and LinkedIn audience.
- He says coding and writing positioning copy use totally different parts of the brain, so splitting the roles helps. As a dev, doing both is very hard, so he suggests compartmenting days (marketing one day, dev the next).
- Now that he launches projects without Thomas, he struggles on positioning and the deep pain point, and he calls Thomas "much better" at that. His larger audience makes distribution easier, though.
- His view: "it's hard to be on every front".

## Selling Tweet Hunter and Taplio

- The initial plan with Thomas was to build a project to 10k MRR, sell it for $500k to $1M, and repeat. The product grew much faster and sold for much more.
- They hired a US broker and spoke with big US and Indian companies, including HubSpot. It was hard because:
  - they were small (around 15M ARR at the time);
  - churn was high and KPIs looked B2C even though the product was somewhat B2B;
  - they had only 12 months of track record.
- Acquirers also talked about being locked in for 2 years inside their culture.
- Tibo meanwhile had lunch with Guillaume Moubeche of Lemlist, who was already a Taplio customer. He had asked for 17 licenses so his whole team would post on LinkedIn.
- Lemlist had similar growth, a similar company size and a similar trajectory, so they chose it over a US buyer. They signed in summer 2022 and negotiated the terms and price in a WhatsApp group of three with Thomas.
- About 80% of the price was tied to growth milestones over the two years after the acquisition. That reduced risk for the buyer given high churn and acquisition dependence.
- Tibo insisted on keeping control of the products: "if you tie the whole value of the acquisition to milestones... you have to leave us the reins".
- Results: ARR was just over 15M at the sale and above 8M reported later in the conversation (he said they passed 8 million ARR, but the figure is ambiguous with the 15M mention, so treat with care). He expected to land just under the 100M ARR target; this figure is unclear as stated.
- Reasons to sell:
  - it was the plan from the start;
  - growth was so fast they needed help from people who had gone through similar growth, and Lemlist had;
  - they wanted to de-risk, having taken little cash relative to the company's value.
- Risks he named: platform dependency (Elon Musk's Twitter takeover) and Taplio not being fully compliant with LinkedIn's rules.

## Platform risk: the Twitter API price hike and LinkedIn

- Twitter's API was free and then priced at $42,000 per month. After they asked to pay, access was cut off 3 days later (not 30), and support was gone after mass layoffs.
- About 48 hours later they found a contact, filled in the right form and started paying $42,000 per month, which they still do by manual transfer.
- Tibo says they could afford it, but he would never have built Tweet Hunter if he'd had to pay that from the start. He thinks it killed small players, and no new competitor has appeared in a year. He still finds the rule change "disgusting".
- On LinkedIn, the API is restrictive (no access to views or likes, no carousels, no mentions). They use a Chrome extension only for those gaps, with moderate usage, a nearby proxy per user and secure handling to protect user accounts. He contrasts this with heavier extensions like Waalaxy.
- He says they can adapt if LinkedIn changes things.

## Current projects and one-person company ambition

- Tibo is working on Typefully-style video tools: TypeFrame (acquired) and Revid.ai, a spinoff.
- The idea is to apply the Tweet Hunter playbook to video: find viral videos, pull script pieces, repurpose content and let people without editing skills create videos for TikTok or YouTube Shorts.
- Revid reached more than $9,000 MRR. He has hired one full-time developer for the video editor, the hardest part.
- Tibo handles the search engine, transcription and AI generation parts, plus the landing page and blog.
- His longer-term ambition: a multi-product strategy with several products for the same audience (creators), promoted by sharing on Twitter and LinkedIn. He references Sam Altman's prediction of a one-person billion-dollar company, but says he is not that ambitious.
