## Why Tibo builds: the early stage of a new product

- He compares building to "playing Lego" at five years old. Money is not the driver; the best feeling is when people use something he built.
- He craves the earliest stage of a product, a mix of excitement and fear of not finding product-market fit. Once you have lived it, he says, "you desperately want to live it again."
- He uses that pressure as fuel. It pushes him to ship daily, change how people use the product, and look for new growth vectors.
- He recommends the book *It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work* as a counterpoint to that culture, while running his own projects the opposite way.

## Why he and his co-founder sold Tweet Hunter and Taplio

- The two products sold for over $10M, to Lempire (the company behind Lemlist).
- His co-founder wanted to sell more than he did. Tibo thought they could go much further.
- Platform dependence drove the decision. Tweet Hunter relied on Twitter, and Taplio relies on LinkedIn, plus a Chrome extension that pulls data beyond the public API.
- The SaaS was skyrocketing, but "we were definitely not getting a big money from the SaaS." Everything could collapse and they would get almost nothing from the success.
- He says the sale looked right once Twitter cut off API access, because it happened after the sale.

## Platform risk: the Twitter API cutoff and Chrome-extension products

- In March 2023 Twitter announced developers would need to pay $42,000 per month for API access. It gave 30 days to transition and a form to fill in. Four days later their API access was cut with no warning.
- A few competitors were hit too. Tibo's team worked with them to find the right person at Twitter. One competitor tried to profit by trashing them publicly and grabbing their subscribers, which did not really work.
- After roughly 48 hours they reached the right contact, signed contracts fast, and got access back.
- Churn was under 1%. "It was more than an awful feeling than the actual truth."
- During the outage there was nothing to do except watch churn metrics. Because he builds in public, he "becomes the SaaS" and takes platform bugs personally.
- On Chrome extensions: he calls them a goldmine for indie makers. Big companies cannot allow teams to do "shady" things with private APIs, so solo founders face little competition there.
- His ethical line: does the product put the user's account at risk? For Taplio the answer is no, so he continues.

## Selling: why a French buyer, and how the deal closed

- Tibo and his co-founder Tom looked at international buyers. They expected a US buyer to pay more.
- Both products always had high churn. Large American players feared that churn; Lempire's founder Guillaume did not.
- The buyer was Guillaume, a middle-school classmate. Tibo had looked up to Lemlist as a model of personal branding and building in public, and reached out with a tweet asking if he wanted to grab a coffee.
- They met for lunch about three times to talk products before any sale talk. When Tibo and Tom wanted to sell, he mentioned it to Guillaume.
- They settled all the details over WhatsApp in about two weeks. Same culture, age, country and language was a clear signal for him.
- Staying in control mattered. They talked to big players like HubSpot but disliked becoming a small piece of a large company. Tibo and Tom kept running the business after the sale.

## How revenue tripled after the acquisition

- At acquisition the products were below $2M in annual recurring revenue. About a year and a half later, he says they are nearing $6-7M.
- He says he does not have one exact answer. The strategy with Tom is to do more: ship more features, SEO content and social media content. Many efforts fade, but a few succeed in a big way.
- The standout is a yearly LinkedIn and Twitter growth challenge, run January to March each year (third year at the time). They did not expect it to work that much.
- The pitch is that people tell themselves "this year is going to be the year" they get serious about Twitter or LinkedIn. The challenge motivates them daily with rankings and a leaderboard, and brings in new subscribers.
- He also ships new products and free mini tools to bring in revenue.
- The team is about 8 to 10 people and kept lean on purpose. This lets them jump on an idea and make it live "instead of going through three levels of validation."

## Building stable software with a tiny team

- He is pro new tech (Next.js, Vercel), not Pieter Levels' PHP approach. Vercel-style tooling lets you update code and get warnings when something breaks.
- He used to be CTO at a scale-up managing 30 developers, where deploys and devops regularly pulled the whole production team in. He says that overhead is now "basically non existent," freeing developers to focus on what matters and sit closer to the end user.
- He says one developer can now do the work that used to take around 10.
- AI-based products like Tweet Hunter and Taplio deliver value through many small, mostly independent tools. That creates less technical complexity and fewer bugs.

## AI risks and de-risking for indie makers

- He is bullish on the AI opportunity. He sees two practical risks for indie makers today:
  - OpenAI going down, so your product stops working.
  - A user finding a loophole and generating thousands or millions of outputs, leaving you with a bill of around 1,000 euros.
- To de-risk, he does not want to run his own servers. He has looked at Mistral through another provider and at OpenRouter, which "can definitely save your ass" when OpenAI is down.

## Acquiring Typeframes and running a side project

- Over three years Tweet Hunter and Taplio acquired about 10 tiny products. Each was a day to a month of an indie maker's work, and they were used to drive traffic to the main products.
- Typeframes was launched on Twitter by its founder Liam, made a lot of noise, then stalled when Liam stopped working on it.
- Tibo reached out and, drawing on those small acquisitions, structured a deal. He did not want to pay the full valuation up front, so Liam received a revenue share on what Typeframes generates over the coming months or years.
- The deal closed in September. Typeframes was not subscription-based; he switched it to subscriptions. From September to December it went from zero to $4K in monthly recurring revenue.
- It is a side project for him, since his main focus is Tweet Hunter and Taplio. He used acquisition proceeds to hire a developer whose main focus is Typeframes.
- His bet: Twitter and LinkedIn content is moving from text toward video and media, so a video product fits that shift.

## Building in public, content, and support through DMs

- On video: do not overthink it. You may not need a script or editing; screen record, mess around with your own tool and publish.
- "Quantity leads to quality." Waiting for the perfect setup means 90% never start. Ship messy, be okay with the shame, use it to do better next time, and never miss a day or a week.
- Early content shows your progress in public, which he values.
- He credits real-time support as a game changer compared with his two earlier failed startups. The support link at the bottom of the page sent people to his Twitter DMs.
- He checked DMs all day, saw feedback and often shipped a fix in about five minutes. Users watching were impressed and the product improved from real feedback.
- With no audience yet, you are reachable, which is an advantage that bigger accounts like Danny Postma and Pieter Levels no longer have. He says he can no longer do this at his current size.

## Nomad life and community

- He lived six months in Thailand (met Pieter Levels) and spent time in Bali (met Danny Postma), but is now in France with family and a new kid on the way.
- In Bali a group of 10 to 30 indie hackers coded from 9 to 4 on their own projects, then ran demos with feedback from 4 to 5. He says indie hacking can be lonely, and a small idea from someone else can change your perspective.
- He finds Paris less open to new people than Bali, where he got about 30 invitations in the first week.
- He shares his journey on Twitter as Tibo, answers DMs as best he can, and runs a newsletter at tibo.beehiiv.com.
