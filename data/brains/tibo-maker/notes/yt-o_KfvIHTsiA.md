## Validating ideas: revenue is the only signal

- Tibo defines validation narrowly: "validation is about people paying for the product." Free users and people saying nice things don't count.
- His reasoning: people don't want to hurt your feelings, so they give positive feedback. If they aren't buying, you may not have product-market fit.
- Early on he raised VC money and built two failed startups. He wanted to avoid a third two-year failure, so he looked for ways to reach revenue fast.
- He charges from the start, even for betas. He gets angry comments such as "why should I pay for a beta?" and says that is part of the game; you ignore the haters.
- He rejected the idea that having a team signals success. His first startups had staff and VC money but no revenue. His current business is much more successful, yet he has a harder time explaining it to friends without saying "I have a team of 10."

## Shipping a product a week until one sticks

- With his co-founder he shipped 11 products in about 11 weeks, one a week, to shorten the time to product-market fit. They kept going until something stuck, and Tweet Hunter was the one that did.
- It was not random. They picked one target (creators and solopreneurs) and one problem (how to sell your products/grow an audience).
- They were their own users, so they could tell whether each product was useful. Every launch also deepened their understanding of the audience and grew their following, so each later launch was easier.
- Tweet Hunter's path:
  - Tweet B: GPT-3 tweet generator. It didn't work because GPT-3 wasn't mature enough.
  - A tweet digest analytics email: about $300 monthly revenue, not enough.
  - Tweet Hunter: a wall of old viral tweets (3 to 6 months old), clustered and categorized with GPT-3, as an inspiration layer for writing. It worked from day one.
- Insight behind it: content recipes that worked before keep working, so you find a new way to talk about the same ideas.
- He now tries, at least once a week, to come up with an idea in the morning and ship it by end of day. This came from a daily "hackathon" group in Barcelona: introduce yourself at 9, work all day, demo at 4.
- His view: about 80% of what you ship won't work, but the 20% that does might never have been built otherwise. "Quantity beats quality" because you can't predict what takes off. He suggests carving out about 20% of your time for new shots.
- He cites that most A/B tests fail anyway, so more shots on goal beats heavy upfront planning.

## Influencer distribution deal: 25% of the product's profit

- Tweet Hunter had a small following, so Tibo and his co-founder gave an influencer (JK) a distribution deal. In the transcript the share is described as 25%, and Tibo says they started at 30% ("not 25").
- Structure: it was a deal on the product, not the company. JK got 25% of monthly profit and 25% of any exit tied to that product. In return he owned marketing and had to actively promote it with campaigns and a referral program.
- They did the same for the LinkedIn product Taplio, scoping the deal to that product only, and found new partners for it.
- Results: they announced the partnership and launched together in September 2021. Monthly recurring revenue went from $3K to $20K in a few weeks. The goal for the whole year had been $10K, and they were at about $1K MRR when the talks began.
- Trust mechanics:
  - Full transparency: bank statements and Stripe access were shared.
  - Founders' salaries were not counted as costs.
  - The only way to manipulate profit would be to overpay contractors, which transparency made hard.
- His point: big influencers don't make as much money as people assume and want their own products. JK was around 21 and had no significant revenue, so the deal worked for both sides.
- For the exit, value was split by each product's revenue share. Tweet Hunter was about 80% of revenue; Taplio was 3 to 4 months old at the sale.

## Mini free tools as an SEO and backlink engine

- Tibo launched many small, single-purpose free tools (tweet generator, bio generator, LinkedIn video downloader), each unbundled from a feature of the main product. Most launched on Product Hunt.
- Product Hunt gives a one-time spike, but the backlinks and Google rankings compound over time and become a long-term acquisition channel.
- Example: the Taplio LinkedIn video downloader generates about 1 million visits a month. He says it isn't closely tied to Taplio's main use case, but some visitors convert. It started as a test to evaluate a developer he was hiring. A developer built it, they shipped it, and about two months later it ranked first, with no launch and no strategy.
- He has shipped a tool in one or two days without telling anyone at least five times; sometimes one is an outlier. He credits being action-oriented and not weighing effort versus opportunity.
- He calls Product Hunt's maker of the year recognition a result of launching 10 to 15 times in a year, not of the two main products. He says he didn't capitalize on it.
- He is replicating this on every product, including his current project. He notes Vid.io-style variants are even easier: many "tools" that are just landing pages redirecting to one app with different entry points.

## Pricing, churn and trials

- Taplio/Tweet Hunter monthly churn stayed between 10% and 15% for three years. He attributes it to a volatile audience: creators who decide to become a LinkedIn influencer, then switch to Instagram two weeks later.
- After three years he accepted churn couldn't be fixed and focused instead on activation and acquisition.
- Tibo went through about 10 price increases on Tweet Hunter. The biggest was 20 to 25%, and every one went well. They never lowered prices.
- Raising prices actually lowered churn, because it filtered out low-commitment, lower-quality users.
- He argues cutting price in half would need twice the subscribers or half the churn, which he doesn't think is achievable.
- They offered 7-day free trials with the credit card required up front.
- He was irritated by Beehiiv raising his price from $100 to $250 a month (2.5x for the same features), contrasting it with his own max of about 25% increases.

## Acquisition, earn-out and platform risk

- Tweet Hunter and Taplio were sold to Lemlist in 2022 for up to $10M. Structure: about 10% upfront, about 80% as earn-outs over two years tied to six revenue milestones. They hit five of six and received about $8M.
- Reason for the structure: platform risk. The company was barely a year old, Elon Musk had just taken over Twitter, and Taplio wasn't fully compliant with LinkedIn rules.
- Post-sale, revenue grew from $1.5M to $8M ARR over two years with a team of about 7 growing to about 10 (3 or 4 developers) plus some agencies. Growth came from acquisition, not churn fixes. Tibo says Taplio drove growth for all of Lemlist while Lemlist was stagnating.
- They stayed independent: no meetings, no integration pressure, because they hit their goals. Tibo says an earn-out is not a relief: he worked "like crazy" for two years.
- Twitter API episode: Twitter announced the free API would become a $42K/month plan with 30 days notice, but cut access after 3 days of the Enterprise form being submitted. Few Twitter staff were reachable. Another tool-builder shared a key account manager's email, and that is how Tweet Hunter ended up paying $42K a month.
- LinkedIn: analytics aren't available via the API, so Taplio's Chrome extension reads them from the page HTML, which breaks LinkedIn's rules. About 6 months after leaving, Tibo's personal LinkedIn account was suspended. He says other tools do the same.

## Buying products, then forking and focusing on distribution

- His thesis: the hardest part of startups is reaching product-market fit, so he buys small SaaS products that exist and focuses on growth.
- Typeframe (product videos) was bought for "tens of thousands." He forked it into Revid (short videos) and found a way to make it about 10x more profitable. He ran both for six months, then killed Typeframe and redirected its traffic to Revid. He says he did it a bit late and has never regretted killing a product.
- Feather (a Notion-to-blog tool) was bought for $250K. It grows slowly, has very low churn and is low maintenance.
- He then built newsletter features in Feather after the Beehiiv price rise. He ran 20% of his subscribers on Feather alongside 80% on Beehiiv to compare open and reply rates. Deliverability was much harder than expected. Beehiiv closed his accounts after his public criticism and what they called reverse engineering, so he moved fully to Feather.
- Worry and relief: when Notion launched Notion Sites in June he feared his $250K investment was worthless, but it increased attention on Notion-to-site tools.
- AI thesis: as AI makes software creation cost close to zero, the value shifts to distribution and integrations. He expects extremely niche software (for example a Notion for lawyers or accountants) and says distribution is "monetizable right away."

## Building in public and getting started with AI

- Why build in public: it builds authority and personal attachment, and makes it easier to sell any product later. A small community gives feedback and buys early. Downside: copycats, who copy successful products and cut prices.
- He shares revenue milestones less now because it attracts copycats.
- Advice to non-coders (PMs):
  - Start a public account (Twitter/LinkedIn), document your goals and show progress daily, so social commitment creates pressure.
  - Use ChatGPT to learn Cursor, then build in Cursor with AI. Paste errors back to the AI.
  - Pick something fun, such as a project for your kid or a passion, because the first product probably won't work.
  - Don't spend hours on tutorials. Learning sticks when you hit the problem. Deploy on a platform like Vercel for free.
  - Show your work even if it is bad. Building one thing generates 10 more ideas. Then repeat product after product until something takes off.
  - Tech stack matters much less than starting; pick what's trendy. One or two hours a day gets you somewhere in a few months. No-code tools like Bubble also exist.

## Product and team management

- Prioritization: he builds what makes his own life easier as a user. Backlog is only 2 to 3 weeks of work, because a long backlog is discouraging. He discards most feedback and only adds a request after hearing it two or three times.
- He doesn't heavily A/B test: he runs one or two tests at a time and doesn't optimize his landing page. One test on Revid showed that giving new users more free credits increased conversion, the opposite of his bet.
- Team: no meetings, no retros. He once promoted story points and sprints but now sees them as unnecessary. He works Founder Mode style, hands-on, with a small team of autonomous people. He says every question to him takes his time and the person's job is to take a topic fully off his plate.
- On PM roles: he believes product owner and product manager should be the same person, and that builders find the specs' mistakes by building, so detailed specs are less valuable.
- Time: 2 to 3 hours of deep work in the morning, then messaging, Twitter and other tasks. He tackles the big hard problems quickly instead of procrastinating.
