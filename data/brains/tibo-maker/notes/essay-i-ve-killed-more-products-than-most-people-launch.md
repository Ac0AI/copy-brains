## Why Tibo kills products: recurring use beats one-time value

- Tibo says people only see his wins (Revid.ai at $600k MRR, Outrank.so at $300k MRR, SuperX.so at $25k MRR), never the 7 products he killed. He frames each as a datapoint: "Every 'failed' product made my next product better."
- ProductLift was a search engine for communities where people post when they launch. It got a few sales, then nothing. People only launch once, maybe twice if they pivot.
- His rule: "You can't build a recurring business on something people need once." If the problem comes up once, "you don't have a SaaS."
- He now builds only tools used daily or weekly. Examples: Outrank's backlink checker (used every week), Revid's video editor (used for every piece of content), SuperX's AI features (used daily).
- "Recurring revenue = recurring value."

## Monthly subscriptions need high-frequency use cases (AnimStats)

- AnimStats made short animations from stats. It reached $1k MRR quickly, then stagnated because churn was brutal.
- People make stat animations about once per quarter, "maybe". Users subscribed, created 3 animations, cancelled, and came back months later.
- It was the opposite problem from ProductLift (used once ever); once per quarter still doesn't justify a monthly subscription.
- "High churn kills growth. You're running on a treadmill, replacing churned users instead of growing."
- Takeaway: only build products with daily or weekly use cases.

## Timing: some ideas are just too early (Tweet Butler)

- Tweet Butler generated tweets with GPT-3 in 2021. The outputs were robotic and generic, and nobody wanted to post them. He killed it in 2021.
- By 2022, GPT-4 was good enough that the AI feature in Tweet Hunter became one of the most used parts of the product. He later sold Tweet Hunter for $8m.
- "Timing is everything." Some ideas are "2 years too early. That doesn't make them bad ideas. It makes them bad ideas for right now."
- He credits SuperX working partly because he learned to wait for the technology to be ready.
- His test for a struggling project: is it a bad idea or bad timing? Sometimes the answer is "kill it", sometimes "wait 6 months and try again". "Knowing the difference is the entire game."

## A feature is not a product, and custom work is not a product

- Tweet Butler Digest delivered AI-powered Twitter analytics by daily email. It hit $300 MRR quickly, then stalled. People wanted a full dashboard to dive into, not analytics in their inbox.
- He notes $300 MRR felt like validation but wasn't: people tried it because it was cheap. "A feature pretending to be a product will always feel incomplete." Products must be complete solutions.
- ConvertRec made GIFs and short videos for product launches. He "accidentally made an agency": every client needed custom work, and AI wasn't good enough to automate it.
- He dislikes running agencies: you trade time for money, can't scale without hiring, and every client needs hand holding.
- "If you have to do custom work for each customer, you don't have a product." And: "If a product can't work without me, it's not a product, it's a service."

## Customer type matters more than revenue (GeoLeads)

- GeoLeads scraped geo-based leads from Google Maps. It reached $1k MRR, but the best customers were enterprise clients who needed daily communication and lots of calls, which he hates.
- "$1k MRR from the wrong customers is worse than $0 MRR." He killed the product because it attracted customers he didn't want to serve.
- This changed his positioning: "My products are self-service or nothing."
- He credits Outrank.so working partly because he learned to avoid enterprise clients.

## Unit economics from day one (Daily Nuggets)

- Daily Nuggets was personalized daily news tailored to your interests. Traction was low and costs too high: AI, data and delivery costs make personalized news expensive infrastructure.
- He also realized people want the take of someone they see as an expert. That is authority, and "AI doesn't have authority."
- OpenAI later built essentially this exact product into ChatGPT. His lesson: "being right about an idea doesn't matter if you can't make the economics work." Sometimes you're right but too small to execute.
- "Unit economics matter from day one."

## Launch fast, kill fast: cheap education

- He killed these products in weeks or months, not years. Each cost him less than $5k and a few weeks of time. He contrasts this with founders who spend 2 years building something nobody wants.
- His summary: "Launch fast. Kill fast. Learn fast." "Fast failures are cheap education."
- Revid.ai works because he learned recurring use matters; Outrank.so because he learned to avoid enterprise clients; SuperX.so because he learned to wait for the technology.

## Why he still writes a weekly newsletter

- Responding to a claim that nobody making $1M/month would write newsletters, he says he still shows up weekly because of the trust it builds and the support from readers.
- He cites DMs from readers: one closed a first SaaS client using his DM templates, one killed a bad idea after reading about validation (saving 6 months), and three started building their first product after seeing it's possible.
- He asks readers to reply with one thing they got from the newsletter, reads every reply, and often writes back. He calls this "not something you can automate".
