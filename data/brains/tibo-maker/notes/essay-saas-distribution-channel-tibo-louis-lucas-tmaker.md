## Distribution is the moat when AI makes building cheap

- Tibo says that with AI making building so easy, "the success of products is going to be based mostly on distribution." He moved into the distribution space deliberately.
- At TMAKER he is "the distribution guy" on every product and partners with a co-maker who builds. Product decisions are shared between him and the developer co-maker.
- He builds reusable systems: an ads pipeline, an SEO playbook, an influencer network. Every product benefits from the same systems, which he finds easier than building products.
- When you are the builder, he says it is harder to work on multiple things. The distribution role has a higher ceiling on how many products you can run.
- TMAKER is a holding company with five live projects. A few weeks before the conversation it crossed $1M per month (about $12M ARR), with a team of 10 and no outside funding.
- Revid (acquired as the small tool TypeFrame) does more than $600,000 per month, "insane compared to how much I paid for it."

## Ship many products and validate with revenue only

- After two failed startups, Tibo and co-founder Tom wanted strong signals fast and wanted to avoid "working again two years on a product" that goes nowhere.
- Plan: pick a niche and a positioning (founders and creators, riding the creator economy boom), then build one product per week until something sticks.
- The only metric was revenue: would people put in a credit card? His earlier mistake was treating weekly active users or downloads as success.
- Strongest signals: people paying for a second month, and new signups or subscriptions arriving on days he does nothing (no tweeting, no Reddit posts). That means someone returned to an old email or told a friend.
- Example kill: a search engine for communities (Slack, Discord, Telegram groups) to launch in. It earned revenue but people mostly launch once, so retention was weak and they moved on.
- He holds a high bar for product-market fit, so even products that made some money were dropped.

## How he builds a product in a week

- He reuses everything: one backend for all products, recycled databases and components, "spaghetti code" optimized for fast shipping.
- Often a full product was not built first. They shipped a landing page with a request-access form and counted email addresses. If the count was high, they built the product.
- Each product refined the process of creating and validating the next one.
- Having built many products gives a baseline. Founders with one product cannot tell whether their traction is average, high or low.

## Spotting real demand and product-market fit

- Core test: when people truly want something, "they chase you, not the other way around." If you must convince people, follow up and handle objections, it is weak. If they DM asking "is this ready? When can I try it?", it is real.
- His job is to process many conversations and spot outliers, the product that gets more attention than the baseline.
- Method: tweet a lot, see what resonates, ship MVPs to real people, and watch whether they try it once or keep engaging and asking for features.
- Tweet Hunter signals: he built it for himself and used it daily, his co-founder did too, and a flow of signups arrived that "started to get overwhelming." True PMF is "when you get so much demand that it's getting hard to serve" (DMs, feature requests).
- It reached about $1K MRR in about three weeks, on a smooth curve.
- He says many founders stall below $1K a month without real PMF; passing it means you ship real value, after which you copy systems between apps.
- Advice: "build for me in the first place." If you are the user and find it cool, many similar people will too.

## The JK Molina equity deal and the LinkedIn fork

- Tweet Hunter was around $3K monthly revenue when Twitter influencer JK Molina launched it to his audience and email newsletter. Revenue went to $20K in 2 to 3 weeks.
- His payment: up to 25% of the product, structured like a profit share plus an exit share. He received 25% of monthly profit (which grew, as they were bootstrapped and profit-driven) and 25% of any acquisition.
- Contract treated him as a co-founder with co-founder duties. If he did not fulfil them he could lose all his shares (it never happened). He kept running promotion campaigns monthly and helped shape the product description.
- Product-led growth then took over because users tweeted about the product.
- Taplio: because JK would get 25% of any LinkedIn revenue if it lived inside Tweet Hunter, they forked a separate LinkedIn product and hunted a LinkedIn influencer (Alex) for the same deal. He also wanted single-platform focus against generalist tools like Later and Buffer, with deeper features.
- Both influencer deals ended when the company was acquired, and the influencers took 25% of the upfront payment.

## The exit and why he regrets it

- Sold Tweet Hunter and Taplio to Lempire: $2M upfront, up to $10M via six milestones. They hit 5 of 6, so the deal ended at $8M.
- Revenue was about $1.5M annual at sale and $8M annual at the end of the earnout, so he says he "sold an 8 million company for 8 million."
- He structured independence (no boss, control of all product decisions), so teams stayed separate, even with separate Slacks. He believes they could have done it alone, though Lempire's money let them take bigger acquisition and marketing risks.
- After the earnout he felt a "big void." Being recognized as a successful founder makes it hard to show new work publicly, for fear of becoming the founder who shipped something bad. "No one cares," but you feel it.
- He says you cannot stop building if you are a true builder. He started Revid before fully leaving Lempire.

## SEO as the most durable channel

- Tibo ramps SEO "very fast and very aggressively." It captures the highest-intent leads: people searching for a solution to a problem they have.
- It lasts. Three years after selling Tweet Hunter, the free tools he built there still rank.
- Outrank (the SEO product) grew a lot in the last year. His claim: LLMs get information from training data (the web) or search queries, so ranking and being mentioned in top blog posts is what gets a product cited by ChatGPT, Perplexity and Gemini. "You need to have an amazing SEO to be mentioned by LLMs."

## Focus, ego and early mistakes

- Early mistake: his first two startups were ego-driven. He raised 200K (first round) without validating the idea and had no recurring revenue. Easy funding made him confident in the idea and less willing to pivot.
- Lesson: you must pivot many times to find PMF, so remove friction (VC, big teams). A one or two person team pivots easily.
- On focus: he disagrees with "be focused" on a single product. Building more gives more references for what a good product is.
- He works with five agents in five windows, switching context every two minutes, and ends days burned out. His focus is on the same audience, acquisition channels and core need: helping small business owners make money.
- He calls ego your worst enemy: "doing things because it's going to make you look more important."
