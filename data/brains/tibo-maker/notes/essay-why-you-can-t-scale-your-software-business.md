## Pricing: one band, decided once, never revisited early

- Tibo prices every product he builds in the same $40 to $100 per month range. He made this decision years ago and hasn't revisited it.
- That band attracts prosumers, "People with business intent." If the product helps their workflow they buy, with no negotiation or hand-holding.
- He argues this single decision drives the rest: subscription funnel design, account handling, team invitations, product complexity, support expectations and churn tolerance.
- Price bands map to customer types:
  - $10/month attracts consumers who "churn on vibes" and cancel because they "don't use it enough," even if it works perfectly.
  - $50/month attracts prosumers who need ROI. They stay if you deliver value and leave if you don't.
  - $500/month attracts businesses that expect personalized support, deep integration and implementation calls.
- Pick the band based on who you want to serve, then build everything around that customer type.
- Don't revisit pricing until $100k MRR or unless something big happens. Before that it's a distraction.
- Tibo says most founders spend 10 hours a month thinking about pricing and zero on whether they're solving the right problem for the right customer.
- His rule of thumb: spending more than 1 hour a month on pricing before $100k MRR means optimizing the wrong thing. Price it, ship it, see if people buy. Adjust only if nobody buys or everyone churns immediately.

## Decide foundations once, optimize execution daily

- Tibo runs 5 products at a combined $1M MRR. His summary: "Foundations: once. Execution: daily."
- Foundations to pick once and keep consistent across products: customer type, pricing band, tech stack, funnel design, positioning.
- He says most decisions don't need to be unique per product, and optimization belongs at the execution layer (growth, product quality, systems), not the foundation layer.
- Founders stay stuck at under $5k/mo for years because they tweak foundations constantly (new pricing, new positioning, new stack) while growth stays flat, the product has bugs and support is manual. Tibo's framing: they start from zero every few weeks.
- Typical distractions he lists: tweaking pricing monthly, redesigning dashboards, chasing competitor features, switching tools.

## Build systems instead of doing tasks

- Tibo says he doesn't do tasks anymore, he builds systems. The shift: stop asking "how do I do this task?" and ask "how do I make this task run without me?"
- His test for every task: "Will I need to do this again?" If yes, build a system (agent, hire or automation, "doesn't matter"). If no, do it manually once and move on.
- Examples he gives:
  - Instead of launching one campaign, build a campaign workflow and skill you can deploy on Claude any day.
  - Instead of manually onboarding one user, build an email-based onboarding flow that works for every user.
  - Instead of answering the same support question 50 times, write a mega doc and put an AI chatbot on the site.
- Repeatable tasks to systematize now:
  - Weekly metrics review becomes an automated dashboard.
  - Customer onboarding becomes an email sequence plus setup videos.
  - Feature requests become a public roadmap with voting.
  - Social posting becomes scheduled posts with templates.
  - Support becomes help docs plus an AI chatbot.
- Build these before you think you need to, because by the time you "need" to, "you're already drowning."
- This is how he runs 5 products: "I'm not doing 5x the work. I'm building 5x the systems." He says it matters even more for single-product founders, since "You can't scale if everything requires you."

## Use your own product (dogfooding)

- Tibo uses every product he builds, multiple times a day when possible and at least once a week.
- He uses Revid.ai to create videos, Outrank.so for SEO content and PostSyncer.com to schedule posts. Each time he finds something to improve, and that becomes the roadmap.
- Benefits: spot problems before users complain, see opportunities competitors miss, and build what people need rather than what sounds good on the landing page.
- If you don't use your own product, either you're not your ICP (fix positioning) or the product isn't good enough yet (fix the product).
- The test question: "Would I pay for this if it wasn't mine?" If no, you have a problem.
- He calls dogfooding "not optional" and the difference between building features and building solutions.

## What Tibo optimizes every day

- **Bottlenecks:** ask what's preventing growth right now, which is "the only thing that matters." Identify it, fix it, ignore the rest. Example: Outrank.so's bottleneck last month was content quality, since users said it sounded too robotic. The team fixed it and shipped an update this month that he says makes the SEO content at least 10x better.
- **User behavior:** compare what people do against what he expected, and ask why there's a gap. He watches PostHog session replays daily to see where users get stuck and where they succeed, and values what they actually do over what they say they do.
- **Systems breaking:** if something that used to run autonomously now needs manual intervention, treat it as a red flag, patch it immediately and don't let it grow.
- **New use cases:** watch how people use the product in ways he didn't anticipate, and see whether it can be generalized into a feature everyone can use.
