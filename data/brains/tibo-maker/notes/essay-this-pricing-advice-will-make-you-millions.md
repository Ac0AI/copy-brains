## Why Tibo requires a credit card before the free trial

- Tibo's products usually ask for a credit card before anyone can try the free tier. Users enter a card, start a trial, and can cancel anytime.
- He knows people tell him he is "leaving money on the table", since the industry standard is to let people try without a card. His answer: "They're probably right - but I don't care."
- Without a card gate, users sign up, click around for a few minutes and leave. Before leaving, they send support emails about things already answered in the docs. At 500 free signups a week, support gets swamped by people who were never going to pay.
- Most of his products are run by two people (him and a co-maker). A few have three to four, and none have more. A small team cannot absorb that flood.
- A card creates commitment. People who enter one are serious, so they set the product up and give it a real shot. He says this brings higher-quality feedback, higher-quality users and a higher trial-to-paid conversion rate.
- The trade-off is lower volume, which he accepts: "I'd rather have 100 serious trials than 1,000 people who sign up and ghost."
- Card-gating works for B2B products that need setup time, complex tools that need configuration, and small teams that can't scale support.
- It does not work for viral consumer apps where volume is the game, simple tools with nothing to configure, or large teams with dedicated support infrastructure. He says to know which one you are before changing your model.

## Free tier: customer acquisition cost or charity

- Tibo reframes the question from "should we have a free tier?" to "what job is the free tier doing?"
- If the free tier builds word-of-mouth and filters for people who will upgrade later, it is a customer acquisition cost. You pay in server costs, support time and feature work to find buyers.
- If it only serves people who will never pay, it is charity.
- AI products make this sharper. In 2019 a generous SaaS free tier cost almost nothing. In 2026 every free interaction with an AI product costs real money through API calls.
- He says this is why many AI tools deliberately limit their free tiers: they avoid subsidizing infinite usage from people who will never pay.
- His test: does the free tier bring people who eventually pay, or just people looking for free stuff? If you can't answer with actual data, "you don't have a freemium strategy. You have a free product with some paying customers on the side."

## A/B testing as a pricing and conversion habit

- Tibo says he runs hundreds of experiments, "because guessing what to do in business is really expensive". He notes everyone has heard of A/B testing and almost nobody does it.
- Mechanism: each new signup is assigned a variable. If it is 1 they see landing page A, if 2 they see landing page B, and so on. Then you track the success metric and see which variant wins.
- Example from Revid.ai: the export button is shown in several ways (bigger, different copy, higher on the page). The metric is exports per session, and the variant with the most exports wins.
- Things to test:
  - Landing page headlines
  - Pricing display, such as monthly vs annual first
  - Feature prominence in the dashboard
  - Onboarding flows (number of steps, order)
  - CTA copy ("Start free trial" vs "Try it now" vs "Get started")
- You don't need a big system. He uses PostHog and Amplitude, and focuses on one metric at a time.
- His loop: "Pick the metric. Test the thing. Ship the winner. Move to the next test."
- His closing principle: keep testing new things, because that is what leads to compounded growth.

## Charge more: $99/month beats $9/month for small B2B teams

- Tibo's "semi-controversial" take is that more indie hackers and small teams should make their apps fully paid and charge more, not less.
- Typical failure: a founder launches at $9/month because they fear nobody will pay $49. They get 200 users at $9, spend all their time on support, and can't afford help.
- The alternative: launching at $99/month, getting 20 users, making similar revenue, and having time to improve the product.
- Charging more forces a better product. At $99/month customers expect it to work, which pushes you to ship features that matter, fix bugs immediately and care about performance. In his words: "$9/month lets you get lazy. $99/month doesn't let you hide."
- Freemium creates a massive support load because every free user has questions and expects features. A team of 1-3 people can't handle that.
- His advice: make it paid, and expensive enough that only people who need it will pay.
- For business software, he says the $40 to $100 per month range works well. Buyers at that price expect ROI, and they stay if the product delivers and leave if not, which he calls "a clean signal".
- This does not apply to consumer apps or tools for teenagers, which are a different game of lower prices and higher volume.
- His conclusion for B2B SaaS: "$99/month beats $9/month every time."

## Price for your own constraints, not other companies' constraints

- Tibo warns against copying what works for other companies, because they have different constraints.
- His rule: "Build for the constraints you have, not the ones you wish you had."
- Team size, support capacity, product complexity and marginal cost per user (especially for AI products) should decide whether you use a credit-card gate, a free tier, or a high-priced paid-only model.
- The one non-negotiable across all pricing models is continuing to run experiments.
