## Talk to users before you build

- Tibo says "You're not talking to enough users. I know you think you are, but you're not."
- Every successful product he built started with dozens of conversations. Every failure started with assumptions.
- His instruction: "Stop coding. Start calling."

## Subtract features instead of adding them

- He argues a product needs "fewer, better" features, not more.
- He has never seen a product fail because it did too few things well. He has seen hundreds fail because they did too many things poorly. His rule is to cut ruthlessly.
- He warns against ego-driven features. A fancy feature is often wanted because it is interesting to build, not because it earns revenue.
- He admits he used to focus on things that would make him look cool "at family dinner".
- His rule: focus on revenue, kill fast, because "ego costs money."

## Volume of shipped products creates luck

- Tibo says success comes from creating more, which means shipping more, not coding more.
- He launched 10+ products before finding winners. Most people launch one or two and quit.
- His formula: "Volume creates luck. Luck creates success."
- Finishing matters. Unfinished projects in folders count for nothing until they are live.
- A finished mediocre product beats a perfect unshipped one every time. His rule is to ship now and improve later.

## Ship an embarrassing V1 and don't fear a failed launch

- He says the first version should make you cringe: "If you're not embarrassed by V1, you launched too late."
- Example: SuperX sat at $1k MRR for 10 months because the team kept "improving" before launching properly. The perfectionism cost time and momentum.
- His advice is to ship the embarrassing version and improve it publicly.
- On fear of failing: nobody will remember or care about a failed launch, and it won't become an industry topic. If it fails, nobody notices. If it succeeds, you win.

## Ideas, competition and execution

- Tibo says your idea isn't unique. Someone else is building the same thing right now, which he sees as a good thing.
- The difference is who ships faster, markets better and iterates smarter. He says to stop protecting the idea and start executing it.
- "Someone's already doing this" is a validation signal, not a stop sign.
- Examples: scheduling tools already existed when he launched PostSyncer, and tweet schedulers existed when he built SuperX.
- His conclusion: competition proves market demand.

## Charge more

- He says "Your prices are too low. I know this without seeing your product."
- Example: he raised Tweet Hunter prices 5x, revenue doubled and churn decreased. Higher prices attract better customers.
- His test: if nobody is complaining about price, you are leaving money on the table.

## Affiliate programs amplify growth, they don't create it

- Tibo says an affiliate program won't save a failing product.
- Example: Outrank's affiliate program works because the product already had traction. The team amplified existing momentum and did not manufacture it from nothing.
- Order of operations: get growth first, then scale it with affiliates.

## Skip the paperwork and the courses, then act

- He made his first $10k before registering any business entity. His advice is to set up an LLC when revenue actually matters, and not to use legal structure as an excuse to delay.
- On courses: every hour in a course is an hour not building, and "The market teaches faster than any curriculum."
- He adds that many course creators haven't built anything themselves, so courses often don't cover what you need to know.
- He says most advice, including his own newsletter, may not apply to you, so take principles rather than tactics.
- His closing point: "You don't have an information problem. You have an execution problem." The remedy is to build the thing, ship the product, talk to users and iterate fast.
