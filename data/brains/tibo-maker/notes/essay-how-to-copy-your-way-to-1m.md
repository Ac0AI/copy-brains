## Why Tibo copies existing products instead of inventing new ones

- Tibo runs a portfolio at $1M MRR across 5 products. None are original ideas. Short video (Revid.ai), SEO automation (Outrank.so) and scheduling tools (PostSyncer.com) all existed before his versions.
- He did not find untouched markets. He found crowded markets where one type of user was being ignored and built something better for them.
- His rule: the category already exists, and the job is to own a corner of it better than anyone else.
- Notion (note-taking), Figma (design software) and Slack (team communication) were not first in their categories, yet each became the go-to product.

## Why he rejects Blue Ocean thinking

- He argues the 2005 book "The Blue Ocean Strategy" (find empty markets, invent new categories, be the next Facebook) sold over 4 million copies and produced almost no successful bootstrapped founders.
- It is good advice for building a $10B company, but bad advice for building a business in general. "You don't want to risk your entire life at a 1% chance of success."
- The model only really worked for VCs. They need moonshots because one win has to cover 99 losses in their fund.
- For a founder with a limited number of attempts, one loss can be dramatic.
- He fell for it coming out of university: years spent trying to invent something nobody had built, "years wasted, almost no money earned."
- His closing question: are you building for a 1% shot at $1B or a 50% shot at $1M? That answer defines how you think, build and learn.

## The question to ask about every product: Mark Pincus's approach

- Tibo cites Mark Pincus, who launched 10 products, 8 of which became hits, without ever inventing a new category. FarmVille was a farm simulation game and Words With Friends was a word game like many others.
- Pincus asked one question each time: "What does the best possible version of this look like for the person being ignored by the current market leader?"
- Tibo says that question is worth more than any original idea.
- He notes Pincus calls the resistance to copying "moral arbitrage". School taught us copying is cheating, and that guilt keeps the edge available for whoever drops the ego first.

## Three-question framework before building anything

1. **Who is already paying for a solution to this problem?** If nobody is paying, the problem is not painful enough. "Full stop."
2. **Who is that solution not working for?** Every market leader ignores someone: they get too big, too bloated, or too focused on a certain kind of client. That gap is where you live.
3. **What would make that person switch today?** Not eventually, immediately. That is the one thing to be meaningfully better at.

## Why incumbents leave the gap open

- Existing companies will not fix the gap themselves. Solving it disrupts their workflows, upsets current customers and threatens their pricing model.
- They have every incentive to leave the problem open, and you have every incentive to close it.
- This makes a neglected segment of a proven market a more reliable opening than a new category.
