## Why Tibo says VC money is not validation

- Tibo has raised VC before, and says that is exactly why he won't again. His reasons: what it does to "your math, your mindset, and your freedom before anything even goes wrong."
- When a VC writes a check, a founder feels validated, as if smart people with pattern recognition said yes and so the idea must be right.
- That feeling cost him two years, twice. He kept building products nobody was buying because he "confused investor confidence with market demand."
- His rule: "VC money is not user validation. It never was." The only validation that counts is someone opening their wallet and paying for the product. "Everything else is noise."
- On congratulating founders for raising: it is like congratulating a chef on getting the vegetables. The meal hasn't been cooked yet.
- His closing line to readers: "user validation over investor validation. Always."

## How VC math works against founders

- VCs run a power law model. They expect 9 out of 10 bets to fail and need just one company per fund to return everything.
- So they are not optimising for a founder's sustainable, profitable, free life. They are optimising for a lottery ticket that pays 100x.
- If a business is doing well but not on a billion-dollar trajectory, investors stop caring. They give no more money and respond coldly.
- The pressure then starts:
  - The founder chases growth numbers someone wrote on a whiteboard.
  - They stop building for users and work just to raise the next round.
  - Tibo says they lose "80% of the freedom you started building for."

## Why Tibo stays bootstrapped (and may never sell)

- When you bootstrap and your products print cash, there is no pressure to exit and nobody writing numbers on paper for you to follow.
- Because of this, he says he'll probably never sell again either.
- Status as of June 2026: $1M MRR across 5 products, every one bootstrapped and profitable.
- He says the best part isn't the number. It is feeling completely free to move fast, change direction, double down, or slow down, whatever feels right for the business.
- He says that freedom is why he started building in the first place.
- His background: built Tweet Hunter and Taplio (sold for $8M). He is now growing revid.ai, feather.so, superx.so, outrank.so and postsyncer.com.

## The rare cases where VC makes sense

- Tibo isn't anti-VC entirely. He sees two cases where it genuinely fits:
  - True infrastructure plays where you need scale before revenue.
  - Regulated industries where compliance costs are very high.
- His view: most internet businesses don't need it. "You need users who pay. That's the only math that matters."

## The question to ask before raising

- His test: "Are you raising because you need the money to build, or because raising feels like progress?"
- If it's the second, his advice is to go find a paying customer instead. "That's real validation."
- He also points to the horror stories common among founders who raised, such as:
  - A GP falling asleep during a $15M pitch.
  - Term sheets signed but the money never wired.
  - A founder offering his co-founders' stock in exchange for firing them.
- He says almost every founder he knows who raised has a story like this, but the deeper problem is the false validation and the loss of freedom.
