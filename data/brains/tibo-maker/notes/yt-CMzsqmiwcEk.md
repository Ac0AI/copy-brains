## Failing fast: Tibo's path from two failed startups to Tweet Hunter

- Tibo's first startup was a kids' chores motivation app. He calls it a predictable failure: he and a few students were desperate for an idea and picked one that sounded great to everyone around them, but never got real market validation from users.
- His second was a kids' coding game, built as a video game to compete with other games for kids' attention. He raised about $400,000 and grew the team to 15-20 people (artists, game designers, front-end and back-end developers).
- That company had plenty of free players but no revenue. His verdict: "if people do not convert to paid user you don't have a real product like you don't have a real business."
- He reacted to the first failure by thinking he needed more money, a bigger team and a more ambitious project, and the second failure came from doing exactly that in an industry he knew almost nothing about.
- Total of roughly four years across the two failures, on a very low salary. He names that as his biggest sacrifice.
- His resolution afterward: "if we need to fail again it needs to be in two weeks and not in two years."

## Ship a product a week and judge only by revenue

- Starting over, Tibo and his team shipped a new product roughly every week until one produced revenue. They did this for about four months.
- They looked at revenue only to decide whether a product worked, not vanity metrics like engagement or free users.
- Tweet Hunter was the 11th product shipped in that window and it took off from the start.
- Each product showed the same pattern: two acquisition channels brought most users, Reddit and Twitter. Reddit was hard to systemize with a repeatable recipe. Twitter had many things that could be automated and that worked again and again, so they built for Twitter.
- The path: first a GPT-3 tweet generator (didn't work well, GPT wasn't good enough then), then GPT-3 used to cluster and categorize content (worked "so so"), then Tweet Hunter, which resurfaces old viral tweets and repurposes them.
- He built Tweet Hunter for himself first and was its first user; it helped him grow a Twitter audience daily. He calls this founder-product fit.

## Build for your own problems

- The main thing that works now and didn't before: becoming an expert in the problem he's solving, by solving his own problems.
- Earlier he solved problems he thought he understood, but "the degree of understanding that you have is very low level." You miss the deep core need of the audience because it's usually not what they tell you.
- His advice to new founders in 2025: the first idea will probably fail, but build it anyway so the second or third succeeds. To raise the odds, start by building something for yourself, since "building a good product is in the first place a true understanding of the problem that you are solving."

## Launching Tweet Hunter with an influencer co-founder

- Tibo messaged many big names on Twitter to get them using Tweet Hunter. One was already teaching a course on the exact technique built into the product, loved it, and wanted to join as a co-founder.
- It wasn't the plan, but Tibo gave him up to 25% of the product and says he didn't regret it.
- They launched on Twitter in September 2021 using this partner's launch plan. In three weeks they went from $3K monthly revenue to well above that (the transcript cuts off the exact figure, "more thank").

## Growth levers: personal branding, SEO and building in public

- Two levers drove a 7x rise: social media and personal branding, and SEO.
- On social: build in public, share how you grew, what you did, user stories and success stories.
- On SEO: constantly publish new pages and new tools that rank on Google. It compounds into more traffic over time. He thinks startups underrate SEO because talking about what you do on Twitter is trendy and getting saturated.
- Why build in public: tips you hold back will probably be shared by someone else, who gets the credit. Sharing tips and true numbers daily earns trust: people believe you ship quality products and treat customers well. That trust turns into feedback, purchases, promotion and people plugging your new product into their newsletters.
- He started Tweet Hunter with about 1,000 followers, 99% of them bots or dead accounts, and built his audience by sharing useful things daily. His audience came from sharing; Tweet Hunter didn't succeed because of an existing audience. He now has 130K+ on Twitter.
- He says the newer Twitter algorithm rewards interesting content rather than follower counts, so anyone can still get reach.
- LinkedIn: many salespeople, who buy quickly if you show value, and higher buying power with less scam and low-quality users than Twitter.
- Downside of personal branding: LinkedIn suspended his account over his involvement in Taplio (a LinkedIn tool built on a Chrome extension they can't fully control), even after he had left the product. He lost the account. Lesson: "you do not own your followers." What you do own is a newsletter subscriber base, which can be exported and sold.

## The acquisition: 20% upfront, 80% on revenue milestones

- The original plan was to reach $10K per month and sell for about $1M. They grew far past that, to roughly $130K per month at sale.
- Buyers wanted founders to stay two years, because the business was risky: Elon Musk was changing Twitter's rules, and Taplio wasn't compliant with LinkedIn's rules.
- He chose a French buyer (about 50 people, same culture and language, whose founder was a former middle-school classmate and Tweet Hunter customer) over a US and an Indian company he knew nothing about.
- Deal structure: only 20% paid upfront, the other 80% unlocked via revenue milestones over two years. They hit five of six milestones, growing from $1.5M to $8M per year in under two years.
- The buyer gave them full independence, which helped execution but left few bridges between teams. He thinks tighter collaboration could have grown it more, though he wanted the freedom.
- In March 2023 Twitter raised its API price from zero to $42K per month and cut access within days. Tibo says most Twitter products died, and a few acquired Twitter tools strengthened Tweet Hunter's position. He notes the fee is a barrier for new entrants but manageable for products with revenue.

## Feather: buying a product and undercutting Beehiiv on price

- Beehiiv emailed him a price rise of about 2.5x for his newsletter. His own price rises were typically 10% to 30%, and grandfathered existing users.
- He called them out on Twitter, got traction, and started wondering about building his own newsletter platform.
- He was already buying Feather (a blog platform that turns Notion docs into a blog), his biggest acquisition so far (he has done more than 10, mostly much smaller), for $250,000 of his personal money, "the biggest risk I ever took."
- He found another tool that sends newsletters from Notion, asked the founder to sell, and merged it into Feather. It took far longer than expected because sending newsletters is hard.
- Pricing difference: Beehiiv charges per subscriber (he cites about $250 per month at 1K+ followers, roughly $500 per month at his scale, and says he isn't sure of the exact figure). Feather charges about half that and is aimed at solopreneurs who send rarely. He sends a daily newsletter to about 137,000 people on Feather.
- Beehiiv locked him out after noticing he was testing his own newsletter feature against theirs. He had already exported his subscribers, and says he'd never use a platform that doesn't allow export.

## Ego, teams, and how he works with makers

- Advice to his younger self: a big team looks good at a family dinner, but "that's ego feeding." What matters is selling: "people giving you money for your product is the ultimate validation that you are doing something useful."
- He has no employees. He runs four products, each partnered with one skilled maker who owns a share and manages themselves.
- He finds partners on Twitter, running several candidates in parallel with a paid two-week trial, and tells them honestly that several people are competing. He believes a real trial beats any interview.
- After the trial the partner gets equity or revenue share.
- His red-flag rule: a red flag never shrinks, it only grows, so pull back when you see one.
- Daily habit: tackle the hardest task first, start coding or writing with coffee, and get two hours of focused work with no distraction before email or DMs. His goal is to ship something each day that others can see or use.
- His view on AI: building costs are heading toward zero, so value shifts to distribution, storytelling and educating users. He uses Cursor daily for coding and expects AI-driven tools to catch security bugs in AI-written code.
