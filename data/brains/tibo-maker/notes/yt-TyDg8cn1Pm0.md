## Why Tibo left venture-backed startups and bootstrapped

- His first two venture-backed companies "crashed." He says he is not a good manager and that school pushes you toward managing big teams.
- His lesson from those two: "You cannot create a good product with 10 interns."
- To reach product-market fit, he argues the founder must be hands-on, the most motivated person, and the one who can run the most experiments fastest.
- Having a big team felt good at the family dinner, but it didn't produce product-market fit.
- He chose bootstrapping partly for freedom. During covid he and his wife were nomading, and raising venture money needs investor meetings and a hired team.
- He recommends bootstrapping first. A founder who raises usually gives away a large share of equity, and AI has pushed the cost of building SaaS toward zero.
- He now gets offers from people wanting to invest and declines: "I honestly don't need money to create SaaS right now." Taking money "adds so many issues."
- If he did take money, it would come from other indie makers and founders who have done what he is doing and can give advice.

## Shipping one product a week and following only revenue

- With his co-founder Tom (a micro founder duo, two people total), he tried to ship one product per week "until something sticks." It ran about four months, with roughly 10 to 11 products. The last one was Tweet Hunter.
- If an idea came out of a Zoom brainstorm, he would end the call and start coding it the same day.
- He says the process was "a nightmare at first" but it let them pivot quickly until they hit something valuable.
- His key rule: revenue was the only metric they followed. Free users and a few people saying they like it just reassure you, "your mom is going to tell you that your product is awesome." In his first two startups, free users kept them working on weak ideas.
- One product reached $300 in monthly revenue and they still killed it. They wanted to see $1K in monthly recurring revenue before going all in.
- He says $1K was not a magic number, just a strong signal.
- An even better signal than revenue: new users and signups still arriving on days when they weren't promoting anything, because they were heads-down building. That meant people found the product without being pushed.
- On killing projects: he says to "listen to the market," run experiments, double down on what works and "relentlessly" kill what doesn't.

## Sunk-cost mistakes and outsourcing development

- His first startup took two years on an idea that should have taken three months to judge. After the initial four-month app didn't work, they raised money to build a better version of the same app, which he calls a mistake.
- This year he outsourced an app to a developer. It was meant to be a one-month project and was still not working after three months, with obvious poor fit between developer and idea.
- The trap: each "add two more weeks" increases the money spent, so you feel you can't kill it.
- He says outsourcing only works if you pay a very high package for top talent, or hire a junior and manage them closely. He did neither.
- His new approach is equity partnerships with people who have real skin in the game. He says "it's incredible how people are motivated when it's their ideas and not yours," so he mostly works on ideas that are not his.

## Equity partnerships on existing small products (Outrank, Feather)

- Example: Outrank (outrank.so), a keyword and blog post generation tool that publishes a new blog post each day to rank on specific keywords.
  - He found a solo founder with about $300/month in revenue, which he sees as a real signal of value even if the packaging is poor.
  - They split equity. He got hands-on with the app, improved generation and prompting, and focused on distribution while the founder focused on building.
  - Launch: about 50 new free trials the day after launch, already at $2K monthly revenue, with a hope of $5K within weeks.
- How he finds targets: he scrolls Twitter/X, notices interesting products, engages and asks many questions. He says you must set up Twitter properly (lists, and tools like his own Super X) or you only see the day's viral memes.
- His staging, per product:
  - Month 1: build a working version of the shared vision and put it in users' hands. DM a few people for feedback.
  - Month 2: deliver more value and try to get a bit above $1K in revenue.
  - If that works, launch and then activate SEO and new acquisition channels.
- He puts in no money, only his time, his audience and his distribution channels. He thinks paying a founder cash while they hold some equity leaves them with less skin in the game.
- Feather: he wanted to buy it after selling Tweet Hunter. It had healthy metrics, very low churn and steady growth, and he was a user. During the acquisition, Feather raised prices so that he would pay 2.5x more ($100/month to $250/month).
  - He expected many users to be in the same spot, so he bought a second tool that sends newsletters from Notion, merged its capabilities into Feather and turned it into a newsletter tool. The newsletter feature was much harder to build than expected, but growth kicked in once released.
- On pricing: on Tweet Hunter he raised the price five times, always grandfathering existing users, with increases of about 20% or 30%.

## Micro-acquisitions: buy assets, not companies

- He recommends buying assets or products rather than companies, because selling a whole company involves selling equity, paying tax and heavy contracts.
- He says when working at Tweet Hunter they acquired more than 10 tools, around $10K assets each.
- The process he describes: wire the seller $5K, receive all the assets, wire $5K more at the end. The contract is under one page, generated with GPT-4.
- He notes a company-level deal can still be needed for tax and accounting reasons.
- A similar speed point: another speaker's newsletter acquisitions took 30 minutes over Twitter DMs, and Tibo describes the same low-friction approach for small assets.

## Creator equity as a distribution engine (Tweet Hunter)

- It started by accident at a little above $1K monthly revenue. He reached out to Twitter creators using the product, and one, GK Molina, asked to "get in" at co-founder level. His audience matched the target audience exactly.
- They gave him up to 25% of Tweet Hunter. It was "very generous."
- They then repeated it at a much smaller scale: 17 creators received 0.1% each.
- Result: about 17 big creators strongly incentivized to grow the product, so every feature launch and announcement had a pool of people pushing it.
- He stresses they didn't plan this; they listened to what creators hinted at and followed it.
- He mentions a related build: Mega-style creator partnership and sponsored post features are now being explored by others.

## Moats, building in public and platform risk in the AI era

- Because AI makes copying easy, he says products are hard to defend. A newcomer can clone an app and sell it for half the price.
- The moats he sees:
  1. Proprietary in-house data.
  2. A distribution channel others lack (a YouTube audience is his example).
  3. Users and network effects (X, where even presidents and CEOs publish because most people listen there).
- Building in public: when he started more than three years ago it was clearly beneficial. Now he asks why share all your secrets when you have no moat. His view:
  - If you're a nobody with no audience, you have nothing to lose and it can be a great start.
  - With 100K followers, every thing you share gets copied, so you must be comfortable with 100 copycats fast.
  - The more engaged the audience, the more valuable it is, so it's a balance between that value and the copy risk.
- Platform risk is real. His LinkedIn account was suspended at one point, and his former LinkedIn product (Taplio) was 23% of revenue when he sold it in April, so he is relieved he sold it.
- He is building a new app for X again and worries about getting banned, since X is his primary platform. He also notes platforms are a black box, so no one knows whether falling engagement means algorithm changes, fewer bots or fewer users.
- On the future of SaaS: if AI can build full SaaS from a prompt, companies may build their own tools. Otherwise the market stays competitive but still values well-built software. He now often buys SaaS because he likes and wants to support the founder (example: Dan Chen of Testimonial), so personal brand becomes a buying reason.

## The Tweet Hunter and Taplio exit deal

- The original plan: build to $10K monthly revenue, sell for $1 million in under a year, then start over. It didn't go that way.
- Tweet Hunter grew well past $10K monthly revenue, and no buyer would take it without the founders staying two years.
- The business was only about a year old, with risks: X under Elon Musk, and LinkedIn rule non-compliance.
- Deal structure: 20% of the price paid up front ($2 million) and 80% tied to performance milestones based on monthly revenue. There were six milestones that could take the total from $2 million up to $10 million. They unlocked five, for a final price of $8 million.
- He recommends creative earn-out structures in similar situations, but says the two years were very stressful. The milestones meant they worked even more than before, and there were Twitter and LinkedIn suspensions along the way. He believes they would probably have earned more by not selling.
- Today he would not accept any sale requiring a two-year stay, because he values his time far more.
- Buyer choice: big US and Indian companies made it hard to talk constructively. The buyer was a French company of about 60 people, with founders and executives easy to talk to.
- Negotiation tip: everyone focuses on the headline number, but the terms matter just as much, "the terms is going to make you bless your lawyer or want to kill him."

## Co-founders, multi-product strategy and personal beliefs

- Goal: five companies at $100K MRR each within three years. He expects maybe two or three to hit that range, one around $10K to $20K and hopes one blows up well above $100K. He feels he is near maximum capacity at four products and says more than five would be crazy.
- He wants to build a strong acquisition engine across multiple products and cites Andrew Wilkinson's umbrella of companies as inspiration. He notes it goes against the usual "focus on one thing" advice. Each product has its own co-founder, so he won't be the only decision maker.
- Co-founders don't need managing, and with AI he says each of them is worth about 10 people from five years ago.
- Why Tom worked: easy to communicate frustration, they disagree often but want the same direction, and complementary styles. Tibo ships first and favors quantity over quality; Tom forces higher quality. They've known each other about 10 years.
- With new co-founders he is trying to take the "quality guy" role in some cases, and says it's not working yet.
- Co-founder issues: raise a latent problem early, because waiting only makes it harder and eventually you blow up.
- Product-market fit signal: "Founders never know when they need to quit but they definitely know when they need to continue." When he reached PMF, he was overwhelmed with support, growth and tech not holding up.
- First-time founders: take your first idea, ship it and show it, because it probably won't work but that is how you get feedback.
- His contrarian belief: quantity beats quality. He cites a study (he thinks Harvard) where a group graded on quantity produced the best papers.
- Burnout: after his first two ventures failed he went to court to close a company with debts and took a CTO job at a scale-up, which brought him into B2B SaaS. He advises not comparing yourself to others' posted revenue milestones, and keeping community, exercise, healthy eating and sleep.
