## Ship one product a week until something sticks

- Tibo's early startups failed because he delegated the hard parts. After engineering school he had been told for five years that hands-on coding was a low-status job and that engineers should become project managers. So he hired a 10-15 person team of rotating junior interns and handed off the hardest tasks: building the product, understanding what people want, and marketing.
- He later reversed this: "being handsome" (hands-on, building himself) is enough, and with AI a very small team can now out-produce a 10-person team from 15 years ago.
- In 2020 he and his co-founder Toma started a "ship one new product every week until something sticks" experiment. In early 2021 they shipped roughly 10 or 11 projects in about four months.
- The weekly rhythm:
  - Build for a week.
  - The week after, announce on Twitter, collect feedback and talk to users.
  - While doing that, start the next product.
  - Keep what works and throw away what doesn't.
- Tweet Hunter came out of this loop and launched in early May 2021. He describes it as forcing opportunities instead of waiting for luck.

## Build for a problem you have, and use the product daily

- Twitter was their main acquisition channel, and they wanted a bigger audience. Tibo started hacking together a database of viral tweets to learn which patterns worked, and that became the first Tweet Hunter product.
- Users entered a topic such as coding or SEO and got viral tweets on it, filtered toward their own writing style. GPT-3 was used to label, cluster and match tweets, not to generate content.
- He was the primary user, spending 30 to 60 minutes a day writing tweets with it, then fixing whatever annoyed him in the code. That daily use gave him both the deep problem understanding and a feel for tiny details like a misaligned button or a bad feedback effect.
- His advice: "You are basically an expert on your own problems." With today's market size, at least a thousand other people likely have the same problem.
- Fixing small things daily is what makes a product feel completely different.

## Validate with payments, not free usage

- His first idea was an app that gamified motivation for kids' chores (brushing teeth, homework). It took two years to realize it wasn't working.
- The core mistake was that they never tried to validate the idea. They kept trying to sell it and convince people it was good.
- Structural problem: two clients (parents pay, kids must use it daily) and competition with every game on the kid's phone.
- Second mistake in both early startups: forcing adoption by going free first, "like Facebook," and finding the business model later. This only validates that people use something free.
- His current approach is the opposite: "The validation comes from the payments." He wants to prove people will pay, not just use.
- A good problem isn't enough. Habit and motivation content is hugely consumed, yet very few people act or pay.
- The product that works does what people want, not what the founder wishes the world worked like. He gives the example of startups built on the idea that people should want to meet strangers. You don't create new wants; you find existing needs and serve them in a new way.

## Influencer partnership and the growth curve

- About two or three months after launch, Tibo did influencer outreach to people with 30K, 50K or 100K followers. One creator who taught people how to go viral on Twitter replied "I want to be in" after two or three DMs.
- The fit was strong because he taught the same method: building a swipe file of good viral content, which is what Tweet Hunter automates.
- They ran a joint launch that the partner handled entirely on Twitter. Monthly revenue went from 3K to 20K in September 2021.
- After that they grew about 20% month over month until reaching 100K-150K in monthly revenue.
- Behind the visible revenue chart was relentless daily work:
  - Adding features almost every day.
  - Shipping a free SEO tool almost every week to rank for keywords.
  - Talking to two or three people a day on Twitter to understand their pain points.
  - Fixing UI issues continuously.
- Tweet Hunter and its LinkedIn sister product Taplio were both acquired in 2022. Buyers required a two-year transition, so Tibo was free to start new projects only in 2024. He now works on four projects, one of which has taken off.

## Risk, failure and money

- Tibo says he never saw startups as risky: he had no family to support, about 15,000 from his parents that he used over two years, and a working partner.
- The skills from failed startups paid off. After two failures he took a job paying about twice what 99% of his engineering-school peers earned, because he had hired a team, managed budgets and banks, and touched everything from front end to back end to server management.
- He admits the risk is genuinely higher for people with large salaries and family obligations.
- His parents' financial literacy shaped him. Today he faces a cash-flow squeeze: the payment provider pays him more than 30 days after a sale, but AI providers need payment immediately, so faster growth stretches cash further.
- His school gave him his co-founder. He thinks you can now find a strong first circle on X if you are consistent and motivated, and he now builds with people he hasn't met in person.

## Building in the AI era

- He sees far more people shipping on X now (he estimates 10 to 20 times more), helped by the indie-hacker wave and AI lowering the skill bar.
- Copycats are common. After he talked publicly about a product (Revit) and how well it was working, an almost exact copy of the landing page and example videos appeared within a week. As a result he talks less about revenue on X than before.
- He tries not to judge whether this is good or bad and focuses on using the tools available to build the best products, noting there is a lot to build and it is much easier than before.
