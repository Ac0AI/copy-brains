## Cofounders are the foundation, so pick on character and commitment

- Paul reports "the surprise mentioned by the most founders" was how carefully cofounders must be chosen and how hard the relationship must be maintained.
- Founders who failed wished they had weighed character and commitment over ability. His lesson: "don't pick cofounders who will flake."
- Character is tested harder in a startup than anywhere else. One founder: "You haven't seen someone's true colors unless you've worked with them on a startup."
- One founder preferred a friend over a higher-output stranger, because the emotional and social support outweighs the lost output.
- YC's application has more questions about the founders' commitment and relationship than about their ability.
- Successful founders talked less about choosing and more about maintaining the relationship. Several said it turns from friendship into a marriage, which Paul explains by the greater stress and by founders being the whole company at first. "It's the basis of everything."

## A startup is not a job: it takes over your life and swings emotionally

- Running a startup never stops, unlike a job or being a student. Founders spend nearly every waking moment working or thinking about it, and time seems to stretch so that a month feels huge.
- Total immersion can feel exciting and never like "work", though Paul notes the founder who said this was only a few months in.
- The emotional swings are larger than founders expect, from "the next Google" to pondering failure within hours. The lows, and keeping the team motivated through them, are the hard part.
- Keeping going without major success "is siphoned from the founders themselves". If you can't continue, Paul says it's not the end of the world, since plenty of famous founders had earlier failures.
- The highs are real too. Many founders found it fun and more fulfilling than friends' jobs, mainly because of the freedom.
- Paul deliberately doesn't oversell this: starting a startup is fun "the way a survivalist training course would be fun", and not at all if you're not into that. YC's application weeds out people who wouldn't like it.

## Persistence and a long-term view matter more than intelligence

- Founders were surprised by how much persistence is required and by how often persistence alone dissolved obstacles, even problems like immigration.
- Several said persistence matters more than raw intelligence. Paul extends this to ability in general, which is why character matters in cofounders.
- Everything takes 2-3x longer than expected, especially dealmaking. Paul's cause: fast-moving founders hit "shocking amount of shear stress" wherever they touch bureaucracies like big companies or VC funds, which is why fundraising and the enterprise market kill so many startups.
- Founders are also overconfident. Told only 1 in 100 successful startups has a YouTube or Facebook trajectory, "they all think 'we're going to be that 1.'"
- A successful founder's view: for most successful startups it's "at least 3 years and probably 5+."
- Thinking long-term helps. Working patiently is less stressful and produces better work, and things improve at ramen profitability when you can "shift into a different mode of working".
- Tactic from his notes: don't put a slow organization on the critical path. Fork off separate processes to deal with them, and avoid depending on closing a deal to move forward.

## Launch something minimal, talk to users, and change the idea

- Success comes from lots of little things, not one magic idea or killer feature. "There is no such thing as a killer feature. Or at least you won't know what it is." Hacker-founders won't get to program all day: "If you spend all your time programming, you will fail."
- Because you don't even know which basket is best, the strategy is to try lots of different things.
- Launch "the absolute smallest thing that can be considered a complete application." Pride is the usual reason people launch late. Paul: "If your first version is so impressive that trolls don't make fun of it, you waited too long to launch" (a variant of Reid Hoffman's embarrassment principle).
- Over-engineering "is poison": it's like telling a lie you then have to remember so you don't contradict it.
- Product development is a conversation with users that doesn't start until launch. Before launch you're like a police artist before showing the witness the first sketch. Treat the first version as a trick for getting users talking.
- Customers reveal what they value and will pay for. They often won't like what you built, but there are other things they'd like that are trivially easy to implement.
- Treat the idea as "a hypothesis rather than a blueprint". Determination without flexibility is "a greedy algorithm" that finds a mediocre local maximum.
- One founder's advice: find a problem that's easy for you to solve, since "you can make enormous gains playing around in problem-space." Fast iteration is key.
- Judging startup ideas, especially your own, is very hard, and experienced founders keep an open mind. Paul applies this to YC itself, which is "a decent hypothesis".

## Competitors are rarely the threat, but getting users is hard

- Worrying about competitors is mostly a false alarm. Apparent competitors usually have a different goal, and scares are forgotten weeks later.
- People overreact because they overvalue ideas. Execution is what usually matters.
- Hype doesn't create satisfied users for something as complicated as technology: "Competitors riding on lots of good blogger perception aren't really the winners."
- Getting users is harder than founders expect. When it fails, it's hard to tell whether the problem is exposure or a bad product, and even good products can be blocked by switching or integration costs. Small companies asking other companies' developers to do work aren't seen as urgent.
- Paul's note: the test for what you've built isn't whether it's good but whether it's good enough to supply the activation energy required.
- The sharpest criticism of YC was too little focus on customer acquisition cost. Paul concedes this may be true, especially for games.
- Technically challenging products can rely on word of mouth, as Google did. Other types of startups may win more through deals and marketing than features.

## Deals and investors: expect the worst, and sell yourself

- Deals fall through constantly. Startups are powerless and good ideas generally seem wrong, so everyone is nervous about closing with you.
- Paul's pessimistic advice: assume you won't get investment, and if someone offers some, assume you'll never get any more. One founder wished they'd assumed no outside money, which would have focused them on finding revenue early.
- Another founder's counterpoint, which Paul endorses: if someone offers you money, take it.
- Be optimistic about your ability to make something great, but not about things you can't control, like big companies or investors.
- Founders were surprised by investors' cluelessness. Angels were generally better than VCs because they usually have startup experience.
- Paul's explanation: VCs seem formidable because their profession is to. They win money from limited partners by seeming confident and seeming to understand technology.
- So you may have to play games. "The degree to which feigning certitude impressed investors" surprised one founder. At a YC alumni fundraising talk, nearly all the advice was about investor psychology, and the founders were more cynical than Paul.
- VCs often don't realize that the startups they like are the best at selling themselves to VCs. Paul's analogy: the dumber the customers, the more effort goes into selling rather than making.
- "VCs get money by seeming confident to LPs, and founders get money by seeming confident to VCs."

## Luck, community, respect, and how the job changes as you grow

- Luck is a big factor, especially in deals, because there are "two such random linkages in the path between startups and money". His example: where would Microsoft be if IBM had insisted on an exclusive license for DOS?
- Paul's model is that the outcome is "the product of skill, determination, and luck", so zero luck means zero outcome.
- Founders who fail fast blame themselves, and those who succeed fast don't see their luck. It's the ones in the middle who see how important luck is.
- Community was a surprising value, both the YC peer group facing similar obstacles at similar times and the wider Silicon Valley scene, where you can't help hearing news and meeting useful people. Founders were surprised by how willing people were to help and how accessible important people are. Paul: creating wealth isn't zero-sum, so you don't have to stab people in the back to win.
- Outside the startup world, founders get no respect. Most good ideas seem bad, and about 95% of random people instinctively think your idea will flop.
- As you grow, the technical founder/CEO's job is rewritten every 6-12 months: less coding, more managing, planning, hiring and cleaning up messes. The employee equation differs from the founder equation.
- One founder said about 75% of the stress was gone after reaching cruising altitude, though Paul says 75% is probably on the high side.

## The super-pattern: everyone's model of work is a job

- Paul notes that every surprise is something he already tells people. Starting a startup was "like I said, but way more so."
- His explanation: everyone's unconscious model of work is a job, so they expect a startup to resemble one. Jobs have far more damping, and no need to carefully choose and maintain relationships with coworkers.
- The same model explains why the good times are surprisingly good, since most people can't imagine such freedom.
- His fix is awareness. When you think "I can't believe it will be that bad", the next thought should be: "the reason I can't believe it will be that bad is that my model of work is a job."
