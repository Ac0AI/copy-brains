## Persistence versus obstinacy are different behaviors, not just outcomes

- Paul argues the two are genuinely different kinds of behavior, not labels applied after the fact based on who turned out right. If it were only hindsight, "be persistent rather than obstinate" would just mean "be right rather than wrong," which is useless advice.
- Both are "hard to stop," but in different senses: "The persistent are like boats whose engines can't be throttled back. The obstinate are like boats whose rudders can't be turned."
- They look identical only when a problem has a single solution path, where the only choice is whether to give up. As problems get more complex, the difference shows.
- Terminology: Paul uses "persistent" for the good kind of stubborn and "obstinate" for the bad kind, and admits this stretches normal usage.

## Where each one is attached: goals versus ideas

- "The persistent are attached to the goal. The obstinate are attached to their ideas about how to reach it."
- The persistent are attached to points high in the decision tree, not minor ones lower down. The obstinate "spray 'don't give up' indiscriminately over the whole tree."
- Obstinate people tend to cling to their first ideas, which are the least informed by experience working on the problem. So they are disproportionately attached to wrong details.
- The persistent will give way on any low-level point if its expected value drops enough, while energy and resilience keep them pushing toward whatever they chose higher up.

## How to tell them apart in conversation

- The obstinate won't listen. When you point out problems, "their eyes glaze over, and their replies sound like ideologues talking about matters of doctrine."
- Successful people are "never more engaged than when you disagree with them."
- Example: the Collison brothers are the most determined people he can think of, yet when you point out a problem they listen "with an almost predatory intensity." If there is a hole in the bottom of their boat, they want to know.
- Paul says the difference he senses in conversations ("Wow, that guy is determined" versus "Damn, that guy is stubborn") is not only about whether the person seemed right.
- Obstinacy can win in some domains, such as politics, but not where you must pass external tests. Obstinate political leaders are famous for getting power, not for using it well.

## Why people are obstinate: it is personality, not circumstance

- His first theory was that the obstinate are overwhelmed and grab onto ideas like handholds on a rolling ship. He rejected it.
- Evidence against: handing the Collisons a much harder problem would make them less obstinate, since they would know they must be open to anything. And the obstinate stay obstinate on easy problems.
- Conclusion: obstinacy comes from within. It is "a reflexive resistance to changing one's ideas," closely related to stupidity and a form of not giving up that is easy for the stupid to practice. You skip the tradeoffs and dig in your heels.
- It even works on simple problems, "up to a point."
- The two are not opposites. Paul compares them to aerobic and anaerobic respiration: obstinacy is the more primitive process, but it has uses, like leaping away from a threat.
- The optimal amount of obstinacy is not zero. An unthinking "I won't give up" as a first reaction to a setback helps prevent panic. But the further toward obstinate someone is, the less likely they are to solve hard problems.
- Obstinate people do sometimes succeed: by luck (a stopped clock right twice a day), or when obstinacy cancels another error. Example: a leader with overcautious subordinates whose estimates are always biased one way, so a mindless "push ahead regardless" is usually right.

## The five qualities that make up real persistence

Obstinacy is simple, and animals have it. Persistence has a complex internal structure of five qualities:
- **Energy:** the persistent "persist rather than merely resisting." They keep trying things.
- **Imagination:** to keep trying things you must keep thinking of things to try. Energy creates demand for ideas, and imagination gives energy somewhere to go. Energy plus imagination alone gives the caricature of an artist or poet.
- **Resilience:** not having morale destroyed by setbacks. Without it you can only do good work on a small scale. It differs from obstinacy: "setbacks can't change your morale, not that they can't change your mind."
- **Good judgement:** persistence often requires changing your mind. The persistent are rational and focus on expected value, which lets them work on unlikely-to-succeed things without being reckless.
- **A goal:** see below.

Anyone can do obstinacy ("kids and drunks and fools are best at it"). Very few people have enough of all five, but when they do "the results are magical."

## Choosing problems: be irrational at the top, pick a specific goal

- The persistent are often irrational at the very top of the decision tree. Between two problems of roughly equal expected value, the choice comes down to personal preference. They often classify projects into deliberately wide bands of expected value so the one they want still qualifies.
- Paul says this is fine empirically, for two reasons: people work harder on problems they love, and preferences among problems aren't random. Loving a problem others don't often means you've unconsciously noticed it is more important than they realize.
- A vague aim like "do something great" is the most inclusive motivator but useless in practice, because it doesn't tell you what to do at this moment.
- The goal must be fairly specific, but "not too specific, or you might miss a great discovery adjacent to what you're searching for, but not too general, or it won't work to motivate you."
- Tactic: start by erring on the small side. A too-broad goal gets you nowhere, while a too-small one at least keeps you moving. Once moving, expand the goal.
