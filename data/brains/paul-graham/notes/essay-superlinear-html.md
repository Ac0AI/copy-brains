## Returns for performance are superlinear, not linear

- Paul says one of the most important things he didn't understand as a child was that returns for performance are superlinear. Teachers and coaches implicitly said they were linear: "You get out what you put in."
- His business example: "If your product is only half as good as your competitor's, you don't get half as many customers. You get no customers, and you go out of business."
- He argues this is a feature of the world, not an artifact of capitalism's rules. The same pattern shows up in fame, power, military victories, knowledge and benefit to humanity. "The rich get richer."
- He says you can't understand the world without it, and that for the ambitious "this will be the wave you surf on."
- All superlinear returns reduce to two fundamental causes: exponential growth and thresholds.

## Exponential growth: why growth rate matters for startups

- Startups can grow exponentially. Some achieve high growth rates and most don't, so outcomes differ qualitatively. High-growth companies tend to become immensely valuable, while lower-growth ones may not survive.
- Y Combinator tells founders to focus on growth rate rather than absolute numbers. Paul gives three reasons:
  - It keeps founders from being discouraged early, when absolute numbers are low.
  - It works as a compass for deciding how to evolve the company.
  - The main one: focusing on growth rate tends to produce something that actually grows exponentially.
- If growth rate is proportional to performance *p*, the reward over time *t* is proportional to *pt*. Paul still finds that "startling" after decades of thinking about it.
- Whenever how well you do depends on how well you've done, you get exponential growth. Humans don't find this natural (the rice-on-a-chessboard story surprises every child).
- He notes sufficiently fast exponential growth guarantees both the shape and the magnitude of the return curve, because something that grows fast enough gets big even if it starts trivially small. Thresholds only guarantee the shape.
- Historical examples of exponential growth: conquest (territory makes your army stronger, which wins more territory) and scholarship (the more you know, the easier it is to learn more).

## Thresholds and winner-take-all dynamics

- A sports match is a step function: the winner gets one win whether they win by a lot or a little. The source is thresholds in the outcome, not competition itself. Thresholds exist even when you're alone, like proving a theorem or hitting a target.
- The two sources often coexist and feed each other:
  - Crossing thresholds leads to exponential growth (the winning side in a battle usually suffers less damage, so it is likelier to win again).
  - Exponential growth helps you cross thresholds: "in a market with network effects, a company that grows fast enough can shut out potential competitors."
- Fame combines both. It grows exponentially because fans bring new fans, and it is concentrated because "there's only so much room on the A-list in the average person's head."
- Thresholds alone don't make a game worth playing (Russian roulette has a threshold). "Seek competition" is also useless if the prize isn't worth it, and a clamoring crowd chasing one problem is often trumped by someone quietly working on another.
- His test for whether a threshold game is worth playing: if something is mediocre yet still popular, it could be a good idea to replace it. If people dislike a product yet still buy it, they'd presumably buy a better alternative.
- Caveat: be careful with this rule. Popular mediocrity often has a hidden reason, such as monopoly or regulation making competition hard, customers with bad taste, or broken buying procedures.

## How to find work with superlinear returns

- **Seek work that compounds.** It can compound directly (doing well in one cycle helps the next, as with building infrastructure, an audience or a brand), or by teaching you, since learning compounds.
- Learning-based compounding can feel like failure. You may miss your immediate goal but still be growing exponentially. This is why Silicon Valley tolerates failure, but only if you're learning from it.
- Heuristic: always be learning. If you're not learning, you're probably not on a path to superlinear returns. Don't over-optimize what you learn or limit it to things already known to be valuable, or "you'll lop off the outliers."
- **Look for fields where a few big winners outperform everyone else.** Superlinear returns imply inequality, so work where everyone does about the same is unlikely to have them. His examples: sports, politics, art, music, acting, directing, writing, math, science, starting companies and investing.
- Excluding sports, politics and fame effects, the remaining fields are exactly those where you must be independent-minded and your ideas must be novel, not just correct. In investing, believing a company will do well only pays if most other investors don't.
- For intellectual thresholds, he suggests being driven by curiosity rather than careerism. Problems that lead to new fields tend to seem mystifying but perhaps unimportant, while ones that seem legit but boring rarely lead to discoveries.

## Do things that don't scale: paying the initial cost

- Superlinear returns seem small at first ("At this rate, I'll never get anywhere"), but because the curve rises steeply at the far end, "it's worth taking extraordinary measures to get there."
- In startups this is "do things that don't scale." If you pay "a ridiculous amount of attention to your tiny initial set of customers, ideally you'll kick off exponential growth by word of mouth."
- The same applies to learning: make the initial effort to get a toehold, because it gets easier the more you learn.
- At the far end of the curve incremental effort is a bargain, and there is less competition there because people find the prospect intimidating and few even try. It's a bargain even to try.

## Why this territory is expanding, and who should pursue it

- The territory is growing in both directions: more types of work offer superlinear returns, and the returns themselves are larger. Two causes: progress in technology and the decreasing importance of organizations.
- Fifty years ago you needed an organization for resources, colleagues and distribution, so your prestige was the organization's. Organizations damped variation in returns. Now many ambitious projects need little initial funding, and there are new ways to learn, earn, find colleagues and reach audiences.
- Without that damping, outcomes vary more: people who do well do even better, and those who do badly do worse. "Exposing oneself to superlinear returns is not for everyone. Most people will be better off as part of the pool."
- Who should shoot for it: people who know they're so good they'll come out net ahead in a higher-variation world, and those, particularly the young, who can afford to risk trying to find out.
- He expects many new winners to be people institutions would never have let in, making the democratization of opportunity greater and more authentic than what institutions would offer themselves.
- Wealth now comes more from discovering or building something uniquely valuable than from capturing resources like an emperor, and the ratio has shifted dramatically toward discovery in the last half century.
- Work is not the same as a job. Your work is whatever you're studying or creating, a portfolio you carry between jobs.

## Paul's recipe for doing exceptional work

- To be an outlier you need to get nearly all the variables right. You must be interested in the work, since "mere diligence is not enough." Knowing what you're interested in matters even more in a superlinear world. Choose work that suits your circumstances (for example, energy-intensive work while young and without children).
- His one-paragraph recipe:
  - Choose work you have natural aptitude for and deep interest in.
  - Develop a habit of working on your own projects, however excitingly ambitious.
  - Work as hard as you can without burning out. This eventually brings you to a frontier of knowledge, which looks smooth from afar but is full of gaps. Explore the gaps, and one may expand into a whole new field.
  - Take as much risk as you can afford. "If you're not failing occasionally you're probably being too conservative."
  - Seek out the best colleagues, develop good taste, learn from the best examples, and be honest, especially with yourself.
  - Exercise, eat and sleep well. "When in doubt, follow your curiosity. It never lies."
- Luck matters, more so when working on your own than in an organization. The answer is to take multiple shots, which is another reason to start taking risks early.
- Ambition makes you climb existing peaks, but curiosity may be more powerful: "if you stick close enough to an interesting enough question, it may grow into a mountain beneath you."
