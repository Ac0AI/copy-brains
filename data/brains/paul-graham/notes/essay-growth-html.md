## A startup is a company designed to grow fast

- Paul defines a startup as "a company designed to grow fast." Growth is the only essential trait. Technology, venture funding and an "exit" are not required, and being newly founded does not make a company a startup.
- Most new companies are service businesses such as restaurants, barbershops and plumbers. They are not startups. A search engine is designed to grow fast, and a barbershop is not.
- "Designed" means two things: intended, since most startups fail, and different by nature. He compares a redwood seedling with a bean sprout. Google was different from the beginning and was not "a barbershop whose founders were unusually lucky and hard-working."
- Growth works as a compass. If a founder knows growth is the goal, "you can use growth like a compass to make almost every decision you face." If you get growth, "everything else tends to fall into place."
- "Startup" is "a pole, not a threshold." Starting one is at first a declaration of ambition. It is like an actor who starts out as a waiter going to auditions.

## Two requirements for growing big: what people want and how to reach them

- To grow really big, a company must (a) make something lots of people want and (b) reach and serve all of those people. Most businesses are tightly constrained on (a) or (b). Successful startups are not.
- A barbershop does fine on (a), since almost everyone needs a haircut. It fails on (b) because it serves customers in person and few will travel far.
- Software solves (b) but can still be limited on (a). Software to teach Tibetan to Hungarian speakers reaches most of its market, but the market is small. Software to teach English to Chinese speakers is "startup territory."
- Strictly, what matters is a big market: customers times what they pay. Having too few customers is dangerous even if each pays a lot. Individual customers' power over you can turn you into "a de facto consulting firm." Paul advises erring toward the broadest type of product for the market.
- He thinks most people should not try to start startups. It is fine to build a software company constrained on (a), the way a restaurant is constrained on (b).

## Why startup ideas are hard to find

- The market is "(fairly) efficient." Big-market ideas draw ferocious competition because the prize is larger. Tibetan-for-Hungarians has little competition. English-for-Chinese has a lot.
- The constraints on an ordinary business protect it and define it. A bar in a neighborhood is "a sufficient idea for a small business." A search engine competes with the whole world. The biggest protection is not from competitors but from the difficulty of coming up with a new idea.
- Obvious ideas for large markets are already taken, so a startup usually has to work on something everyone else has overlooked.
- Paul says successful startups mostly don't start by consciously hunting for overlooked ideas. Founders are different enough that ideas few others see seem obvious to them. Later they may notice they are in a blind spot and deliberately stay there. Y Combinator helps founders step back and see what they found intuitively.
- Examples:
  - Wozniak wanted his own computer in 1975, an unusual problem then. He could build one, and chip advances made it affordable.
  - Page and Brin could tell existing search engines were not as good as they could be, and they knew how to improve them. As the web grew, their problem became everyone's.
  - In both cases the company was entrenched by the time the market was obvious to ordinary people.
- Technological change creates startup ideas in two ways. It changes solubility (Apple) or bigness (Google's web). Technology also makes formerly bad ideas good without anyone noticing, so being good at technology and facing problems it can solve is a strong combination.
- Startups create new ways of doing things, and Paul counts these as new technology in the broad sense. He corrects "How to Make Wealth": a startup is not only a small company taking on a hard technical problem.

## Measure growth rate, not absolute numbers

- A successful startup's growth has three phases:
  1. A slow or no-growth period while it figures out what it's doing.
  2. Rapid growth once it knows how to make something lots of people want and how to reach them.
  3. Slowing as it becomes a big company, from internal limits and market limits.
- Together these form an S-curve. The ascent defines the startup, and its length and slope set how big the company gets.
- "If there's one number every founder should always know, it's the company's growth rate." Without it you don't know if you're doing well or badly.
- "We get about a hundred new customers a month" is not a rate. What matters is the ratio of new customers to existing ones. A constant number of new customers each month means the growth rate is falling.
- At YC, growth is measured per week. Time before Demo Day is short, and early startups need frequent user feedback.
- Benchmarks during YC:
  - 5-7% a week is good.
  - 10% a week is exceptional.
  - 1% a week suggests the founders haven't yet figured out what they're doing.
- Revenue is the best thing to measure. For startups not yet charging, active users is the next best, since revenue will probably be a constant multiple of active users.
- Caveats:
  - Companies building a Facebook or Twitter-style product with no clear way to make money need a higher growth rate, because they need huge numbers of users to succeed at all.
  - Watch for fast spread with high churn. Net growth looks good until the potential users run out, then it stops suddenly.
  - Weekly measurement suits startups that have launched or can launch during YC. A startup building a new database probably won't do it. Launching something small and using growth rate as evolutionary pressure is valuable enough that any company that can start this way probably should.

## Use a weekly growth target as a decision rule

- YC advises startups to pick a growth rate they think they can hit and "just try to hit it every week." Hitting it means the week was a success. Missing it means failing at "the only thing that mattered," and founders should be alarmed.
- This turns starting a startup into an optimization problem, like optimizing code. It reduces a "bewilderingly multifarious problem" to one. Paul says programmers will find the narrow focus effective and surprisingly fast.
- Decisions get settled by the target: "anything that gets you the growth you need is ipso facto right." He lists these questions as examples:
  - Spend two days at a conference?
  - Hire another programmer?
  - Focus more on marketing?
  - Court a big customer?
  - Add feature x?
- Weekly measurement doesn't limit thinking to one week ahead. Missing a target makes founders want to avoid future misses. They might hire a programmer who won't help this week but will ship a feature in a month. He sets two conditions:
  - The distraction of hiring won't make them miss short-term numbers.
  - They are worried enough about whether they can keep hitting the numbers without the hire.
- "It's not that you don't think about the future, just that you think about it no more than necessary."
- Local maxima are a theoretical risk that "in practice never happens." Having to hit a number every week forces action, and "acting versus not acting is the high bit of succeeding." He says that nine times out of ten, sitting around strategizing is procrastination. Founders' intuitions about which hill to climb are usually better than they realize. Fairly good ideas are usually adjacent to better ones.
- Growth can discover the idea. If you modify your plan to keep hitting something like 10% weekly growth, you may end up with a quite different company, and "anything that grows consistently at 10% a week is almost certainly a better idea than you started with." Follow the growth constraint wherever it leads, as a scientist follows the truth.
- Paul warns against believing a successful startup is "the embodiment of some brilliant initial idea." Look for an idea that can evolve into a great one. Early adopters have different needs from the rest of the market. The idea that evolves into Facebook "is a site for Harvard undergrads," not a subset of Facebook.
- Don't cheat the metric. Within YC, "ipso facto right" excludes:
  - Buying users for more than their lifetime value.
  - Counting users as active when they're not.
  - Bleeding out invites at an increasing rate to manufacture a perfect growth curve.
  - Even if these fool investors, they throw off your own compass.

## Why small differences in growth rate matter

- Weekly rate and yearly multiple:

| Weekly | Yearly |
| :-: | :-: |
| 1% | 1.7x |
| 2% | 2.8x |
| 5% | 12.6x |
| 7% | 33.7x |
| 10% | 142.0x |

- A company making $1000 a month (a typical number early in YC) and growing 1% a week makes $7900 a month four years later. That is less than a good programmer's Silicon Valley salary. At 5% a week it makes $25 million a month after four years.
- A company growing 1% a week for 19 years would match one growing 5% a week for 4 years. Paul says such slow trajectories may be common in real estate development but are rare in technology, where slow growers tend not to grow as big.
- Human intuition is poor at exponential growth, so fast growth surprises even founders.
- "Small variations in growth rate produce qualitatively different outcomes." This is why startups behave differently from ordinary companies, such as raising money and getting acquired, and why they fail so often.

## Expected value, failure rates and why investors and acquirers want startups

- Expected value:
  - If a success could make a founder $100 million, a 1% chance of success gives an expected value of $1 million.
  - For the right people, such as the young Bill Gates, the odds might be 20% or even 50%.
  - In an efficient market, the number of failed startups should be proportionate to the size of the successes.
  - Most startups at any time are working on something that will go nowhere.
- Paul says to judge startups by the average outcome, not the median. Judging by the median makes the concept look like a fraud and requires inventing a bubble to explain why founders and investors act as they do.
- Utility of money varies by person. Younger or more ambitious founders have a flatter utility function, which may be part of why the most successful founders tend to be young.
- Why VCs like startups:
  - The return-to-risk ratio is good.
  - VCs are paid by getting capital back through an IPO or acquisition. An investor taking dividends from ordinary private companies would have to scrutinize the books closely, because controllers can funnel revenue to themselves, for example by buying overpriced components from a supplier they control.
  - Startup investments are easy to oversee because "the founders can't enrich themselves without also enriching the investors." This holds for the biggest winners, where all the returns come from.
- Why founders take VC money:
  - If you have a scalable idea and grow too slowly, competitors will grow instead. This is especially dangerous with network effects.
  - Raising money is like buying insurance. The most successful startups raise even when they are or could be profitable, because "raising money lets you _choose_ your growth rate."
  - The best startups can get money on terms they'd be crazy to refuse, because VCs need them more than they need the VCs.
- Why acquirers want startups:
  - A fast-growing company is valuable, and also dangerous because it might expand into the acquirer's territory. Most product acquisitions "have some component of fear," so acquirers often pay more than ordinary investors.
  - His example: it was a good thing eBay bought PayPal, since PayPal is responsible for 43% of eBay's sales and probably more of its growth.
  - Instagram was bought by Mark Zuckerberg because it was valuable and dangerous, and growth made it both.
  - Acquisitions are either of the business or just of the employees. He calls the latter "HR acquisitions" and says acquirers see them as closer to hiring bonuses.

## Starting a startup means searching for a rare fast-growth idea

- Growth explains the whole ecosystem. It is why startups usually work on technology, why starting one is a rational economic choice, why VCs invest, why top startups raise money they don't need, and why they get acquisition offers.
- Paul says understanding growth is what starting a startup "consists of." You are committing to solve a harder type of problem than ordinary businesses do: searching for one of the rare ideas that generates rapid growth. Because these ideas are so valuable, finding one is hard.
- The startup is "the embodiment of your discoveries so far."
- He compares founding to becoming a research scientist: you don't know which problems are soluble, but you commit to discovering something no one knew. A founder is "an economic research scientist. Most don't discover anything that remarkable, but some discover relativity."
