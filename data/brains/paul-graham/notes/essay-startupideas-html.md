## Start with a problem you have, not an idea you invent

- Paul's core claim: "The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas. It's to look for problems, preferably problems you have yourself."
- The best ideas share three traits: the founders want it themselves, they can build it themselves, and few others realize it is worth doing. He says Microsoft, Apple, Yahoo, Google and Facebook all began this way.
- Working on your own problem guarantees the problem exists. He calls solving problems no one has "by far the most common mistake startups make."
- His own example: in 1995 he started a company to put art galleries online. Galleries did not want to be online. He spent 6 months on it because he "didn't pay attention to users" and worked from an invented model of the world. He only noticed when he tried to get users to pay.
- Test he asks YC founders: "Would you use this thing yourself, if you hadn't written it?" The answer is often no.
- The need must be strong. Any made-up idea can be described after the fact as something you need. Compare it to how much Drew Houston needed Dropbox, or Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia needed Airbnb.

## "Sitcom" ideas and why plausible ideas are dangerous

- YC calls made-up ideas "sitcom" startup ideas. A TV writer has to invent a startup idea, and it sounds plausible but is bad.
- Example: a social network for pet owners. Friends with pets do not say "I would never use this." They say "Yeah, maybe I could see using something like that." Summed across a population, that reaction yields zero users.
- Trying to think up ideas is doubly dangerous: it yields few good ideas, and bad ones that sound plausible enough to fool you.
- Portal-for-x and social-network-for-x ideas are "stone soup": you post a sign saying "this is the place for people interested in x" and expect them to show up. The statistics on millions of interested people mislead, because one person may have 20 affinities and will not visit 20 communities regularly.
- Paul does not claim to know the pet idea is bad. He knows it the way he knows randomly generated DNA would not produce a viable organism. The set of plausible ideas is many times larger than the set of good ones, and many good ones do not sound plausible. If all you know is that it sounds plausible, assume it is bad.

## Build a well, not a broad shallow hole

- At launch, some users must need the product urgently, not just "see themselves using it one day." That initial group is usually small, because anything many people urgently needed and a v1 could deliver would probably already exist.
- Choose between something a large number of people want a little and something a small number want a lot. Paul says choose the latter: "nearly all good startup ideas are of that type."
- Picture a graph with how many people want it on x and how much on y, with y inverted so companies are holes. Google is an immense crater. A new startup digs either a broad shallow hole or a narrow deep well.
- Examples of wells: Microsoft's Altair Basic (only a couple thousand Altair owners, who otherwise programmed in machine language) and the first Facebook (exclusive to Harvard students, a few thousand of them who wanted it a lot).
- Question to ask: who wants this right now, so much that they will use a crappy v1 from an unknown two-person startup? If you cannot answer, the idea is probably bad.
- Depth is what you need. Narrowness is a byproduct of optimizing for depth and speed.
- Activation energy matters. Enterprise software sold through traditional channels has very high activation energy, so you would need to be far better. Switching to a new search engine has low activation energy.
- A well is not sufficient. It needs a fast path out. Facebook worked because colleges are similar, so what works at Harvard works at any college, then you let everyone else in. Microsoft's path: Basic for Altair, Basic for other machines, other languages, operating systems, applications, IPO.
- Paths out are hard to see, even for experienced people. Airbnb's founders initially only planned to rent floor space during conventions.

## Live in the future and build what's missing

- Paul says startup ideas should be "noticed," not "thought up." The recipe: "Live in the future, then build what's missing" (combining Paul Buchheit's "live in the future" with Pirsig).
- If you are the right sort of person at the leading edge of a fast-changing field, your hunches are more likely to be right. "The way to have good startup ideas is to become the sort of person who has them."
- You can be at the leading edge as a user, not only a builder. Mark Zuckerberg already lived online in 2004, when most 40-year-olds would have been horrified at publishing their lives semi-publicly.
- Organic ideas come from an external stimulus hitting a prepared mind: Gates and Allen hearing about the Altair, Drew Houston forgetting a USB stick. Many people had those stimuli; only the prepared acted.
- Apple, Yahoo, Google and Facebook were not even supposed to be companies at first. They grew from things founders built because there was a gap.
- Anyone reasonably smart can reach an edge of programming (e.g. building mobile apps) in about a year. A startup consumes at least 3-5 years, so a year of preparation is a reasonable investment.
- Programming is not necessary but is "for the foreseeable future sufficient." It lets you think "I'll try building an initial version tonight." It is best when you are also the target user, since the build-and-test cycle happens in one head.
- Give it time. Houston worked on an SAT prep startup before Dropbox, a much better idea and a better fit for his skills. Sam Altman's point: taking time to find an idea is like an undervalued stock, because few founders do it, while many compete over mediocre made-up ideas.
- Preparation gets harder with age, because the walls between career paths get higher.

## How to notice ideas: turn off the filters

- Once living in the future, look for what seems missing. Turn off every other filter, especially "Could this be a big company?" Apply that test later.
- The most powerful filter is taking the status quo for granted. Question things: why is your inbox overflowing, because of volume or because it is hard to get email out? Is an inbox the optimal tool?
- Pay attention to things that chafe you. Annoyance can mean you are living in the future.
- A good problem should feel obvious to you. At Viaweb, online stores were hand-built HTML pages, and it was obvious to the programmer founders that they should be generated by software.
- Keep a background process running: work on hard problems out of curiosity while a second self notes gaps and anomalies. Possible journal: each night, list the raw gaps and anomalies you noticed, not startup ideas.
- Work on projects that seem cool. Things dismissed as "toys" often become important: microcomputers (then "hobbyists"), BackRub, the Facebook. YC is excited when startups look like something forum know-it-alls would dismiss as toys.
- Refined version: "Live in the future and build what seems interesting."
- Paul Buchheit's trick for bad ideas: tell founders to go sell the product ASAP before building it. They learn nobody wants it and "very often come back with a real idea that they discovered in the process."

## Schlep and unsexy filters

- Most programmers want to write brilliant code, push it to a server and get paid, avoiding tedious real-world problems. This preference is so widespread that convenient ideas have been "stripped pretty clean." Messy, tedious ideas are "just sitting there."
- Stripe is his example of turning off the schlep filter. Thousands of programmers knew payments were painful but unconsciously shrank from them. Stripe's schlep was tolerable, and because others stayed away, users were "desperately waiting," so Stripe did not have to try hard to be heard in user acquisition.
- The unsexy filter blocks problems you despise rather than fear. He and his cofounders overcame it for Viaweb: they were not interested in ecommerce per se but saw it needed solving.
- The schlep filter matters more, because it is more likely an illusion and a worse form of self-indulgence. Any sufficiently good idea has as many schleps (investors, hiring, firing).
- The unsexy filter is partly useful: at the leading edge, what you find sexy correlates with what is valuable, and you work harder on ideas you like.
- Strategy: seek out ideas that are unsexy or involve schleps. "Don't try to start Twitter... Make something unsexy that people will pay you for."

## Competition and picking an idea when you need one now

- A good idea feels obvious, so you feel late. Worrying you are late is a sign of a good idea. Ten minutes of web searching usually settles it. Startups are so rarely killed by competitors that you can almost discount it, unless a competitor has lock-in that stops users choosing you.
- Rule: focus on users, not competitors. If you have something no competitor does and some users urgently need it, you have a beachhead. A small beachhead is big enough if its people do something many more will do later (e.g. a product that only works on the newest phones).
- Err toward ideas with competitors. Inexperienced founders give them too much credit; success depends far more on you. "Better a good idea with competitors than a bad one without."
- A crowded market signals demand and that existing solutions are not good enough. You need a thesis about what incumbents overlook, more precise than "an x that doesn't suck." Best is that they lacked the courage of their convictions, as with search engines before Google, which shied away from the fact that the better they worked, the faster users would leave.
- Any success either enters an existing market with a secret weapon (Google) or a market that looks small but is big (Microsoft).
- Plan B recipes if you need an idea now (more discipline required, because you will see many bad ideas):
  - Stay in areas where you have expertise, since you can judge quality there.
  - Start with things you need. Ask: in a previous job, did you say "Why doesn't someone make x? If someone made x we'd buy it in a second"?
  - Look at what is unusual about you, especially if you are different in a way people will increasingly be. Being young (under 23, say) helps understand peers; Hotmail began as a tool for talking about the founders' previous startup idea.
  - Talk to many people about gaps, tedium and annoyances in their work, without forcing it.
  - For a blurry need that is not yours, act like a consultant retained to solve one user's problem. Nearly all the code will be reusable, and you start certain you have reached the bottom of the well.
  - Make others' problems your own: Rajat Suri of E la Carte took a job as a waiter before writing software for restaurants.
  - Ask what you wish someone else would build and what you would pay for right now.
  - Look at dying industries and imagine what will later be called a replacement. Replacements usually come in "from the side," starting with a small, important market the big players ignore, ideally with disdain. Example: HP turned down Wozniak's computer partly because it used a TV as a monitor, which was "intolerably déclassé."
  - Look for waves (e.g. falling gene sequencing and 3D printing costs). Looking for waves simulates the organic method: "you don't have to look for waves; you are the wave."
- Warning: when an idea you came up with deliberately feels like an inspired "just knew" idea, you are probably mistaken.

## Advice for students

- Paul advises students to ratchet themselves into the future rather than learn "entrepreneurship," which you learn by doing. Classes only teach "the words for things."
- Clash of domains is fruitful. A programmer learning another field (e.g. genetics, or a summer job at a biotech company) will see software-solvable problems, since that domain's people are less likely to have solved them with software and the newcomer does not take the status quo for granted.
- Or just build things, preferably with other students. That produces organic ideas with organic founding teams, "empirically... the best combination." Do not worry about making them startups; "that's premature optimization."
- Microsoft and Facebook both started in January, which at Harvard is Reading Period with no classes. The best a college can do is "leave them alone in the right way."
- Beware of research. A project undergrads' friends all start using is likely a good idea; a PhD dissertation almost never is (for IT startups; biotech differs). Research is too narrow a constraint to also solve users' problems.
