## Funding stages and what this essay covers

- Paul describes a typical path in three phases: (1) a few tens of thousands from something like Y Combinator or individual angels, (2) a few hundred thousand to a few million to build the company, (3) later rounds to accelerate growth once the company is clearly succeeding.
- Reality is messier: some companies raise twice in phase 2, some skip phase 1, and YC sees more companies arriving with hundreds of thousands already raised.
- This advice is for phase 2, the round startups raise on Demo Day.
- Fundraising is hard like lifting a weight (convincing people to part with large sums is irreducibly hard) and hard like a puzzle (an alien world to most founders). He wants to remove the puzzle part.
- Investors are pinched between fear of backing startups that fizzle and fear of missing startups that take off. Because winners grow fast, "if you wait till a startup is obviously a success, it's too late." Investors would like to wait, but if they wait too long others take the deal, so they all wait as long as they can and then rush at once.
- Investors are often opaque and sometimes deliberately misleading, which combines badly with founders' wishful thinking. His "rule number zero": the rules exist because powerful forces push you the other way, and inexperienced founders cannot trust their intuitions.

## When to raise, and fundraising mode

- Rapid growth, not fundraising, is what makes a company a startup. Raise only if outside money would help you grow faster and your growth potential makes it easy to attract. If you wouldn't want to grow faster, or money wouldn't help, don't raise.
- Don't raise before you can convince investors. You waste time and burn your reputation with those investors.
- Fundraising is distracting because it becomes "the top idea in your mind," not because of the hours. Early-stage growth comes mostly from founders making it happen, so growth drops sharply if they look away. A startup should be either in fundraising mode or not, and when raising, give it your whole attention, finish fast and get back to work.
- Outside fundraising mode, take money only if it needs no convincing (zero time on meetings or materials) and no negotiation. Example: a reputable investor on a standard-paperwork convertible note, uncapped or capped at a good valuation.
- If an investor says they're ready but wants you to come in for one meeting with partners, say no politely while not in fundraising mode: "that's fundraising." Say you're focusing on the company and will get back to them when you're raising.
- Investors try to lure you into fundraising early to get a shot before everyone else. A cold email from an associate at a VC firm is not worth a meeting even in fundraising mode: "I don't know of a single VC investment that began with an associate cold-emailing a startup." Associates have no decision-making power, and partners discriminate against deals that reach them that way.
- Delay "just chat" meetings with partners too. "Investors never just want to meet and chat." Avoid casual conversations with potential acquirers as well; they can distract even more than fundraising.
- Tacking on a few investors after you leave fundraising mode is fine if they need no convincing or negotiation.

## Getting intros and reading investors

- In phase 2 you need introductions. Best: a well-known investor who just invested in you, so ask each committed investor to introduce you to others they respect (a suggestion from Joshua Reeves: ask for two more intros each). Next best: founders of companies the investor funded. Also lawyers and reporters. Don't ask investors who said no for intros; it is often an anti-recommendation.
- AngelList, FundersClub and WeFunder are auxiliary sources. Raise first from leads you get yourself, which are on average better investors, and it is easier to raise on those sites once you've closed well-known investors.
- "Hear no till you hear yes": treat investors as saying no until they give a definite offer with no contingencies. Investors lead you on right up to the no, and some never say no and just stop replying, hoping for a free option if you turn out hot. Some use language that sounds like a commitment but isn't, and wishful founders meet them halfway.
- If you think an investor committed, get them to confirm it in writing. Till they do, regard them as saying no. YC built a protocol for this.
- Know where you stand by actions, not words. Never leave a meeting without asking what happens next: what they need to decide, whether another meeting, what internal steps, how long. Vague or resistant answers mean assume the worst; serious investors are already running through the path to wiring money.
- If you're inexperienced, confess it and ask how their process works and where you are in it. "Larry and Sergey were noobs at fundraising." Another signal (Ian Hogarth): serious investors expend resources on you after the first meeting.
- Don't imitate a smooth salesperson. A founder who ended meetings with "So, can I count you in?" pulled it off, but "nothing is more unconvincing" than a nerdy founder delivering lines meant for a smooth one. Be a good nerd.

## Running the process: parallel, expected value, first commitment, closing

- Talk to investors in parallel, not serially, as breadth-first search weighted by expected value. Parallel saves time and gives each investor the pressure of the others.
- Expected value = probability they say yes × how good it would be. An eminent hard-to-convince investor can equal an easy-to-convince obscure angel. An obscure angel who invests little and needs several meetings has very low expected value; meet such investors last if at all. Some of the most useless investors are also the highest maintenance.
- Be disciplined assigning probabilities: don't let how much you want an investor inflate your estimate of how much they want you. This also makes you drift away from flaky investors at the same rate they drift from you.
- Packing meetings tightly is good, but too tight leaves less time for the pitch to evolve. Some founders schedule a few lame investors first to debug the pitch.
- The biggest factor in most investors' opinion of you is the opinion of other investors, so the first substantial commitment can be half the total difficulty. $50k from a well-known VC or angel is usually enough to get things rolling; friends and family money usually doesn't count.
- Signalling risk (a prestigious VC's small seed makes others read a later pass as a sign you suck) rarely matters in practice. If you can choose, excluding VC firms plays it safe, but it isn't critical.
- "It's not a deal till the money's in the bank." Founders say "We've raised $800,000" with zero in the bank. Investors are prone to buyer's remorse, and events (a competitor appears, a C&D, a cofounder quits, market shocks) cluster around fundraising. After a yes, get the timetable and babysit it until the money arrives; you may have to hunt angels down for a check. Inexperienced investors are likeliest to welch, though even top-tier VCs have.
- Competitor lawsuit threats timed to your raise: be forthright with investors. Experienced ones know the trick, and evasiveness alarms them more than disclosure.

## Investors who won't "lead," and the amount you raise

- Some investors say "I want to invest, but I don't lead," or "I'll invest once you have a lead." Paul calls this a "contemptible subspecies." The term originally meant the lead negotiated terms in a round with shared paperwork. Series A rounds still work that way, but earlier raising now happens investor by investor, so "lead" now just means their interest is a function of other investors' interest.
- Translate "I don't lead" as "No, except yes if you turn out to be a hot deal," which is every investor's default view of every startup, so it tells you nothing. Their initial expected value is zero; talk to them last if at all. It's fine if "lead" means they invest unilaterally and also help raise more. Claims that they'll only invest if you raise $x elsewhere because you'd otherwise be "undercapitalized" are "almost always bullshit."
- Investors who decide quickly are extra valuable early because getting the first yes matters most.
- When investors ask how much you're raising, it is like a salesperson asking your budget: they check fit with their check size and judge your ambition, reasonableness and progress. You shouldn't have one fixed number. Have multiple plans: e.g. profitable with no more money; a few hundred thousand lets you hire one or two smart friends; a couple million, a whole engineering team. Match the plan to the investor: a series-A-only VC gets your most expensive plan; an angel writing $20k checks to a first-time raiser gets the cheapest.
- Upper limit rule of thumb: people you want to hire × $15k × 18 months. Example: 20 people × $15k × 18 = $5.4 million. $15k per person per month includes benefits and office space and is deliberately high as a margin of error; don't actually spend that much. Add other costs like manufacturing at the end.
- Underestimate how much you want. If you want $500k, say $250k: at $150k you're more than half done, which signals you're doing well and must be decided on quickly; at a $500k target you'd be under a third done and read as stalling. Saying $250k doesn't cap you; most very successful fundraisers end up raising more than planned. He says not to lie, just lower initial expectations. Metaphor: angle of attack. Announce a $5 million series A out of the gate without a very strong position and "you not only won't get that but won't get anything"; start low, build speed, then raise the angle.

## Profitability, valuation and terms

- Be profitable if you can. Include a plan for raising zero dollars: "We'll succeed no matter what, but raising money will help us do it faster." Fundraising resembles dating: "No one wants you if you seem desperate," and the best way not to seem desperate is not to be. This is why YC urges low expenses and ramen profitability before Demo Day.
- Paul distinguishes type A fundraising (you don't need the money) from type B (you do). Inexperienced founders see famous startups doing type A and raise without a path to profitability, doing type B, then find it hard and unpleasant. Without profitability, fundraising from strength needs other advantages like extraordinary growth or exceptionally formidable founders, and gets harder over time. One YC founder said he'd leave up-front-capital-intensive ideas to founders with established reputations.
- Don't optimize for valuation. Priorities: (1) get the money you need, (2) good investors, (3) valuation, "at best third." Fundraising is not the test; revenue is. Being proud of your valuation is "like being proud of your college grades."
- Evidence: Dropbox and Airbnb, the most successful companies YC had funded at the time, raised after YC at pre-money valuations of $4 million and $2.6 million.
- The first committing investor sets your default asking price. Raise later investors at higher prices if there's interest. If the price was set too high you can lower it (give earlier investors the same terms), but you may lose leads. To avoid being stuck with an over-eager investor's high price, take their money on an uncapped convertible note with an MFN clause, so the cap is set by the next investors. Also a convertible-note warning: a very high valuation from an eager investor can set an impossible target for the next round.
- Lower prices are easier to close even though they shouldn't be: phase 2 prices vary at most 10x while big successes return at least 100x, yet many investors care about price. A startup investors like but won't back at cap $x may close at $x/2.
- Yes/no before valuation: if an investor pushes you to name a price before you've closed anyone, say valuation isn't the priority, you haven't thought much about it, you want partners, and you should first discuss whether they want to invest at all. Closing the first investor is the priority. YC usually tells founders to give the first committing investor as low a price as they need to, provided they handle "valuation sensitive" investors correctly (YC partners can estimate a market price if an investor insists you set it).
- "Valuation sensitive" investors are compulsive negotiators who eat time. Never approach them first; approach near the end and say "this is the price everyone else has paid; take it or leave it." If one pops up early, slow down your interactions. If you get a lowball offer, treat it as a backup offer and delay responding; lowballing "is a dick move that should be met with the corresponding countermove."

## Accepting offers, dilution and team

- Accept offers greedily (like a greedy algorithm that doesn't look ahead). If someone makes an acceptable offer, take it; with multiple incompatible offers, take the best. Don't reject an acceptable offer hoping for a better one. As you near your target, the threshold for "acceptable" rises. Roll up investors as they say yes.
- Offers last for stretches of time. With an acceptable offer that is incompatible with others, tell other investors you have an offer good enough to accept and give them a few days. Exploding offers: three working days is acceptable; shorter signals a sketchy investor and you can usually call the bluff. The best investors explode less (Fred Wilson never gives exploding offers). Respond promptly to clean offers with no deadline.
- "Get the best investors" rarely conflicts with greedy acceptance if you talk in parallel and push back on short deadlines. The best investors are the most selective and reject nearly everyone, so trading a definite offer from an acceptable investor for a potential offer from a better one is a bad average-case trade. In phase 1 they do conflict, because incubators stagger schedules; apply so the ones you want most decide first.
- If a series A investor starts talking, keep taking smaller investments until they give a termsheet. Convertible notes convert into the A. Tell the A investor about each smaller investment as it happens; you owe them cap table updates, and it pressures them to act. They can't legitimately ask you to stop until they give a termsheet with a no-shop clause. Relax a little only if the investor has a great reputation, is clearly moving fast, and a third party like YC is involved.
- Don't sell more than 25% in phase 2, on top of under 15% in phase 1. Above about 40% total sold, VCs worry about founder motivation and an A gets harder. On uncapped notes, guess the eventual valuation conservatively. Tentatively assume the path to huge passes through an A; Weebly skipped it, reaching profitability on a $650k seed after bad terms in fall 2008.
- Have one person handle fundraising, the CEO and most formidable founder, even if another founder is the salesperson. Insulate the others from the details. Bring all founders only to meet an investor who will invest a lot, as a final step ("like introducing a girl/boyfriend to your parents"). A single founder in the room also lets you say "I need to circle back with my co-founder" instead of negotiating in real time against professional negotiators.
- Growth still matters during the raise: if numbers grow between meetings investors get hot to close; flat or down numbers give them cold feet.
- You need a one-page executive summary: matter-of-fact language on what you'll do, why it's a good idea, and progress so far, to remind investors who saw many startups. A deck is traditional (Sequoia describes contents), but Paul is ambivalent and many successful phase 2 startups never make one. Materials will leak; treat it as a cost of business (he can't name a startup whose outcome it affected). Don't send a deck or summary before a meeting; requesting it is a sign they're not interested.

## Stopping, pitfalls, and being nice

- Stop fundraising when "you start to get a lot of air in the straw," like drinking through a straw near the end of the liquid.
- Don't get addicted: good fundraisers can enjoy lunches with famous investors more than listening to users complain about bugs, and start to feel already successful and lose their taste for schleps. "When I see a startup with young founders that is fabulously successful at fundraising, I mentally decrease my estimate of the probability that they'll succeed." The opposite risk is demoralization from rejection.
- Don't raise too much. A company's valuation is expected to rise each round, so a $30 million post-money in phase 2 means the next round needs at least $50 million pre-money, and "you have to be doing really, really well" for that. More money also means more spending and more people, which hurts profitability and makes it harder to change direction. If you raise a huge amount, don't spend it.
- The bar is higher next time: "Assume the money you raise in phase 2 will be the last you ever raise." Investors now spray money at early startups and ruthlessly cull at the next stage. In phase 3 the experiment must have worked, usually shown by profitability, so phase 3 is usually type A fundraising.
- Two ways startups hose themselves between phases 2 and 3: being too slow to become profitable (raise two years' money, make no effort for a year, then can't), and letting expenses grow too fast by hiring too many. Don't hire 8 people right after raising; wait for growth to justify them. VCs tell you to spend too much, partly because they solve problems with money and partly because they want to sell you more of your company later. "Don't listen to them."
- Be nice. Playing hardball per these rules (refusing meetings, slowing slow investors, treating contingent offers as no, leaving some out) requires cushioning with soft words. Blame YC or "PG said startups shouldn't ___, and since we're new to fundraising we feel like we have to play it safe." Never seem arrogant, especially when you're doing well: "pride goeth before a fall."
- Be nice to investors who reject you too. The best aren't wedded to their first opinion and often invest in phase 3; those who spent time deciding probably came close, often with an internal champion needing a bit more evidence. Treat a rejection as the start of a relationship.

## The one-sentence version

- "Avoid investors till you decide to raise money, and then when you do, talk to them all in parallel, prioritized by expected value, and accept offers greedily."
- Don't add complicated optimizations or let investors introduce complications. Fundraising is a means; the goal is to get it over with and return to "making things and talking to users." His closing line: "don't leave the path."
