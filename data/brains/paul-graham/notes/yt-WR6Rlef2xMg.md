## Why go to the center of your field

- Paul says that whenever many people work intensely on something, one place is its center: Paris for painting in 1870, Göttingen for math in 1900, Hollywood for movies in 1950. Ambitious people there always ask "Should I go?"
- His answer is always yes. "You can go there for a bit and then come back, but you should at least go."
- The logic is the same as moving from a small village to the capital. It doesn't change when you cross "a dotted line on a map."
- What you get is the best peers. The talent pool grows in two ways: the people are better and there are more of them, and they cluster together.
- He compares a YC batch to a room like the one he's speaking in: "every dinner feels like this," every week for 3 months.

## Serendipitous meetings and speed

- Paul says serendipitous meetings are "enormously important." Biographies of people who did great things constantly feature an unplanned meeting that changed their life.
- He is unsure why unplanned meetings beat planned ones. His candidate explanations:
  - There are simply more of them, so more outliers are the unplanned kind.
  - Planned meetings are too conservative. Needing a reason beforehand "lops off the outliers," like deliberately trying to find startup ideas.
  - Unplanned meetings involve more selection. You can decide after a few sentences whether to keep talking.
- In a big center you have more of these meetings with people working on the same ambitious thing.
- Things also move faster in centers. People are better and more confident, so more decisive, and they egg one another on, both encouraging and competing.
- In villages, people "half had" ideas and never acted. Years later, when a big startup appears, they say "I thought of that."

## Why Silicon Valley investors decide faster

- Paul says Valley investors decide much faster than European ones, partly because they are more confident and partly because there is much more competition.
- Investors there can't sit on a good idea: "the more right this investor is, the more likely that opportunity is to time out." The more right you are, the less time you can wait.
- Example: Yuri Sagalov invested immediately on meeting Max because "anybody else who meets this guy is going to invest in him."
- Investors complain about high valuations and being rushed. Empirically it doesn't hurt them: Silicon Valley investors get better returns than European investors despite deciding so quickly.

## Respect back home, and the Dropbox story

- Paul says investors outside Silicon Valley implicitly assume any local startup is second rate ("No one is a prophet in their own country"). He says it's not uniquely Swedish.
- Going to Silicon Valley reverses this, and you rise in local investors' opinion. Sometimes you only need to announce you've been accepted to Y Combinator.
- Dropbox, summer 2007, was in a Boston batch. A big Boston VC firm had watched them for a year, giving encouragement and advice but no money.
- Boston investors were so lame that YC flew Boston startups to Silicon Valley for a second demo day so they could raise money.
- Dropbox came to Sequoia's attention there. When the Boston firm heard, it faxed founder Drew a term sheet with a blank valuation: "Let us invest."
- It was too late. Drew went with Sequoia. In 2018 Dropbox was the first YC company to go public.

## Seeing how big a fish you are

- Paul says the biggest advantage of a center is what it does to you. In a small pond you don't know how big a fish you are. In a big pond you can measure yourself against known big fish.
- Surprisingly often the news is good. You meet someone like Brian Chesky or Sam Altman and think: impressive, but "not a different species from me. I could do what he did if I worked that hard."
- He calls this the most important part, and the condition matters. It's "I could be like that guy if I worked as hard." Startups are really hard.
- Seeing such people sets a new standard you have to meet. It makes the goal seem "no longer... an impossible goal, just a hard one." For an ambitious person, "there is nothing better than a high but definite threshold."
- He uses Max (a Swedish founder at the event, whom he jokingly calls "the Swedish fish") as the example: he is about as big a fish as they get, and you can see how hard he works.
- Big fish who speak at YC try to seem accessible and encouraging, not frightening. That's why fewer people than expected conclude "I could never do what they do."

## Pay-it-forward culture

- Paul says in Silicon Valley "people help you for no reason." Saying so there would sound as odd as saying "in Sweden the streets are clean for no reason."
- He asked a founder who had just moved from England how it differed from expectations. He recommends this as the best question to ask anyone who has tried something new: "How is it different than you expected?"
- The founder said the surprise was how helpful everyone was. Every conversation seemed to end with "What can I do to help you?" In England, apparently not. Paul says this is not politeness, since English people are more polite than Americans.
- His explanation for how it evolved: Silicon Valley is where people go from nobodies to billionaires fastest. Someone who was kind to nobodies ended up with powerful friends, and probably rich. Even if it started as calculation, it no longer is. The custom is about 60 years old and now seems like how everyone behaves.
- Ron Conway is his example. He helps people all day, doesn't track whether they're his portfolio companies, and doesn't remember most favors. That lets him operate on a much larger scale, like honesty: you don't have to track lies.
- With many people doing this "there is no longer a conservation law for favors. There's just more favors." Paul and Ron have traded favors for 20 years and he assumes Ron is ahead.
- He says moving to the Valley will make you more helpful to others.

## How Sweden can thrive as a startup hub

- Paul says both questions have the same answer: go for a bit, then come back. Coming back is one of the best things you can do for Stockholm as a startup hub.
- He says it isn't controversial. It would not have helped 19th-century mathematicians to boycott Göttingen and stay to develop national math. Government fellowships of the time were conditional on leaving and studying abroad.
- Going to Silicon Valley and returning helps Sweden in three ways:
  - Your own startup improves, raising the average quality of local startups.
  - You likely bring back money from Silicon Valley investors.
  - You import Silicon Valley culture, which evolved over decades to be optimal for startups. He calls it compatible with Swedish culture, since it has the high-trust part.
- He thinks Sweden should lose the "tall-poppy thing" anyway.
- He admits it sounds like "talking my own book," but says the best way to spend time in the Valley is to do YC, which is designed to concentrate these advantages.
