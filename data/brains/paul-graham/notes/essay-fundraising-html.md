## Why fundraising is brutal and why morale is the real risk

- Paul calls fundraising "the second hardest part of starting a startup." The hardest is making something people want, which is why most startups die.
- Investors judge startups the way customers judge products, not the way bosses judge employees. A valiant effort that fails earns no credit for this startup. It might help with the next one.
- Raising money is harder than selling to customers because there are so few investors. A startup rarely has more than 10 interested, so one investor's randomness can swing the outcome.
- Paul says all investors, YC included, are "by ordinary standards incompetent." They make big decisions about things they don't understand, so they get skittish. One day they seem ready to write a check, and the next they don't return calls. He says this is indecision, not game-playing.
- Investors' strongest influence on each other is each other's opinion, so there is no damping. Nobody wants a "bargain" startup that everyone else hates.
- His advice: "The most important thing is not to let fundraising get you down." Startups live or die on morale, and lost morale becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy.
- The biggest danger is surprise, that founders cruise through the early steps and then give up when fundraising turns out to be hard. His fix is to warn them in advance.

## Bootstrapping is really consulting, and the trade-off

- Paul says raising money is usually the lesser evil, but it still has to be treated as a threat to survival. The process itself can kill a startup.
- Founders need money to live on, and "you can't create instant customers." Viaweb charged about $140 per user per month, yet it would have taken at least a year for revenue to cover even its "paltry" costs. The founders didn't have a year of savings, so they could not have bootstrapped it.
- Once you exclude founders who funded themselves with savings or a day job, "bootstrapped" companies either (a) got really lucky or (b) began as consulting companies and gradually became product companies.
- Consulting is "the only option you can count on," but the pain is spread over years. That delay can be fatal if the idea is obvious. Delicious could be built on the side only because no one else saw the idea.
- Famous bootstrapped companies are famous because they are rare. "If it worked so well, it would be the norm."
- He expects bootstrapping to get easier as startups get cheaper, but "technology tends to get dramatically cheaper, but living expenses don't."
- His framing: "choose your pain," either the short, sharp pain of raising money or the chronic ache of consulting. For the same total pain, raising money is the better choice because new technology is usually more valuable now than later.
- A VC who read the essay told Paul they avoid companies bootstrapped with consulting because it "creates very bad behaviors/instincts that are hard to erase from a company's culture."

## Expectations, focus and conservatism

**Have low expectations**
- Fundraising kills morale because it is much harder than founders expected. Lower expectations mean less disappointment.
- Assume investors will let you down, and acquirers too. A YC mantra: "Deals fall through." Paul says "the predictive power of this simple rule is amazing."
- Deals don't progress linearly like other human interactions, and they often fall through at the last moment. The other party often doesn't decide what it wants until then. Founders should "tie yourself to the mast" and become "pathologically cynical."
- It is flattering when eminent investors show interest, which makes it easy to believe the raise will be quick. It hardly ever is.

**Keep working on the startup**
- Fundraising sucks up attention. One investor meeting a day burns the whole day, counting travel, prep and afterthought.
- Best option: one founder handles investors while the others keep building. This works better with 3 founders than 2, and when the CEO isn't also the lead developer. Even then the company moves at about half speed.
- Usually the company stalls. A "2 week interruption" turns into a 4-month one, and a company that hasn't done anything new in 4 months stops looking dynamic. Investors lose interest partly because of "the damage done by their own indecision."
- Put the startup first: fit investor meetings into spare moments in the development schedule, not the reverse. Keep releasing features, growing traffic, doing deals and getting written about. That makes meetings more productive and keeps your own morale up, which investors use to judge you.

**Be conservative**
- As conditions worsen, the best strategy gets more conservative. Paul advises approaching fundraising "as if it were always going badly."
- What he tells most funded startups: if someone reputable offers funding on reasonable terms, take it. Some startups held out for a better offer and got it, but he'd give the same advice again: "Who knows how many bullets were in the gun they were playing Russian roulette with?"
- If an investor seems interested, don't let them sit. Interest isn't real until you try to convert it into money. A hot prospect should be closed now or written off, and unless you already have enough funding, that means close now.
- "Startups don't win by getting great funding rounds, but by making great products."

## Flexible round sizing and how to answer VC questions

- Two VC questions not to answer: "Who else are you talking to?" and "How much are you trying to raise?"
- Paul says VCs don't expect an answer to the first. They ask it just in case. The optimal answer is to say naming names would be improper while implying you're talking to many VCs about to give you term sheets. Only try this if you understand how, because "nothing annoys VCs more than clumsy efforts to manipulate them."
- On the second question, he says you shouldn't have a fixed amount you need to raise. The fixed-amount custom is left over from when startups needed factories or 50 hires.
- Instead, tell investors there are several routes depending on the amount:
  - As little as $50k pays for founders' food and rent for a year.
  - A couple hundred thousand gets office space and a few smart hires from school.
  - A couple million lets them "really blow this thing out."
- The message, and the fact behind it, should be: "we're going to succeed no matter what. Raising more money just lets us do it faster."
- For angel rounds, the size can change on the fly. Start small and expand, rather than aiming big and risking the investors you already have.
- A "rolling close" has no predetermined size: you sell stock to investors one at a time as they say yes. This breaks deadlocks, since you can start when the first investor is ready.
- The downside of expanding on the fly is that the valuation is fixed at the start. A sudden rush of interest forces a choice between turning investors away and selling more of the company than intended.

## Independence and ramen profitability

- A startup with a couple of founders in their early twenties can have expenses low enough to be profitable on as little as $2000 per month. That revenue is negligible, but the effect on morale and bargaining position is large.
- YC calls this being "ramen profitable": making just enough to pay living expenses. After that, "you may still need investment to make it big, but you don't need it this month."
- If a little more effort on sales would carry you over that threshold, do it.
- Investors like it because it shows you've thought about making money, have the discipline to keep expenses low, and "above all, it means you don't need them."
- "There is nothing investors like more than a startup that seems like it's going to succeed even without them."
- Paul says that when YC predicts which funded startups will do well, it says things like "Oh, those guys can take care of themselves. They'll be fine." It doesn't say "those guys are really smart" or "working on a great idea."
- The qualities cited in those predictions are toughness, adaptability and determination. To the extent YC is right, those are what founders need to win. He notes this comparison is among YC startups, who have already cleared a certain intelligence threshold.
- Sam Altman conveys this without profit: "You could parachute him into an island full of cannibals and come back in 5 years and he'd be the king." Paul says he himself lacks that deal-making ability. Founders without it can "let the numbers speak for you."

## Handling rejection precisely

- Rejection is common. David Hornik of August described his own funnel for a year:
  - 500 to 800 plans received and read
  - 50 to 100 initial one-hour meetings
  - about 20 companies he got interested in
  - about 5 he got serious about
  - 1 to 2 deals done
- Paul says a VC partner does at most 2 new investments a year however many good startups approach. "The average VC is a rejection machine." Angels reject less.
- Investors are also poor judges because great startup ideas tend to seem wrong. An idea must be both good and novel, so it probably seems bad to most people or someone would already be doing it. Good startup investors need to be intellectual contrarians.
- Most VCs are "money guys, not people who make things." Angels appreciate novel ideas better because most were founders. The best VCs tend to be the least VC-like.
- How to read rejection:
  - Don't ignore it, since it might mean something, and don't get automatically demoralized.
  - If an investor gives specific reasons, check whether they are right and fix real problems. "You're supposed to be the domain expert; you have to decide."
  - A rejection doesn't necessarily say anything about the startup, but it suggests the pitch could improve. Don't just think "investors are stupid." Work out precisely where you lose them.
  - Sort and analyze rejections rather than letting them pile up as "a depressing, undifferentiated heap." Then "no one likes us" becomes a known size of problem with a known fix.
- "You're too early for us" really means "we can't figure out yet whether you'll succeed." An hour old is not too early if it's the right startup.

## Downshifting into consulting and avoiding novice investors

**Downshift into consulting (if appropriate)**
- Consulting is a dangerous way to finance a startup but better than dying. Paul compares it to anaerobic respiration: not optimal long term, but it can save you from an immediate threat.
- It fits some startups better. It wouldn't have suited Google, but a company making software for building websites could degrade gracefully by building sites for clients with it.
- Be careful not to get sucked in permanently. Upsides: you understand users well, and you may land big-name users you wouldn't get as a product company.
- At Viaweb the founders initially operated like a consulting company because they were desperate for users, and offered to build merchants' sites if they signed up. They never charged for it, so merchants wouldn't treat them as consultants and call whenever they wanted a change. They knew they had to stay a product company "because only that scales."

**Avoid inexperienced investors**
- Novice investors seem unthreatening but are the most dangerous because they are nervous, especially relative to the amount they invest. "Raising $20,000 from a first-time angel investor can be as much work as raising $2 million from a VC fund."
- Their lawyers are inexperienced too. One YC startup negotiated a tiny round with an angel and got a 70-page agreement from his lawyer. The lawyer couldn't admit the mistake in front of his client, insisted on the draconian terms, and the deal fell through.
- If you take novice money, either (a) drive the process yourself, including supplying the paperwork (YC publishes Series AA paperwork), or (b) use them only to fill out a larger round led by someone else.

## Knowing where you stand and prioritizing investors

- Paul says the most dangerous thing about investors is indecisiveness. The worst case is "the long no," the no that arrives after months of meetings. Rejections are like design flaws: inevitable, but much cheaper to find early.
- While talking to investors, constantly collect data on where you stand:
  - How likely are they to offer a term sheet?
  - What do they have to be convinced of first?
  - You needn't always ask outright, since that can get annoying, but always be gathering data.
- Investors resist committing except to the extent you push them. They want maximum information with minimum decisions.
- Best way to force action: competing investors. You can also focus the discussion by asking what specific questions they need answered, then answering them. If you clear several obstacles and they keep raising new ones, "assume that ultimately they're going to flake."
- Be disciplined, or investors' wish to lead you on will combine with your wish to be led on and produce "completely inaccurate impressions."
- Weight your strategy by investor value, which is how good a yes would be times how likely they are to say it. Put the most weight on likelihood, because "the most important quality in an investor is simply investing."
- Focusing on hot investors doesn't sacrifice lukewarm ones. One investor over the threshold makes the others much more interested, since the biggest factor in investor opinion is other investors' opinion.
- Investor opinions also spread indirectly through founders. Many interested investors raise your confidence, which makes you more attractive. No VC admits to being swayed by buzz, but few can say they aren't swayed by confidence.

## The fundraising opportunity and where the market is heading

- Paul says YC is working to fix the inefficient market by growing the number of investors as the number of startups grows. "As t approaches infinity, Demo Day approaches an auction," but t is still far from infinity.
- Most investors don't realize that dealing with them is a threat to a startup's survival. They don't see that 10 other investors also want a little more information, and that talking to all of them can stall a startup for months.
- Because investors don't understand this cost, he sees room for a competitor to undercut them by deciding faster. YC cut its own decision time to 20 minutes: 5 minutes reading an application, a 10-minute interview and 5 minutes of discussion. Bigger checks would justify longer, but "should it take anyone longer than a couple days?"
- He predicts that either existing investors will decide faster or new investors who do will emerge.
- He expects startups getting cheaper and investors multiplying to make raising money "if not easy, at least straightforward."
