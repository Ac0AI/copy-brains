## Startups are counterintuitive, so don't trust instincts (except about people)

- Paul argues startups are as unnatural as skiing: the instinct to lean back is wrong, so you need a list of counterintuitive rules to override your habits.
- At Y Combinator, "our function was to tell founders things they would ignore." Founders ignore warnings, then come back a year later saying "I wish we'd listened."
- Advice that matches your intuition is not worth much, which is why startup advisors exist the way ski instructors do.
- The exception: trust your instincts about people. Founders often say "I knew there was something wrong about that guy, but I ignored it because he seemed so impressive."
- Engineers wrongly assume a distasteful person is "normal for business." Paul says pick cofounders the way you pick friends: people you genuinely like and respect and have known long enough to be sure. Some people are good at seeming likeable "until your interests are opposed."

## What you need is expertise in your users, not in startups

- Success comes from understanding your users, not startup mechanics. Mark Zuckerberg succeeded "despite being a complete noob at startups" (Facebook was first incorporated as a Florida LLC) because he understood his users.
- Learning the mechanics of an angel round is unnecessary and possibly dangerous, because it encourages "playing house": raising funding at a nice valuation, renting a nice office in SoMa, hiring friends, then realizing they neglected the essential thing, "make something people want."
- Paul traces playing house to a lifetime of training on artificial tests (extracurriculars, exam tricks). He admits he studied for exams by guessing the 20 or 30 likely exam questions and working out answers in advance.

## Growth hacks and fundraising tricks are bullshit

- Young founders look for the trick, assuming fundraising is the measure of success. Paul's answer: the best way to convince investors is to start a startup that is doing well, growing fast, "and then simply tell investors so."
- On growth: "Whenever you hear anybody talk about growth hacks, just mentally translate it in your mind to bullshit." The way to grow is to make something users really love and tell them about it.
- Many partner-founder conversations start with the founder saying "how do I..." and the partner answering "just..."
- Gaming the system stops working in a startup. There is no boss to trick, only users, who are "like sharks": too stupid to fool, only caring whether the software does what they want.
- Faking can work on investors for one or maybe two rounds, but it is self-defeating since founders hold equity in a doomed company.
- Tricks exist but are "an order of magnitude less important." A founder who knows zero about fundraising but has users who love the product will raise more easily than one who knows every trick but has a flat usage graph.
- On fundraising bias (for example against women founders): the fix is the same, make the startup do really well. Paul once tweeted an anonymous, stupendous growth graph of a female-founded startup struggling to raise, noting "growth graphs have no gender."

## Startups are all-consuming, so don't start one in college

- A startup takes over your life for years, maybe a decade or the rest of your working life. Every successful YC founder says "it never gets any easier"; the problems change but the total worry doesn't decrease.
- Paul compares it to having kids: a button that irrevocably changes your life. Most people in rich countries delay it, and he says startups should be delayed too.
- If you start at 20 and succeed, you lose the chance to do things best done in your early 20s: plunging into projects on a whim with no apparent payoff, or traveling cheaply with no deadline.
- A founder who waits is more likely to succeed. If a side project does take off at 20, running with it can be reasonable, but deliberately starting at 20 is "gratuitously stupid."
- Paul says you cannot reliably tell in advance whether you can handle it, because it changes you. Over nine years at YC he found little correlation between confident or doubtful attitudes and who became stars; he compares it to military recruits where swaggerers are no tougher than quiet ones.
- If you are absolutely terrified, probably don't do it; if merely unsure, the only way to find out is to try, just not now.

## Where startup ideas come from: don't try to think of them

- Consciously trying to think of startup ideas yields ideas that are "bad and plausible sounding," fooling you and others.
- Yahoo, Google, Facebook and Apple all began as side projects that weren't supposed to be companies. The best ideas are outliers your conscious mind would reject as company ideas.
- Recipe: learn about things that matter, work on problems that interest you, with people you like and respect. The third part also gets you cofounders.
- Domain expertise is the real component of entrepreneurship. Larry Page became an expert on search because he was genuinely interested in it, not from an ulterior motive. Airbnb's Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia were experts in design and in organizing people, not technology.
- Think of technology as a "fractal stain"; every point on the moving edge is an interesting problem. Get to the leading edge and "live in the future" (a phrase he attributes to Paul Buchheit), and ideas that seem prescient to others will look obvious to you.
- Example: a Harvard grad student wrote his own voice-over-IP software in the mid-90s to talk to his girlfriend in Taiwan without long-distance charges. He never tried to make it a startup, but that is how the best startups tend to happen.
- Heuristic for taste in interesting problems: if you find working on boring things intolerable, you likely have one.
- Best college preparation: learn powerful things out of curiosity, not a vocational entrepreneurship program. His two-word advice: "just learn." Starting a startup is "an ulterior motive for curiosity" best introduced at the end.

## Team, management and school Q&A

- Non-technical founders: in a domain business like Uber, the domain expert (say, from limos) may do most of the work, recruiting drivers, while the technical founder builds the app. In a pure technology startup, the non-technical founder does sales.
- Business school is basically not useful for startups: it was designed to train the officer corps of large companies in management, a problem you only have if you succeed. Design school would serve better, but the real way to learn is to try it. He admits he was wrong to advise people to work at another company for a few years first.
- Early hires should be like founders: self-motivated peers who don't need managing ("not like The Office"). Airbnb took five months to hire its first employee, he notes (via Sam).
- On monoculture from hiring people you like: the benefits of hiring people you know and like far outweigh the blind spots. Empirically, the most successful startups hired "all their pals out of college."
- For a side project, you'll know it's becoming a startup when it takes over an alarming share of your life.
- For slow growth with some users, he points to his essay "Do Things That Don't Scale."
- No kind of startup should avoid YC unless it will fail or the founders are intolerable, since most startup problems are the same regardless of domain.

## Bubbles and what Paul would study

- Paul distinguishes high prices from a bubble. A bubble is when people knowingly pay high prices hoping to unload on a greater fool, as VCs did in the late 90s by funding junk startups to take public. In 2014 he says valuations are high but it is not a bubble.
- Advice to founders: don't assume the next round will be as easy, and "assume the worst" about future recessions.
- Labs that spin off startups might work if the right people run them; Twitter began as a side project at Odeo (a podcasting company; he misspoke the name as Ideo).
- Paul says he would study physics in college, then notes that following your own curiosity is the point and "it'll turn out to be helpful."
- On efficiency, he says he works either under self-imposed forcing structures (YC's application deadlines made him read applications well) or on things so exciting he can't help working on them, and has no efficiency system.
