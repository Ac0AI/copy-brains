## Why startups beat jobs on risk: Y Combinator's early success data

- Paul reports on the first YC batch (summer 2005, eight startups): at least four succeeded. Three were acquired (Reddit, a merger of Reddit and Infogami, plus one he can't name yet) and a fourth, Loopt, was doing well enough to be acquired "in about ten minutes."
- He won't predict that 50% rate will hold, since the first batch "could have been an anomaly." He would "feel safe aiming at 25%," versus the "oft-quoted (and probably made up) standard figure of 10%."
- Kiko was the closest to a traumatic failure: the founders worked on it for a year before being "squashed by Google Calendar." They sold the software on eBay for a quarter of a million dollars, repaid their angels, kept about a year's salary each, and started Justin.TV. Angels held convertible debt, so YC only got 38 cents on the dollar.
- Even failed founders did fine. Two others just moved on to other things after the summer.
- His key statistic: 0% of that batch had a terrible experience, and he expects the share who wish they'd taken a regular job to stay near 0%.
- The puzzle: YC gets only several hundred applications per cycle, not thousands, and most programmers still go "straight from college to cubicle."

## Handling uncertainty about founding: break it into components

- Paul says YC, sitting at the very start of the funding process, is probably the leading expert on people unsure whether to start a company.
- Uncertainty is normal. Larry and Sergey hesitated before Google, as did Jerry and Filo before Yahoo. He guesses the most successful startups are started by "uncertain hackers rather than gung-ho business guys."
- Evidence: several of YC's most successful startups decided to apply at the last moment, some only hours before the deadline.
- Method: most reluctant people have about eight reasons tangled together. Analyze them into components, decide which are justified and which are bogus, and only then judge whether the overall doubt is mostly real.
- He says he is motivated to be honest, unlike confidence-building sellers who are paid when you buy. If he encourages the wrong people to apply, it only creates more applications for him to read.
- His approach is negative: go through the reasons you aren't doing it and show why most (not all) should be ignored.

## Too young, too inexperienced, not determined or smart enough

- **Too young:** YC tried to find the lower bound on founder age and found it fuzzy. The limit may be as low as 16; YC doesn't look below 18 because minors can't legally sign contracts. Sam Altman, the most successful founder funded so far, was 19.
- Age matters less than whether you've crossed the "adult" threshold. Two tests: (1) the kid flake reflex, where a kid says "I can't do it" and gets let off while adults are "ruthlessly pruned" for flaking. (2) Reaction to challenge: told "that's a stupid idea," a kid submits or rebels (rebelling also presumes inferiority); an adult says "Really? Why do you think so?"
- **Too inexperienced:** he used to say founders should be 23 and work a few years first, and no longer believes it. He still thinks 23 beats 21, but "the best way to get experience if you're 21 is to start a startup." A normal job may turn you "into a tame animal who thinks he needs an office to work in and a product manager to tell him what software to write."
- Proof: the Kiko founders made many mistakes, but a year later were "extremely formidable." Working at Microsoft or Google they'd have stayed "diffident junior programmers." His advice is to start right out of college.
- **Not determined enough:** determination is "probably the single best predictor of success." Hackers usually underestimate theirs. Several founders who would first have been delighted with a $2 million acquisition became set on world domination. Test: are you driven to work on your own projects, as Larry and Sergey were?
- **Not smart enough:** if you worry about it, you probably are smart enough. Most companies do mundane work where "the decisive factor is effort, not brains." If you can't do technically hard work, write enterprise software: "Enterprise software companies aren't technology companies, they're sales companies, and sales depends mostly on effort."

## You don't need to know business; build what people want

- Paul says the coefficient on business knowledge "should be zero." Focus on the product; making money is "so easy you can pick it up on the fly."
- Evidence: "pretty much 100% of startups that make something popular manage to make money from it." Acquirers tell him privately they buy for strategic value, meaning the product people want, not revenue. "If users love you, you can always make money from that somehow, and if they don't, the cleverest business model in the world won't save you."
- People argue with him partly because they hate the idea that twenty-year-olds could get rich building something cool that makes no money.
- Principle: "The most valuable truths are the ones most people don't believe. They're like undervalued stocks." When you find a good idea most people disagree with, push aggressively in that direction. Here: seek ideas that would be popular but seem hard to monetize.
- YC's stance: "We'll bet a seed round you can't make something popular that we can't figure out how to make money from."

## Cofounders and ideas

- **No cofounder** is a real problem: "A startup is too much for one person to bear." All investors, without exception, are more likely to fund you with a cofounder.
- YC funded two single founders and told both to find a cofounder first; both did. YC would rather founders already have cofounders committed enough to sign up for something hard.
- Advice: get one, since it's more important than anything else. Move to where people want to start startups, or switch to an idea people want to work on.
- Best time to find one is school, with a big pool. Keep in touch with friends you schemed with. Conferences and user groups are unlikely to work, because you need to work with someone to know. An open source project may be the best way to test this.
- **No idea:** in the average YC startup, he guesses 70% of the idea is new after three months, sometimes 100%. YC was going to let applicants answer "We have no idea." It puts little weight on the idea and cares about "what cool things you've made." Lead developer of a popular open source project counts almost as much.
- Recipe for ideas: "Find something that's missing in your own life, and supply that need—no matter how specific to you it seems." Wozniak built himself a computer. "A need that's narrow but genuine is a better starting point than one that's broad but hypothetical."

## Fallacies and real reasons not to start: room, family, wealth, commitment, structure

- **No room for more startups** is a fallacy. No one claims a limit on people working for salary at 1000-person companies, so why a limit on those working for equity at 5-person ones? Needs don't go away when companies shrink, wealth wants keep rising, and acquirers will "evolve to consume" any value founders will trade for upside.
- **Family to support** is real. He won't advise anyone with a family to start a startup, though he will tell 22-year-olds. Options: start a consulting business and gradually turn it into a product business (chances "very small," never Google, but you keep income), or join an early startup, where you are "roughly 1/n^2 founder" (n is employee number).
- **Independently wealthy:** his own excuse. Startups are stressful and "you can't do it half-heartedly." What makes a founder dangerous is "willingness to endure infinite schleps." Serial entrepreneurs are often driven by wanting to work with interesting people who aren't rich.
- **Not ready for commitment** is legitimate. A successful startup consumes at least three or four years. But a regular job often lasts as long and leaves less spare time than expected.
- **Need for structure:** if you need someone to tell you what to do, don't start or join one. Until about twelve people, no one should tell anyone what to do. He compares it to soccer: David Beckham said language was never a problem at Real Madrid because "everyone was so good they never had to talk." Test: if you'd bristle at being told you aren't independent-minded, you probably are.

## Risk, failure, and why a job isn't the safe default

- **Fear of uncertainty:** his framing is "if you start a startup, it will probably fail... Hope for the best, but expect the worst. In the worst case, it will at least be interesting."
- Failure carries little stigma in the US. Managers at big companies told him they'd prefer to hire someone who tried a startup and failed over someone who spent the same time at a big company. Investors don't hold it against you unless you failed from laziness or "incurable stupidity."
- **Not realizing what you avoid:** summer tech internships aren't real jobs, since firms hire students to recruit them, not for labor. Real jobs mean boring work and the expectation of being present at set hours, including days "you have absolutely no desire to work on anything" and must "pretend to." In a startup, work and life mix and you never have to pretend.
- **Parents want you to be a doctor:** parents are more conservative for their kids because they share more of the bad luck than the good, but "reward is proportionate to risk," so protecting kids from risk also protects them from rewards. Parents also "fight the last war": doctor prestige is dated. Treat their opinions "like feature requests": work out what they need and see if there's a better way to deliver it.
- **A job is the default:** defaults are powerful because they need no conscious choice. The job-as-default is only about a hundred years old; before that it was farming. Paul thinks startups may signal a historic shift in how wealth is created, like the move from farming to manufacturing. He says that is why he works on YC: "It would be an amazing hack to make one happen faster."
