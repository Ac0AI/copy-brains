## Two modes: founder mode vs. manager mode

- Paul argues there are two distinct ways to run a company: "founder mode" and "manager mode."
- Most people in Silicon Valley have implicitly assumed that scaling a startup means switching to manager mode.
- He infers founder mode exists from the dismay of founders who tried manager mode and from the success of their attempts to escape it.
- As far as he knows, there are no books specifically about founder mode, and business schools don't know it exists. All that exists so far are the experiments of individual founders figuring it out for themselves.
- He hopes that within a few years founder mode will be as well understood as manager mode.
- The trigger was Brian Chesky's talk at a YC event. Paul says Chesky's conclusion was that conventional wisdom about running larger companies is mistaken.

## Why the standard scaling advice backfires

- As Airbnb grew, well-meaning people told Chesky he had to run the company a certain way to scale. Paul summarizes the advice as "hire good people and give them room to do their jobs."
- Chesky followed it and "the results were disastrous." He then worked out a better way, partly by studying how Steve Jobs ran Apple.
- Paul says it seems to be working: Airbnb's free cash flow margin is now among the best in Silicon Valley.
- Many of the most successful YC-funded founders in the audience said the same thing had happened to them. They got the same advice, and it damaged their companies instead of helping.
- Paul's explanation: that advice describes how to run a company you didn't found, i.e. how to run it as a professional manager. That mode is so much less effective that to founders it feels broken. "There are things founders can do that managers can't, and not doing them feels wrong to founders, because it is."

## What manager mode looks like and where it fails

- Paul likens the way managers are taught to run companies to modular design. You treat subtrees of the org chart as black boxes: you tell direct reports what to do, they figure out how, and you stay out of the details because getting involved would be micromanaging.
- "Hire good people and give them room to do their jobs" sounds great described that way. In practice, going by founder after founder, he says it often turns out to mean "hire professional fakers and let them drive the company into the ground."
- Paul's footnote concedes one upside of the modular approach: it limits the damage a bad CEO can do.

## Founders being gaslit, and why to trust your own instinct here

- A recurring theme in Chesky's talk and in Paul's conversations afterward was founders feeling gaslit from both sides: by people telling them to run like managers, and by the people working for them once they do.
- Normally, when everyone around you disagrees, your default should be that you are mistaken. Paul says this is one of the rare exceptions.
- Two reasons:
  - VCs who haven't been founders don't know how founders should run companies.
  - C-level execs, as a class, "include some of the most skillful liars in the world."
- His more diplomatic phrasing: experienced C-level execs are often very skilled at managing up.

## Skip-level engagement and Jobs's retreats

- Paul predicts founder mode will break the principle that the CEO engages with the company only through direct reports. "Skip-level" meetings will become the norm rather than a practice unusual enough to have a name.
- Once that constraint is dropped, there are "a huge number of permutations to choose from."
- Example: Steve Jobs ran an annual retreat for the 100 people he considered most important at Apple, who were not the 100 highest on the org chart.
- Paul notes the force of will this would take at the average company, and says it could make a big company feel like a startup. He presumes Jobs wouldn't have kept doing it if it didn't work.
- He has never heard of another company doing this and says it's unknown whether it's a good idea: "That's how little we know about founder mode."
- Footnote idea: if such retreats became widespread even at politics-dominated mature companies, you could measure a company's senescence by the average org-chart depth of those invited.

## Delegation limits and what to expect from founder mode

- Paul is clear that founders can't run a 2000-person company the way they ran it at 20. "There's going to have to be some amount of delegation."
- Where the borders of autonomy fall, and how sharp they are, will vary by company and over time within one company as managers earn trust.
- So founder mode will be more complicated than manager mode, but it will also work better, which we already know from founders groping toward it.
- Prediction: once founder mode is understood, we'll find that many individual founders were already most of the way there, but were regarded by many as eccentric or worse.
- The goal is to tell founders how to run their companies "like Steve Jobs instead of John Sculley." Paul finds it encouraging how little is known, since founders have achieved so much against a headwind of bad advice.

## How "founder mode" will be misused

- Paul predicts people will misuse the concept once it's established.
- Founders who can't delegate even things they should will use founder mode as an excuse.
- Non-founder managers will decide to act like founders. That may work to some extent, but when it doesn't the results will be messy.
