## How startups create wealth: compressing a working life

- Paul treats a startup as a way to compress a whole working life into a few years: "Instead of working at a low intensity for forty years, you work as hard as you possibly can for four." This pays especially well in technology.
- He defines a startup as "a small company that takes on a hard technical problem."
- Back-of-envelope: a good hacker in their mid twenties earns about $80,000 a year, so must produce at least that much value to break even.
  - 2x from working twice the hours
  - 3x from more output per focused hour
  - 2x from removing the drag of a middle manager
  - 3x from being smarter than the job description expects
  - Combined: 36x, so about $3 million a year of work.
- He does not defend the exact numbers but stands by the structure: the multiplier is "certainly more than 10, and probably rarely as high as 100." $3 million is the limit case, working so hard you endanger your health.
- Startups are not magic. There is a conservation law: "if you want to make a million dollars, you have to endure a million dollars' worth of pain." A startup just compresses the pain into three or four years.

## Wealth versus money, and the Pie Fallacy

- Wealth is "stuff we want": food, clothes, houses, gadgets, travel. Money is a medium of exchange, a side effect of specialization and a shorthand for moving wealth around.
- Most businesses do not really make money; they make wealth, meaning they do something people want.
- The Pie Fallacy is the belief that there is a fixed amount of wealth. There is a fixed amount of money in a family's account at a given moment, but not of wealth. Restoring a beat-up car creates wealth without making anyone poorer. "If you plan to start a startup... you're planning to disprove the Pie Fallacy."
- Programmers see this most clearly because they create wealth directly, one line at a time.
  - At Viaweb one programmer was "a sort of monster of productivity" who added several hundred thousand dollars to the company's market value in a long day.
  - A great programmer on a roll can create a million dollars of wealth in a couple of weeks. A mediocre one creates zero or negative wealth.
  - "The top 5% of programmers probably write 99% of the good software."
- Wealth can also be donated: penicillin, FreeBSD and open source software all make people richer without being sold.

## Skepticism about founder-centric ideas

- Because wealth is whatever people want, and "what people will give you money for depends on them, not you," Paul says to be especially skeptical of plans centered on things you like doing.
- Example: a woman at the Internet Bubble who liked the outdoors wanted to start an "outdoor portal." His quip: if you like the outdoors, start a business recovering data from crashed hard disks.
- When starting a business it is easy to slide into thinking customers want what you do. Your idea of what is valuable is least likely to match other people's where your own enjoyment is involved.

## Measurement and leverage: why big companies cap what you can earn

- To get rich you need two things: **measurement** (your performance can be measured, so doing more pays more) and **leverage** (your decisions have a big effect).
- Measurement without leverage is sweatshop piecework: you decide only how fast to work, which might raise earnings 2-3x.
- Both together: lead actor in a movie, CEO, hedge fund manager, professional athlete. A hint that leverage exists is the possibility of failure: "If you're in a job that feels safe, you are not going to get rich."
- Big companies cannot value individual work because contributions are tangled together (engineers, designers, marketers all feed one gadget's sales). So they punt and pay a predictable salary for fairly hard work. Exceptions are sales (revenue is measurable, paid as a percentage) and top management.
- A company that could pay everyone proportionally to the wealth they create would attract people who want to work especially hard and "crush its competitors." In a footnote: "A company will be maximally profitable when each employee is paid in proportion to the wealth they generate."
- You cannot tell your boss "I'd like to work ten times as hard, so pay me ten times as much." A startup is the closest equivalent, except you say it directly to customers, along with a small group of ambitious peers.

## Smallness, the all-star team and the galley

- Revenue can be measured at the level of the whole company. A viable startup might have ten employees, which puts you within a factor of ten of measuring individual effort.
- Galley analogy: a big company is a galley of a thousand rowers. Individual rowers see no result from working harder, and the average rower is average. Ten rowers in their own boat get carrot and stick. Ten of the best rowers, each in the top 1%, get a far better deal averaging with peers than with everyone.
- "You don't want small in the sense of a village, but small in the sense of an all-star team." A startup is "not merely ten people, but ten people like you."
- He agrees with Steve Jobs that success depends on the first ten employees: "If anything, it's more like the first five." The people must be good because their work is what yours is averaged with.
- The larger a group, the closer its average member is to the population average, so a very able person who cares about money usually does better with a small group of peers.
- Startups also give "uninterruptability" (footnote): hacking has a long time quantum, perhaps an hour to load a problem into your head. Startups lack the personnel department and other interrupters.

## Technology as leverage, and running upstairs

- Technology is technique. Solving a technical problem many people care about multiplies value by everyone who uses it, "the fishing rod rather than the fish." That is the difference between a startup and a restaurant or barber shop, which serve one customer at a time.
- Historical examples: Florence in 1200 (new techniques for fine woven cloth), the Dutch in 1600 (shipbuilding and navigation).
- Non-obvious technology: McDonald's franchise rules are "practically a piece of software. Write once, run everywhere." Sam Walton "got rich not by being a retailer, but by designing a new kind of store."
- Small companies suit fast-moving technology because they lack bureaucracy and are less bound by convention. Big companies develop technology mainly where capital requirements keep startups out (microprocessors, power plants, passenger aircraft).
- Viaweb's rule of thumb was **run upstairs**. A nimble guy chased by a big bully should go up the stairs, since climbing hurts the bully more.
  - If two features were equally valuable in proportion to their difficulty, they always took the harder one, "because it was harder."
  - Wrestling all day with a horrible technical problem was a good sign: "something that was hard for us would be impossible for our competitors."
- This is what VCs mean by barriers to entry: how hard would this be for someone else to develop? You need a convincing reason your technology is hard to duplicate, or a big company will copy it with its brand, capital and distribution.
- Patents offer weak protection: competitors work around them or violate them and invite a suit. Example: Philo Farnsworth invented television but RCA made the money. The best defense is "a good offense": pick a hard problem and take the harder choice at every decision point.
- He generalizes this to life: "If you have two choices, choose the harder." The only reason to consider the easier one is laziness.

## Risk, getting users, and getting bought

- Catches: you cannot choose how hard to work, because competitors set it ("as hard as you possibly can"). And payoff is only proportionate on average: you may be 30x as productive and get paid between zero and a thousand times as much. "If the mean is 30x, the median is probably zero."
- A startup is like a mosquito: designed for one thing, to score, with no energy wasted on defense. It is all-or-nothing and you often don't know which until the last minute. Viaweb came close to tanking several times; its trajectory was like a sine wave, and it was bought at the top of the cycle. During the Yahoo sale talks they had to borrow a conference room to reassure an investor about to back out of a funding round.
- Viaweb's hackers were risk-averse and would have preferred a 100% chance of $1 million to a 20% chance of $10 million. The closest option is selling early for a smaller guaranteed payoff, but early-stage acquisitions are hard because acquirers are conservative and "would rather overpay for a safe choice." It is easier to sell an established startup, even at a large premium.
- He thinks getting bought is a good idea if you can: "Running a business is different from growing one," and selling lets you diversify.
- Acquirers are moved by fear of loss. The strongest motivator is the prospect that a competitor will buy you ("causes CEOs to take red-eyes"). Second is worry that you will keep growing and cost more later or become a competitor.
- Acquirers judge by **number of users**, not technology: "Users are the only real proof that you've created wealth."
- Paul's advice: make users the test and treat a startup as an optimization problem measured by number of users.
  - Guesses about what to optimize are almost always wrong; measurement is the key.
  - Number of users matters to acquirers, revenue, competitors, reporters and new users.
  - Avoid "techno-weenies" obsessed with interesting technical problems; solve problems users care about.
  - Avoid premature optimization: "Get a version 1.0 out there as soon as you can."
- In technology "you cook one thing and that's what everyone eats," so any gap between what people want and what you deliver is multiplied. Few companies pay attention to making customers happy.

## Why Bill Gates is a bad model, and wealth and power

- Don't use famous billionaires as examples; press coverage favors outliers. Success has a large random factor: Gates was smart, dedicated and also lucky. Microsoft benefited from IBM's DOS licensing blunder; without it Gates would be "somewhere near the bottom of the Forbes 400."
- The essay is about getting rich by creating wealth, which is more legitimate and more straightforward than the alternatives: "You just have to do something people want."
- Wealth creation only became the main path once the rule of law let people keep what they made. In medieval Europe merchants in towns withstood feudal lords: "the bullies stopped stealing the nerds' lunch money."
- Paul argues that removing the incentive of wealth stalls technical innovation (the Soviet Union, Britain under 1960s-70s labor governments). Developing new technology is "one percent inspiration and ninety-nine percent perspiration," and mundane technologies like light bulbs and semiconductors get built by entrepreneurs.
- The recipe has been the same since Florence in 1200: measurement from a small group, leverage from new techniques. The same recipe that makes individuals rich makes countries powerful.
