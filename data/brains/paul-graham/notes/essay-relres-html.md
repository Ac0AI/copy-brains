## What makes a good founder, in two words

- Paul says he finally got being a good startup founder down to two words: "relentlessly resourceful." Before that, the best he had was the opposite quality in one word: "hapless."
- "Don't be hapless" is not much of a rallying cry, because there is no antonym of hapless. That made it hard to tell founders what to aim for.
- He treats the phrase as the answer to "what should founders aim for?" He found it while writing a talk for investors about what to look for in founders.
- He says "Make something people want" is the destination, but "Be relentlessly resourceful" is how you get there. If he were running a startup, this is the phrase he would "tape to the mirror."
- He doubts the description "could be made more precise."

## What "hapless" means and why it is the opposite

- Paul says dictionaries wrongly define hapless as unlucky. A team that outplays its opponents but loses to a bad referee decision is unlucky, not hapless.
- Hapless implies passivity. To be hapless is "to be battered by circumstances," to let the world have its way with you instead of having your way with the world.
- He thinks the dictionaries are wrong because the word's meaning has shifted. A couple hundred years ago, people were more at the mercy of circumstances, so many words for good and bad outcomes come from words about luck.
- The good founder is the reverse: active, and shaping outcomes instead of absorbing them.

## Why relentless alone is not enough

- He says "Not merely relentless." Pure persistence only makes things go your way "in a few mostly uninteresting domains."
- In any interesting domain, the difficulties will be novel. You can't plow through them, because you don't know up front how hard they are: "a block of foam or granite."
- So you have to be resourceful. You have to keep trying new things.
- His metaphor is a running back: not merely determined but flexible. They want to get downfield, but adapt their plans on the fly.
- He admits the metaphor is weak, since it means little to most people outside the US. "Be like a running back" is no better than "Don't be hapless."

## Where the recipe applies and where it does not

- Paul says the recipe is not just a description of success in general. In writing or painting, the recipe is more to be "actively curious."
- The difference is where the obstacles are. "Resourceful implies the obstacles are external, which they generally are in startups." In writing and painting they are mostly internal: "the obstacle is your own obtuseness."
- Some parts of startups call for active curiosity, when the work is almost pure discovery. He says these are a small proportion of the whole, as they are in research.
- He allows that other fields may share the recipe. He thinks it is the best short description of a good startup founder.
- In big companies and most schools, being relentlessly resourceful is not the recipe. He says the recipe there is "longer and messier," combining resourcefulness, obedience and building alliances.

## Can it be taught?

- After four years of trying to teach it at Y Combinator, Paul says that yes, "surprisingly often it can." Not to everyone, but to many people.
- Some people are "constitutionally passive." Others have a latent ability that only needs to be brought out.
- It is especially true of young people who have so far always been under the thumb of some kind of authority.
- He adds a hedge. He would almost say "most people," but he has no idea what most people are like, and he is "pathologically optimistic about people's ability to change."

## Using the test: founders, cofounders and how many startups there can be

- **For yourself:** to know whether you are the right sort of person to start a startup, ask whether you are relentlessly resourceful.
- **For cofounders:** to decide whether to recruit someone as a cofounder, ask whether they are relentlessly resourceful.
- **For investors:** it is the quality to look for in founders.
- **Limit on startups:** Paul says there is no economic upper bound on the number of startups. There is no reason to think consumers can only absorb so much newly created wealth, "any more than there is a limit on the number of theorems that can be proven."
- So the limiting factor is the pool of potential founders. Now that the quality is named, it is possible to put an upper bound on the pool.
