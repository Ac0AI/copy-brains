## Why startups are counterintuitive, and what YC actually does

Paul says startups are "very, very counterintuitive," and that is why Y Combinator exists. If the right moves were obvious, YC "couldn't really add much by teaching people how to do it." He describes YC's job as telling founders things they ignore.

- Example: YC tells founders not to hire too fast. They hire too fast anyway, then come back and say they wish they had listened.
- Counterintuitive advice sounds wrong, so founders go with their gut and make the mistake, then hopefully catch it in time.
- He sees this in office hours "practically every day."

## Doing things that don't scale: Viaweb's manual start

Paul argues founders should do things in a handmade, artisanal, painstaking way early on, even when they think "there's no way we can keep doing this and become giant." He says you have nothing to lose and you learn a lot.

- Viaweb was an online store builder. Customers said no to using the software, so the founders offered to use it for them. Customers agreed, and the founders built stores for them by hand.
- Paul used his own software to build customers' sites. When it was inconvenient, he changed the software mid-task, shipped it with the Unix `mv` command, and went back to the customer's site. This made the software much better.
- They signed up for every catalog to learn direct marketing and joined the Direct Marketing Association. The catalog business was then called the direct marketing industry.
- Early YC applications were an ASCII form answered by email. The partners printed them out, graded them on paper like exams, and sorted the pile. Software came later: "eventually you build software when you learn what software you ought to be building."
- Growth math: with 10 customers, a 10% weekly goal means finding about one more customer, which can be done manually. A constant growth rate is exponential growth, so "the base number will soon take care of itself."

## Where the Viaweb idea came from

Paul says the idea was not a light bulb. He calls "light bulb" startup stories a cliché and false.

- Everyone then assumed writing software meant writing for the client, which meant Windows. His team knew Unix and refused to learn Windows.
- His first thought was an online store builder that you updated by email. He then asked: if you can use SMTP, why not HTTP? The software could run on the server and be controlled by clicking links in the browser.
- Netscape had already done the work of running on Windows, so the web was "a hole onto Windows."
- They did not foresee the benefits of web apps, which turned out to be great. They simply wanted to avoid learning Windows.
- The first version was clunky, but it worked.
- Ideas usually start as an inkling that you could do something nobody has done, probably a bad idea. Paul likes ideas that are implausible "in the right way," a bit naughty, as if something shouldn't be possible. He cites Facebook as an example.
- He told someone choosing between two ideas to pick the more outrageous one. He adds that laziness here means a gratuitous-work aversion that produces elegant solutions, not real laziness: "there's no such thing as a lazy founder that builds a big company."

## Picking co-founders and hiring

Paul chose Robert Morris because he did everything with him and knew he could work with him. Morris was an exceptional programmer who "could program as fast as he could type."

- Morris was not enthusiastic about the startup. On the board he would respond to lowball acquisition offers by saying that at least it would mean they could stop working on this thing. Paul bet him an earring if he ever made a million dollars out of Viaweb. He did, and Paul and Trevor Blackwell frog-marched him to get one.
- Morris recommended Trevor Blackwell as the smartest person he knew in grad school. Blackwell disappeared for two weeks and came back having rewritten the software in Smalltalk. Paul told him they weren't using that.
- Hiring and co-founder rule: find someone you trust, then find someone they trust. This works for judging intelligence, because smart people can judge other smart people. It does not work for trustworthiness: trustworthy people are often fooled by untrustworthy ones.
- A recruiting trick at Viaweb: Morris had been kicked out of Harvard for a year after spending a semester reconnecting Harvard to the Internet instead of doing coursework. They put up posters at Harvard asking "Did you get kicked out working on some project of your own? We'd like to hire you." Paul says it got them a great programmer.
- Co-founders need to know one another well. The stress of a startup exposes any flakiness, uncertainty or disloyalty. He compares it to "a marriage only harder."
- Paul says he could not have started a company alone and calls solo founding lonely. Co-founders keep each other going when things go badly.
- On unequal commitment: ask whether you would rather have 30% of this person or 100% of someone else. He would rather have 10% of Robert Morris's brain than 100% of almost anyone else's.
- For firing a co-founder, talk to Carolyn Levy at YC and read YC's published material. He suggests one person hold slightly more equity, such as 51/49, to avoid deadlock.

## Determination, intelligence, and what founders get wrong

Paul says determination matters more than intelligence. Take someone 100/100 on both and remove determination, and you quickly get "ineffectual but brilliant." Remove smartness from a very determined person and you eventually get someone who owns many taxi medallions but is still rich.

- Only one co-founder has to be super determined, as long as a tight bond lets that person drag the others along.
- A team also needs someone able to navigate as the idea evolves. Caring about users and following what makes them happy can substitute for creativity, "the way a scientist follows the truth."
- Competitors: of about 1,900 YC-funded companies, one was killed by a competitor. Founders have the same protection light aircraft have in clouds: "space is large." If another runner appears in your lane, run as fast as you can. What mostly kills companies is poor founder execution.
- He lists mistakes founders will make anyway:
  - They won't pay enough attention to users. They invent a "vision" in a cafe instead of finding anyone with a problem they will pay to fix. Best case is a problem the founder has.
  - They won't ship fast enough because they are embarrassed to ship something unfinished and dread the humbling feedback.
- Founder DNA: determination and willingness to try new things, plus negatives. Someone who has worked at a large company for twenty years is probably not a founder, since a good founder couldn't have stood it that long. Some companies' alumni make bad founders for this reason.
- He says a startup founder should not spend all their time thinking about fundraising.

## When to launch

Paul says launch earlier than feels comfortable.

- Reid Hoffman's line he endorses: if you're not embarrassed by what you launched, you launched too late.
- He once told Geoff Ralston, who was working on a PR plan and finishing software six weeks before a summer batch, to "just launch now." They launched within a week.
- YC itself launched less than a week after having the idea, as a website plus an emailed form.
- Rule of thumb: launch as soon as you have "a quantum of utility," meaning at least one person in the world is glad you launched because they can now do something they couldn't before. If nobody would be happy, you are not ready.
- Ten people who really love the product is a good place to be, even if no one else cares.
- Launching in a private beta to a few people beats sitting with your idea in a cafe.
- Launching early is not riskless, but launching late has risk too.

## User interviews, building what people want, and pricing

- Interviewing: find what is actually wrong, not what the user says is wrong. Ask what they would like to do that they can't. Their answer is often a symptom: someone who wants to mark email as unread is really using the inbox as a to-do list. Start with questions about their life, then ask hypotheticals ("what if you could do such-and-such").
- Need vs. want: build something people will choose to use or buy. Need is not the goal; people need healthy food but buy unhealthy food. Be careful with "need," because it often means what you think they need ("cleanse the world of sin"). Pick the solution you think can get usage quickest.
- Pricing: roughly guess what you should charge, or ask friendly early users what they would honestly pay. You can change prices later. Lowering draws no complaints. When raising, grandfather existing users, who are a tiny share if growth is exponential.
- In the early days you mostly need customers who will pay, and early customers teach you, not just pay you.

## Raising money, angels, and starting young

- ICOs and large raises: Paul knows little about ICOs. He says raising too much is often wrong because you dilute more than needed and idle money "has a gravitational pull" that makes you spend it and do stupid things. He gives Magic Leap's $2.3 billion raise as a case that seemed to produce almost nothing.
- Finding angels: angels are looking for you, so go to startup events. The best route is to get one angel to invest, who then introduces you to others. Another tip: find people who work at startups and ask them to introduce you to their investors.
- High-school founders: YC has funded kids in high school, but only when they were already going. He does not encourage it. Between 18 and 22 people should be exploring options, and startups need one hundred percent commitment, not a dabbling fallback.
- He warns that starting a startup is "like catching a dragon by the tail," so be careful when in life you do it.
