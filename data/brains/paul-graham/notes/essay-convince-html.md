## Investors judge founders first, ideas second

- Paul says most investors decide within the first few minutes whether a founder seems like a winner or a loser, and that opinion is hard to change. Every startup has reasons both to invest and not to invest. If investors like the founders they focus on the former, and if not on the latter.
- Example: a rich market with a slow sales cycle. Impressed investors say they want to invest because of the rich market. Unimpressed ones say they can't invest because of the slow sales cycle.
- Investors are usually unclear in their own minds why they like or dislike a startup. Paul notes founders have the same weakness.
- Ideas are "fuel for the fire that starts with liking the founders." When investors like you they say "yes, and you could also do x." When they don't, they say "but what about y?"
- Investors decide whether you're a loser or possibly a winner. If you seem like a loser they are done, at least for roughly the next year, and usually decide in far less than the 50 minutes they may have allotted. Paul says this explains stories of VCs checking messages during pitches: they have already decided.

## Why investors want power-law winners

- Startup outcomes follow a very steep power law. The big successes "dwarf the rest," and conventional wisdom says there are only about 15 a year.
- Investors therefore treat "big success" as nearly binary. Most are interested if you have even a small chance of being one of those 15, and otherwise not.
- A handful of angels would back a company with a high chance of moderate success, but angels like big successes too.
- Paul says there is no reason to think 15 is a constant. Increasing it by encouraging more people to start startups is an explicit goal at Y Combinator.
- Three things make you seem like one of the big successes: formidable founders, a promising market, and (usually) some evidence of success so far.

## Seeming formidable: stick to the truth

- "Formidable" means seeming like you'll get what you want regardless of obstacles. It is close to confident, except confident people can be mistaken. Formidable is roughly "justifiably confident."
- A few people are very good at seeming formidable, either because they really are or because they are more or less con artists. Most founders, including many who later build very successful companies, are not good at it the first time they fundraise.
- Paul says not to imitate the swagger of experienced founders. Investors are not always good at judging technology but they are good at judging confidence, and acting like something you're not lands you in an uncanny valley: "You'll depart from sincere, but never arrive at convincing."
- How formidable you seem varies with what you're saying. Anyone sounds confident saying "one plus one is two." Skilled people can do the same with "we're going to make a billion dollars a year." You can do it with fairly impressive sentences too, as long as you convince yourself first.
- Paul's reason early founders don't seem formidable: family, school and jobs reward cooperation, not conquest, so people emerge "compressed into the shape of the tube." Some find they have wings, but it takes a few years.

## Convince yourself first, then convince investors

- "Convince yourself that your startup is worth investing in, and then when you explain this to investors they'll believe you." This does not mean mind games to boost confidence. It means truly evaluating whether the startup is worth investing in.
- If it isn't, Paul says don't raise money. In fact, change what you're doing, since you're investing your own time. If you're not convinced it's a sufficiently good bet, why work on it?
- If it is worth investing in, you'll be telling the truth, investors will sense it, and you don't need to be a smooth presenter.
- Doing this evaluation requires being a domain expert. Otherwise your conviction looks like Dunning-Kruger to investors, and it usually is. They find out quickly from how well you answer questions. "Know everything about your market."
- When asked something you don't know, don't bluff or give up. Explain how you'd figure out the answer, and if you can work out a preliminary answer on the spot, do so and say that's what you're doing.
- Founders try to convince investors of things they don't believe because school trains us to talk when we have nothing to say (a ten page paper due means ten pages, even with one page of ideas). The quoted professor question: "Which one of these conclusions do you actually believe?"
- Unless you're a good con artist, you can't convince investors if you aren't convinced. "They're far better at detecting bullshit than you are at producing it." Pausing to convince yourself also forces you to organize your thoughts and gives you a provisional roadmap to success.

## When to raise money

- "The time to raise money is not when you need it, or when you reach some artificial deadline like a Demo Day. It's when you can convince investors, and not before."
- At YC, Paul says startups are encouraged to ignore investors and focus on their company until about a week before Demo Day, so most reach a convincing stage well before it. Any startup that isn't ready can defer to a later Demo Day.
- Raising too early wastes both sides' time.

## Market: a good bet, not a sure thing

- You don't have to prove you'll succeed, only that you are a sufficiently good bet. No one knows whether a startup will succeed, and if they did the stock price would already equal the future price and investors couldn't make money.
- Beyond formidable founders you need a plausible path to owning a big piece of a big market. "Founders think of startups as ideas, but investors think of them as markets."
- TAM: if x customers would pay an average of $y per year for what you make, the total addressable market is $xy. Investors don't expect you to capture it all, but it is an upper bound.
- The market must be big and capturable by you, but need not be big yet, and you need not be in it yet. It is often better to start in a small market that turns into a big one, or from which you can move into one. You need a plausible sequence of hops to dominating a big market a few years out.
- Plausibility standards depend on age. A three-month-old company at Demo Day only needs to be a promising experiment worth funding. A two-year-old company raising a Series A must show the experiment worked.
- Paul says the next round is much harder: investors' attitude shifts "like the difference between being judged as a kid and as an adult." Growth graphs work at both stages, but at three months they mostly show the founders are effective, while at two years they must show a promising market and a company tuned to exploit it.
- Every huge company is "lucky" in that its growth comes mostly from an external wave. To make the case for becoming huge, identify a specific trend you'll benefit from, usually by asking "why now?" Ideally something recently changed and no one else has noticed.
- Microsoft example: it wasn't going to grow huge selling Basic interpreters, but starting there it was poised to expand up the stack of microcomputer software as microcomputers became powerful enough. Paul says Microsoft probably looked only "good, but not great" a few months in, and no company ever looks more than a pretty good bet that early.
- Paul says at least half of the startups YC funds could make as good a case as Microsoft could have, and asks who can reasonably expect more.

## Handling rejection and "who else is investing?"

- Even a Microsoft-quality case won't always convince. Many VCs would have rejected Microsoft and some rejected Google. The most common investor question is "who else is investing?"
- Skilled formidable-seeming founders imply that several investors are about to commit. Paul calls this arguably permissible ("scamming a scammer"), since it is slightly dickish of investors to care so much about it, but he doesn't recommend it. It is the single most common lie told to investors, so you'd have to be very good at lying to pull it off.
- His recommended approach is to tackle it head-on: explain why investors turned you down and why they're mistaken. Experienced investors know the best ideas are also the scariest and know about the VCs who rejected Google.
- Talking candidly about what scared investors makes you seem more confident, probably improves how you present that weakness, and puts the worry in the open instead of leaving it as a gotcha for the current investors to "discover" and become attached to.
- To use this, find out why previous investors rejected you, even if it requires asking. Make clear you're not disputing the decision, just that you need to know about any weakness in your plans. You won't always get a real reason, but try.
- The strategy works best with the best investors, who are hard to bluff. Paul says the best investors rarely care who else is investing, while mediocre ones almost all do, so the question is a test of investor quality.
- Rejection isn't like college admissions, where getting into MIT implies getting into Foobar State. The best startup ideas initially look like bad ideas, so startups are often rejected by every VC except the best.
- Dropbox example: it was in a Boston YC batch, and the Boston investors all passed ("yet another backup and syncing thing"). A couple of weeks later Dropbox raised a Series A from Sequoia. Paul adds that one East Coast firm wanted to invest but tried to lowball them.

## Explaining it: concise, plain language, and a three-step recipe

- When you convince investors, use the same matter-of-fact language you used to convince yourself. Vague, grandiose marketing-speak doesn't work on investors and "seems a mark of incompetence."
- Be concise. Many investors explicitly test for it, reasoning correctly that if you can't explain your plans concisely you don't understand them. Even those without the rule get bored and frustrated by unclear explanations.
- Alfred Lin's point, which Paul endorses: clarity matters doubly because the pitch must convince at one remove, since the partner you talk to re-tells it to colleagues. At YC, the last step of building a Demo Day pitch is imagining how an investor would sell it to colleagues.
- Raising money asks you to convince investors of something much less speculative than "this will be huge." The question is only whether the company has all the elements of a good bet, so you can convince yourself, then convince them.
- Paul's recipe for founders who aren't naturally formidable:
  1. Make something worth investing in.
  2. Understand why it's worth investing in.
  3. Explain that clearly to investors.
- "As long as you stay on the territory of truth, you're strong. Make the truth good, then just tell it." His opening analogy: lift with your legs (the startup), not your back (the pitch).
