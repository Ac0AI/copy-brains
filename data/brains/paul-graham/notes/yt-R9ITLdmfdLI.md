## Seven startup ideas too big to look at directly

Paul opens by arguing that the biggest startup ideas are frightening because they threaten your identity, not just because they are a lot of work. They say what the Being John Malkovich woman says: "if you ever got me you wouldn't have a clue what to do with me." He calls this one of the most important things to understand about startups: big ideas repel you, so you have to learn to tack into that wind. He gives seven ideas, each big enough to make a billionaire, and expects most listeners to retreat to something like a recipes site.

- Silicon Valley has no architectural center. The center is wherever the people who will make the next generation of stuff are concentrated at that moment.
- The best ideas sit "right on just the right side of impossible," and at the start you can't tell which side you're on.
- Avoiding them is not weakness. Arguably it is sanity.

## Start with the "dinosaur egg": the tiny thing that grows into the giant

Paul's method for attacking gigantic ideas is to find the small seed that turns into the large idea.

- Example, the next Google: build a search engine that the top 10,000 hackers use, and nobody else. Get the first 20% of them in the room. He says this puts you in the same position Google had with its first 10,000 users.
- If you can build it, you are one of those 10,000 hackers, so build the search engine you want. This is a case where self-indulgence is good: make the queries Turing complete, optimize for code search at the expense of everything else, and don't worry about constraining your long-term future. "If you don't win in the short term there won't be a long term anyway."
- Even a UI that is hard to use doesn't matter if you get those first 10,000. He compares it to Facebook's Harvard undergrads: "5% of the way to an IPO" without realizing it.
- The opening he sees in search: Microsoft entering search showed it had lost its way, Google has drifted into social networks, and its results now feel personalized and cluttered with JavaScript, unlike the old Google that was "designed like a Unix utility."

## Replace email: a to-do list protocol, not a messaging protocol

Paul says email was designed for people in one corridor at Bell Labs to send each other lunch invitations, and is now used as a to-do list.

- His inbox is a to-do list, and email is the protocol for putting things on it. It is a bad one because anyone can add to it.
- A better protocol would give power to the recipient: control over who can put things in your inbox, and a server that simply says it isn't accepting more. He calls the away message a sign something is wrong.
- Messages should carry what the sender wants done, whether it is physical-world action, the deadline, and when it expires. He cites conference invitations from 2007 still buried in his inbox.
- He says tweaking the inbox won't be enough. You need a new protocol that can degrade to the old one.
- He calls it an irresistible force meeting an immovable object. Entrenched protocols are impossible to displace, but he doesn't believe people in the future will live in the same email hell, so "if it's going to get replaced eventually why not now."
- Go-to-market: a friend, an early Google employee, said his only real problem was the volume of email. Powerful people in pain are an opportunity to make lots of money, and they can say "you want to put something on my to-do list, use this" without caring about interoperability. That solves the chicken-and-egg problem.
- Make it fast: Gmail "has become painfully slow." A Gmail equivalent that is much faster could pull users away on speed alone.
- Pricing: he would pay $50 a month without a problem, and would be justified paying $1,000 a month if it made his life better.

## Replace universities, kill Hollywood

**Universities.** Paul doesn't expect them to disappear or be replaced wholesale. He expects them to be replaced by many small things that each do what the monolithic four years did, without people noticing.

- He calls them expensive country clubs that are not fun for students or professors, where you don't learn a lot.
- He cites Y Combinator as arguably one instance.
- Credentialing would have to be supplied separately, and a wave of secondary effects follows.
- Go after universities before high schools: high schools bring bureaucratic obstacles, and startups shouldn't take on things that slow them down.
- In Q&A he endorses the idea of getting a credential from a person (an adviser or lab) rather than an institution. That is how medieval guilds originally worked. "Eat away at that big lump of cheese."
- He agrees people must still meet in person, noting that YC moves founders to California and they shake hands. You can build things that involve meeting in person.

**Hollywood.** The idea came from Paul Buchheit; Paul wrote the RFS. Hollywood's mistake was being slow to embrace the internet, and he calls the winner of the entertainment delivery mechanism: the internet, not cable.

- Cable clients (TVs) are terrible; his own solution was to bolt an iMac to the wall.
- Attention can be stolen by social networks or games, but a residual demand remains for people who want to sit and watch a story unfold.
- The question is how to deliver drama over the internet. It will need to be larger scale than YouTube clips, with known characters and predictable length, or big movies known in advance.
- Two outcomes: a big company like Netflix or Apple becomes the app store for entertainment, or it is too restrictive and companies spring up to supply producers a la carte, creating a need for infrastructure companies.

## A new Apple, and the case that it must be a startup

Paul says someone who knew Apple well told him the current leadership could not keep producing new things the way it did under Steve Jobs: "no, there will be no new stuff" beyond what is in the pipeline.

- His reasoning: existing players aren't run by product visionaries, and the only way a visionary becomes CEO is by starting the company and not getting fired. So the next Apple will have to be a startup.
- It sounds preposterously ambitious, but a founder now has Apple's example. Jobs showed what one person can do, and that you can do much better than a product manager choosing features and handing them to a designer.
- Jobs "unrolled the future like a carpet," and people had unconsciously signed up to use whatever Apple made. Now the future is open.
- The next Steve Jobs would not need to equal Jobs, only to be better than Samsung, HP and Motorola. He urges such founders to apply to YC.
- Q&A on hardware: don't start by competing with the iPad, and start with something for hackers, like Arduino, which doesn't look like a threat to Apple. Cheap consumer devices (a Jawbone-style headset) may be possible. He mentions a current YC startup that is a "print button" for circuit designs, with parts arriving from China.

## Bring back the old Moore's law: make many CPUs look like one fast one

Paul says Moore's law actually said circuits get twice as dense, not twice as fast. The old version meant slow software could simply wait for faster processors. Now you must rewrite software to be parallel, which is far more work.

- The opportunity: a startup that makes many CPUs look to the developer like one big fast CPU.
- Most ambitious route: a compiler that automatically converts programs written the old way into programs for the new hardware. This is the "sufficiently smart compiler," a byword for impossibility. He asks whether it is truly impossible, and says if it isn't impossible but merely very hard, the expected value could be high.
- Why the value is high: with web services, users never see the processors, so software that restores the old convenience is indistinguishable from being the processor manufacturer, and Intel would be worried. Programmers like convenience and would flock to it.
- Less ambitious route: build from more parallelizable Lego blocks like Hadoop and MapReduce. The programmer still does most of the work, and he thinks there are many startup ideas there.
- Middle route: a semi-automatic version that looks like the smart compiler but has humans in the loop optimizing, either as employees or as a marketplace for optimization. Bots would likely start doing the optimizing, and if they did it all you would have created the sufficiently smart compiler that no one else has a copy of.
- Partner reactions were split: Robert Morris thought he would sound like an idiot, while Paul Buchheit and Aaron Iba said "maybe it'll work." Paul likes the idea for all the ways it is wrong: it runs against decades of software trends, and compilers are supposed to come from open source projects, not startups.

## Ongoing diagnosis, and the tactics for taking on huge problems

**Ongoing diagnosis.** One of Paul's idea-generating tricks is to imagine how we will seem backward to people in the future. He expects it to seem barbaric in 50 to 100 years that we waited for symptoms to diagnose heart disease and cancer.

- Example: in 2004 Bill Clinton, with the best care available, felt short of breath and learned his arteries were over 90% blocked, then had a quadruple bypass three days later. He had to wait to learn that number.
- He expects eventually knowing such numbers the way we know our weight, via non-destructive testing, and likewise a "radar screen" for cancer. We may all have tens or hundreds of micro-cancers at any time without knowing.
- Obstacles are horrific: technical, bureaucratic, and going against thousands of years of medical tradition. Doctors dislike "fishing expeditions" and call discoveries "incidentalomas." He gives the example of a friend whose brain scan showed an apparent large tumor that was a harmless cyst, after weeks of terror.
- He thinks false alarms are an artifact of current limitations: with continuous scanning people will learn that something is just a birthmark.

**Tactical advice for huge problems.**

- Don't attack frontally or announce you're replacing email, or employees and investors will constantly ask "are we there yet" and haters will line up. Say you're building to-do list software; people notice later, as with iPads replacing Windows.
- Empirically the very big things start small: Microsoft began with a BASIC interpreter for a machine with a couple thousand users, and Facebook began as a site where Harvard undergrads could stalk one another.
- It is also for your own sake. Neither Bill Gates nor Mark Zuckerberg knew how big their companies would get; they knew they were on to something. Big initial ambitions take longer to realize, and the longer the projection the likelier you are wrong.
- Best model is Columbus: "there's something to the West, I'll sail westward." Start with something small that works, then move west when the opportunity comes. The popular image of the visionary with a precise view of the future is wrong; "it's probably better to have a blurry one."

## Q&A: property, copies, and funding ethics

- On music and copyright: you can't practically charge for copies of stuff anymore. Property has historically been defined by what is convenient to make property (land was not property for hunter-gatherers). Files now move around like smells, so charging for them doesn't work. Some people will still pay, but you can't "print money" as the labels did in the 80s. He notes technology doesn't need copyright protection (Amazon Web Services isn't protected by copyright), and that writing books never made much money and was done mostly out of vanity.
- On funding things that could affect the human genetic code: he hadn't thought about it. YC tries to fund founders who seem likely to succeed, avoids ideas that are overtly evil, and plays it by ear. Paul Buchheit is the half-joking "official morality consultant." He notes that moral questions need other people's advice more than technical ones.
- On Hacker News: it was built by writing an HTTP server without reading the spec, and tweaking it until the browser got pages back. YC hired someone to make Hacker News faster.
