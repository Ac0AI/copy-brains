## Single founder, location, and co-founders

- **One founder is a warning sign.** Paul notes that few successful startups had a single founder, and even "Oracle" usually turns out to have more. It reads as a vote of no confidence: the founder could not talk any friends into joining.
- **Brainstorming and morale.** Founders need colleagues to brainstorm with, to "talk you out of stupid decisions," and to cheer them up. The low points are "so low that few could bear them alone."
- **Esprit de corps.** With several founders, each thinks "I can't let my friends down." Paul calls this one of the most powerful forces in human nature, and a solo founder lacks it.
- **Location matters.** Startups prosper in Silicon Valley first, then Boston, Seattle, Austin, Denver and New York. Even New York has probably a 20th of Silicon Valley's startups per capita. Houston, Chicago and Detroit are too small to measure.
- **Why hubs win.** That is where the experts are. Standards are higher, people are more sympathetic, the right hires want to live there, supporting industries are there, and chance meetings are with people in the same business.

## Choosing the idea: avoid marginal niches and imitations

- **Marginal niche (mistake 3).** Most Y Combinator applicants pick a small, obscure niche hoping to avoid competition. Paul compares it to a child afraid of the ball in outfield, who holds up the glove "for protection" rather than to catch. "You can only avoid competition by avoiding good ideas."
- **The fear is unconscious.** Founders do not consciously reject grand ideas; their unconscious will not let them think of them. His fix: imagine a great startup idea for *someone else* to do.
- **Derivative idea (mistake 4).** Many applications imitate an existing company. Few successful startups started as imitations. Most began with a specific unsolved problem the founders identified.
- **Examples of problem-first origins:**
  - His own startup made software for building online stores, when sites were hand-made by consultants at great expense. If online shopping took off, stores would have to be generated by software.
  - Apple: Wozniak wanted a computer.
  - Google: Larry and Sergey couldn't find stuff online.
  - Hotmail: Bhatia and Smith couldn't exchange email at work.
- **Method.** Don't start from companies and work back to problems. Start from problems and imagine the company that solves them. Ask what people complain about and what you wish existed.

## Know your user and stay flexible on the plan

- **No specific user in mind (mistake 10).** Perhaps "you create wealth in proportion to how well you understand the problem you're solving, and the problems you understand best are your own." The converse is not a theory: "if you're trying to solve problems you don't understand, you're hosed."
- **Vague target markets are a red flag.** Teenagers, "people interested in local events" (a "perennial tarpit"), or "business" users. Which business users? Gas stations, movie studios, defense contractors?
- **If you build for others, fly on instruments.** Shift gears consciously and be empirical. You cannot guess what will work, so find users and measure their responses. If you cannot talk specific users into using it, "you're on the wrong track."
- **Obstinacy (mistake 5).** Sticking to a vision works for something well-defined like an Olympic gold medal. Startups are "more like science, where you need to follow the trail wherever it leads." The original plan is probably wrong, and most successful startups end up doing something so different it barely seems the same company.
- **Tune openness correctly.** Switching ideas weekly is equally fatal. Test: does each new idea reuse most of what you built before? Then the process converges. Restarting from scratch each time is a bad sign.
- **Ask your users.** If you consider a new direction and users seem excited, it is probably a good bet.

## Hiring and platform choices

- **Bad programmers (mistake 6).** Most founders he knows are programmers, so this is rarely fatal for them; they can do the work themselves in a pinch. It killed many 1990s e-commerce startups run by business people who thought you "had some clever idea and then hired programmers to implement it."
- **Why it fails.** Business founders can't tell good programmers from bad (a resume line like "Microsoft Certified Developer"), and no one really good wants to implement a business person's vision. Such a startup "lumbers along like a World War II bomber while competitors scream past like jet fighters." He admits he has no answer for how a non-programmer picks good programmers.
- **Wrong platform (mistake 7).** Platform can mean an OS, language or framework; it both supports and limits, like a foundation.
- **Examples:**
  - Many Bubble-era startups built server apps on Windows.
  - Hotmail kept running on FreeBSD for years after Microsoft bought it, presumably because Windows couldn't handle the load.
  - PayPal's new CEO wanted to switch to Windows even after Max Levchin showed their software scaled only 1% as well on Windows as Unix. PayPal switched CEOs instead.
  - Java applets "killed just about 100% of the startups who believed" in them.
- **How to pick.** Hire good programmers and let them choose. If you aren't a programmer, visit a top computer science department and see what they use in research projects.

## Launching: not too slow, not too early

- **Slowness (mistake 8).** Software is "always 85% done," and it takes an effort of will to release. Test of an excuse: if the software were 100% finished and launchable at the push of a button, would you still be waiting?
- **Two reasons to launch fast.**
  - It forces you to finish a quantum of work. Nothing is finished until released.
  - Only by bouncing the idea off users do you fully understand it.
- **Delays hide several problems:** working too slowly, not understanding the problem, fear of dealing with users, fear of being judged, working on too many things, and perfectionism. The cure is to force yourself to launch *something* fairly quickly.
- **Launching too early (mistake 9).** Slowness has killed "a hundred times more startups" than speed, but a weak launch can ruin your reputation because early adopters may never come back.
- **Minimum to launch.** Identify a core that is (a) useful on its own and (b) incrementally expandable into the whole project, and finish it ASAP. Early adopters are tolerant: the product has to do *something*, not everything.

## Money: raising, spending, and managing investors

- **Funding is measured in time (mistake 11).** Runway: when it runs out you are "airborne or dead." Raise enough to reach the next visible step: prototype if you have an idea, launch if you have a prototype, significant growth if launched.
- **Take more than you think.** Note: 50% to 100% more, because software takes longer to write and deals longer to close. Advice: keep both spending and the first goal low, with the initial goal a solid prototype.
- **Spending too much (mistake 12).** Hard to separate from raising too little; "if you raised five million and ran out of money, you probably spent too much." Hiring is the classic burn, and it also slows you down (Brooks, *The Mythical Man-Month*).
- **Three hiring rules:**
  - Don't hire if you can avoid it.
  - Pay with equity rather than salary, to attract committed people.
  - Hire only people who will write code or get users.
- **Raising too much (mistake 13).** Quote from a VC: "Once you take several million dollars of my money, the clock is ticking." You move to proper offices and hire employees, who are less committed and bring politics. "When you raise a lot of money, your company moves to the suburbs and has kids."
- **Harder to pivot.** With a sales force built for companies, you won't notice you should sell to consumers. Large raises also take enormous time, and VCs "never quite say yes or no."
- **Take the first reasonable deal.** From a reputable firm, at a reasonable valuation, without onerous terms: "Who cares if you could get a 30% better deal elsewhere?" Startups are all-or-nothing, so bargain-hunting wastes time. (Caveat: some VCs lowball to test you, so push back a bit.)
- **Investor management (mistake 14).** Don't ignore investors, since they may have insights, but don't let them run the company. "If investors had sufficient vision to run the companies they fund, why didn't they start them?" Ignoring them is less dangerous than caving.
- **Control follows money.** At VC scale, a board majority makes them your bosses; with an even board, they only need to convince the outside directors. Apple's board fired Steve Jobs, and Google reportedly got grief from investors early on.

## Users before profit, and doing the dirty work

- **Don't sacrifice users to profit (mistake 15).** The business model isn't unimportant; making something people want is simply much harder. Leave the model for later "just as you'd leave some trivial but messy feature for version 2."
- **Core problem.** Create wealth (= how much people want something x the number who want it), not convert wealth into money. Google made search work first, then worried about monetization.
- **Key line:** "It *is* irresponsible not to think about business models. It's just ten times more irresponsible not to think about the product."
- **Getting your hands dirty (mistake 16).** Most programmers prefer ideas to the messy business side. Larry and Sergey first tried to sell their algorithm to another company. But "there's not much of a market for ideas"; no one trusts one until you embody it in a product with users.
- **Users convince acquirers.** Acquirers can tell themselves they're buying users rather than cleverness. Example: Google paid $1.6 billion for YouTube 18 months after the founders could not have sold the same advice for $10 million.
- **Go find users.** In the summer 2005 YC batch, one startup's founder spent half his time pursuing deals with cell phone executives, and it was the most successful of the group "by an order of magnitude." At least one hacker must do business stuff.

## Founder fights and half-hearted effort

- **Founder fights (mistake 17).** About 20% of YC-funded startups had a founder leave. YC now advises founders to vest, giving an orderly way to quit, though it doesn't require it.
- **Departures are survivable.** The least committed founder usually leaves. Blogger got down to one person and bounced back; Apple originally had three founders.
- **Choose co-founders carefully.** Most disputes come from the people, not the situation. Don't suppress misgivings; fix problems before the company starts. Don't add your housemate so he won't feel left out, and don't partner with someone you dislike for a skill you need: "The people are the most important ingredient in a startup."
- **Half-hearted effort (mistake 18).** The most common failure is a side project that is gradually abandoned. Most founders of failed startups kept their day jobs; most successful founders quit.
- **Interpretation.** Many would-be founders may lack the needed determination and know it. But he guesses the number who could have made it by quitting is perhaps an order of magnitude larger than the number who do.
- **Conclusion.** "Most startups fail because they don't make something people want, and the reason most don't is that they don't try hard enough." He admits he lacked the determination himself, bailing twice since Viaweb "without the spur of poverty."
