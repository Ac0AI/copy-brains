## Ambient thought and the top idea in your mind

- Paul says what he thinks about in the shower in the morning matters more than he used to believe. He already knew it was a good time to have ideas. Now he says "it's hard to do a really good job on anything you don't think about in the shower."
- He calls the effect "ambient thought": thinking you do without trying to. A typical case is working hard on a problem, failing, then seeing the answer later while doing something else.
- He is "increasingly convinced" this kind of thinking is necessary for hard problems, not just helpful. You can only control it indirectly.
- Most people have one top idea in mind at any time. It is the idea your thoughts drift toward when they drift freely. That idea gets all the benefit of ambient thought, and every other idea is starved of it.
- So "it's a disaster to let the wrong idea become the top one in your mind."

## Why startups stall when they raise money

- Paul had noticed that startups got far less done once they started raising money. He only understood why when his own team raised money for Y Combinator.
- The cost is not the hours spent meeting investors. Raising money becomes the top idea in your mind, so it is what you think about in the morning shower. Other questions stop getting that thinking.
- He had hated raising money at Viaweb and had forgotten why until the YC raise.
- Money matters are especially likely to become the top idea because they have to. Money doesn't arrive by default. It only happens if you let it occupy your shower thoughts, and then you make little progress on what you'd rather work on.
- The pattern is not unique to him. He says "just about every startup I've seen grinds to a halt when they start raising money," or when they start talking to acquirers.
- Neither fund he raised for YC was difficult, yet each process dragged on for months. Moving large sums is never treated casually. The attention required rises with the amount: "maybe not linearly, but definitely monotonically."
- He sees the same thing in professors, who have become "professional fundraisers who do a little research on the side."

## Controlling your top idea indirectly

- You can't directly steer where your thoughts drift: "If you're controlling them, they're not drifting."
- You can steer them indirectly by choosing which situations you get into. His lesson is "be careful what you let become critical to you."
- Aim for situations where the most urgent problems are ones you want to think about.
- Control is incomplete. An emergency can push everything else out. Barring emergencies, you have a good deal of indirect influence.

## The two thoughts to avoid: money and disputes

- Paul names two types of thought that push out more interesting ones, "like the Nile Perch."
- Money: getting it is "almost by definition an attention sink."
- Disputes: they are engaging in the wrong way. They have the same velcro-like shape as interesting ideas but no substance. His advice is to avoid disputes if you want to get real work done.
- Corollary: avoid becoming an administrator, or your job will consist of dealing with money and disputes.

## Newton and the cost of disputes

- After publishing his theory of colors in 1672, Newton was distracted by disputes for years. He concluded that the only fix was to stop publishing.
- Newton wrote that "a man must either resolve to put out nothing new or become a slave to defend it."
- His critics included Linus and his students at Liege. Biographer Westfall thought Newton overreacted, since the "slavery" was five replies totalling fourteen printed pages over a year.
- Paul sympathizes with Newton. The cost was not the 14 pages. It was the pain of having a stupid controversy repeatedly reintroduced as the top idea in a mind that wanted to think about other things.

## Forgiveness as a selfish habit

- Someone who injures you hurts you twice: by the injury, then by the time you spend thinking about it afterward. Ignoring injuries avoids the second half.
- His method is to tell himself, "this doesn't deserve space in my head."
- He is delighted to find he has forgotten the details of a dispute, because that means he wasn't thinking about it.
- His wife thinks he is more forgiving than she is, but he says his motives are "purely selfish."

## Diagnosing what your top idea actually is

- Many people aren't sure what their top idea is. Paul is often wrong about his own, because he tends to name the idea he'd want to be on top rather than the one that is.
- The test is to take a shower and notice which topic your thoughts keep returning to.
- If it isn't what you want to be thinking about, change something about your situation.
