## Evaluating founders: people over plans

- The earlier the stage, the more the investment is a bet on the founders. "It's location for real estate." Early-stage investors therefore need to be good judges of people.
- Paul says his wife Jessica Livingston is a much better judge of people and social protocol than he is. He puts part of that down to natural ability and part to caring less about those things.
- His own edge: he can tell whether someone actually understands an idea, especially a technical one, or only has a "reading the newspapers level of understanding."
- Determination is hard to read from demeanor. Everyone walking into a YC interview thinks they must look tough, which is "painful to watch." He would rather they just be themselves.
- He judges determination through stories. He asks what happened to them, and looks at what they did when something went wrong: did they persevere instead of giving up?
- On Sam Altman, he sees a technical person who can also put the pieces together and run things: "a technical guy who also is very formidable, and that's a good combination."
- He is unsure the "great founders in their 20s" story is true. He plans to check with YC partners. His hunch is that hindsight is distorting it: "anybody who claims they knew early on that Patrick Collison was going to be a big star, show me the equity." Stripe's early rounds did not attract everyone.

## How YC interviews work and where they fail

- The first 10 minutes of a YC interview is effectively the start of YC. You see what it's like to work with the founders by working with them for 10 minutes, and that is enough.
- He has studied the point where a decision stops changing. The chance of changing your mind is high after minute 1 or 2, and low after about minute 7. He says YC interviews could be 7 minutes rather than 10, and that 10 is "almost insultingly short" already.
- 7 minutes is where you are unlikely to change your mind if things are going badly. It does not mean 7 minutes tells you everything.
- Early flips are often a misunderstanding. The founders are near an idea YC knows is bad, YC assumes they're doing it, and then they say "no, we're doing this." They then earn extra credit for understanding why the bad idea is bad.
- YC tracks its failures obsessively. It keeps a list of companies that applied, were rejected and went on to succeed, and studies the common causes.
- Example fix: an email-sending company got a "C" and the comment "spam company" from the first reader (Robert Morris, the designated wet blanket). Nobody else saw it, and it later went public. YC then changed the software so every application is read by at least two people.
- This is also why he is "contemptuous" of university admissions: YC measures its misses and fixes them, while admissions offices don't.

## Making founders more ambitious

- Founders are held back by fear of thinking big, because it seems like overreaching and like a lot of work.
- He compares his role to a fitness instructor. He can tell someone already working hard to "push harder" at no effort cost to himself, and surprisingly often they can.
- Introducing ambitious people to better peers and pulling them away from peers who aren't good enough is part of it, though he doesn't think it's the main thing.
- He says there is a real skill in "blowing up" an idea, meaning making it bigger: take an idea and ask how it could be bigger. YC interviews often do this, and founders walk out thinking their idea is much better than they thought, even though an interview is nominally a negotiation.
- Weak ambition isn't about ability. He is optimistic about the UK because people there "are not slack, they're not lazy and they're not stupid." Teaching British founders to aim for fast growth "is like teaching them a foreign language, but they do learn it."

## Founder heuristics and startup structure

- The heuristic of preferring young founders with side projects is a way to match founders to ideas. Young founders with no experience of the world get things wrong when they sit down and brainstorm startup ideas. Older founders can start things like supersonic aircraft or geriatric care and get them right.
- Solo founders: he wouldn't start a company alone because of the weight involved. But it is hard to find a partner as good as you, so going solo is also easier in a sense.
- His advice to teens and people in their early 20s: don't sit around thinking up startup ideas. Work with other people on projects. You'll get a startup idea you'd never have thought of, and a co-founder, but "you can't get co-founders instantly. You got to have some patience."
- When technical founders hate sales, he tells them to do it in small steps. After a few months they get used to it, even if they never like it.
- He compares this to how he cured his fear of flying: he learned hang gliding gradually, starting by running on flat ground and moving up the hill, then took flying lessons. He says he never consciously used the lesson in selecting talent.

## Venture capital, Microsoft and the shape of the market

- VC works where growth is high. Half the companies fail completely, so the winners need astonishing returns to compensate.
- VCs' ownership per round has shrunk. In the 1980s a round might buy 50% of a company, when YC started about 30%, and now around 10% for a given round size.
- Microsoft: his old essay was about Microsoft's culture and was not a claim that it wouldn't make money. He says Mark Zuckerberg was the founder puzzled that Microsoft could be a threat. Today no startup plans around the fear that Microsoft will enter its space.
- On Sam Altman's remark questioning "find product-market fit, then scale," he says OpenAI may be a special case because it needs giant warehouses of GPUs, so it may need advance planning. He says he doesn't know what goes on inside it.

## Florence, Harvard and building a talent magnet

- Paul says Florence was good at art because it was the most progressive city, "New York City in 1450 with 60,000 people," not because of a special Florentine aesthetic. He says he realized he had gone to the place where the puck used to be.
- He sees YC as deliberately modeled on Harvard: a small area that draws talent in and produces many winners. YC's first office was within Harvard's footprint, and "we've consciously tried to make YC the Harvard of startups."
- Harvard's admissions are corrupt (Dean's favorites, legacies), but because other universities are similarly messed up, Harvard keeps its draw. He thinks only Caltech may do undergraduate admissions properly.
- On fame: he didn't realize how prominent YC had become until long after. YC itself stayed a few people doing the same thing each batch while its portfolio companies grew to thousands of employees. He calls this one of his biggest mistakes with YC: he didn't realize how many people were watching, or that some would be out to get it.

## AI, work habits and Lisp

- On AI regulation he says he has no idea what it should look like. His one observation: safe AI is like a secure operating system or a fraud-proof tax code, built from patches in response to successful hacks, so any regulation will be wrong, with overregulation in some places and holes in others.
- He doesn't think AI makes programming more like painting, since you are still building something.
- AI optimists and pessimists differ mainly in which aspect they focus on. He holds both views at once: "unimaginably good and unimaginably bad."
- He thinks AI-code-trained AI won't converge on better code by itself, and recalls research where training on AI output "converges on crap." You need a large initial corpus of human-written examples in a language.
- Lisp is intimidating at the notation: an "initial hump" in front of people, and they will take a long detour to avoid even one obstacle. He says Clojure is successful and that JavaScript is, except for syntax, a Lisp.
- Activities with high interruption cost are those where you hold a "giant house of cards" mental model in your head, such as programming and math. Painting is less like that.
- Walking: he finds it best for learning from another person, less good for generating his own ideas. Classic YC office hours were walks down the block, which has the side benefit that people talk more freely when they aren't looking at each other. For solo thinking he suggests walking the same route somewhere undistracting.
