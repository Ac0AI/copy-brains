## "Default alive or default dead" as the first question for any startup older than 8-9 months

- When Paul talks to a startup that has operated for more than 8 or 9 months, the first thing he wants to know is whether they make it to profitability on the money they have left. The assumptions are that expenses stay constant and revenue growth stays what it has been over the last several months.
- If yes, the startup is "default alive". If no, it is "default dead".
- The rest of the conversation depends on the answer. Default alive: talk about ambitious new things they could do. Default dead: talk about how to save it, since the current trajectory ends badly and they need to get off it.
- He says half the founders he talks to don't know which one they are. Trevor Blackwell built a calculator to find out (growth.tlb.org).
- Founders don't ask because the question is meaningless early on, "any more than it makes sense to ask a 3 year old how he plans to support himself." As the company ages it turns critical, and that switch often takes people by surprise.
- His fix is to start asking too early rather than too late. Worrying early that you're default dead is "probably not that dangerous", but worrying late is "very dangerous".

## The fatal pinch

- The fatal pinch is default dead + slow growth + not enough time to fix it.
- Founders end up in it by not realizing that's where they're headed.
- Paul covered this in an earlier essay, "the fatal pinch" (paulgraham.com/pinch.html).
- Asking the default alive/dead question early is meant to set off the alarms soon enough to avoid it.

## Don't count on investors saving you

- Founders skip the question because they assume it will be easy to raise more money. Paul says that assumption is often false, and "the more you depend on it, the falser it becomes."
- Separate facts from hopes instead of thinking of the future with vague optimism. Say it explicitly: "We're default dead, but we're counting on investors to save us." He expects that sentence to set off the same alarms in the founder's head that it does in his.
- Investor interest is, as a rule, a function of growth. With revenue growth steeper than about 5x a year, you can start to count on investors being interested even if you're not profitable.
- Steep usage growth also interests investors, since revenue will ultimately be a constant multiple of usage. But investors discount merely predicted revenue, so if you measure usage you need a higher growth rate to impress them.
- Investors are fickle, so you can only ever "start to count on them". Something about your business can spook them even when growth is great.
- Fundraising is therefore never more than a plan A, however good the growth. Always have a plan B:
  - Know, and write down, precisely what you'll do to survive if you can't raise more money.
  - Know, and write down, precisely when you'll have to switch to plan B if plan A isn't working.

## Spending and growth are weakly connected

- Growing fast versus operating cheaply is "far from the sharp dichotomy many founders assume it to be."
- In practice there is surprisingly little connection between how much a startup spends and how fast it grows.
- Fast growth usually comes from the product hitting a nerve, meaning it hits some big need straight on.
- High spending usually comes from a product that is expensive to develop or sell, or from simple waste.

## Hiring too fast is the biggest killer of funded startups

- To avoid being default dead, Paul's answer is: don't hire too fast. He calls hiring too fast "by far the biggest killer of startups that raise money."
- Founders say they need to hire in order to grow, but most overestimate that need. His reasons:
  - There is so much work to do that naive founders think enough hires will get it all done.
  - Successful startups have lots of employees, so it seems like what one does to succeed. He thinks the large staffs of successful startups are "probably more the effect of growth than the cause."
  - Founders with slow growth don't want to face the usual real reason: the product is not appealing enough.
- Startups that don't raise money are saved from hiring too fast because they can't afford to. That still isn't a reason to avoid raising money, "any more than that total abstinence is the only way to avoid becoming an alcoholic."
- He quotes Sam Altman: YC's most successful companies have never been the fastest to hire, and one of the marks of a great founder is being able to resist this urge.
- He also quotes Paul Buchheit on premature scaling. Founders take a small business that isn't really working (typically bad unit economics) and scale it up for impressive growth numbers. This makes it much harder to fix once it's big, and they bleed cash fast.

## VC incentives push founders to overhire

- Founders who have just raised money are often encouraged by their VCs to overhire.
- Kill-or-cure strategies are optimal for VCs because the portfolio effect protects them. "VCs want to blow you up, in one sense of the phrase or the other."
- A founder's incentive is different: "You want above all to survive."
- Paul wouldn't be surprised if pushing founders to overhire isn't even in the VCs' own interest. They don't know how many of the companies killed by overspending might have done well had they survived, and he guesses a significant number.

## The common death spiral, and what to do instead

- The pattern Paul describes:
  1. The founders make something moderately appealing and get decent initial growth.
  2. They raise a first round fairly easily, because they seem smart and the idea sounds plausible.
  3. Growth is ok but not great, since the product is only moderately appealing. The founders and their investors agree that hiring a bunch of people will boost growth.
  4. The growth never comes, and they are rapidly running out of runway.
  5. They hope further investment will save them, but high expenses plus slow growth make them unappealing to investors. They can't raise more, and the company dies.
- What they should have done is address the fundamental problem, that the product is only moderately appealing. Hiring is rarely the way to fix that, and more often than not it makes it harder.
- At this early stage the product needs to evolve more than to be "built out", and that is usually easier with fewer people.
- Asking the default alive/dead question can push founders to seek growth in other ways instead of overhiring. His examples are doing things that don't scale (paulgraham.com/ds.html) and redesigning the product "in the way only founders can." He says for many if not most startups these are the paths to growth that actually work.
- Example: Airbnb waited 4 months after raising money at the end of Y Combinator before hiring its first employee. The founders were terribly overworked, but they were overworked evolving Airbnb into the "astonishingly successful organism it is now."
