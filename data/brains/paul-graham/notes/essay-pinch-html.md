## When a startup is in the fatal pinch

- Paul defines the "fatal pinch" as the point, a few months before many startups die, where the company has a significant amount of money in the bank but is losing a lot each month, and revenue growth is nonexistent or mediocre.
- Typical example: about 6 months of runway, or "6 months before they're out of business."
- The founders expect to escape by raising more from investors. Paul: "That last sentence is the fatal one."
- He admits he tries to resist coining phrases, but names this situation so founders may realize when they are in it.
- Exception: a handful of companies can't reasonably expect revenue for the first year or two because what they build takes so long. For them, substitute "progress" for "revenue growth."
  - A company is one of these only if its initial investors agreed in advance that it was.
  - Even these companies wish they weren't, because the illiquidity of "progress" puts them at investors' mercy.

## Why the second fundraise is much harder than the first

- Paul says founders are prone to delude themselves about how interested investors will be in giving them additional funding. They expect the first raise to be hard but not the second.
- Three forces combine the second time:
  1. The company is spending more than it did when it first raised.
  2. Investors hold companies that have already raised to much higher standards.
  3. The company is starting to read as a failure. At the first raise it was too early to call it a success or failure. Now the question can be asked, and "the default answer is failure, because at this point that is the default outcome."

## Why the pinch is self-reinforcing, and how to avoid it

- Founders overestimate their chances of raising more, so they are slack about reaching profitability, which further lowers their chances of raising.
- Y Combinator's advice: founders who raise money should act as if it's the last they'll ever get.
- The loop also works in reverse: "the less you need further investment, the easier it is to get."

## What to do if you're already in the pinch

- Step one is to re-evaluate the probability of raising more money. Paul's estimate: "the probability is zero."
- Variant: existing investors seem to promise more money. Often the founder reads them as promising, while the investors think they are only mentioning the possibility.
  - Fix, if you have 8 months of runway or less: try to get the money right now.
  - Either you get it, which solves the immediate problem, or you stop investors from helping you stay in denial about your fundraising prospects.
- Three options remain: shut down, increase revenue, or decrease spending.
- Shut down only if you're certain the company will fail no matter what you do. Then you can return the remaining money and save the months you'd spend riding it down.
- Paul notes companies rarely have to fail, and that the shutdown option mostly gives founders a chance to admit they've already given up.

## Cutting costs: firing, salaries and overhiring

- In most startups, expenses = people, so cutting expenses means firing people.
- Eliminate significant non-salary expenses first, if there are any.
- The easiest firing decision: people you already know you should fire but are in denial about. If that gets you to profitability, or lets you reach it on the money left, the immediate danger is avoided.
- Otherwise the options are firing good people, getting some or all employees to take less salary, or increasing revenue.
- Pay cuts are a weak solution.
  - They work only when the problem isn't too bad, for example when a small cut gets you over the profitability threshold and you can make that case to everyone.
  - Otherwise you're probably just postponing the problem, and the affected people will see that.
  - Exception: if the founders' own high salaries are the problem, cut them to the minimum you need. Paul says it's a bad sign if you needed this essay to realize that.
- Lean toward firing if the trouble comes from overhiring.
  - Example: hiring 15 people before you knew what you were building "created a broken company."
  - Figuring out the product is probably easier with a handful of people, and those 15 may not be the ones you need anyway.
  - The solution may be to shrink, then figure out what direction to grow in.
  - He argues you're not doing those people favors by flying the company into the ground with them aboard, since they eventually lose their jobs and the time spent on a doomed company.
- Keep the eventual goal in mind: to be a successful product company, "having a single thing lots of people use."

## Making money differently: everyone sells, and consultingish work

- With only a handful of people, focus on making more money, not by trying harder but by making money in a different way.
- Example: if one person sells while the rest write code, have everyone work on selling. "What good will more code do you when you're out of business?"
  - Write code only if it's needed to close a specific deal. Work only on whatever gets the most revenue soonest.
- Sell different things, especially more "consultingish" work.
  - Between making products and pure consulting is a long slippery slope, and you don't have to go far down it before offering something attractive to customers.
  - Startup programmers are often far better than the ones customers have, or the startup may have expertise in a new field customers don't understand.
  - Shift the sales conversation from "do you want to buy our product?" to "what do you need that you'd pay a lot for?" and it may become much easier to extract money.
- Be "ruthlessly mercenary": make customers pay a lot, quickly.
- Avoid the worst pitfalls of consulting. The ideal setup is a precisely defined derivative version of your product built for the customer, otherwise a straight product sale: you keep the IP and there is no hourly billing.
- Best case, this work becomes the "thing-that-doesn't-scale" that defines the company. Don't expect it, but as you dive into individual users' needs, watch for "narrow openings that have wide vistas beyond."

## The slippery slope and surviving with least damage

- Demand for custom work is so large that, unless you're really incompetent, there is some point down the consulting slope at which you can survive.
- Customers' insatiable demand for custom work will keep pushing you toward the bottom of the slope. The problem then becomes surviving with the least damage and distraction.
- Plenty of successful startups have passed through near-death experiences and gone on to flourish. The key is realizing in time that you're near death, "and if you're in the fatal pinch, you are."
