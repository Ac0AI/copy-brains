## Counterintuitive nature of startups

Paul argues that startups are so counterintuitive that trusting your instincts produces a lot of mistakes. He compares it to skiing: the instinct to lean back when you want to slow down makes you fly out of control, so you have to learn to suppress it.

- When he ran Y Combinator he joked that YC's function was "to tell founders things they would ignore." Founders ignore the partners' warnings, then come back a year later and say "I wish we'd listened."
- Counterintuitive ideas contradict your intuitions, so they seem wrong. You only need advisors for advice that surprises you. That is why there are many ski instructors and few running instructors.
- Some founders listen more than others, and this predicts success. He remembers how intently the Airbnb founders listened during YC.
- The exception is instincts about people. If you have misgivings about a cofounder, employee, investor or acquirer (they seem slippery, bogus or a jerk), trust your gut. A common young-founder mistake is ignoring misgivings because someone "seemed so impressive."
- Work with people you genuinely like and have known long enough to be sure.

## Expertise: be an expert on users, not on startups

- The way to succeed is not to be an expert on startups but to be an expert on your users and the problem you are solving for them. Mark Zuckerberg succeeded despite being "a complete noob at startups" because he understood his users really well.
- Mechanics like how to raise an angel round can be learned when needed and forgotten afterward.
- Knowing everything about convertible notes, employee agreements and class FF stock sets off alarms for him. It is a sign of "playing house," where founders imitate the outward forms of a startup: a plausible-sounding idea, money raised at a good valuation, a cool office, a bunch of hires. They neglect the one essential thing, making something people want.

## Gaming the system stops working

- Young founders go through the motions because that is what they were trained to do their whole lives. Paul admits he gamed college exams himself: he listed the 20 or 30 ideas shaped like good exam questions and worked out answers in advance.
- Founders look for the trick at every step. Fundraising looks like the measure of success, so they ask for tricks to convince investors. YC says the best way to convince investors is to build a startup that is growing fast and tell them. Then they ask for tricks to grow fast, and the answer is to make something people want. Partner conversations often go "How do we..." / "Just..."
- Starting a startup is "where gaming the system stops working." There is no boss to trick, only users, and "Startups are as impersonal as physics." You prosper only to the extent you make something people want.
- Faking does work somewhat on investors. A good talker can fool them for one or even two rounds. It is not in your interest, because the company is doomed and you just waste your time riding it down.
- Tricks exist but are an order of magnitude less important than solving the real problem. A founder who knows nothing about fundraising but has made something users love raises money more easily than one who knows every trick but has a flat usage graph. That founder also goes on to succeed after the raise.
- Startup schlep work is "merely unglamorous, not bogus." If your true calling is gaming the system, he says, go into management consulting.

## Startups are all-consuming, so don't start one in college

- A startup takes over your life to a degree you cannot imagine, for several years at least, maybe a decade or the rest of your working life.
- Larry Page started running at 25 and hasn't stopped. Every day brings problems only the CEO can handle, a week's vacation creates a week's backlog, and as "the company's daddy" he can never show fear or weakness. The difficulty is concealed because billionaires get "less than zero sympathy."
- Every YC founder of a big success says the same thing: it never gets easier. The problems change but the total volume of worry never decreases, and if anything it increases.
- He compares it to having kids: a button that changes your life irrevocably, and many things are easier to do before.
- Universities can teach about startups, but that is not what you need to know. You need to learn your own users' needs, and you can't until you actually start. If you have significant numbers of users, you have started the company whether or not it is incorporated.
- You can't start a startup for real as a student, because you are then no longer really a student. His advice is blunt: "Do not start a startup in college."
- Starting a startup is "like a brutally fast depth-first search"; at 20 most people should still be searching breadth-first.
- Early-20s freedom (plunging into projects on a whim, traveling cheaply with no deadline) is valuable for ambitious people. Darwin was 22 and so unoccupied it alarmed his family when he got the HMS Beagle invitation. Zuckerberg will never get to bum around a foreign country.
- Waiting costs nothing, because you are more likely to succeed if you wait. If a side project takes off like Facebook did, running with it may be reasonable. But the usual way startups take off is founders making them take off, and doing that at 20 is "gratuitously stupid."

## You can't tell if you're up to it, so try later

- Starting a startup is really hard, and you can't tell in advance if you can handle it. It will change you, so what you are estimating is what you could grow into.
- Over 9 years, his job at YC was predicting who would have what it takes. Intelligence was easy to judge. Toughness and ambition were hard to predict. His conclusion on how much an expert can know: "not much," and he kept a completely open mind about which startups in each batch would be stars.
- Founders' initial attitudes, whether swaggering confidence or fear of being found out, had little correlation with results. He has read the same is true in the military: swaggering recruits are no more likely to be tough than quiet ones.
- His rule: if you are absolutely terrified, probably don't. If you are merely unsure, the only way to find out is to try, "just not now."

## How to get startup ideas without trying to think of them

- Before a startup you need only an idea and cofounders, and the method for getting both is the same. The way to get startup ideas "is not to try to think of startup ideas."
- Deliberately generated ideas are "not merely bad, but bad and plausible-sounding," so you waste time before realizing it.
- Instead, turn your mind into the type that ideas form in unconsciously, so you don't even realize at first they're startup ideas. Apple, Yahoo, Google and Facebook all began as side projects, not meant to be companies. Great ideas are such outliers that your conscious mind would reject them as company ideas.
- His recipe: (1) learn a lot about things that matter, (2) work on problems that interest you, (3) with people you like and respect. The third part also gets you cofounders at the same time as the idea.
- The prescription isn't limited to technology. Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia were good at design and at organizing groups and making projects happen. What matters is working on problems demanding enough to stretch you.
- His test for real problems is that they are interesting, and he works on interesting things even if no one else cares ("especially if no one else cares"). He did YC itself only because it seemed interesting.
- A heuristic for whether you have the taste: do you find known boring ideas intolerable? Could you endure studying literary theory or working in middle management at a large company?

## Live at the leading edge and just learn

- Think of technology as a fractal stain spreading. Every moving point on the edge is an interesting problem. Getting to the leading edge of some technology, to "live in the future" (Paul Buchheit's phrase), makes ideas that look uncannily prescient to others seem obvious to you.
- Example: in the mid 90s at Harvard, a grad student who knew networks wrote his own voice over IP software to talk to his girlfriend in Taiwan without paying long distance. He never turned it into a startup, but "this is exactly the way the best startups get started."
- The best preparation in college is not a vocational "entrepreneurship" program but classic education for its own sake: learn powerful things by following genuine curiosity.
- The component of entrepreneurship that matters is domain expertise. Becoming Larry Page meant becoming an expert on search, driven by curiosity and not an ulterior motive. "At its best, starting a startup is merely an ulterior motive for curiosity," best introduced toward the end of the process.
- Students can stick closer to a liberal education, without worrying about how courses look to employers or shying from hard classes for GPA. Users don't care about your GPA, he has never heard of investors caring, and YC never asks about classes or grades.
- His advice in two words: "just learn."
