## Who Y Combinator funds and how it works
- YC invests in 25-30 startups at a time, twice a year, "in batch mode". Each batch works intensively for three months.
- The batch ends with demo day, where each startup gets about five minutes to present to investors. The hope is that they then raise the next round.
- Paul says the main useful thing YC does is "try and figure out what to build first". He also arbitrates conflicts between founders, which he wishes he didn't have to do.
- Everyone has dinner together once a week for the first three months. Paul used to cook it himself until about a year earlier.
- YC stays in touch after the 90 days. Only the weekly dinners stop.
- Paul focuses on the problems of the very beginning (for example, a founding team shrinking from three to two). He says he knows nothing about IPOs.

## What Paul looks for in founders
- Paul wants founders who are "determined, smart, have a sense of design, and get things done".
- The "proven" he cares about is evidence that someone likes to build things, not a track record as a proven entrepreneur.
- The key question is "what have you made outside of school or work because you liked it?" To be really good at something you have to like it, not do it out of duty. Paul says someone who really likes programming rarely reaches 23 without building something on their own.
- Ideal founders build something they want to use themselves. The next best is something a person very close to them, such as a spouse or sibling, wants. That gives a deep sense of what is essential in the product, which "you can't intellectualize".
- He doesn't treat a founder couple as a plus or minus. What matters is that both would have been in the startup even if they weren't married. He warns against dragging in a spouse only because they're a spouse.

## Why most YC founders are young
- The average founder YC funds is about 25 or 26.
- Paul's explanation is not that youth is better. More people start startups at that age. By their 30s, people have families and mortgages, and the friends they'd start with are tied down too.
- 25-year-olds can say "what the hell, we'll try it".
- Paul says YC has funded some older founders. His answer to "should I recruit a 25-year-old" was not to do that. Mike answered that older founders should email him or Paul directly.

## Revenue forecasts and pitching
- YC has a "don't ask, don't tell" policy on revenue forecasts. Paul says "we don't even ask".
- A founder who doesn't know how to make money can say so. Paul's example: "we have no idea how to make money, we're worried about that, we know that's a problem, talk to us, let's figure out how to make money".
- Paul's BS detectors are generic language and buzzwords. He says never use the word "passion"; just seem like you care.
- On competitive analysis, the grid where the founder's column is all checked and the competitors' columns are empty is a bad sign. His advice is to be candid, and the smarter the investor, the more candid you should be.
- Tell investors what you'd say among yourselves, for example "we're worried about such-and-such company because of X, but we think we have an edge because of Y". Paul compares it to a doctor: "don't lie about your symptoms".
- A smart investor will notice if you lie.

## Early-stage expectations on product
- With tools like MySQL, Drupal and Rails, Paul expects a founder to show up with some kind of demo.
- Between the interview invitation and the interview, YC gives about three weeks, enough to book a cheap plane ticket. In that time they should be able to throw together something so the conversation isn't only talk. A working demo is "a good sign if they do".
- The exception is something that takes a long time to build, such as a hardware device.

## When to give up
- Paul says that as long as the founders haven't given up, they can morph the company into anything. In the early phase, a couple of people eating beans and writing code can turn it around tomorrow if they want.
- When founders ask him "can I give up now?", he says "probably the answer is yes". If they're asking, they've usually already decided and want him to be okay with it.
- If they say "we're a year in, our software still sucks, we have no customers, but we're not giving up", his answer is "keep going, I'll help you". He helps as long as they keep fighting.
- Founders often call an urgent meeting, with "need to get together" in the email, to deliver bad news: a missed quarter, or running out of money sooner than expected. Paul says he has never heard anyone say "I really need to come see you to tell you how great everything's going".
- He notes that bad news tends to arrive suddenly and good news gradually, as with plane crashes versus slowly improving air safety.

## Boards and predicting the next platform
- Paul has never been on a board and says he never will. YC doesn't take board seats.
- He says he can give any advice a board member could without the formal standing. If it's good, "they'll listen, one hopes".
- Nobody can predict the next big wave. If you could, you'd be funding those companies. Paul's practical heuristic is to look at which platforms hackers themselves use, because they build for the platforms they use.
- His example: many startups were building for the iPhone because their founders all had iPhones. None were building for the BlackBerry, which had market share but not among startup founders.
