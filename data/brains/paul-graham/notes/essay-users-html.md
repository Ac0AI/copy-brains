## Why "explain what you've learned from users" is the best advice per word

- Paul told Y Combinator applicants that the best advice for getting in, per word, was: "Explain what you've learned from users."
- He says the question tests three things at once: whether founders are paying attention to users, how well they understand them, and how much users need what the founders are making.
- He then asked himself the same question about YC's own users, the startups it funds. The lessons below are his answers.

## Most startups share the same problems, which is why funding many early companies works

- No two startups have exactly the same problems, but the problems stay surprisingly similar regardless of what the company makes.
- After advising 100 startups that all do different things, Paul says you rarely meet a problem you haven't seen before.
- He did not know this when YC started. He only had his own startup and friends' startups as data points.
- A YC partner gets this experience in the first year or two. Later-stage investors may not advise 100 startups in a whole career, so many never see the pattern.
- Funding large numbers of early-stage companies gives more data, "not just because you're looking at more companies, but also because more goes wrong."
- Knowing nearly all the problems does not mean advising can be automated or reduced to a formula. Each startup is unique and needs a specific partner who knows it well.
- He dislikes calling YC a "bootcamp." It is intense like one, but opposite in structure: instead of everyone doing the same thing, each startup talks to partners to find out what it specifically needs.

## The batch that broke YC: advising doesn't scale as a pool

- Until the summer 2012 batch, partners were treated as a pool. A startup requesting office hours got the next slot posted by any partner, so every partner had to know every startup.
- This worked up to 60 startups. At 80 it broke: halfway through the batch the partners still didn't know all the companies and were confused and unhappy.
- Paul's diagnosis: "We were using an O(n2) algorithm. So of course it blew up." A third more startups broke it because pairwise knowledge grows quadratically.
- The fix was the classic one: shard the batch into smaller groups, each overseen by a dedicated group of partners. It has worked ever since.
- Founders probably didn't notice a problem, and that batch did unusually well. "Broken" meant it felt wrong to the partners.
- Lesson: advising startups has to be individualized.

## Founders misjudge their problems, and the biggest one is often hidden

- Founders often come in to discuss one problem, and a much bigger one turns up during the conversation.
- Common example: founders say they struggle to raise money. Digging in shows the company is doing badly and investors can tell.
- Another example: founders worried they haven't cracked user acquisition. The real cause is that the product isn't good enough.
- Paul has asked "Would you use this yourself, if you hadn't built it?" and had founders answer "No" after thinking. "Well, there's the reason you're having trouble getting users."
- Even when founders know their problems, they often don't know their relative importance. A typical visit has three worries: one moderate, one irrelevant, one that will kill the company if not fixed immediately.
- His analogy is a horror movie heroine upset about her cheating boyfriend and only mildly curious about the door that's mysteriously ajar: "never mind about your boyfriend, think about that door!"
- Office hours let partners say that. Startups still die, but "it's rarely because they wandered into a room containing a murderer."
- He links this to research showing people are much better at answering questions than at judging how accurate their answers are.

## Why founders don't listen, and why that is useful

- Founders often don't take YC partners' advice. A partner told Paul: "They come back a year later and say 'We wish we'd listened to you.'"
- Stubbornness is part of it, but the more important reason is that so much about startups is counterintuitive. When you tell someone something counterintuitive, "what it sounds to them is wrong." Founders don't listen because they don't believe, until experience teaches them.
- Startups are counterintuitive because they differ from most people's other experiences. Nobody knows what it's like except people who've done it, which is why YC partners should usually have been founders.
- The counterintuitiveness is also part of why YC works: if startups weren't counterintuitive, founders wouldn't need the advice.
- Exception: the Airbnb founders were particularly good at listening, partly because they were flexible and disciplined, and partly because they'd had such a rough time the year before. "They were ready to listen."

## Focus enables speed: weekly cycles of action and measurement

- Early-stage startups have a hundred problems and only the founders to work on them. If founders focus on things that don't matter, nobody is focusing on the things that do.
- The essence of YC, in his words: figure out which problems matter most, cook up ideas for solving them "ideally at a resolution of a week or less," try them, and measure how well they worked. The focus is on action with measurable, near-term results.
- This does not mean rushing regardless of consequences. If you correct course often enough, you can be "simultaneously decisive at a micro scale and tentative at a macro scale." The path winds but is executed very fast, like a running back's path downfield.
- In practice there is less backtracking than expected. Founders usually guess the right direction, especially with an experienced partner to test hypotheses against, and when they guess wrong they notice fast because they report results at office hours the next week.
- Better navigation has a double effect: the path is shorter, and you travel faster along it when more certain it's right.
- His summary: "Speed defines startups. Focus enables speed. YC improves focus."
- The right unit of decisiveness depends on how long results take. Negotiating with investors might take a couple of days; building hardware might take months.

## Why founders are uncertain, and the habit of hacking the test

- Founders are uncertain partly because startups do something new, so nobody knows how to do it or even what "it" is, partly because startups are counterintuitive, and partly because ambitious young founders were trained to win the wrong way.
- The education system in most countries trains people to win by hacking the test instead of doing what the test is supposed to measure. That stops working in a startup.
- Part of what YC does is retrain founders to stop hacking the test. It takes a surprisingly long time: a year in, founders still revert to old habits.
- YC is not simply experienced founders passing on knowledge. It is "more like specialization than apprenticeship": partners have encyclopedic knowledge of startup problems, founders have deep domain knowledge, and neither would find it worthwhile to acquire the other's.
- That is why even an experienced founder can benefit from YC, like an experienced athlete benefiting from a coach.

## Colleagues matter as much as advice, and founders help each other

- Paul thinks colleagues may matter even more than partners' advice. Great work clusters in places and institutions: Florence in the late 15th century, Göttingen in the late 19th, The New Yorker under Ross, Bell Labs, Xerox PARC. "However good you are, good colleagues make you better."
- Very ambitious people probably need colleagues most, because they are so starved for them in everyday life.
- YC was deliberately designed to be such a cluster, and Paul says it is now the biggest cluster of great startup founders.
- Before YC, most people assumed colleagues and independence couldn't be combined: loneliness was the price of independence. That was how it felt when he started his own startup in Boston in the 1990s: a few older advisers of varying quality, but no peers to commiserate with about investors or speculate with about technology.
- YC was designed to be what he wanted as a founder, in line with his advice to make something you yourself want.
- Seed funding without "making the rounds of random rich people" has become a commodity, at least in the US. Great colleagues can't become a commodity, because they cluster in some places and are proportionally absent elsewhere.
- Of the energy in the room at a YC dinner: "like nothing else I've experienced."
- The happiest thing he has learned about founders is how generous they are in helping each other. YC noticed it in the first batch and designed the program to magnify it, so founders are surrounded by partners, alumni and batchmates who want to help and can.
