## Why Paul says ambitious founders should go to the center of their field

- Paul argues that every intense pursuit has one global center at a given time: painting in Paris (1870), math in Göttingen (1900), movies in Hollywood (1950), startups in Silicon Valley now.
- His answer to "should I go?" is always the same: "Yes, you should. You can go there for a bit and then come back, but you should at least go."
- The logic is the same as moving from a village to a capital within one country. The reasoning "doesn't even know the dotted line is there" when the move crosses a border.
- What you get is the best peers. The talent pool is better and larger, and the people cluster. Paul says a YC batch feels like this: every dinner is full of ambitious people working on the same thing.
- Being among people working on the same hard thing also lifts morale. He cites a founder who said that on reaching Silicon Valley he "finally met his people."

## Serendipitous meetings and speed in the big centers

- Paul says serendipitous meetings seem "enormously important." Biographies of people who did great things keep showing an unplanned meeting that changed their lives. He admits he isn't sure why.
- His candidate explanations:
  - There are simply more unplanned meetings, so more of the outliers are the unplanned kind.
  - Planned meetings are too conservative. They need a reason beforehand, which lops off outliers, "just like deliberately trying to find startup ideas lops off the outliers."
  - Unplanned meetings are self-selecting. After a few sentences you can decide whether to keep talking ("Oh, you're interested in that, too? We need to talk").
- Big centers also move faster. People are better and more confident, so they are more decisive. They "egg one another on," both encouraging and competing.
- In villages, people half have an idea and sit on it. Ten years later they say "I thought of that" about someone else's company.

## Why fundraising is faster in Silicon Valley

- Paul says Silicon Valley investors decide much faster than European ones. The cause is partly confidence and partly competition.
- The more right an investor is about a startup, the less time they can wait, because the opportunity will time out. This is an unusual situation where being more right means you can wait less.
- Example: Yury Sagalov invested immediately on meeting Max, reasoning that anyone else who met him would invest too.
- Investors complain that valuations are too high and they are rushed. Paul notes that empirically it doesn't hurt them: Silicon Valley investors get better returns than European investors despite deciding so quickly.
- Moving to the center also raises your standing at home. Investors outside Silicon Valley implicitly assume any local startup is second rate ("no one is a prophet in their own country"). Leaving reverses the rule. Telling local investors you were accepted to YC often makes them "trip over themselves" to invest.
- Dropbox example (summer 2007, a Boston batch):
  - A large Boston VC firm had spent a year offering encouragement and advice, but no money.
  - YC flew Boston startups to Silicon Valley for a second demo day because Boston investors were "so lame."
  - Sequoia got interested in Dropbox. The Boston firm's opinion changed instantly and it faxed Drew a term sheet with a blank valuation.
  - It was too late. Drew went with Sequoia. Dropbox was the first YC company to go public, in 2018.

## What moving to the big pond does to you

- Paul says the biggest advantage is not what the center does for you but what it does to you.
- In a small pond you can't tell how big a fish you are. In the big pond you measure yourself against known big fish. He says "surprisingly often, the news is good."
- Seeing people like Brian Chesky or Sam Altman up close, you can think "he's not a different species from me. I could do what he did if I worked that hard."
- He stresses the second half of that thought: you also see how hard they work. Startups are really hard, so meeting such a person "sets a new standard that you have to meet."
- The key point is that the goal stops looking impossible and becomes hard but definite. "For an ambitious person, there is nothing better than a high but definite threshold."
- He says it clears away the fog at the top of the mountain: the summit is visible and high, but not "invisibly high."
- He admits he half felt intimidated himself while calling law firms, but says that reaction is rarer than statistics would suggest.
- Part of the reason is that big founders who speak at YC batches try to be accessible and encouraging rather than intimidating.

## The pay-it-forward culture of Silicon Valley

- Paul says "in Silicon Valley, people help you for no reason," in the way Swedish streets are clean for no reason.
- He asked a founder friend from England who had moved to Silicon Valley how it differed from expectations. Paul calls "How is it different than you expected?" the best question to ask anyone who has gone to a new place. The answer was how helpful everyone was, with nearly every conversation ending in "What can I do to help you?"
- This isn't politeness, since the English are more polite than Americans. It is a pay-it-forward culture.
- His origin story: Silicon Valley is where people go from nobody to billionaire fastest. Someone who liked being nice to nobodies ended up with powerful friends and probably got rich. Whatever calculation started it, the custom is about 60 years old and is now just how people behave.
- Ron Conway is his example. He helps people all day, doesn't track whether they are his portfolio companies, and doesn't remember most of the favors. Paul compares this to honesty: you don't have to track your lies.
- With many people doing this there is no conservation law for favors, "there's just more favors." Paul says he and Ron have traded favors for 20 years and he assumes Ron is ahead.
- Moving to Silicon Valley will make you more helpful to others.

## How Sweden can thrive as a startup hub: go, then come back

- Paul says the answer to "how does Stockholm thrive as a startup hub?" is the same as the first question: founders should go to Silicon Valley for a bit, then come back.
- Precedent: 19th-century mathematicians didn't help their countries by boycotting Göttingen. The government gave fellowships conditional on leaving and studying math abroad.
- Returning founders help in three ways:
  - Their own startups are better, which raises the average quality locally.
  - They bring Silicon Valley investor money back.
  - They import a culture evolved over decades to suit startups. It is compatible with Swedish culture, with high trust, but without the "tall poppies" attitude, which Paul says Sweden should probably lose anyway.
- He admits this sounds like talking his own book, but says the optimal way to do it is YC:
  - It concentrates what is distinctive about Silicon Valley, like "a little super valley within the valley."
  - Everyone around you is a founder, you get instant colleagues, and the helpfulness norm is even stronger.
  - Investors decide even faster, "down to the minute."
  - It takes about four to six months.
  - It is funded by Silicon Valley investors, so it costs a government nothing. A government could just "call our API," with no sales process.

## Do founders who go home do worse, and the Silicon Valley of Europe

- Paul says YC's data shows startups that go home after YC are only about half as likely to become unicorns as those that stay.
- He gives three reasons not to be discouraged:
  - Selection bias. The data also measures the confidence and determination of founders willing to move countries.
  - It measures valuation, and Bay Area companies raise at higher valuations.
  - Half as good is still very good. Having $500 million instead of $1 billion makes little difference (in Swedish, "5 billion kronor").
- He adds that "money isn't everything": once you have kids, you care more about them than the company, and where they grow up becomes the most important question.
- The upside is that Stockholm could become the Silicon Valley of Europe. He says that job is still open, since asking "where's the Silicon Valley of Europe?" has no obvious answer, unlike "where's the Silicon Valley of America?"
- Evidence that small, off-center places can win: Mountain View was a backwater in 1955, when Shockley Semiconductor was founded, yet it was the geometric center of Silicon Valley until 2012.
- His formula: "All you need is a place founders want to live and a critical mass of them." He thinks Stockholm is the kind of place founders want to live. Critical mass is only visible once you hit it, so Stockholm may be closer than it thinks.
