## Schlep blindness: what it is and why it hides the best startup ideas

Paul defines a schlep as "a tedious, unpleasant task." *Schlep blindness* is his name for the way people fail to see startup ideas that involve painful schleps. The aversion is largely unconscious: "Your unconscious won't even let you see ideas that involve painful schleps." Great ideas lie around unexploited because the mind filters them out before they are ever considered.

- He compares it to physical fitness. Most people don't consciously decide against being in Olympic shape. Their unconscious mind decides for them by shrinking from the work.
- Nobody weighs "fix payments or build a recipe site?" and picks the recipe site. The hard option never makes it onto the list.

## Schleps are what business consists of

Paul says hackers dislike schleps most. They hope to write clever software, put it on a server and watch the money roll in, without talking to users, negotiating with other companies or dealing with other people's broken code. He says he hasn't seen that work.

- YC teaches hackers that schleps are inevitable: "No, you can't start a startup by just writing code."
- He went through this himself. In 1995 he was still trying to convince himself he could start a company by just writing code. Experience taught him that "schleps are not merely inevitable, but pretty much what business consists of."
- "A company is defined by the schleps it will undertake."
- His advice is to treat a schlep like a cold swimming pool and "just jump in."
- He does not say to seek out unpleasant work for its own sake. The point is to never shrink from it when it is on the path to something great.

## Stripe as the clearest example

Paul calls Stripe, or rather Stripe's idea, the most striking example of schlep blindness he knows.

- For over a decade, every hacker who had processed payments online knew how painful it was. Thousands of people must have known about the problem.
- Many of them still started recipe sites or aggregators for local events. These were problems few care about and no one will pay for, when they could have fixed "one of the most important components of the world's infrastructure."
- The schleps that scared people off:
  - Making deals with banks, with no obvious way to do it.
  - Fraud, and people trying to break into your servers, because you are moving money.
  - Regulations to comply with.
- Starting this kind of company is far more intimidating than starting a recipe site, so the idea was in plain sight but never seen.

## Why scary ideas are more valuable

Paul argues that the scariness of an ambitious idea is itself a source of value.

- Ambitious ideas have intrinsic value, and in addition they are "like undervalued stocks" because there is less demand for them among founders.
- Pick an ambitious idea and you face less competition, because everyone else was frightened off by the challenges.
- He notes this is also true of starting a startup in general.

## Antidote one: ignorance, and why young founders win

Paul says the most valuable antidote to schlep blindness is probably ignorance.

- Most successful founders would say that had they known the obstacles ahead, they might never have started.
- This may be one reason the most successful startups so often have young founders.
- In practice, "the founders grow with the problems," but no one seems able to foresee that, not even older, more experienced founders.
- Young founders make two mistakes that cancel out: they don't know how much they can grow, and they don't know how much they'll need to. Older founders make only the first mistake.

## Antidote two: ask what you wish someone else would solve

Ignorance can't solve everything, because some ideas obviously involve alarming schleps that anyone can see. For those, Paul recommends taking yourself out of the picture.

- Don't ask "what problem should I solve?" Ask "what problem do I wish someone else would solve for me?"
- Anyone who had struggled with payments before Stripe, and asked that question, would have put Stripe among the first things they wished for.
- He says it is too late to be Stripe, but "there's plenty still broken in the world, if you know how to see it."
