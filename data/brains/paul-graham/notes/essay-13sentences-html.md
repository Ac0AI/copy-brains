## Cofounders and early launch as the foundation

- Paul tells startups to pick good cofounders. His analogy: "Cofounders are for a startup what location is for real estate. You can change anything about a house except where it is."
- A startup's idea is easy to change; its cofounders are hard to change. He says "the success of a startup is almost always a function of its founders."
- He tells startups to launch fast. The reason is less about getting to market early and more that "you haven't really started working on it till you've launched."
- Launching teaches founders what they should have been building. Until they know that, they are wasting their time.
- He sees the main value of the initial launch as a pretext for engaging users.

## Let the idea evolve and understand users

- Paul calls launching fast and letting the idea evolve two halves of one practice: launch fast and iterate.
- He calls it a big mistake to treat a startup as implementing some brilliant initial idea. "As in an essay, most of the ideas appear in the implementing."
- He pictures the wealth a startup creates as a rectangle: one side is the number of users, the other is how much the startup improves their lives. Founders have the most control over the second side, and growth in the first is driven by how well they do on the second.
- He says the hard part is asking questions, not answering them: seeing something new that users lack. The better founders understand users, the better the odds of seeing it.
- That is why many successful startups make something the founders themselves needed.
- If he could keep only one of the 13 principles, it would be "Understand your users." Once you know what to make, "it's mere effort to make it, and most decent hackers are capable of that."
- Understanding users sits behind half the list: launching early, evolving the idea, surprisingly good customer service, and morale ("having just ten users who love you will keep you going").

## Make a few users love you, and serve them surprisingly well

- Paul learned from Paul Buchheit that it is better to make a few people really happy than a lot of people semi-happy.
- Early on, a startup must choose between satisfying all the needs of a subset of potential users or a subset of the needs of all potential users. His advice: take the first.
- Reason one: "It's easier to expand userwise than satisfactionwise."
- Reason two: it is harder to lie to yourself. If you think you are 85% of the way to a great product, you cannot tell whether it is 70% or 10%, but you can easily know how many users you have.
- He says to offer customer service that is not merely good but surprisingly good. Customers are used to being maltreated by quasi-monopolies, which unconsciously lowers founders' ideas of what is possible.
- Go out of your way to make people happy; "they'll be overwhelmed."
- In the earliest stages it pays to offer service that would not scale, because it is a way of learning about users.

## Measure what you want to improve

- Paul learned "you make what you measure" from Joe Kraus. Merely measuring something tends to improve it.
- His tactic for growing users: put a big piece of paper on the wall and plot the number of users every day.
- Founders will be delighted when it goes up and disappointed when it goes down. Soon they notice what moves the number and do more of it.
- His corollary: be careful what you measure.

## Spend little and get ramen profitable

- Paul stresses that a startup must be cheap. Most startups fail before they make something people want, and the most common form of failure is running out of money.
- He says being cheap is almost interchangeable with iterating rapidly. It is not exactly the same, because markets do not stand still: working twice as fast is better than having twice as much time.
- A culture of cheapness keeps companies young "in something like the way exercise keeps people young."
- "Ramen profitable" means the startup makes just enough to pay the founders' living expenses.
- He frames it as a way of hacking the investment process more than rapid prototyping of business models. Once crossed, it completely changes the relationship with investors, and it is great for morale.

## Avoid distractions, morale killers and falling-through deals

- Paul says nothing kills startups like distractions. The worst are those that pay money: day jobs, consulting, profitable side projects. Founders keep interrupting the startup to answer calls from people paying them now.
- He calls fundraising this same kind of distraction, so founders should minimize it too.
- The immediate cause of death is usually running out of money, but the underlying cause is usually lack of focus. Either the people are stupid (which advice cannot fix) or smart people got demoralized.
- Starting a startup is a huge moral weight. He advises making a conscious effort not to be ground down, "just as you'd be careful to bend at the knees when picking up a heavy box."
- Even if demoralized, don't give up: "You can get surprisingly far by just not giving up." He notes this is not true in all fields (some people could never become good mathematicians however long they persisted), but in startups sheer effort is usually enough "so long as you keep morphing your idea."
- Deals fall through. At Viaweb, he says, they probably had 20 deals of various types fall through. After the first 10 or so they treated deals as background processes to ignore until they terminated.
- Depending on deals closing is dangerous for morale, because they often don't close, and depending on them makes them less likely to.
