## The three requirements and why ideas matter little

- Paul says a startup needs three things: "good people, to make something customers actually want, and to spend as little money as possible." Most failures come from missing one of these.
- A startup that does all three "will probably succeed." None needs brilliance; each is "hard, but doable."
- An idea is only a starting point. VCs will tell you to get lost if you demand an NDA, so "the market price is less than the inconvenience of signing an NDA."
- Plans change en route. Microsoft started by selling programming languages and found its real model only when IBM "dropped it in their lap five years later."
- "Good people can fix bad ideas, but good ideas can't save bad people."

## Finding ideas: do it in a way that doesn't suck

- Google's plan was "simply to create a search site that didn't suck." Its three ideas were indexing more of the Web, ranking by links, and clean pages with unintrusive keyword ads. The result brought in about a billion dollars a year (Paul's note: roughly $2B in revenue, half from ads on other sites).
- His main heuristic: look at something people are trying to do and figure out how to do it better. Dating sites in 2005 were worse than pre-Google search because they modelled "database matches" instead of how dating works.
- Look for people unlike you. The only software you can build without studying users is software for which you are the typical user, which is mostly open source. So ask what people who are not like you want from technology.
- Niche markets give the best odds. For every Apple or Google there are twenty startups in niches or infrastructure. Corporate IT departments are a prime target: companies spend huge sums on software and get "crap in return."
- Tactic: spend a couple of weeks watching a middle-sized non-technology company use its computers.

## Hiring and founders: the "animal" test

- Paul's best hiring rule: "Could you describe the person as an animal?" It means someone who takes their work a little too seriously: a salesperson who won't take no, a hacker who stays up till 4 AM rather than leave a bug, a PR person who cold-calls New York Times reporters.
- To apply it, imagine the sentence "so-and-so is an animal." If you laugh, they aren't one.
- Programmers also had to pass three tests: genuinely smart, able to get things done, and bearable to have around.
- A sign of real smarts is the ability to say "I don't know," "Maybe you're right," and "I don't understand x well enough." Robert Morris was the model.
- Hire through friends. Being friends with someone for a couple of days tells you more than interviews can.
- Aim for two to four founders. One is too heavy a moral burden, and more than that breeds factions. "You don't want mere voting; you need unanimity."
- Founders must include technical people. Business people can't tell which hackers are good ("for business people it's roulette") or which problems are hard.
- Business is no great mystery: "You just try to get people to pay you for stuff." Few Forbes 400 members have MBAs, so learning to hack beats an MBA.
- On hiring generally: "hiring people is the worst thing a company can do." Staff are a recurring expense, they slow decisions into meetings, and they push you out of your space. "The only reason to hire someone is to do something you'd like to do but can't." Fewer employees at the same revenue is more impressive.

## Making what customers want

- Failed businesses almost always failed because customers didn't want the product. Restaurants with great food prosper despite crowding, noise or bad service; Paul says he can't name a startup with a massively popular product that failed.
- "Ran out of funding" is only the proximate cause. The deeper cause is usually that the product was "a dog."
- Release version 1 as soon as possible and refine it from user reactions. He rejects the "Hail Mary" approach of planning elaborately, hiring engineers, and spending two million dollars on something nobody wants.
- Viaweb changed plan repeatedly. Web consultants disliked it because the easy, hosted software made them easy to fire. Catalog-company middle managers saw the Web as extra work in 1996. Most users ended up being small individual merchants, so the team focused on ease of use.
- Ease of use compounds: "A 10% improvement in ease of use doesn't just increase your sales 10%. It's more likely to double your sales."
- Method for learning what users want: watch them. Viaweb used trade shows as market research rather than lead generation, showing people how to build real stores and watching them use the software.
- Put users before advertisers: "get all the users, and the advertisers will follow." Design to please users first, then work out how to make money.
- Understanding your business is the key. Google understood search; Yahoo treated it as a shrinking share of page views, though search is where sessions start.

## Selling to small customers and owning the low end

- Start by selling to smaller companies. Big-company sellers spend heavily on sales: "you can outhack Oracle with one frontal lobe tied behind your back, you can't outsell an Oracle salesman."
- Smaller customers are also strategically more valuable because "the low end always eats the high end." It is easier to make a cheap product more powerful than a powerful product cheaper.
- Examples: Sun over mainframes, Intel over Sun, Microsoft Word over Interleaf and Framemaker, Avid over specialized video editing, Henry Ford over earlier car makers.
- "It's very dangerous to let anyone fly under you." Own the cheapest, easiest product or you're in the crosshairs of whoever does.
- Avoid selling to the government (note 4).

## Raising money and setting up the company

- Paul advises taking investor money: self-funding means starting as a consulting company, which is hard to turn into a product company. "Financially, a startup is like a pass/fail course", so maximize the chance of success, not the stock you keep.
- Seed capital is a few tens of thousands of dollars from angels, often tech-rich people. Checks can arrive within a week on a half-page agreement. Viaweb began with $10,000 from Julian, who also gave legal and business help, and in return took 10%. The company was valued at $1 million because Julian said no one would invest at a lower valuation.
- The business plan at this stage is a brief description of what you'll do, how you'll make money, and founders' resumes. It takes a couple of hours to write.
- Valuation has "no rational way" of being set at this stage. A 5% stake for $100,000 implies a $2 million pre-money valuation.
- Stock split rule of thumb: "When everyone feels they're getting a slightly bad deal... the stock is optimally apportioned."
- Have all founders sign that their ideas belong to the company and that it is their only job. Ask everyone about prior IP agreements. At Viaweb, an early employee's grad-school sponsor agreement threatened the acquisition; it nearly killed the company, then Yahoo bought it six months later for more than the first acquirer had offered.
- Start talking to VCs before cash runs low, since they take months to decide. VCs may want to install a mature "newscaster" CEO; Viaweb avoided this by funding entirely with angels.
- Your leverage is higher than you think because it is a seller's market. Treat a VC mainly as a source of money and favor "whoever offered the most money the soonest with the least strings attached."
- Don't be overtly secretive with VCs, but keep a few critical technical details back. Talk to many VCs even if you don't want their money: they may sit on the board of a future acquirer, and impressing them discourages them from funding competitors.

## Not spending money and growing slowly

- Almost every failed startup's proximate cause is running out of money. Despite millions in the bank, "you're still poor": a rich company is one with large revenues, and investor money isn't revenue.
- Brand advertising is a "sign of breakage" unless products are undifferentiated, like cigarettes or vodka. Google never advertised and still overtook Yahoo, Lycos, Excite and Altavista.
- "Get big slow." Viaweb launched in early 1996 and had about 70 users by year-end. That year was a lab for improving the software, so competitors never caught up. The founders learned every job, including sales and customer support (Paul fixed bugs while customers were on the phone).
- Smart early users are the most valuable asset: they tell you how to make a winning product, and they pay you.
- Culture of cheapness: Yahoo's David Filo called himself "Cheap Yahoo." Model the company on a grad student, not a law firm. Viaweb had office chairs whose arms fell off.
- Office tactics: rent an apartment instead of corporate space. People work better there, and locations with restaurants nearby let teams eat dinner together and return to work. "The key to productivity is for people to come back to work after dinner."

## Who should start a startup

- Paul's profile: a good hacker aged roughly 23 to 38 who wants to "solve the money problem in one shot."
- The lower bound of 23 exists so you've seen an existing business first (negative lessons count) and so VCs, customers and employees take you seriously. The upper bound reflects stamina and financial risk.
- A startup "compresses your working life into the smallest possible space": work like hell for four years instead of 40. Paul worked nearly constantly for three years, and only about 10% of the work was fun.
- "So mainly what a startup buys you is time."
- Grad students can stay in school at first; two of Viaweb's three original hackers kept their degrees.
- His closing summary: "Build something users love, and spend less than you make."
