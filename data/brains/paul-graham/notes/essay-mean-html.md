## Why meanness correlates with failure in startups

Paul noticed that almost none of the most successful people he knows are mean, and that Jessica Livingston (his wife and Y Combinator cofounder) sees the same pattern. She came from investment banking and is struck by "how consistently successful startup founders turn out to be good people, and how consistently bad people fail as startup founders."

- Meanness isn't rare. The internet exposed the "long tail of meanness" that used to be hidden.
- He admits selection bias: he only knows startup founders, programmers and professors. He allows that hedge fund managers or drug lords may be mean and successful.
- His claim is narrower: there are big chunks of the world that mean people don't rule, and that territory is growing.

## Being mean makes you stupid, and fights waste your brain

Paul hates fights because "being mean makes you stupid."

- Winning a fight is a function of the specific situation and people involved. You win with tricks that work in one particular case, not with big ideas.
- Fighting takes as much effort as thinking about real problems, but it produces nothing general. His image: "your brain goes fast but you get nowhere, like a car spinning its wheels."
- He says you never do your best work in a fight.
- Startups "don't win by attacking. They win by transcending." Usually the way to win is to race ahead, not stop and fight.

## Mean founders can't hire the best people

- A mean founder can hire people who tolerate him because they need a job.
- The best people have other options. A mean person can't persuade them to join unless he is "super convincing."
- Having the best people helps any organization, but Paul says it is critical for startups.

## Benevolence is an advantage in building great things

- The richest founders are not the ones driven by money. Those driven by money take the big acquisition offer that nearly every successful startup gets along the way.
- The founders who keep going are driven by something else. They may not say so, but they are usually trying to improve the world, so people with that desire have a natural advantage.
- Caveats he adds: founders who don't take the offer aren't driven only by money, and money can have benevolent motives (supporting family, freedom to work on projects that improve the world).
- Not every successful startup improves the world, but founders, like parents, truly believe theirs does. Successful founders are in love with their companies, and that love is as blind as romantic love but genuine.

## The shift from zero-sum to non-zero-sum games

Paul argues startups are the future of work, not a random niche.

- For most of history, success meant controlling scarce resources, won by fighting: literally (pastoral nomads driving hunter-gatherers into marginal lands) or metaphorically (Gilded Age financiers assembling railroad monopolies). In those zero-sum games meanness was probably an advantage.
- Increasingly the games that matter are not zero-sum. You win by "having new ideas and building new things" rather than seizing a scarce resource.
- Peter Thiel's counterpoint, which Paul accepts as largely true: successful founders still get rich from monopolies, but ones they create rather than capture. Paul says this still means a big change in who wins.

## Why this shift needs civil order

- Ideas only win when people feel what they create can't be stolen. That requires more than absence of war: it also means preventing economic violence like that of nineteenth-century magnates or communist states against their citizens.
- Archimedes won by ideas, until an invading Roman army killed him (by accident; the Roman commander had ordered him spared).
- In sufficiently disordered times, "even thinking requires control of scarce resources, because living at all is a scarce resource."
- Thinkers were first to benefit: the non-ruthless successful figures of history are mathematicians, writers and artists. Paul says their way of operating is leaking into the real world, "reversing the historical polarity of the relationship between meanness and success."

## Paul's takeaway for parenting

- He and Jessica teach their kids not to be mean. They tolerate noise, mess and junk food, but not meanness.
- The essay gave him an extra argument for cracking down: "being mean makes you fail."
