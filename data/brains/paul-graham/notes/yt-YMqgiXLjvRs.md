## How YC started and the summer-program origin

- Paul says YC started for two reasons: he felt guilty he hadn't gotten around to Angel Investing, and Jessica was talking to a Boston VC fund about becoming their VP of marketing. The VCs took so long to decide that he said "[ __ ] them we'll just start our own thing."
- The first batch (summer 2005, Cambridge) was pitched as a summer program for undergraduates. Compared to a regular summer job ("work in a cubicle", "work on not what you would want with people you don't want to work with"), it was a great deal: work on whatever you want with whoever you want, get paid, and get dinner once a week.
- Hundreds of applications came in, more than expected. The first batch included Sam Altman (Loopt), Reddit, Kiko (founders went on to Justin.tv/Twitch) and Aaron Swartz (Infogami, which merged with Reddit).
- The three-month length exists because that was summer vacation. Weekly dinners were modeled on the Society of Fellows at Harvard. Demo Day was only decided about halfway through the first batch.
- Paul's summary: "a bunch of accidents that turned out to be good." Three months "turned out to be just about right."
- After Foo Camp he concluded someone would start the YC of Silicon Valley "and it better be us," so they moved the next batch across the country. They ended up in Mountain View because a friend, Trevor, had a building. The application form said the location (Cambridge or Silicon Valley) was undecided.

## Deal terms and why YC is not an old-style incubator

- The deal was modeled on Paul's own startup (Viaweb). A lawyer, the husband of his painting teacher, gave them $10,000, incorporated them and sat on the board. He "saved us from stepping in a bunch of dog poops" (landmines). Paul first thought it was a great deal for the lawyer (10% for $10k), then realized it was a great deal for the founders too.
- YC thought 10% was too much, so it asked for 6%, which later became 7%.
- The cash amount came from asking an MIT professor what MIT grad students got for the summer: $2,000 a month. So YC gave $6,000 per founder, because you could live on that in Cambridge.
- The 1990s incubators were the model of "what not to do." They took 30-40% (or more), had you work out of their building, and ran your accounting, so you were "almost like a subsidiary" and "very much like an employee."
- Paul had been a founder and asked himself whether he would have taken that deal. He saw it as an adverse selection filter: the best founders would refuse.
- YC was meant to be "first gear," after which founders raise money at market rates from whoever they like. He notes the word "incubator" now effectively means YC.

## Spotting good founding teams

- Paul says the patterns visible across 632 startups are "astonishing." The big one: founders must get along. "You don't know how good friends you are with somebody until you try to start a startup with them." Founder conflict shows up in a class often, and Apple had three founders, not two.
- Interview tells that a team is dysfunctional:
  - One founder looks in terror at the other before answering.
  - A founder rolls their eyes while the co-founder speaks.
  - A founder doesn't show up and the other says "I don't know, he was supposed to be here." (All of these have happened.)
- He is skeptical of the business-school founder who has an idea and recruits a meek 20-year-old CS major to build it. That is a poor founding team.
- Working model for strong teams: each founder is king of their own domain. Wozniak built the Apple I and Jobs sold it; Jobs didn't change what Woz built. Each is master of their own domain and they don't step on each other's toes.
- On solo founders: YC prefers more than one founder. Drew Houston applied to YC as a solo founder; YC told him to get a co-founder, and he did before the interview (about two weeks). Paul's reaction to someone pitching an idea: "who's your co-founder? That's what I care about."
- MBAs are not automatically penalized. Rescale's CEO has an MBA but also deep domain expertise in the aircraft industry.
- A signal he has changed his mind on: people who are very smart but ineffectual. "We used to have more faith in brains." Now: "you can be surprisingly stupid if you're sufficiently determined." Being "fearsomely effective" matters more than raw smarts.

## Interviews, decisions and the "unpredictable" winners

- YC does about 20 ten-minute interviews a day and must decide right after each one, while memory is fresh. After years the partners can signal "this person is in" with their eyes. There are a few borderline cases, and many clear yes or clear no cases.
- Dropbox, Airbnb and Stripe were all no-brainers: "we didn't even need the full 10 minutes."
- Paul does not claim he predicted they'd be worth billions: "you cannot predict that, that is unpredictable." Drew Houston's application answered the old question "how much would you take if someone wanted to buy you after YC?" with far less than $10 billion, so YC dropped the question as predicting nothing.
- Many billion-dollar companies get there partly because acquirers' M&A people are not smart enough to pick them off early.
- Valuation is not value: if a company hasn't raised for a year or two and grows about 5x a year, its last valuation is off by something like 25x after two years. He called Dropbox (about $10 billion at the time) and Airbnb peers by value, and said Stripe is in that group too.
- A rejection lesson: one company (applied to YC, later successful elsewhere) was read by only one partner, Robert Morris, who gave a harsh grade and the comment "Spam company." YC now has a second person review any application Robert grades badly, and about 10 people read applications.
- Companies who come to YC already launched are not predictably better or worse: Airbnb had been live a year but flatlined before YC.

## Scaling YC: the N-squared mistake

- The summer 2012 batch (80-something startups) felt too big. The cause: until then every partner had to know what every startup was doing, and office hours were assigned FIFO to whoever was in the queue longest.
- Paul's diagnosis: "an N squared algorithm" and "we're all computer science people and we made this mistake." Some startups were halfway through the batch before he knew what they did.
- Fix: shard it. YC now has three silos, each overseen by a group of Partners, "basically three little Y combinators." With sharding, 68 startups per batch is "no problem."
- On the $150K-style uncapped note given to every startup: it caused fights over money when founders split, since "$150,000 is a lot of money" and a departing founder would demand "my $75,000" (it was the company's money). He considered eliminating the notes but polled startups; about 80% said the money helped significantly, so YC kept a smaller version.
- Org changes: Sam Altman takes over as president because YC has to grow and Paul is "no good at running giant things." Paul will do office hours a couple of days a week, which is about a tenth of what he used to do. The rest was internal overhead. He would stop reading applications and doing interviews, and expected to get his brain back after being unable to stop thinking about YC "in the shower in the morning."

## Advice on focus and fundraising

- "The most important thing for startups to do is to focus." Of the thousand things you could do, one is the most important. It works like optimizing software: find the current bottleneck and work on that.
- Founders usually know the answer but don't ask themselves. At office hours YC asks "which one is the most important?" and then "so why aren't you doing that?"
- Fundraising: shift into fundraising mode, raise, and stop. Do not "promiscuously meet with investors in the middle of the day" just because they email "let's grab coffee."
- Funding climate (2014): valuations are high, but that isn't a bubble. A bubble means people believe they can sell the overpriced thing to a greater fool later, and that needs a public-market bubble, which he didn't see. Prices move like a sine wave, so high prices will likely come down somewhat.
- Valuation advice is not a blanket rule. For some startups, if anyone offers money you should take it on any terms.
- Being YC-funded does not automatically get you customers, unless you sell to startups. A consumer product still has to earn its users.

## How to grow: the small intense fire

- Start with "a small intense fire." You can't make something a large number of people want a lot, so find a small number who want it a lot. That is how Apple and Facebook started.
- Example: the Apple I, about 500 units. Find those first users, know how you'll get them, then "just sit down and have a party with those first few users" and make them super happy.
- A mobile app might need 5,000 rather than 500, but the principle holds.
- Example from the current batch: a team building a mobile email client whose beta group is one user, Sam Altman. His demands make him a good mold; if they satisfy him they can satisfy many.
- Tactic for extreme cases: act like a consultant hired by that one user (it could even say his name at the top of the screen).
- The test of product-market fit: the user "would be bummed if you stopped working on this." Friends saying "oh yeah it's good, I'll use it" is not the test.
- On Google Wave as a launch lesson: it was launched "like New Coke" as a thing that would change everything, doing so much it seemed to do nothing. Paul says it should have launched as an apparently inconsequential real-time typing tool and let users see how much bigger it could be. YC funded EtherPad (real-time collaborative writing) four times and thinks the idea will happen.
- Starting a startup is painful. "There's a conservation law there": don't expect it to be easy and also a great way to get money. He says if it isn't a certain amount of painful, "you're probably not trying hard enough."
