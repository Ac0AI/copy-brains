## Startups take off because founders push them, so recruit users manually

- Paul rejects the belief that startups either take off on their own or the market must not exist. He says "startups take off because the founders make them take off."
- His metaphor is the hand crank on pre-electric-starter car engines. Once the engine runs it keeps going, but getting it going is a separate, laborious process.
- The most common unscalable task is recruiting users one at a time. "You can't wait for users to come to you. You have to go out and get them."
- Even Stripe, whose problem was urgent, was famous within YC for aggressive early user acquisition. YC calls the technique the "Collison installation": when someone agreed to try Stripe, the Collisons said "Right then, give me your laptop" and set them up on the spot. Diffident founders instead say "we'll send you a link."
- Airbnb's early version was going door to door in New York, recruiting users and helping existing hosts improve their listings. About 30 days of in-person engagement made the difference between success and failure. Paul says marketplaces are so hard to start that you should expect "heroic measures."
- Reasons founders resist:
  - Shyness and laziness. At least one founder (usually the CEO) must spend a lot of time on sales and marketing. You can't avoid sales by hiring someone to do it. Do it yourself first, then hire a real salesperson to replace you.
  - The absolute numbers look too small. He says this underestimates compound growth.
- Manual recruiting usually works if the market exists. Start manual, then gradually switch to less manual methods.

## Measure weekly growth and expect compounding

- Paul encourages every startup to measure progress by weekly growth rate.
- Example: with 100 users, you need 10 more next week to grow 10% a week. 110 doesn't look much better than 100, but at 10% a week you reach 14,000 users after a year and 2 million after 2 years.
- Growth must slow eventually, and you'll do different things when acquiring users a thousand at a time.
- Size starts to help growth. He quotes Patrick Collison: "At some point, there was a very noticeable change in how Stripe felt. It tipped from being this boulder we had to push to being a train car that in fact had its own momentum."

## Early startups are fragile; ask how big they could get

- Almost all startups are fragile at first. Inexperienced founders, investors, reporters and forum commenters judge "larval" startups by the standards of established ones, like looking at a newborn and concluding it can't accomplish anything.
- Outsiders dismissing you is harmless. Investors will change their minds when they see growth. The real danger is the founders dismissing their own startup. Paul says even Bill Gates returned to Harvard for the fall semester after starting Microsoft, which he wouldn't have done if he'd seen how big it would get.
- The right question is not "is this company taking over the world?" but "how big could this company get if the founders did the right things?" The right things often seem "both laborious and inconsequential at the time."
- Examples: Microsoft was a couple of people in Albuquerque writing Basic interpreters for a few thousand hobbyists. Airbnb's founders were taking "professional" photos of their first hosts' apartments.
- Finding users to recruit:
  - If you build for your own problems, you only need to find your peers.
  - Otherwise, do a comparatively untargeted launch, see which users are most enthusiastic, and seek out more like them. Ben Silbermann saw that many early Pinterest users were into design, so he went to a conference of design bloggers to recruit users, and it worked well.
  - For things like enterprise software with no connections, you'd rely on cold calls and introductions. He asks whether you should be working on such an idea at all.

## Delight users and make the experience insanely great

- Take extraordinary measures to make early users happy, not just to acquire them. Wufoo sent each new user a hand-written thank-you note for as long as it could, which was surprisingly long. Early users should feel signing up was one of their best choices.
- Why founders resist:
  - Engineers aren't trained in customer service.
  - They worry it won't scale. Paul says a larval startup has nothing to lose, and having too many users to delight would be a great problem. Delight scales better than expected, partly because it will have permeated the culture.
  - They've never experienced such attention, because their standards come from big companies. "Tim Cook doesn't send you a hand-written note after you buy a laptop. He can't. But you can."
- "I have never once seen a startup lured down a blind alley by trying too hard to make their initial users happy."
- Garry Tan's trap: founders imitate big companies' flaws, like indifference to individual users, to seem "professional." Paul says to embrace being small and use its advantages.
- He borrows Steve Jobs's "insanely great." In a larval startup it isn't the product that should be insanely great but "the experience of being your user." The product is one component. You can give an insanely great experience with an early, incomplete, buggy product if you make up the difference with attentiveness.
- Over-engaging with early users is a necessary part of the feedback loop that makes the product good.
  - The first thing you build is never quite right.
  - Except where mistakes carry big penalties, don't aim for perfection. In software, get something in front of users once it has "a quantum of utility," then see what they do.
  - "Perfectionism is often an excuse for procrastination," and your initial model of users is always inaccurate, even if you're one of them.
- Direct feedback from your earliest users will be the best you ever get. Later you'll wish you could watch users in their homes and offices instead of using focus groups.

## Start in a deliberately narrow market (the contained fire)

- Paul compares it to keeping a fire contained at first to get it really hot before adding more logs.
- Facebook began only for Harvard students, a market of a few thousand people. Because they felt it was really for them, a critical mass signed up. It then stayed college-by-college for a long time. Zuckerberg told Paul that creating course lists for each school was a lot of work but made students feel the site was their natural home.
- Marketplaces usually must start in a subset of the market, but it works for other startups too. Always ask whether there's a subset where you can reach critical mass quickly.
- Most founders do this unconsciously by building for themselves and friends who are early adopters. The danger is discarding part of the pattern, for example if you come from the corporate world and your friends aren't early adopters.
- Best early adopters among companies are other startups. They're open to new things, haven't made all their choices yet, and grow fast when they succeed. This gives B2B startups an instant market among YC companies.
- If choosing between the segment that signs up quickest and the segment that pays most, usually pick the quickest. They're probably early adopters, influence the product better, need less sales effort, and you don't need much money early on to hit your target growth rate.

## Consult, use your own software, and do things manually

- Consult for one user (for B2B). Pick a single user and act like a consultant building just for them. "The initial user serves as the form for your mold." Tweak until it fits their needs, and you'll usually find others want it too. One user who really needs something and can act on it is a toehold.
- Don't be paid for it. It's safe while you're a product company being extra attentive. Once they pay by the hour, "they expect you to do everything." He doesn't worry about building something useful to only one user, since those cases are usually obvious.
- Use your software on users' behalf. At Viaweb, some merchants who declined the software let them build a store for them, so they did, selling luggage, pens and men's shirts. It taught them how merchants would feel using the software. Sometimes feedback was near instant: mid-build Paul would find a missing feature, spend a couple of hours implementing it, then resume building the site.
- Be the software. With few users, do by hand what you plan to automate later. You launch faster and know exactly what to build because of the muscle memory. Stripe delivered "instant" merchant accounts by manually signing users up for traditional merchant accounts behind the scenes.
- Some startups could be entirely manual at first: solve a real problem by hand, then gradually automate the bottlenecks. Paul says this is less frightening than "having something automatic that doesn't yet solve anyone's problems."

## Hardware: pull a Meraki

- Hardware startups face a catch-22. Minimum factory runs usually cost several hundred thousand dollars, and without a product you can't generate the growth needed to raise the money to make it.
- "Pulling a Meraki" means assembling the product yourselves. Meraki's founders, Robert Morris's grad students, assembled their routers themselves. Paul didn't fund them.
- Pebble assembled the first several hundred watches itself. Paul says without that phase they probably wouldn't have sold $10 million worth of watches when they went on Kickstarter. Crowdfunding (more precisely, preorders) has helped, but he still advises pulling a Meraki if you can.
- Being the factory lets you tweak the design faster and learn things you'd never otherwise know. Eric Migicovsky of Pebble learned "how valuable it was to source good screws."

## Big launches and partnerships usually don't work; think of ideas as vectors

- Big Launch: founders who think startups are projectiles that need high initial velocity, such as launching in 8 publications with embargoes on a Tuesday. Paul says that all you need from a launch is an initial core of users. "How well you're doing a few months later will depend more on how happy you made those users than how many there were of them." Ask how many launches of successful startups you remember.
- He suspects an inverse correlation between launch magnitude and success. He remembers only flops like the Segway and Google Wave, and thinks Wave was a great idea partly killed by its overdone launch.
- Founders believe in launches out of solipsism and laziness. Getting users is always gradual, because great things are novel and users have other things on their minds.
- Partnerships usually don't work, especially for starting growth. Inexperienced founders think a big-company partnership will be their big break. Six months later they say it was far more work than expected and got them practically nothing. Google grew on the back of Yahoo, but Yahoo was its customer, not a partner.
- "It's not enough just to do something extraordinary initially. You have to make an extraordinary effort initially." Any strategy that omits the effort is "ipso facto suspect."
- Think of a startup idea as a vector: what you'll build, plus the unscalable things you'll do initially to get going. Usually the second part is recruiting users manually and giving them an overwhelmingly good experience.
- An idea with an empty second component, such as no way to find users to recruit manually, is probably a bad idea for those founders.
- Best case, the unscalable work becomes company DNA. Aggressive user acquisition when small tends to continue when big, and so does working hard to delight users.
