## Why nice founders lose little: the growth-curve math

Paul argues that startup founders can safely be nice, because making great things compounds and rapacity (squeezing every penny from customers) does not. His reasoning came from a founder's email. The founder worried he was "fundamentally soft-hearted," gave away too much for free, and thought he might need "a little dose of sociopath-ness."

- Paul told him not to worry. If he built something good enough to spread by word of mouth, he would have a superlinear growth curve.
- Being bad at extracting money only shrinks that curve by a constant multiple below 1.
- A constant multiple of any curve has the same shape. The Y-axis numbers are smaller, but the curve is just as steep, "and when anything grows at the rate of a successful startup, the Y axis will take care of itself."
- Paul's one-line summary: "The reason startup founders can safely be nice is that making great things is compounded, and rapacity isn't."

## Worked example: extracting half the money costs 15 weeks

Paul's numbers assume a company making $1000 a month and growing 5% a week.

- After two years it makes about $160k a month.
- A founder who extracts only half as much from users makes $80k a month at that point instead.
- The un-rapacious founder catches up to where the rapacious one would have been in "a mere 15 weeks." After two years, he is only 3.5 months behind.
- Paul's footnote gives the reason: since 2 ≈ 1.05^15, a founder extracting half as much is always 15 weeks behind the one extracting everything.

## Optimize growth rate, not extraction

Paul says that if you are going to optimize one number, it should be your growth rate. He extends the same example:

- The nice founder still extracts only half as much from users, but grows 6% a week instead of 5%.
- After two years he is already ahead: $214k a month versus $160k for the rapacious founder.
- A year later he makes $4.4 million a month, against the rapacious founder's $2 million.
- Small differences in weekly growth outweigh large differences in how much money is taken per customer.

## When rapacity might help (and why it usually doesn't)

Paul concedes rapacity helps where growth depends on it. His claim is that for startups it usually doesn't.

- Startups usually win by making something so great that people recommend it to their friends.
- Being rapacious "not only doesn't help you do that, but probably hurts."
- Second possible benefit: startups usually lose money at first, so higher revenue per customer makes it easier to reach profitability before the initial funding runs out.
- Paul notes it is very common for startups to die from running through their funding and being unable to raise more. But he says the underlying cause is usually slow growth or excessive spending, "rather than insufficient effort to extract money from existing customers."

## The deal founders can make with themselves

Paul's practical advice is a conscious tradeoff:

- Tell yourself you can be as nice as you want, so long as you work hard on your growth rate to compensate.
- He says this deal "will both make you happy and make your company successful."
- Most successful startups make this tradeoff unconsciously. Paul suggests that making it consciously may let founders do it even better.

## What drives the most successful founders

Paul contrasts his childhood "cartoon" of a successful businessman with what he has seen in startups.

- The cartoon was a rapacious, cigar-smoking, table-thumping man in his fifties who wins by exercising power and "isn't too fussy about how."
- He says few of the most successful startup founders are like that. He is unsure about other industries, but not startups.
- Many assume founders are driven by money. Paul says the secret weapon of the most successful founders is that they aren't. If they were, they would have taken one of the acquisition offers that every fast-growing startup gets on the way up.
- What drives them is what drives most people who make things: the company is their project.
