## Release early, then pump out features

- Paul's most-repeated recipe: "get a version 1 out fast, then improve it based on users' reactions."
- "Release early" means minimal, not buggy. "Users hate bugs, but they don't seem to mind a minimal version 1, if there's more coming soon."
- He has repeated this about software since 1993. He has seen many startups die from releasing too slowly, "and none because they were too quick." Startups can die from a buggy release that isn't fixed fast, but he knows of none that died from a stable, minimal, very early release followed by prompt improvement.
- Reasons to release early:
  - Web startups don't know their users (Reddit had almost half a million unique visitors a month and "they have no idea" who they are), so guessing what users want is dangerous. Release and let them tell you.
  - Wufoo released its form-builder before the underlying database. 83,000 people came, and Linux users complained about too much Flash, so Wufoo rewrote the software to avoid it. Waiting would have buried the problem deeper.
  - The first release is a shakedown cruise. A bad idea or founders who hate each other get exposed by the stress of shipping, and you want that early.
  - It makes you work harder. Unreleased, "problems are intriguing"; released, "problems are alarming." People delay because they know they'll have to work harder after release.
- Second half of the rule: "pump out features." Starting minimal only works if you improve fast, and this applies for as long as you want to be considered a startup.
- A "feature" means "one unit of hacking, one quantum of making users' lives better," not added complexity. Improve the system in some small way every day or two.
- Improvements beget improvements, like running: the more ideas you implement, the more you have.
- Constant improvement is also marketing. Users expect a site to improve, and judge a site as an animation of many frames, not a snapshot. Paul says the rate of improvement matters more to users than the current state.
- Improving in response to comments creates "fanatical loyalty" because customers are used to being ignored. "You won't need to advertise, because your users will do it for you."
- Teams stop improving because they get used to the product and its current features become its identity (few at Yahoo or Google imagined better webmail until Paul Buchheit showed them).
- Fix: assume the product is far short of what it could be and keep asking what you would change. If it seems finished, "(a) it is finished, or (b) you lack imagination. Experience suggests (b) is a thousand times more likely."

## Make users happy: convert casual visitors

- Startups can't force anyone to use their software or do deals, so "a startup has to sing for its supper." That is why successful ones make great things.
- Paul's wind metaphor: users are the most powerful wind. They can loft you up (as with Google) or leave you flat, and "if they take you up, no competitor can keep you down." The rational move is to "curl yourself into a shape the wind will catch."
- Design for the casual majority, not the people who care and will find things themselves. The median visitor arrives "with their finger poised on the Back button," trained by the web that most links are lame.
- Two things make people pause:
  1. Explain concisely what the site is about. A startup should describe what it does in one or two sentences, for users and also investors, acquirers, partners, reporters and employees. Paul says you probably shouldn't start a company whose product can't be described compellingly in one or two sentences.
  2. Give people everything you've've got right away. Put the impressive stuff on the front page, since most visitors only see that. The paradox is that pushing good stuff forward makes visitors explore further.
- Best case: show instead of tell. "Nothing will explain what your site does so well as using it."
- Don't make users register to try the site. They've been trained to expect that registration-gated things suck.
- Caveat: a startup need not always tell users plainly what it is. MySpace, basically a replacement mall for mallrats, was wiser to pretend at first that it was about bands.
- The job of the site is "conversion," turning casual visitors into users. Measure it by growth rate: "If you have decent growth, you'll win in the end, no matter how obscure you are now. And if you don't, you need to fix something."

## Fear the right things

- Paul often says "don't worry about this; worry about that instead." Startups are right to be paranoid but fear the wrong things.
- Visible disasters are normal and won't kill you unless you let them: a founder quits, a patent covers your idea, servers crash, an insoluble technical problem, a forced name change, a deal falls through.
- Big companies are not the threat, not even Google. Their people are no smarter than you, are less motivated because one failed product won't put Google out of business, and face bureaucracy.
- Fear other startups you don't know exist yet. They are more dangerous than Google because "like you, they're cornered animals."
- Compete against what someone else could be doing, not only visible competitors. Having no visible competitors is no reason to relax: "No matter what your idea, there's someone else out there working on the same thing."
- Paul disagrees with Caterina Fake that more people starting startups makes it a bad time to start. Most college graduates still think they need a job.
- More startups hose themselves than get crushed by competitors. The three main ways, each enough to kill you: internal disputes, inertia, and ignoring users. Ignoring users is the worst.
- The recipe for a dying startup: founders with a great idea they know everyone will love, which they will build "no matter what."
- Almost every initial plan is broken. If companies had stuck to initial plans, Microsoft would sell programming languages and Apple would sell printed circuit boards. In both cases customers told them what the business should be and they listened.
- Paul quotes Feynman ("the imagination of nature is greater than the imagination of man"). You find better product ideas "by smashing a beam of prototypes into a beam of users" than by thinking alone.

## Determination beats intelligence

- Paul says the most important quality in a founder is determination, "not intelligence." He would like to believe Viaweb succeeded because of smarts, but concluded otherwise, partly from years in New York.
- "You can lose quite a lot in the brains department and it won't kill you. But lose even a little bit in the commitment department, and that will kill you very rapidly."
- VCs overinvest in eminent professors. That may work in biotech, but in software back students: Microsoft, Yahoo and Google were founded by people who dropped out. Students make up for lack of experience with dedication.
- Running a startup is "like walking on your hands." An ordinary employee asked to do a founder's job would be indignant: code ten times faster while also answering support calls, administering servers, designing the site, cold-calling customers, finding office space and fetching lunch, amid constant disasters. Disasters give anyone inclined to quit an excuse.
- Commitment shows. People who deal with startups give little attention to ambivalent founders, so good things happen to competitors and not to you, and you feel unlucky. Committed founders are "a local, not just a tourist."
- Y Combinator sometimes mistakenly funds teams who plan to try for three months and stay only if something great happens (an acquisition or millions in investment). That attitude makes "something great" unlikely, since acquirers and investors judge you by commitment.
- Acquirers who think you'll stick around may buy sooner, since your price rises as you grow. Paul says investors, even big VCs, are motivated "not [by] the hope of good returns, but the fear of missing out." Show you'll succeed regardless and only need them to go faster.
- Why VCs behave this way: in a typical fund half the companies fail, most others return little, and one or two "make the fund," so missing a few top deals can sink it.
- "You can't fake this." You must actually be ready to fight to the death.
- Be determined, not stubborn. Be "determined, but flexible, like a running back": improvise, run around obstacles, even briefly run the wrong way, but never stand still.

## Don't get your hopes up: protect yourself in deals

- This was practically the corporate motto at Viaweb. Treat optimism like a nuclear reactor core: a power source that needs shielding, pierced only in a few places.
- Draw the line between expectations of yourself and of others: "It's ok to be optimistic about what you can do, but assume the worst about machines and other people." Startups push limits, so things change suddenly and usually for the worse.
- On deals, assume it won't happen. "The VCs who say they're going to invest in you aren't. The company that says they're going to buy you isn't. The big customer who wants to use your system in their whole company won't." Then you can be pleasantly surprised.
- The point isn't avoiding disappointment but avoiding leaning the company on something that will fall over.
- Tactic: when someone says they want to invest, you tend to stop looking for other investors, and the person proposing the deal wants you to stop. Doing deals is a pain and raising money is a huge time sink, so force yourself to keep looking.
- Keep looking even if you take the first deal, because you get better terms. Deals have many subsidiary questions after the handshake, and if the other side senses you need it, they will be tempted to squeeze you in the details.
- VCs and corp dev people are professional negotiators, trained to exploit weakness, and do it more often than you. Don't try to bluff them. "The only way a startup can have any leverage in a deal is genuinely not to need it."
- Y Combinator never negotiates valuations, because it isn't made of professional negotiators and doesn't want to become one.
- Hypnotic suggestion: when you hear "we want to invest in you" or "we want to acquire you," think "don't get your hopes up" and keep running as if the deal didn't exist. "Nothing is more likely to make it close."
- Focus on getting lots of users and "keep walking swiftly toward it while investors and acquirers scurry alongside trying to wave money in your face."

## There is always room for new startups

- Paul rejects "tapped out" thinking. A founder told him the social thing was tapped out, but he doubts the only social sites in a hundred years will be Facebook, MySpace, Flickr and Del.icio.us.
- People adjust to how things are and assume that's how they must be. Even a better search engine than Google seems crazy, yet he doubts people will search the same way in twenty or a hundred years.
- On the claim that startups will be disappointed because Google and Yahoo can only buy so many: no one says there is a limit to jobs in an economy of big companies of a couple thousand people, so why a limit for small companies of ten? The only limit is people willing to work that hard.
- The real limit is the wealth that can be created, since startups "make things people want." He sees no limit short of cosmological ones: "I still don't even have a flying car."

## Startups are about speed, not money

- Founders all say it was harder than expected.
- Paul regards making money as "a boring errand to be got out of the way as soon as possible" and says it's ridiculous to take business too seriously.
- Economically, a startup is "not... a way to get rich, but... a way to work faster": a way to finish making a living quickly instead of dragging it through your whole life.
- Life is short; Paul cites Franklin: "if you love life, don't waste time, because time is what life is made of."
- Compressing the dull task of earning a living into the smallest possible time "show[s] respect for life."
- A footnote notes two ways to do work you love: make money then work on what you love, or get paid to work on what you love. Early phases of both are mostly unedifying schleps, and the second is less secure in its later phase.
