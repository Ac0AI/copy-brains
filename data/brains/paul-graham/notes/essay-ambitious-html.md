## Why the biggest startup ideas feel frightening

- Paul says the most ambitious startup ideas are frightening, and that this is "one of the most important things you can understand about startups."
- The fear is not only about workload. The biggest ideas seem to threaten your identity, and you wonder whether you have the ambition to carry them through. He compares it to the line from *Being John Malkovich*: "If you ever got me, you wouldn't have a clue what to do with me."
- Big ideas tend to repel rather than attract. They are invisible to most people hunting for ideas because the subconscious filters them out.
- Even very ambitious founders are probably best off approaching these ideas obliquely.
- VCs miss this too. Most expect a founder to arrive with a clear plan for the future. Paul notes that in the biggest successes there is the least correlation between the initial plan and what the company becomes.

## Where to look: the "right side of impossible" and the future's view of us

- Paul's rule of thumb is that the best ideas are "just on the right side of impossible."
- One of his idea-generating tricks is to imagine the ways we will seem backward to future generations, then build the fix. His example is that people 50 to 100 years from now will find it barbaric that we wait for symptoms before diagnosing heart disease or cancer.
- Another source is a dominant player that has lost its way. Microsoft lost the plot when it entered search out of fear of Google. That made Google set Microsoft's agenda, and the agenda was stuff Microsoft was not good at. He frames it as Microsoft : Google :: Google : Facebook.
- A good sign is when an idea is wrong in many ways at once. He likes the sufficiently smart compiler idea because the forum troll he has internalized "doesn't even know where to begin in raising objections."

## Search engine: win the top 10,000 hackers first

- Paul sees cracks in Google's fortress. Its results used to be fast, uncluttered and right, "like the output of a Unix utility," and now feel cluttered and personalized. For the first time in over a decade, switching seems thinkable to him.
- The route is to build the search engine all the hackers use. A product whose users were only the top 10,000 hackers would be in a very powerful position, as Google was when it had that audience.
- Since anyone able to start this company is one of those 10,000 hackers, the instruction is to build the search engine you yourself want. Be excessively hackerish, for example by making code search excellent, or even making queries Turing complete.
- Constraints you create for the long term do not matter: "if you don't get that initial core of users, there won't be a long term."
- If you build something you and your friends genuinely prefer to Google, you are "about 10% of the way to an IPO, just as Facebook was" when it had the Harvard undergrads.

## Replacing email with a todo list protocol

- Paul's framing is that email is not a messaging protocol. His inbox is a todo list and email is how things get onto it, and it is a disastrously bad todo list.
- He suspects tweaking the inbox is not enough and that a new protocol is needed.
- The new protocol should give the recipient more power:
  - Restrict what others can put on the todo list.
  - Require senders to say what they want from you, how important it is, and when it is due.
  - Include some mechanism to stop everyone from marking everything important.
  - Treat "read the following text" as a degenerate case.
- On adoption, he calls this an irresistible force meeting an immovable object. Entrenched protocols are hard to replace, but it is unlikely people in 100 years will still live in email hell, so why not now?
- He suggests the chicken-and-egg problem may be avoidable because some of the most powerful people in the world would switch first, since they are also at the mercy of email.
- Speed is a wedge. GMail has become painfully slow because Google cannot afford to spend much on it. Something no better than GMail but fast could pull users away.
- On pricing, he says people will pay for this. He would have no problem paying $50 a month. Given the hours he spends in email daily, he thinks he would be justified paying "at least $1000 a month."

## Replacing universities, and internet drama

**Universities**
- He thinks universities will not disappear wholesale. They will lose their de facto monopoly on certain types of learning, and many new ways to learn will exist, some looking quite different. He cites Y Combinator as arguably one of them.
- If learning breaks into small pieces, credentialling may separate from it, and campus social life may need replacements.
- High schools could be replaced too, but bureaucratic obstacles would slow a startup, so universities are the place to start.

**Internet drama**
- He says the race between delivery mechanisms now has a winner: the Internet, not cable. He and his family replaced their TV with an iMac bolted to the wall because they hated the TV interface.
- Attention can be taken by unrelated things (social apps), closer things (games), but residual demand for passive conventional drama will remain.
- Whatever is made must be bigger than YouTube clips. Viewers want either a series with familiar characters or a longer "movie" with a known premise.
- Two ways delivery and payment could play out: a Netflix or Apple becomes the app store for entertainment, or those players overreach or are technically inflexible, and companies arise to supply payment and streaming a la carte to producers. In the second case there is also a need for infrastructure companies.

## The next Steve Jobs and bringing back Moore's Law

**Next Steve Jobs**
- Paul asked someone who knew Apple well whether the current team could keep creating new things as under Jobs. The answer was a flat "no," with no qualification. Revenue may keep rising, but he notes "revenue is a lagging indicator in the technology business."
- No existing player is run by a product visionary, and "empirically you can't seem to get those by hiring them." The way you get one as CEO is for him to found the company and not get fired. So the next hardware wave likely comes from a startup.
- Aiming to be as big as Apple is no more ambitious than Apple becoming Apple, and a new startup has Apple as an example. He compares this to Roger Bannister (showing what is possible) and Augustus (making people believe one person could unroll the future).
- The "next Steve Jobs" only has to do a better job than Samsung, HP and Nokia, which he calls pretty doable.
- Tactical note: you may not want to start with consumer electronics. Start with something hackers use, or something popular but apparently unimportant like a headset or router. "All you need is a bridgehead."

**Moore's Law**
- Moore's Law actually promises circuit densities doubling every 18 months, not clock speeds. Until about 2002 the misreading was harmless. Now Intel can give more CPUs, not faster ones, so slow software must be rewritten for parallelism.
- The idea: software that makes many CPUs look like one very fast CPU. The most ambitious version is a compiler that parallelizes code automatically, "the sufficiently smart compiler," a byword for impossibility. He argues that if you think it impossible you should try to prove it, and if it is merely hard, "the expected value would be high even if the chance of succeeding was low."
- Expected value is high because of web services: offering it as a service could capture practically all users, and since nobody sees server processors, it would be indistinguishable from being a processor maker that still turned density into clock speed.
- Approaches by ambition:
  - Least ambitious: offer parallelizable building blocks like Hadoop and MapReduce, with the programmer still optimizing.
  - Middle ground: a "semi-automatic weapon" with humans in the loop. It looks like the smart compiler to users but inside has people (employees, or a marketplace for optimization) using tools to find bottlenecks.
  - An optimization marketplace would build the compiler piecemeal as participants write bots, possibly with no one holding a complete copy.

## Ongoing automatic medical diagnosis

- Paul expects this to face even greater resistance. His example: in 2004 Bill Clinton, presumably with the best care available, still had to wait until his arteries were over 90% blocked and he felt short of breath, then had a quadruple bypass 3 days later. He expects future people to know such numbers the way we know our weight, and cancer to show up on a radar screen immediately.
- Radar may reveal tens or hundreds of microcancers at any time, most amounting to nothing.
- Obstacles: it goes against the grain of medicine, which starts from patients presenting problems. Doctors dislike "fishing expeditions" and call findings "incidentalomas."
- The false alarm worry: a friend's research brain scan showed an apparent large tumor that was a harmless cyst, costing days of terror. He argues this is an artifact of current limitations. With constant scanning and better judgment she would have known about the cyst her whole life, like a birthmark.
- He says there is room for many startups, facing technical obstacles, medical-bureaucratic obstacles, and thousands of years of tradition, but "it will happen."

## Tactics: start deceptively small and head west

- Do not attack a giant problem head-on. Don't announce you will replace email, because it raises expectations, employees and investors keep asking "are we there yet?", and haters wait for failure. Say you are building todo-list software, and people notice you replaced email when it's a fait accompli.
- "Empirically, the way to do really big things seems to be to start with deceptively small things." Examples: a Basic interpreter for a machine with a few thousand users (microcomputer software), and a site for Harvard undergrads to stalk one another (the universal web site).
- Start small for your own sake too. Neither Bill Gates nor Mark Zuckerberg knew at first how big their companies would get. Big initial ambitions take longer and project further into the future, making errors more likely.
- Operate like Columbus and head in a general westerly direction rather than constructing the future like a building from a blueprint that is probably wrong. Start with something you know works, and expand westward.
- "The popular image of the visionary is someone with a clear view of the future, but empirically it may be better to have a blurry one."
