## The two schedules

Paul describes two ways of using time. The manager's schedule is the traditional appointment book, with each day cut into one-hour intervals. It is the schedule of bosses and "the schedule of command", and most powerful people use it. The maker's schedule is common among programmers and writers, who "generally prefer to use time in units of half a day at least."

- He says "you can't write or program well in units of an hour. That's barely enough time to get started."
- On the manager's schedule, meeting someone is "merely a practical problem": find an open slot, book it, done.
- Each schedule works fine alone. Problems arise "when they meet."

## Why meetings wreck a maker's day

- A single meeting can blow a whole afternoon by splitting it into two pieces, each too small to do anything hard in.
- A maker also has to remember the meeting. A manager always has something coming on the next hour, so there is nothing extra to think about.
- Paul says a meeting is "like throwing an exception." It changes the mode in which you work, not just the task.
- The effect cascades. If he knows the afternoon will be broken up, he is slightly less likely to start something ambitious in the morning.
- His reasoning: ambitious projects are close to the limits of your capacity, so "a small decrease in morale is enough to kill them off." A maker's spirits rise at the thought of an entire day free of appointments, so they sink when there isn't one.

## What powerful people should do

- Most powerful people run on the manager's schedule, so they can make everyone "resonate at their frequency" if they want to.
- Paul says the smarter ones restrain themselves when some of their people need long chunks of time.
- He suggests founders may increasingly be able to resist, or at least postpone, turning into managers. He compares this to founders starting, a few decades earlier, to resist switching from jeans to suits.
- He notes that nearly all investors, including every VC he knows, operate on the manager's schedule. Y Combinator runs on the maker's schedule. Paul, Rtm and Trevor do because they always have, and Jessica mostly does because she got in sync with them.

## Simulating a manager's schedule inside a maker's: office hours

Paul's method for advising many startups on a maker's schedule is office hours.

- Several times a week he sets aside a chunk of time to meet funded founders.
- The chunk sits at the end of his working day.
- He wrote a signup program that clusters all appointments within a given set of office hours at the end.
- Because the meetings come at the end of his day, they are never an interruption. In busy periods they can get long enough to "compress the day", but they "never interrupt it."
- The founders chose the appointments, so presumably each one is worth it to them even if it cuts into their own day.

## Splitting the day into two workdays

When Paul and his partners worked on their own startup in the 90s, he used another trick.

- He programmed from dinner until about 3 am, because at night no one could interrupt him.
- He slept until about 11 am, then came in and worked until dinner on what he called "business stuff."
- In effect he had two workdays each day: one on the maker's schedule and one on the manager's. He did not think of it in those terms at the time.

## Speculative meetings and the coffee problem

- On the manager's schedule you can have speculative meetings, meeting someone "just to get to know one another." An empty slot costs nothing, so "why not?" They are so common that there is distinctive language for proposing them, such as "grab coffee."
- On the maker's schedule speculative meetings are "terribly costly."
- Because everyone assumes YC is run like other investors, people introduce them to contacts or propose coffee. Paul says they then have two bad options: meet and lose half a day's work, or avoid the meeting and probably offend the person.
- His proposed third option is to explain the two types of schedule so the conflict becomes widely understood, and hopefully less of a problem.
- His compromise: makers accept that they have to have some number of meetings. "All we ask from those on the manager's schedule is that they understand the cost."
