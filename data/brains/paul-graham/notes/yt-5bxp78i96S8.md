## Founder ambition and why "formidable" is the key quality

- Paul says he already knew before YC started that founders have to be really ambitious. He and his co-founders had run a startup themselves, so they knew it was grueling. Something has to drive a founder through the obstacles, and "being beautiful is not enough. The obstacles are too fearsome for mere beautifulness to do it." He calls that driver ambition.
- Ambition is mostly inborn. He says stories of founders who gain ambition during the batch are rare. Sam Altman "was extremely formidable the first time I met him before we even accepted him," and Paul says that is the rule, not the exception.
- The one exception he sees is a founder who has been trained not to show ambition. A young person in obedient situations, such as with pushy parents, may never have let themselves go. He argues that person was ambitious all along.
- "Formidable" was Maya's and Jessica's private word before YC. Paul's test is: someone who "gets what they want" in any situation. "How formidable are you if you don't get what you want?"
- Investors want formidable founders because interests align. If the founder gets what they want, the investor holding stock in the company gets what they want too.
- On the next trillion-dollar company, he says it comes "from the right founders," not from a particular idea. Ideas are mutable. Founders now look the same as they did 20 years ago, and he sees no reason that would change.

## What actually motivates founders day to day

- Paul says founders are not mainly driven by the thought of becoming billionaires, even though that is the upside. At any given moment the motivator is fear of failure: disaster, looking like a fool, the server crashing.
- His example: if your online service's server is crashing, you are not thinking "if I fix this I'll become a billionaire." You are thinking "oh no, the server is crashing." He compares it to saving a model train set whose engine is falling off the table.
- Founders put their heads down on the "model train set" for 10 years, then look up and find that their shares at the last round's valuation make them a billionaire. It takes them by surprise.
- Paul sometimes does the math for founders himself: "Wait, according to your last round, you're a billionaire."

## Startups are a bad credential

- Paul says people started applying to YC for prestige quite early in its history. He sees that as a mistake in how they think about startups.
- Harvard comparison: if you get into Harvard, you can choose an easy major and still leave with an impressive degree. A startup has no easy major. It is "like applying to Harvard and being forced to study theoretical physics."
- A failed startup is not much of a badge. A successful one is your life's work, so it is not a badge either.
- Starting a startup is "about the least efficient way" to seem cool. It is brutally hard, and you won't seem cool until after years of that hardness.

## Ambitious ideas and serious startups

- Paul says YC startups today are more serious than in the "supposedly good old days." People have said YC "jumped the shark" since about 2008. Critics must admit it was once great, so they claim it has fallen.
- He notes that old-days companies like Reddit, however valuable, are "not exactly intercontinental ballistic cargo." He did office hours with an intercontinental ballistic cargo startup ("like an ICBM except instead of exploding, it lands").
- Cancer: he says to fund many approaches, since any one working would be good. One end is vaccines and the opposite end is therapies. A startup in the current batch does on-demand research for people with cancer. He likes that it inflicts "the death of a thousand cuts" on cancer, which may be how it is beaten.
- That startup's team helped Sid of GitLab with his cancer. Paul says Sid approached it like a startup, with them as de facto co-founders, and calls his "founder mode on my cancer" a good description. After that, Paul thought someone should do for everyone what Sid did for himself, and now the people who did it for Sid are doing it.
- He says his 2012 "frighteningly ambitious ideas" essay has largely come true. One idea was a new Google. His prediction was that you don't make a new Google by attacking them head-on. You wait until things change so much that their model is obsolete. That is what OpenAI is. After using it he realized he no longer used Google, because he wanted information, not web pages.

## Starting lean and what AI changes

- Paul hasn't read *The Lean Startup* and isn't sure what "lean startup" means, so he answers on the narrower question of whether starting with little money is dead. He says no.
- Technology always gets cheaper. Token prices are high now only because of a GPU shortage. He says inference prices at a given quality level fall dramatically, "like 30x a year or something like that," and you also get higher-quality tokens over time.
- Even a rocket startup can start on little money. You adjust to the money you have. You can't build the rocket, but you can build a design, simulate it, and show it to experts. If it's convincing, investors give you a bit of money for the next stage. "You're always able to raise another round" as long as you reach some milestone.
- On StarCloud, a founder said they wrote a white paper and booked a launch. Paul says nothing is as convincing as booking a launch. But that founder is a huge famous expert with automatic credibility, so it's easier for him than for a recent graduate.
- Shipping speed: Paul says the best predictor of startup success is still the pace at which they ship. Many startups in the batch with powerful AI tools "are not shipping fast enough." Speed isn't just output rate, because you have to think of the ideas first.
- Almost everything about starting a startup is the same as before. The one new thing is cost structure. Salaries used to be the big cost, with laptops and people. Now companies face giant AI bills, "tens of thousands of dollars a day in tokens."
- He says most of starting a startup is always the same, whether the tech is microprocessors, AI or the internal combustion engine.

## Why a batch helps founders, and how YC came about

- Paul gives three reasons the batch helps. Starting a startup is normally lonely, with full control but no colleagues, and the batch is like an office of colleagues who each work on their own startup. You can learn from other startups, such as asking someone who already hit your technical problem. And there is the "YC GDP": you can sell your product to other startups in the batch. They are the right users, early adopters who decide quickly and "have to at least listen to your pitch."
- Origin: the first idea was to be an angel firm. VCs did giant late-stage rounds and angels were individuals investing their own money. There were no angel firms. The idea was to be like a VC but invest small amounts earlier with standardized paperwork.
- They then decided they needed to learn to be investors, so they would fund many startups at once. College students got summer jobs at Microsoft, so YC would be an alternative to a summer job. That is why the batch runs in the summer. He says the length turned out to be perfect by accident.
- They expected undergrads wouldn't mind that YC wasn't a real investor, since they weren't real founders. In practice YC became real investors and the students became real founders quickly. The batch worked so well they kept it.
- On scale, YC has changed little: "the same thing but more of it." The startups' problems are the same. People complained about size back when there were 40 startups in a batch. Dividing YC into pods of 70 startups makes it like 2012, when a batch had 70.

## AI as Paul sees it

- Paul studied AI in the 1980s, a kind of AI he says would never have worked. The expected path was a "perfect fly," then mice, cats, monkeys and humans, perfect at each level.
- What happened was the reverse: a full human-level but unreliable system. Early ChatGPT was like an undergrad bullshitting through a paper. Instead of starting perfect and working up to human, "you start with human and then work your way towards perfect." Nobody expected the first plausible AI to be a bullshitting undergraduate.
- He says it still has some of that flavor. AI is reportedly solving open math problems, yet he can't get it to say when restaurants are open. He calls this the "jagged frontier."
- On AGI, he says the Turing test is a good definition. One sign AGI was close was that he had to look up what the Turing test was. He expected AGI to be a clear finish line, but "the line had dimension." His best answer is that "we're on the smear."
