## How YC grew and why it spread

- Paul says YC never made a conscious marketing effort to get applicants. It set an application deadline, announced it, ran Startup School every year, and applications grew each cycle.
- His essays carried a link: "Interested in starting a startup? Apply to Y Combinator." He thinks that probably brought in some applicants.
- Hacker News began around 2007, so it was not a factor in the first couple of years.
- He thinks the main driver was word of mouth from founders. "I think we actually helped the startups a lot. And so they would tell one another."
- His analogy: friends tell you to go to a restaurant when the food is good. Alumni told friends that YC helped them get investment, incorporate, and more.
- He calls this "a recipe for exponential growth": each new founder becomes another person telling others it helped.
- Young programmers know each other from school and hang out together, so "if you please that group, word spreads quickly."
- Alumni also helped by dropping by dinners, introducing new startups to their investors, and sometimes becoming customers.

## Press only matters if your audience reads it

- The New York Times article ("a hatchery for startups") described YC as a new phenomenon in the investment world. Paul called it a useful historical article.
- His rule: for YC, the only publicity that counts is whether potential applicants know about you, meaning 22-year-old programmers.
- If those people don't read the outlet, "it might seem to you you've gotten a lot of publicity, but actually it didn't really help much."
- Parents also mattered. In 2005 parents would freak out that their kids were leaving school with no job, so it looked like unemployment rather than founding.
- Once YC had a brand, founders could say, "Look, Mom, I'm not just working on my loser startup. I got into YC."
- YC needed a consumer-style brand, because it takes open applications from people just out of college and is not "just doing series A's". Paul says this visibility also made YC a target for press and online mobs.

## Real advice he gave startups

- **Airbnb:** he told them to focus on New York, one initial market, "cuz you need to get this ball rolling." He says it made all the difference, and that they were effectively dead when they arrived, though YC didn't know it.
- This is the dream case. Usually "you would tell people something and they would ignore you. And then they would fail."
- **Time spent:** YC spends most of its time on knotty problems from companies that never amount to much. Airbnb was one of about 25 startups in its batch and got about 1/25 of the time. Trouble caused is inversely proportional to eventual size.
- **Expanding ideas:** in roughly a third of his office hours he and the founders figured out how to make the idea much bigger. He still does this, often early in a batch, when there is room to expand.
- **Naming:** he helps startups find names, often at instant domain search on the kitchen island. He cites Imgix and Phind as names he is proud of.
- **Advocacy:** the partners sometimes had to talk to a startup's investors, like parents of kids in a fight, when someone was mistreating a startup.

## Demo Day: the "vertebrae" method

- Investors see 50 startups, now closer to 300, so their brains are wiped clean by the end. At best they remember about five sentences.
- So decide the five sentences in advance. These are the "vertebrae" of the pitch, and "the rest is just filler."
- The five sentences should each do a job. His example chain: it's a giant market; we already solve the problem better than anyone; it's ours to lose; at scale there are network effects, so it's winner takes all.
- "Demo day presentation shouldn't be like fast-talking marketing speak. They should be almost like logical proofs of why this company is good to invest in." If the investor agrees with each step, the expected value comes out high.
- Process: he did vertebrae office hours, founders built slides saying exactly that, then practiced at YC while he sat in the audience "like a playwright" cutting lines and changing words.
- Pacing: YC was always trying to get people to talk slower.
- At Demo Day he watched from the audience taking notes, hoping each founder would land specific lines he already knew were coming.

## Inflection point office hours and "palliative care mode"

- Inflection point office hours were for startups in terrible shape. Several partners met with the founders to work out how to save the company.
- This was for startups whose founders still looked promising.
- If the startup seemed doomed and the founders would be better off with jobs, the partners went into "palliative care mode": just be really nice, so they were cheerful as it died. No tough love.
- Tough love was reserved for cases where it might work.
- Paul says he worked harder than anyone at trying to save failing startups, and his attitude was "This time will be different."
- On feedback, he says YC treated food complaints as a good sign: if the only complaint is the Tuesday food, "we're doing it right." Carolyn adds the post-batch survey was how they checked.

## Choosing and handing over the YC president

- Paul knew Sam Altman from the first batch. He says it was instant: "this must be what Bill Gates was like when he was 19." Sam was the class president type, raised serious money, and got Sequoia to invest.
- Paul's reasoning for picking him: Sam gets whatever he wants, so "if the only way he can succeed is by making Y Combinator succeed then Y Combinator cannot fail."
- His mistake: he should have said Sam could only do YC and nothing else. His view is that Sam would have accepted at that stage.
- On OpenAI, Paul says YC would have been "psyched" if Sam had picked someone else for AI and focused on YC. "All we wanted was him to pick." He denies that YC fired Sam.
- Succession: the partners told Sam he couldn't do both and agreed to find a successor. They chose Jeff, an established YC partner, who served 3 years.
- Garry then took over. Paul likes that Garry had done YC himself, so he knows what founders feel when raising, chasing growth, or facing Demo Day. LPs also trusted him from his results at Initialized.
- Paul says it is good for the president to have gone through YC. He says YC is now the best he has felt about it in about 10 years, with no nagging worry.

## Hacker News and the cost of side projects

- Paul says Hacker News was a huge stress, "more than half of the total stress of running YC." Servers ran out of file space, there were denial-of-service attacks, and users fought on the site.
- He says that in retrospect it was like running a startup alongside YC, and "maybe that was a mistake."
- He wrote every bit of YC's software and every word on the website. The one exception was the up and down arrow JavaScript on Hacker News, which PB wrote.
- The Peter Thiel episode: part-time partners were unpaid experts who did office hours. YC was facing pressure to remove Thiel for being openly Republican.
- Sam had planned to drop him anyway because he had done few office hours. Paul told Sam not to set a precedent of firing someone for supporting the wrong political party, so YC kept him.
- Paul says doing the job was less stressful than being attacked for it. He says outsiders knew only YC, so problems were blamed on Silicon Valley and then on him.

## Paul's view of startups in the AI era

- AI progress surprised him. He did not expect this in his lifetime, and he notes how quickly people adjusted.
- So far AI's main effect is as a source of startup ideas and as leverage to displace incumbents. He says starting a startup is not structurally different yet.
- He sees a possible risk that model companies copy startups, like the Samwer brothers did, joking about "the Sams instead of the Samwers."
- His encouraging point: the best source of startup ideas is your own needs (Facebook, Google, Airbnb). "AIs don't have any needs," so they may not replace human founders.
- Other surprises: founders keep getting younger (17 or 18 and skipping college), and Demo Day valuations are "like $60 million now." He has heard of multiple startups raising at a $60M cap, about 20 times Airbnb's post-money valuation.
- He opposes skipping college, wants founders to go back, and is thinking of writing an essay "Go to College."
- B2B is where he sees age as a handicap: 18-year-olds will struggle to convince a CIO to buy seat licenses. They could work on the idea meanwhile and start later.
- On "it'll be too late if I don't start now," he asks whether people were right about that 2 years ago.
- His energy for office hours comes from interest, not benevolence: founders bring a "constant stream of new puzzles."
