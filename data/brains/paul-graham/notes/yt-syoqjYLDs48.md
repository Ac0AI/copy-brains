## Paul's rule: launch fast, test what matters, and let the product find its use

- Paul and Sam interview three startups live. The first team (the multiplayer coding game) was told afterward that this was their YC interview, and they were accepted into the next batch.
- Paul's tests for the three companies are mostly empirical: does the product work with real users, will people share it, and have the founders actually tested the thing they say will drive growth?
- He pushes teams to launch and measure instead of polishing in private.

## Sam and Paul's read on a game that teaches programming

- The company builds a multiplayer programming game: players write code to control units and fight. It is meant as a more engaging alternative to Codecademy.
- It launched the day before the office hours with a post on Reddit. About 15,000 people came and the server maxed out. Many users saw 404s ("hug of death") because the architecture couldn't scale.
- Paul asks how the game stays educational. Can advanced concepts "produce advanced weaponry"? The founders answer with levels that force recursion, restricted methods and performance limits.
- Beta lesson: the founders assumed too much prior knowledge. Formal notation was the single biggest obstacle for complete beginners.
- Paul's advice on open sourcing: look at what the games industry does and use that as the default starting point. He asks if the founders know the gaming business, and they say they are learning and have game designers advising them.
- The business model is recruiting: leads from coding challenges go to companies. Paul notes that "recruiters famously say all sorts of crazy stuff", so this is not yet validated.
- Paul on founder profiles: when reading applications, someone who worked a long time at a large company counts against them. "The best startup founders probably could not stand that."

## Why "Give 100" should test its sharing loop before the waitlist opens

- Give 100 (give00.com, as written in the transcript) is a video site where people pick a skill and post a short video of their progress every day for 100 days. Clips are capped at 10 seconds.
- It was in private beta with 50 users and about 4,300 on a waitlist. The founder's own year-long video of learning to dance got about 3 million views and led to the idea.
- Reported numbers: about 1,000 views a day, 20 to 30 of the 50 beta users returning daily, and an average of about 18 videos per user. The longest streak was a beatboxer at about day 85.
- Paul and Sam press the founders on why they are holding back the waitlist. The founder wants social sharing features first.
- Sam's pointed critique: YouTube is the planned marketing channel, yet the beta never tested whether the clips get shared and watched there. "It seems like that would have been really important to test during the beta."
- Paul also asks what third parties would want to watch, as opposed to what the practicers do. He notes parents would love a record of a child's progress, since new memories overwrite earlier ones.

## Don't wire in an outcome too early

- When the Give 100 founders are unsure whether dance, babies learning to walk, or something else will be the main use case, Paul says not to over-optimize early.
- His advice: "Let it grow into whatever it's going to grow into." The kids use case may become the big thing or not.
- He says to be empirical about it and not "wire in some outcome too early."
- Sam adds that a baby learning to walk is very different from someone teaching themselves to unicycle, so the product shouldn't be built around a single use case before the data points to one.

## Flexport: a customs brokerage as the entry point to logistics

- Flexport is a licensed US customs brokerage built around a web app. Brokers are heavily licensed and need an FBI background check. A broker files up to about 120 forms per shipment, depending on the product.
- The founder has about 12 years in the industry. He has imported roughly 1,000 containers and has a customs worker on staff. His previous company sold a database of 300 million shipping manifests, which gives him a list of every importer in America.
- Traction: three customers and a waitlist of about 300. The first clearance was planned for November, delayed because the government shutdown held up the broker onboarding.
- Pricing and market: about $100 to $300 per shipment from incumbents, 30 million shipments a year entering the US. The founder plans to charge $100 per clearance regardless of size, with about 75% gross margin, and extra for FDA-type paperwork.
- The founder says that once he is the customs broker he knows the whole supply chain, and could later sell freight, warehousing, inspection and trucking.
- Paul and Sam probe why no one has done it: heavy regulation, and until 2007 a shipment could only be cleared at a local port.

## Paul on market-size claims

- When the Flexport founder cites logistics as a "$2.3 trillion industry", Paul cuts in: "really big markets are bad for startups, not good."
- His reasoning: if you say too big a number, investors just don't believe it.
- Paul asks for the real number for the actual first product, the customs brokerage itself. The founder models about $30 million a year in profit at 1% share, with each customer worth roughly $200 to $3,000 a year. Paul and Sam do not agree on the market figure: the whole US customs clearance business is estimated at about $3 billion to $5 billion a year, and the founder concedes he lacks exact figures.
- Paul and Sam also ask how he will win customers from brokers with long relationships. The answer is his importer database and a plan to possibly hire sales staff, which he says "isn't that fun to manage" but works.
