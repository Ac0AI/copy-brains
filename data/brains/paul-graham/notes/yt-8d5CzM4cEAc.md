## Don't try to think of startup ideas; build a brain that produces them

- Paul argues that consciously trying to come up with startup ideas yields ideas that are "not only bad but bad and plausible sounding," so you and everyone else get fooled and waste a lot of time before seeing they are no good.
- His alternative is to take a step back and turn your brain into the kind that has startup ideas unconsciously, so unconsciously that you don't realize at first that they are startup ideas.
- Yahoo, Google, Facebook and Apple all started this way. None were supposed to be companies at first; they were side projects.
- The very best ideas almost have to start as side projects, because they are such outliers that your conscious mind would reject them as ideas for companies.
- Three ways to get a brain that produces ideas:
  1. Learn a lot about things that matter.
  2. Work on problems that interest you.
  3. Work with people you like and respect. This is also how you get co-founders, at the same time as the idea.

## Expertise in your users matters more than expertise in startups

- Paul says what you need to succeed is not expertise in startups but expertise in your own users.
- Mark Zuckerberg succeeded despite being "a complete noob at startups." Facebook was first incorporated as a Florida LLC. He won because he understood his users very well.
- Paul worries that learning the mechanics of starting a startup is not merely unnecessary but possibly somewhat dangerous.
- The characteristic young-founder mistake is going through the motions of a startup:
  - Come up with a plausible-sounding idea.
  - Raise funding at a nice valuation.
  - Rent a nice office in SoMa.
  - Hire a bunch of friends.
- While imitating all the outward forms, such founders neglect the one essential thing: "make something people want."

## What a startup actually does: make people's lives better

- Paul says a successful startup, whether it ends in an IPO or just a great business, has to "make something that actually makes people's lives better."
- He thinks it is "funny how straightforward it is." People assume business needs trickery, like cornering a market, but you just have to improve people's lives.
- His model of value is a rectangle. One side is the number of people, the other is how much better you make each person's life. "Big rectangle big value you're rich it's really that simple."

## Co-founders: friends who know each other's capacities

- The YC team looks for founders who have been friends for a while and have worked on things together, so they know one another's capacities.
- It dislikes teams that came together only for the purpose of doing the startup. In that case the startup is the only thing holding them together.
- Almost every startup hits a point where it seems doomed and worthless. If nothing but the startup binds the founders, they quit and go do other things.
- Friends keep working on it so as not to let the other down. It turns out they were wrong that it was worthless. Paul's summary: continuing out of "misplaced loyalty to your pal" is oddly a good thing.

## Fund the founders more than the idea

- YC has learned that, at the stage it funds, the founder is much more important than the idea.
- Its application form used to ask many questions about the idea. It now asks far fewer about the idea and many more about the founders and the relationship between them.

## Early on, do things by hand and get customers

- Paul says doing things very manually for early customers is so important that "if you have to do a ton of manual stuff that's okay you'll learn a lot from it."
- He and his co-founders did this themselves and thought they were doing it wrong ("this is so lame"). It was exactly the right thing.
- Their company made an online store builder:
  - They asked prospective customers if they wanted to use the easy online store builder, and the answer was no.
  - They asked "you want an online store right?" and the answer was yes.
  - They then offered to use their own software to build the store for the customer, and the customer said all right.
- Paul's takeaway: they could not get anyone to use the software, but people would let them use it for them.
- He says many of the things YC teaches startups are things he did himself without realizing they were common lessons.

## What kills startups, and the iteration loop

- Paul's answer to what kills startups: they make something users don't like. "They make dog food the dogs do not like to eat. That is the killer of all startups."
- The first version does not have to be good, as long as you get it out very quickly.
- Putting it out starts a conversation with users, who say things like "I sort of like this but I wish you would do such-and-such."
- You do such-and-such and repeat that process roughly 200 times, "and then you have something really good."

## Counterintuitive advice founders ignore

- Paul says what Y Combinator does is tell founders things they ignore. The advice is counterintuitive, not obvious.
- His example is "don't hire too fast." Founders hire too fast anyway, then come back later and say they wish they had listened.
- Counterintuitive advice "sounds wrong," so founders go with their gut and do the wrong thing, and hopefully catch the mistake in time.

## A startup takes over your life, so weigh the timing

- Paul warns that a startup will take over your life "to a degree that you cannot imagine." If it succeeds, it does so for several years at the very least, maybe a decade, maybe the rest of your working life, so there is a real opportunity cost.
- Larry Page is his example. He started running as fast as he could at 25 and has not stopped to catch his breath since.
  - Every day something happens within Google that only the emperor can deal with.
  - A week of vacation creates a backlog.
  - As the company's "daddy" he can never show fear or weakness, and a billionaire gets zero sympathy for complaining.
- The difficulty of founding a successful startup is concealed from almost everyone who has done it, like Olympic 100-meter winners who make it look easy.
- Paul frames the real problem as "how to have a good life." A startup can be a component of that, but the early 20s are not necessarily the optimal time. Two things work better then than before or after: plunging deeply into projects on a whim with no obvious payoff, and traveling super cheaply with no deadline.
- For ambitious people this is "a really valuable sort of exploration." If you start a startup at 20 and succeed, you never get to do it. He says Zuckerberg will never get to bum around a foreign country, because success has "taken a lot of the serendipity out of his life."
- Serendipity also gives you more options to choose your life's work from.
