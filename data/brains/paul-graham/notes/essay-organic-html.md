## Organic ideas beat made-up ideas

Paul splits startup ideas into two types: those that grow organically out of your own life, and those you decide from afar some other class of users will need. Apple was the first type: Steve Wozniak wanted a computer and, unlike most people who wanted one, could design it. Viaweb (online store software, founded when Paul was 30 and Robert Morris 29) was the second type.

- Core question: "what do you wish someone would make for you?"
- There is no sharp line between the types, but "the most successful startups seem to be closer to the Apple type than the Viaweb type."
- Examples of organic origins: Bill Gates writing a Basic interpreter for the Altair, Larry and Sergey writing the first Google.
- Apple still works this way: the iPhone is the phone Steve Jobs wants. Paul infers Apple will be weak where Jobs isn't a user (e.g. gaming).

## Why young founders should stick to organic ideas

- It takes experience to predict what other people will want. Paul and Robert only managed Viaweb because they were old enough to have "seen enough to know users would need this type of software."
- "The worst ideas we see at Y Combinator are from young founders making things they think other people will want."
- Young founders are the best source of organic ideas, though. They use the latest technology, so they discover valuable types of fixable brokenness first.
- Advice for anyone without an idea: focus initially on organic ideas.

## How to spot what's broken

- Ask: what's missing or broken in your daily life? Sometimes the answers are immediate. It was obviously broken that the Altair could only be programmed in machine language.
- You may need to "stand outside yourself a bit," because people get used to brokenness and take it for granted.
- Example: in 2004 it was ridiculous that Harvard undergrads still used a Facebook printed on paper.
- "There are always great ideas sitting right under our noses." Obvious ones exist today, and are overlooked for the same reason Facebook would have been overlooked in 2004.

## Focus on the idea, not the startup

- Organic ideas usually don't look like startup ideas at first. Putting undergraduates' profiles online wouldn't have seemed like much of a startup.
- Mark Zuckerberg told a YC dinner he wasn't trying to start a company when he wrote the first Facebook; it was just a project. Woz didn't think he was starting a company with the Apple I.
- If they had thought they were starting companies, they might have been tempted to do something more "serious," "and that would have been a mistake."
- Advice: fix things that seem broken, regardless of whether the problem seems important enough to build a company on. Keep pulling such threads and you'll likely make something valuable to many people, and "surprise, you've got a company."

## Toys are a good sign

- Don't be discouraged if people dismiss your first version as a toy. That's probably why everyone else overlooked the idea.
- Precedents: the first microcomputers, planes and cars were all dismissed as toys.
- At YC, when users like something that forum trolls could dismiss as a toy, that makes Paul's group "especially likely to invest."

## Unmet needs that are just becoming fixable

- "There's nothing more valuable than an unmet need that is just becoming fixable."
- If you find something broken that you can fix for a lot of people, "you've found a gold mine." You still have to work hard to extract the gold, but you know where the seam is, "and that's the hard part."

## Become your own user

- In hindsight, Paul says Viaweb should have *become* direct marketers. Done again, he'd open his own online store, which would have given them a much better understanding of users.
- Advice: "become one of its users, however unnatural it seems."
- Caveat: it's hard to compete directly with open source software. You can build things for programmers, but there has to be some part you can charge for.
