## Why founders can now start companies young

- Paul notes that the founders of Yahoo, Google and Microsoft averaged 24 years old, so "grad students can start successful companies." He asks, "if grad students can do it, why not undergrads?"
- Starting a Web startup now costs so little that "it has disappeared into the noise." The main cost is "food and rent," so "you can probably start a startup on ten thousand dollars of seed funding, if you're prepared to live on ramen."
- The cheaper it is, the less founders need investors' permission. The group freed up most are people with "everything investors want except experience."
- He thinks undergraduates are undervalued. There is a huge spread in the value of 20-year-olds, and some are "more capable than all but a handful of 30 year olds."
- Young people are hard to judge because they change fast, vary a lot and are individually inconsistent. Filtering out anyone who says stupid things produces many false positives.
- Large organizations will always undervalue the most productive young people. The young have no performance to measure yet, and guesses about them regress to the mean.

## Starting a startup as a way to be paid market rate

- Any employer is economically a proxy for the customer, and its salary offer is a guess at your value to users.
- A productive young person can skip that guess by starting a company, so users value them directly.
- The market is "a lot more discerning than any employer" and "completely non-discriminatory." Users don't know or care if you are 22.
- Paul asks: "Why go work as an ordinary employee for a big company, when you could start a startup and make them buy it to get you?"
- Most successful startups succeed by getting bought, and acquirers usually want the people as well as the technology.
- Big companies often buy before profitability. "When a startup gets bought for 2 or 3 million six months in, it's really more of a hiring bonus than an acquisition."
- This helps founders, who get money up front. It helps acquirers too, because valuing each person's work is the central problem of big companies. Buying "larval startups" means they "don't pay till the developers have proven themselves," so they are protected on the downside and keep most of the upside.

## Why big companies are bad at product development

Paul says this is "the raison d'etre of startups." Big companies are good at extracting value from existing products and bad at creating new ones. His reasons:

- **Turf:** They protect existing businesses. Microsoft must be ambivalent about Web-based software because it threatens the desktop, so a product like Hotmail ends up coming from outside.
- **Power:** "Disruptive technologies are developed by disruptive people," who have little influence in big companies or don't work there.
- **One of each thing:** With one Web browser you can't take risks. Ten startups building ten browsers, with the best taken, gives something better. There may be 500 startups Microsoft might buy, more than it could run in-house.
- **Pay:** Big-company staff get paid about the same whether the product succeeds or fails. Startup staff get rich or get nothing, so they work harder.
- **Bigness:** Startup developers must talk to users because nobody else does sales and support. "It's painful doing sales, but you learn much more from trying to sell people something than reading what they said in focus groups."
- **Speed:** Product development needs many fast iterations, and everything is slower in big companies.

## What acquirers should do

- Pride is a main obstacle. Buying startups feels like an admission of failure, so companies delay and pay more.
- Paul says acquirers should find startups young, before VCs have "puffed them up into something that costs hundreds of millions." Much of what VCs add, the acquirer doesn't need.
- Grabbing a likely winner for a tenth or a twentieth of the later price means predicting only a twentieth as well.
- He expects a hybrid of investment and acquisition, such as buying a chunk with an option to buy the rest later.
- Buying a startup fuses recruiting and product development. The team is committed to what it works on and its conflicts have been "ironed out under the very hot iron of running a startup." That matters in software because many bugs occur at the boundaries between different people's code.

## Founders' growing power over investors

- Investors' power comes from money, so cheaper startups mean less investor power.
- Conventional VC wisdom has hackers accept MBAs as bosses, taking a title like CTO. Paul expects founders to push back on control.
- Fairchild Semiconductor, the first VC-backed startup, started in 1959 and needed factories. Today, more money "can't get software written faster," and facilities are cheap.
- Money mainly buys sales and marketing, and "marketing is increasingly irrelevant. On the Internet, anything genuinely good will spread by word of mouth."
- Google is his example. Investors required an older, experienced CEO, but the founders delayed a full year and chose someone with a computer science PhD. He suspects Google did better than if they had let an MBA take over after the first round.
- Paul grants that VC-installed business people have value but says they don't need to be bosses. He predicts they will increasingly be COOs rather than CEOs, with founders running engineering directly and the rest of the company through the COO.

## Taking risk early, and the "open cage"

- Risk and reward are proportionate because "people will pay extra for stability." Choosing stability, such as a job at a big company, costs you.
- Young people have time to invest, and "your early twenties are exactly the time to take insane career risks."
- Extreme choices like startups scare most people off, so there is less competition than the prizes suggest.
- The math is "brutal": perhaps 9 in 10 startups fail, but the one that succeeds pays founders "more than 10 times what they would have made in an ordinary job." In a note he says the 1-in-10 success rate is "a bit of an urban legend" and the odds are probably slightly worse.
- Failing at 22 means being "broke and a lot smarter" at 23, which is roughly what a graduate program gives.
- A failed startup doesn't hurt job prospects. Paul asked managers at Yahoo, Google, Amazon, Cisco and Microsoft about two 24-year-olds of equal ability. One had a failed startup and the other had worked at a big company, and all preferred the startup founder. Zod Nazem, head of engineering at Yahoo, said: "I actually put more value on the guy with the failed startup. And you can quote me!"
- He compares this to animals not noticing the cage is open, and to blogging, which only took off about eight years after people could have published online. He thinks most undergrads haven't realized "the economic cage is open."

## Think about users, not employers

- Undergrads spend their lives thinking about what employers want, but "what really matters is what customers want," because customers fund the employer.
- Big companies like "docile conformists," but that is "an artifact of their bigness, not something customers need."
- Paul says the end of school is "the fulcrum of your life," where you go from net consumer to net producer.
- Inexperience usually means not realizing you must make something people want. Technical skill and spending discipline are rarely the problem.
- His fix is to "smack hackers on the side of the head": "Don't sit here making up a priori theories about what users need. Go find some users and see what they need."
- Most successful startups do something very specific and solve a problem people already know they have.
- Real use differs from stated purpose. The stated purpose of PowerPoint is to present ideas, but its real role is to overcome fear of public speaking.
- "The goal in a startup is not to write a cool piece of software. It's to make something people want." Little school software has users, so this takes a mental adjustment.
- Employers are "proxies for users in which risk is pooled." A young, smart person can figure out what users want and doesn't want to pool risk.

## Grad school, dropping out, and caveats

- Grad school is a good launch pad: smart people around you and large blocks of free time. With a tolerant advisor you can develop an idea first.
- David Filo and Jerry Yang started the Yahoo directory in February 1994 and had a million hits a day by fall, but left grad school and started the company only in March 1995.
- Another route is to try the startup first and go to grad school if it fails. Startups that tank usually do so quickly, so "within a year you'll know if you're wasting your time."
- His official advice is "Don't drop out of college to start a startup." He says it may be fine to work for a company for a couple of years first to learn how companies work.
- He concedes he couldn't have told 19-year-old Bill Gates to wait. Gates was at "ground zero of the microcomputer revolution," and waiting four years would have meant missing the Altair Basic interpreter. The Apple II launched two years later.
- Paul admits this is advice "the old tell the young, but don't expect them to listen to."
