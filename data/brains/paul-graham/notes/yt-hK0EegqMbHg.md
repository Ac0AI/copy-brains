## Early-stage investing: small checks, no revenue forecasts, and honest founders

Paul runs Y Combinator, which invests in many startups at once. He funds roughly 25 or 30 startups twice a year, in batches. Each investment is about $20,000, and the group works intensively together for 3 months. At the end of the 3 months comes "demo day", where each startup gets 5 minutes to present to investors. YC is trying to be "first gear" money, and the aim is to help founders get the next round.

- He says the most useful thing YC does is "try and figure out what to build first".
- He also does things he wishes he didn't have to do, such as arbitrating conflicts between founders.
- YC has dinner with the founders once a week for the first 3 months. Until about a year before this talk, Paul cooked the dinners himself.
- Contact doesn't stop after the 3 months. Only the weekly dinners do.
- His expertise is the problems of the very beginning, not later-stage matters like IPOs. One example is that a two- or three-person founding team can lose a member in the first week.

## What Paul looks for in founders

Paul doesn't look for a "proven" team, technology or market in the usual VC sense. He looks for four traits: determined, smart, a sense of design, and gets things done.

- The evidence he wants is that someone really likes to build things.
- His reasoning is that to be really good at something you have to actually like it, not do it out of duty for a job or a class.
- It is hard for someone who really likes programming to reach age 23 without having built something outside of school or work.
- So the big question YC asks is: "What have you made outside of school or work because you liked it?"
- If founders don't like building, he argues, they won't do well as startup founders.
- The ideal is founders building something they themselves want to use. The next best is something a close person, such as a spouse or sibling, wants to use. This gives a deep understanding of what is essential in the product that you can't get by intellectualizing.
- When Paul talks about hackers building for platforms they use, his example is iPhone development. Many YC companies build for the iPhone because founders own iPhones. Not one is building for BlackBerry because none of them use BlackBerrys.
- He treats this as the way to predict which platforms will be big: look at what hackers themselves use. He called it "really bad news" for RIM.

## Revenue forecasts and the "don't ask, don't tell" policy

Paul says YC has a "don't ask, don't tell policy on revenue projections". They don't even ask for them.

- What he cares about is whether the founders are building something people really care about and want. If so, he argues, "the money will follow". If nobody cares, or it's like everything else, there won't be any money.
- It is fine for founders to say they don't know how to make money, as long as they treat it as a problem to solve. He frames it as: "We have no idea how to make money. We're worried about that. Talk to us. Let's figure out how to make money."
- He says you can't fake sincerity. Founders should care without saying so.
- The sentence to avoid is "we have no idea how to make money and it's not a problem".
- He thinks Dropbox was a good example of an early funding where the way to make money wasn't spelled out. The founders were two people from MIT, and the idea was clearly valuable.

## Being candid with investors, including about competitors

Paul's advice on presenting competitors is that the smarter the investor, the more candid the founder should be.

- The typical mistake is a competitor grid where the founder's column is fully checked and every competitor's column is empty. He says no founder at the Garage event ever showed a grid where their own column wasn't fully checked.
- A smart investor will notice if you lie. So with the investors whose money you want, tell them what you would say among yourselves.
- His example wording: "We're worried about such and such company because of such and such, but we think we have an edge because of this."
- His analogy: the better the investor, the more it should be like going to the doctor. Don't lie about your symptoms. Tell them exactly what's going on.
- He lists generic language as a BS trigger. A specific one is describing your idea with the word "passion". His line: "You should show passion... you shouldn't say the word passion. You should just seem like you care."

## Founder age, and why YC funds young founders

The average YC founder is about 25 or 26. Paul says the reason is not that young people are better. It is that more people start startups at that age.

- By the 30s, people have families and mortgages, and the friends they'd want to start a company with are more tied down.
- He calls this an "exponential effect of tied downness as people get older".
- 25-year-olds can say "What the hell, we'll try it."
- He says YC has funded some older founders, and that older founders can pitch and get a response.

## When to give up, and what founders can reasonably expect

Paul's test for whether a startup should quit is whether the founders have given up.

- He says that as long as founders haven't given up, they can morph the company into anything. In the early phase, a couple of people in an apartment "eating beans and writing code" can turn things around tomorrow.
- When a founder asks him "Can I give up now?", he says the answer is probably yes. They have already decided and want his blessing.
- If founders say "we're a year in, our software still sucks, we have no customers, but we're not giving up", he says he'll keep helping them for as long as they keep fighting.

## Board seats, bad news, and how Paul sees the investor role

Paul doesn't take board seats and says he never will.

- He argues you can do a lot of what a board member does without being on the board. Advice from a non-board member just has no formal standing. If it's good, he hopes they will listen.
- On founders bringing bad news, he says founders usually don't have good news when they ask to meet. In his experience, an email saying they "need to get together" means the VP of sales quit, a quarter was missed, or they are closer to running out of money than they thought.
- His observation is that bad things happen more suddenly than good things, in startups and in the news. Gradual improvement, like air safety getting better, isn't a headline.

## Valuation and ownership, and the competition for new platforms

A founder in the audience, who had put in $500,000 of his own money, asked how a VC values an early company. Paul gave a joking rule of thumb for pre-money valuation.

- For every full-time engineer, add $500,000 of pre-money valuation. For every MBA, subtract $250,000.
- His example: three engineers and two MBAs is 3 x $500K = $1.5M, minus 2 x $250K = $500K, which gives $1M. He said this was how to figure it, "very simple".
- Paul framed this as a joke answer. Mike Moritz of Sequoia gave the serious ownership range.
- On predicting the next big wave, Paul says that if he knew, he would have every company YC funds do it. He argues the question can't really be answered.
- His practical version is the platform rule above: watch which platforms hackers use themselves.
- His source of good ideas: think of something you needed at a company you worked for that didn't exist, where you'd say "if somebody would just make blank, we'd pay a lot for that". If you can fill in the blank, he says you are "onto a gold mine".
- On how much of a prototype to expect from founders: YC gives them about 3 weeks between inviting them to interview and the interview itself, which is the time to get plane tickets. He says that in that time they ought to throw together something to look at, unless they're building hardware or something that takes a long time.
