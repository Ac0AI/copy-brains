## Ramen profitability: definition and what it is for

- Paul defines ramen profitable as a startup making "just enough to pay the founders' living expenses."
- This differs from traditional profitability. A hardware company might spend $50 million over 5 years before turning profitable, then earn $50 million a year. That kind of profitability means the startup has succeeded.
- Ramen profitability can arrive after 2 months, at $3000 a month of revenue, when the only employees are a couple of 25 year old founders who can live on almost nothing. That revenue does not mean the company has succeeded.
- What the two share is that the company doesn't need to raise money to survive. Ramen profitability "buys you time."
- It only recently became feasible. It is not feasible for most biotech startups, but it is for many software startups because they are now so cheap. For many, the only real cost is the founders' living expenses.
- "Ramen" means instant ramen, the cheapest food available. Paul says not to take it literally, since it is unhealthy, and suggests rice and beans cooked in a rice cooker.

## The investor advantage: not being at their mercy

- A startup that is still losing money must eventually raise more or shut down. Once ramen profitable, "this painful choice goes away." You can still raise money, but you don't have to do it now.
- Not needing money gets you better terms. Investors who know you need money sometimes take advantage of you. Some deliberately stall, because they know you become "increasingly pliable" as you run out of money.
- Ramen profitability also makes you more attractive to investors, because it shows:
  - (a) you can get at least someone to pay you,
  - (b) you're serious about building things people want,
  - (c) you're disciplined enough to keep expenses low.
- Paul says these address three of investors' biggest worries. Companies with smart founders and a big market usually fail because:
  - (a) people wouldn't pay for what they made, for example because it was too hard to sell or the market wasn't ready,
  - (b) the founders solved the wrong problem instead of paying attention to what users needed,
  - (c) the company spent too much and burned through its funding before making money.
- A ramen profitable company is already avoiding these mistakes.

## Morale and the weight of responsibility

- A new company feels theoretical. It is legally a company, but "you feel like you're lying when you call it one." When people start paying you significant amounts, it starts to feel real.
- Covering your own living expenses is the milestone you feel most, because "the future flips state. Now survival is the default, instead of dying."
- Paul argues the moral weight of running a startup is what makes it hard. He dismisses the usual explanations:
  - Financial risk is not the barrier, since plenty of 25 year olds save nothing anyway.
  - Long hours are not the barrier, since plenty of people in regular jobs work just as long.
  - What keeps people from starting startups is "the fear of having so much responsibility," and the fear is not irrational.
- Anything that takes some of that weight off increases your chances of surviving.
- He says a startup that reaches ramen profitability "may be more likely to succeed than not." That is notable given the bimodal distribution of startup outcomes, where you either fail or make a lot of money.

## Fundraising is a distraction; choose when to raise

- Paul calls not interrupting the company to raise money the fourth advantage, "the least obvious but may be the most important."
- Raising money is "terribly distracting." You're lucky if your productivity is a third of what it was, and it can last for months.
- Startups YC funded usually "grind to a halt" when they switched to fundraising. Paul only remembered why when YC raised money itself.
  - It was comparatively easy, since the first people he asked said yes.
  - Working out the details still took months, and he got hardly any real work done.
  - The reason: "I thought about it all the time."
- At any time a startup has one most urgent problem, the one you think about falling asleep and in the morning shower. Fundraising takes over that slot. "You only take one shower in the morning, and if you're thinking about investors during it, then you're not thinking about the product."
- If you can choose when to raise, you can pick a time when you're not in the middle of something else and probably insist that the round close fast. You may even avoid having the round occupy your thoughts, if you don't care whether it closes.

## What ramen profitability does not imply

- **Not bootstrapping.** It doesn't mean you will never take investor money. Paul says empirically that doesn't seem to work well: "Few startups succeed without taking investment."
  - The money is there waiting to be invested. If startups need it less, they get it on better terms, which makes them more inclined to take it, so an equilibrium emerges.
- **Not Joe Kraus's rule** of putting your business model in beta when you put your product in beta, getting people to pay from the start.
  - Paul thinks that is too constraining. Facebook didn't do it and did better than most startups. Making money right away was probably not just unnecessary but harmful for them.
  - He still finds the rule useful for many startups. When founders seem unfocused, he sometimes suggests they try to get customers to pay for something, hoping the constraint prods them into action.
- **Not the final business model.** A ramen profitable company "doesn't have to be making money the way it ultimately will. It just has to be making money." The most famous example is Google, which initially made money by licensing search to sites like Yahoo.

## The main danger: sliding into consulting

- Paul says the biggest downside is that ramen profitability might turn you into a consulting firm.
- Startups must be product companies, "making a single thing that everyone uses." Their defining quality is that they grow fast, and consulting can't scale the way a product can.
- It is easy to make $3000 a month consulting. That would be a low rate for contract programming. The temptation is to tell yourselves you're a ramen profitable startup "when in fact you're not a startup at all."
- A little consulting-type work at first is fine, since startups usually have to do something weird early on.
- Paul's reminder: "ramen profitability is not the destination. A startup's destination is to grow really big; ramen profitability is a trick for not dying en route."
- A startup could in theory grow big by transforming consulting into something that scales, but then it would really be a product company.

## Investor power and the venture business (side argument)

- Paul thinks a shift in power from investors to founders could actually increase the size of the venture business.
- He believes investors currently "err too far on the side of being harsh to founders." Forced to stop, the whole venture business would work better, like the increase in trade you see when restrictive laws are removed.
- His chain of reasoning: investors are one of the biggest sources of pain for founders. If they caused less pain, being a founder would be better, and more people would do it.
