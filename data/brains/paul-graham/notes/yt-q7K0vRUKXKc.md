## Make something people want, and be benevolent while doing it

- Paul's motto for Y Combinator is "make something people want". A month after YC started they stumbled on it, and he says that if he were choosing again today he would still choose it. He doesn't think it can be shortened to fewer than three words.
- The second most common advice to startups is not to worry too much about the business model at the start. Money is important, but it is much easier than making something great.
- Put together, the two ideas describe a nonprofit. Paul's conclusion is that this is a new discovery, not a bug: very successful startups often behave like nonprofits, and many nonprofit ideas would make good companies.
- Some ideas that look benevolent have to be built as companies to be done right. He once considered a free web email service with really good spam filters, and realized it would have to be a company. He thinks a purely benevolent project to index the web could not have come out much better than Google did.
- He won't say where the territory ends. He floats curing malaria as an extreme case: lifting that weight off a region could unlock growth, but he says explicitly it isn't a startup suggestion. His test for how far an idea extends is the point at which you'd be willing to bet against it, and betting against benevolence feels like betting against a technology.

## Craigslist, Google and Microsoft as models of early-stage niceness

- Craigslist is not a charity but is run like one. Paul suspects, without having done the math, that it makes more money per employee than Google. Its employee count on the lists of most popular sites "looks like a misprint".
- He uses a sailing metaphor from Patrick O'Brian's novels: Craigslist has "the weather gauge" (it is upwind) of enormous revenues. It decides when and whether to engage, and its challenges would be "spin" challenges, unlike companies tacking upwind to force a crappy product on ambivalent customers while spending 10 times as much on sales as on development.
- He does not say startups should end up like Craigslist, but calls it a good model for the first phases.
- Google had no ads for its whole first year and looked like the limit of what a nonprofit web index would produce.
- Microsoft in the beginning was "like Robin Hood" next to IBM. IBM expected to make money selling PC hardware at high prices. Microsoft gained control of the PC standard, which let any manufacturer compete. Prices plummeted and many people who couldn't have afforded computers got them.
- Microsoft's stock rose like Google's back then and has been flat for years since it started frustrating and foisting on users. He suggests there may be a connection.

## Why small companies must charm and big ones can bully

- When you're small you can't bully users, so you have to charm them. When you're big you can maltreat them at will, and tend to because it's less work.
- "You grow big by being nice, but you can stay big by being mean." You get away with it until the underlying conditions change and your victims all escape (his example: the record companies).
- He argues "don't be evil" may be the most valuable thing Paul Buchheit made for Google, more than Gmail, because it may work as "an elixir of corporate youth" that saves them from the laziness of Microsoft and IBM.
- The elixir is free to any company (no IP protection). The catch is that people will hold you to it, so he doesn't expect record labels or tobacco companies to adopt it.

## Three ways benevolence helps a startup

Paul says investing in many startups gave him data on what happens inside them. He found three effects: it improves morale, makes other people want to help you, and above all helps you be decisive.

**Morale**
- Morale is so important that it is almost enough on its own to determine success. Startups are an emotional roller coaster, and the real problem with feeling doomed is that you stop working, which makes the downhill parts a self-fulfilling prophecy.
- If you feel you're really helping people, you keep working even when you feel doomed. Most people are naturally inclined to help someone who needs them.
- A product users come back to daily becomes "a giant Tamagotchi" you need to take care of. Blogger is his example: it ran out of money, everyone left, and Evan Williams came back the next day and was the only one there. Users needing him was part of what kept him going.
- This is one reason to launch quickly: once you launch, the Tamagotchi effect kicks in, and you are forced to figure out what users want, which is very valuable information.

**Confidence with investors**
- Chatterous's two founders decided what they were building was so important they would do it no matter what, even if they had to move back to Canada and live in their parents' basements. After that they stopped caring what investors thought, kept going to meetings, and investors got much more interested because they could tell the founders didn't need them.
- YC itself looks for startups that would do better with them, not ones that would die without them, because a startup that close to dying will probably die with them too.
- If you're really committed and your startup is cheap to run, you become very hard to kill. His model for a startup: "not WebVan, Craigslist, and cockroaches". He calls this the most valuable quality a startup can have, since nearly all startups, even successful ones, come close to death at some point, and you're lucky if it's only once.

## Benevolence makes people rally to you

- Octopart, a search site for industrial components, is his example. Before it there was no good way to search for components online. Its founders dropped out of the physics PhD program at Berkeley after hitting the problem in their own research (they bought a lot of components).
- Digi-Key, the biggest distributor, tried to force Octopart to take prices off the site even though Octopart sent them paying customers for free. Paul's reading: Digi-Key's business model depends on overcharging people with incomplete information about prices, so they don't want search to work.
- Hearing about a big evil company trying to keep search broken made him spend more time on Octopart than on most funded startups. Investors, customers, other companies and potential employees all rally to a benevolent project.
- Employees may matter most in the long run. Good hackers are much more valuable pound for pound than mediocre ones, the best tend to be idealistic, can work anywhere, and choose projects that make the world better. A project that does so is a magnet for smart people, which was a huge advantage for Google.

## Being good as a compass and a stateless algorithm

- A startup has thousands of things to do and two or three people to do them, and the one thing you can't do is vacillate. Paul's answer: "Just do whatever is best for your users." He says to hold on to it "like a rope in a hurricane".
- The loop: figure out what users want, make them happy, keep doing more of that.
- It also answers how to raise money. Most hackers aren't great salesmen, so go to investors through your customers. If users like the product enough to tell their friends, that is a recipe for exponential growth, and the traffic graph convinces investors (he shows Octopart's, which separates human from non-human traffic, with a dip at Christmas because "nobody buys components for Christmas presents").
- Being good is "stateless", like telling the truth. A liar has to remember everything said before to avoid contradiction, whereas a truth-teller remembers nothing. That matters in domains where things change fast.
- YC had invested in 80 startups, 57 still alive "for some definition of alive". Advising that many at once requires a stateless algorithm with no ulterior motives, since you couldn't remember them. Their rule is to do whatever is best for the startups, which may not be optimal for YC but is reasonably close. He says this is because it is the only algorithm that works at that scale, not because they're particularly benevolent.
- He adds that Google likely doesn't manipulate search results for the same reason: once you start, it doesn't scale.
- He says he is not a particularly good person and is suggesting benevolence "because it works", as corporate strategy and as a design spec for software, not as sanctimonious goody-two-shoes behavior. His closing line: "don't just not be evil, be good."

## Q&A: school, finding ideas, and what YC funds

- **Dropping out for an idea:** Paul's view is there's no huge rush. College is also for fun, learning and friends, and the idea that seems so great you'd drop out may not turn out to be that great. If you wait until after school, you'll probably think of an even better one. (An audience member strongly disagreed, arguing that dropouts end up employing degree holders; Paul left it at "maybe".)
- **Finding startup ideas:** "Look for problems. Don't try and think of solutions." Look for things that seem broken and let the problems drive the solutions. Component search was broken and the Octopart founders noticed because they had to buy components in their research.
- **Why so many web 2.0 companies:** YC does fund infrastructure, including two database companies, which haven't launched because that kind of software takes so long to build. But they fund what people apply with, and most people apply with some form of Facebook or independent music. "You can only catch the fish that are floating down the stream."
- **Selection criterion:** YC tries to fund the founders it likes and who seem likely to succeed. A benevolent startup that doesn't succeed doesn't achieve its aims, so success is the limit on how benevolent YC can be. Founders it likes tend to do benevolent things because being good works.
- **Mottos:** Paul says encouraging startups to have mottos is a good idea, but not if making one delays launch by a couple of hours.
