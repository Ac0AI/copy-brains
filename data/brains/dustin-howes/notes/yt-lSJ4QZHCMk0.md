## What "unlimited" means at Trackdesk

- Dustin raised a concern from his own experience: he has "been burned by this before" in affiliate programs where "unlimited" on a pricing page didn't mean unlimited.
- He frames the buyer's job as reading the small print behind the "unlimited" hook and asking vendors to state on the record where the ceiling is.
- The question he wants answered before signing with any vendor, Trackdesk included: what happens to your bill once the program starts producing sales?
- The ceilings he names as common: a click cap, a "contact sales" wall, or a traffic spike that turns into a support ticket.
- Trackdesk's stated position (from its CEO Martin, whom Dustin interviewed): no fees on affiliates, transactions or conversions. The one limit is clicks, set by default at 20 times the closest competitor's, and the smallest plan carries that same default.

## Why click-based pricing hurts low-value traffic verticals

- Dustin's point of view as an operator: click caps and overage charges matter most in verticals where a click is worth a few cents, not $10-15.
- If overage fees per click approach what the network earns per click, a network owner ends up asking affiliates to stop sending traffic. That is the failure mode he wants buyers to screen for.
- The questions he asks any vendor:
  - Where exactly does "unlimited" end (clicks, contacts, support)?
  - What does the bill do when a program does well?
  - Why is a platform charging more when the program succeeds?
- Martin's answer, which Dustin accepted as plausible: old click-based pricing dates from when servers were costly and software was built 15-20 years ago. A platform rebuilt from scratch to handle billions of daily clicks can carry healthy margins without per-click overages.

## Vetting a vendor's handling of traffic spikes

- Dustin tests vendors with a concrete scenario: an affiliate "blows up" overnight and sends 100,000 clicks. He asks whether anything breaks and whether the bill goes up.
- What he wants to hear is a process, not just capacity:
  - Someone monitors volume around the clock across time zones.
  - The vendor contacts you on your preferred channel (Telegram, WhatsApp, Slack) and decides with you whether to keep the traffic or block it.
  - Fraud signals are checked, such as conversions firing milliseconds after the click.
  - Because the vendor serves many customers sharing the same affiliates, it can warn you when a traffic source is behaving badly elsewhere too.
- He values this because forced or fraudulent clicks happen, and not every network will listen when you ask to reverse them.

## Evaluating postback setup and AI features

- Dustin calls postbacks the "scary" part of the affiliate space, from his experience as both an affiliate manager and an affiliate.
- The Trackdesk feature he highlighted: an AI agent that builds postbacks. You tell it who you are connecting with and send a URL, and it returns a URL to pass over Telegram, WhatsApp or whichever channel you use with the advertiser.
- The business case, as relayed in the interview: larger networks employ full-time staff just to set up postbacks between affiliates, networks and advertisers. Automating this frees managers to spend time with affiliates rather than with dev and tech teams.
- Dustin's own view is that the value of affiliate management is relationship time with partners, so he rates tools that remove technical busywork.

## Criteria for choosing affiliate software

- Dustin asked the guest for the deciding factors and treats them as a checklist for buyers. The three offered:
  - Support where the customer already works, with no extra charge for being in your Slack or Telegram.
  - Reliability: nothing breaks during traffic spikes and there is no downtime.
  - Features and AI readiness: over 300 features launched in a year, and chat-based control of the platform (sending affiliate links, adjusting offers, setting postbacks) rather than read-only reporting.
- Dustin noted that he plans to run his own program on the platform and share the experience, so he treats the vendor's claims as something to be tested rather than taken on faith.
