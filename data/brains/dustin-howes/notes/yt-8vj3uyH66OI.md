## Why affiliate recruiting stalls

- Dustin says the biggest cause is expectations. Businesses think "we have a great product, let's build an affiliate program" and assume affiliates will show up, take a link and promote.
- Recruitment ends up last on the affiliate manager's list. Managers who inherit an existing program spend their time on current affiliates, so activation and new recruiting get pushed down.
- He blames some networks and platforms for marketing affiliate as set-and-forget. Networks cite thousands or millions of affiliates but don't say that very few fit your business.
- Reaching the few who can promote you is hard through a platform. In his experience there is no substitute for direct, hands-on searching for the best affiliates in your niche.
- Don't launch a program out of desperation or as your primary channel. The company should be established first, with a high-converting website and landing pages. If traffic doesn't convert, you damage relationships and affiliates move to your competitors.
- Train affiliate managers on the company, the ICP and the sales cycle. He admits that as a young manager he knew too little about the company and tried to dodge it. Knowing which affiliates actually close sales, not just bring top-of-funnel curiosity, is a key part of learning.

## Build partner personas and target quality over quantity

- The biggest mistake he sees is not creating partner personas. Write about five types, like you would for customers: content affiliates, bloggers, listicle sites, influencers on a specific topic or hashtag.
- First work out each persona's motivation to work with you, and don't build a list yet. Motivations differ:
  - Small bloggers will work for free if the pitch is right.
  - Big publications won't work for free; you buy sponsorship unless your product is truly unique and they already have content on it.
  - Influencers with 1,000 to 10,000 followers may work for free gifts or a little content. Above 10,000 you may need to offer sponsorship upfront.
- Each type needs a different approach.
- Program size is a vanity stat. A few affiliates deliver most sales. He described a customer program earning roughly 300K a month in commissions, with about 6,000 affiliates, perhaps 200 earning anything, and the top 10 accounting for about half of historical revenue.
- He cites Nathan Barry (ConvertKit), who framed it as 95-5: not that 5% bring 95% of sales, but that 95% of affiliates bring no traffic or revenue.
- His goal as a manager is to move toward 80-20. Know most active sellers personally and tier them: bottom (potential), middle (extra attention), top (do everything for them). Ask top-tier affiliates monthly how you can help.
- Diversify across partner types, and aim at the moon with big influencers only with realistic expectations. A million-follower influencer won't promote you for free; you negotiate and build trust.

## Where to find affiliates, in priority order

1. Bloggers, listicle writers and content creators ranking on Google for your target keywords. List the ranking sites, find the right person, and keep pursuing a spot on the list until they say no. Ask what motivates them, since you only find out by asking.
2. Media buyers. Harder to find, but they know how to bring quality traffic at volume. Monitor messaging so it matches your brand and isn't misleading.
3. Email marketers who have built lists for years.
4. LinkedIn influencers for B2B and SaaS. Reviews bring less traffic but high-quality customers and good conversion.
5. Community admins, a good source of traffic.
6. Coupon, loyalty and toolbar sites at the bottom of the funnel. Often forced into programs; the industry's "closers".
7. YouTube, TikTok, Instagram and podcasters, which he treats as one influencer category with different channels.

## Tools for finding and reaching partners

- Afy Stash is his first choice. Enter a competitor URL or your own to see which keywords affiliates rank for, then get contact details. It saves a lot of list-building time.
- Upfluence for influencer marketing; search by hashtag for large lists.
- Phantom Buster is underused in the affiliate community. Pull the followers of competitors' brands, download them with follower counts, then follow and engage the best ones. Example pitch: you like product X, here is a better and cheaper one your audience may like, want to promote it?
- Semrush, Similar Web, Hunter.io and Ahrefs (domain authority) as Chrome extensions.
- Waalaxy for LinkedIn outreach automation, used for B2B clients and SaaS influencers.
- He has a guide to the roughly 50 tools he uses at dustinous.com/tools.
- Find the decision-maker. A 100,000-follower influencer likely has someone handling partnerships; use LinkedIn or the website to find them.

## Outreach and negotiation

- Decide first whether you have an exploratory budget and can buy sponsorships. He strongly recommends moving budget from other channels into exploring affiliate. With a budget, he says response rates rise dramatically, even several-fold.
- Without budget, offer free gifts (influencers must actually use the product; for SaaS, have them work with it and build a tutorial), backlinks for content publishers (they value them for SEO), and EPC and conversion rate data so they can decide.
- He finds 10K to 50K follower influencers the most effective on commission when shown EPC and conversion.
- Sponsorship also de-risks the affiliate: they create content and traffic hoping it converts. Paying for their time shows you value it. Often only a few hundred dollars, not much negotiation. For thousands, negotiate down by raising commission or cutting the upfront fee.
- Run a test budget with a few partners, then build case studies showing what they earned versus what they would have earned on commission. Use them as social proof and to justify lower upfront fees.
- Email structure of three parts:
  1. Why their content is great and why you like it (personal).
  2. What your product is and what you offer.
  3. A closing question, such as whether it fits their audience or whether they have time to promote it.
- Avoid AI tells: the words "dip", "dive" and "delve", em dashes, and emojis. He uses Matt McWilliams' tool for email drafts but still humanizes the output.
- Expect 10% or less response rate at scale; targeted, personal outreach can reach 20% or more.
- Run outreach over three channels (DM, email, LinkedIn friending) on three different days to raise response.
- Warm approach for small niche creators: comment on their posts, praise a specific piece, and send something useful, like a video on how to monetize with a $10 tool. He then treats them as friends.
- On LinkedIn creators who prefer sponsored posts: build friendships and give value. He says it's a long road if you can't afford upfront budget, and he tries to be everyone's friend, with favors coming back naturally.
- Don't pay upfront to people without a meaningful audience; offer them the affiliate program.

## Screening applications

- Keep the application short: name, company, URL, social links, and an open box on how they will promote. Skip tax info and anything intrusive.
- Red flags:
  - A website that isn't theirs (e.g. someone entering Google).
  - A site that clearly doesn't fit your niche (cat blog for a SaaS), unless their promotion plan convinces you.
  - Sites that don't show what they do.
  - Inflated traffic claims (100K email subscribers on a tiny blog).
  - Spelling mistakes.
  - Certain countries he is wary of accepting from.
- Green flags: content fits your niche, audience matches your ICP, quality content, they know how to write CTAs, and their brand quality matches yours.
- He gives people the benefit of the doubt and watches borderline applicants more closely once sales arrive.

## Activation and onboarding

- Most programs miss the welcome sequence. He suggests five emails over the first 5 days:
  1. Welcome, who the affiliate manager is, how to reach them.
  2. Their affiliate link, delivered fast.
  3. Marketing assets and resources.
  4. Success stories of other affiliates, plus contact info and a booking link.
  5. How the brand works and how to maximize earnings; the final email says to reach out anytime.
- Keep assets in one easily accessible, organized folder: email swipes, product summaries, best marketing strategies, banners, deep links.
- Use two one-pagers: one for recruiting ("why join") and one for post-signup onboarding with marketing details. A Notion page works too.
- Think of affiliate onboarding like product onboarding; the "aha" moment is their first commission.
- Ongoing activation: bonus structures and volume-based incentives, backlinks to their best content, occasionally paying for traffic to top-performing affiliate sites, sharing campaign wins in your newsletter, and promoting the program on social media to reach fans who didn't know it existed. He says 100 long-tail affiliates making a few sales each add up.

## Commission, CPA and sponsorship decisions

- Commission versus flat fee depends on the brand, upsell ability and lifetime value.
- Best answer is often to offer both: revenue share or CPA.
- If revenue share, cap it at 12 months; retaining the customer is the brand's job.
- If CPA, he suggests something like the first three months of revenue. With a six-month lifetime value, paying three months works for both sides.
- He has seen programs pay up to 200% of first-month revenue, and a company paying up to $100 CPA on a $60 product, accepting the loss because of future upsells and lifetime value.
- Sponsorship rates: no fixed formula. With about 1,000 to 10,000 Instagram followers, he generally wouldn't sponsor and would stay in the affiliate program with free gifts. Above that, think in hundreds and thousands, scaling with size and engagement rate; rates vary a lot by niche (finance, travel, tech, B2B SaaS).
- To persuade advertisers who expect CPA-only: reframe budget from PPC (e.g. a few thousand dollars, he mentions $3,000), show affiliates' data on what they cost, name the first five partners and the expected sales.
