## Open source as a speedrun to product-market fit

- Guillermo treats open source as a filter. The two hardest startup problems are getting people to know you exist and building the right thing, and open source stress-tests both at once.
- His framing: "I've always thought of open source as a speedrun to product-market fit, because if people don't use it when it's free, when it's easy to consume, when the code is available, then you probably should be working on something else."
- He accepts the downside that openness makes replication easier, especially in the age of agents. His counter is that the best open source projects extend into broader platforms, and success surfaces new problems to solve.
- Example: Next.js is only a sliver of the cloud problem enterprises actually face, which is where Vercel's business sits.
- He says founders stall because they worry about the future too early: "What stops entrepreneurs in their tracks is that they worry too much about what's going to happen in the future. In reality, they don't even have product-market fit yet."

## Unbounded vision, ruthless focus (the "bicameral mind")

- Guillermo describes a "bicameral mind": a bold, boundless long-term vision, paired with extreme discipline about what gets built on any given day.
- With three people in the room, unbounded ambition destroys you if it dictates the roadmap: "When you're getting started and you're three people, if all you do every day is unbounded ambition and building two hundred million different features, then you've fucked up."
- Early Vercel tried to make everything deployable: every language, runtime and framework, even databases.
- He forced the question "where are we being truly excellent?" The answer was the front-end layer, a new kind of managed service that didn't exist yet.
- He credits that narrowing with the difference between building something no one would pay for and building something worth hundreds of millions.
- His blunt counterfactual: "If I had continued doing everything, I would have made a company that made zero dollars. Literally zero dollars."
- Why focus pays: "Discerning buyers want the best of each class of problem. They don't settle for the second-best solution."

## In the AI era, choosing what to build is the edge

- When AI makes everything buildable, the temptation is to build everything. Guillermo argues that is backward: when every problem is shallow with enough tokens, picking which problems deserve attention becomes the competitive advantage.
- He warns that many founders are already over-building: "A bunch of you are probably already over-building massively because you notice that you can just prompt it and it'll do it. But the discernment of what problems are worth solving could be one of your biggest differentiators."
- Good use of AI at Vercel: an agent working overnight on a JavaScript optimization that made the platform 20-40% faster for customers.
- Bad use of AI: rewriting internal HR tools.
- He sees the CEO's job as increasingly about helping the company decide what is worth investing in when everything is possible.

## Hiring for proof of work and how people package ideas

- Guillermo looks for people on the rise rather than people who have already arrived: those shipping high-quality work, especially with AI tools.
- He pays attention to something most hiring managers ignore, which is how candidates communicate what they built: "Sometimes the reason I reach out to people is not just what they built, but how they communicate it."
- Example: he cold-DMed a developer after seeing a novel approach to a well-known UI problem, which also came with a new way of packaging and explaining the contribution.
- He ties this to a broader product philosophy, comparing it to Apple's unboxing experience: the website, the store, the feeling of lifting the lid, with every layer thought through.

## Cut the noise from your landing page and positioning

- He criticizes "slop landing pages" from startups, such as a screen recording of the founder's entire desktop.
- What he sees in them: a dock with two hundred applications, a full browsing history, two hundred plugins.
- His rule: "Delete all the things that are not your thing, because they're an excuse for people to get distracted."
- The principle is to show only what is yours, and make the packaging as intentional as the product.

## Choosing what to dismiss is a competitive advantage

- Guillermo argues that in a world where every new tool and framework generates instant hype, saying "no" is underrated: "Silicon Valley has this sharp edge of people just get so f***ing excited about everything. And some of those things are worth dismissing. Choosing what you dismiss can also be a competitive advantage."
- He never outsources judgment. He downloads products, signs up and uses them firsthand. "If I don't fully understand it myself, it's very hard for me to actually build conviction."
- His two-phase process:
  1. Experiment broadly.
  2. Set a high bar for what you actually bring to market.
- When something clears that bar, he goes all in: post it everywhere, email it, present it.
- He warns against confusing the excitement of experimentation with the conviction required to ship.
