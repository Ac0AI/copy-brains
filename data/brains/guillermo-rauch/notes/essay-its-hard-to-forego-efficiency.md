## Efficiency is the one thing you can't forego

Guillermo argues that life and engineering are full of tradeoffs, and that you have to learn to let some things drop to the ground to make progress. Examples he gives: de-optimizing email and meeting availability to hit a deadline, shipping a new company with a weak website design, or shipping a new app without documented APIs, thorough code comments or lots of tests.

His core claim is that one thing is different: once you are used to a certain level of efficiency in a task, system or environment, "it's extraordinarily difficult to forego it."

- Everything else (polish, docs, tests, design) can be traded away for a while.
- Efficiency, once experienced, can't be given up without it feeling like a "backwards change."
- He calls it "a quality worth fighting to preserve" and puts it "at the top."

## The "backwards change" test: why efficiency is sticky

Guillermo uses a simple thought experiment to show the effect.

- Googling is a task you repeat every day.
- Imagine switching to a browser that takes 200 milliseconds to render search results instead of 20 milliseconds.
- It would be "almost impossible to justify such a backwards change" in your daily routine.

The general lesson for product builders: a faster, lower-friction workflow becomes the baseline, and users (and builders) will resist anything slower, even by small absolute amounts, when the task is repeated often.

## You don't need long exposure; knowing something faster exists is enough

He says you don't have to live with superior efficiency for long to feel the pull. Merely knowing that "greener and more efficient grass is out there" does the same thing.

- Example: at your job, deploying an app to production takes hours.
- A colleague deploys in seconds (he links to ZEIT's `now`).
- Once you know that, you feel its "inexorable pull."

Implication: once a faster way is visible to your users, team or competitors, the slower way loses its justification. This is the logic behind building products around dramatically better speed of a core workflow, such as deploys.

## Efficiency applies to any system, not only software

Guillermo says the effect is "by no means limited to programming." He illustrates with his own experience as an immigrant to the United States.

- He spent tireless hours assembling the evidence and paperwork required to become a legal resident.
- It took only "one or two visits" for him to know the effort and bureaucracy were worth it.
- Back in his home country he was no longer his "same productive self." Every process felt painfully slow: getting a bank account, incorporating a company, ordering food, hiring a plumber, the plumber's work, how the plumber got paid, and the plumber's punctuality.

Takeaway: the efficiency of the surrounding system (banking, incorporation, services, payments) shapes how productive people can be, and people will pay a large one-time cost to move into a more efficient environment.

## Einstein on why efficient systems emerge

To support the point, Guillermo quotes Einstein's impressions of America from *The World As I See It*:

> "What first strikes the visitor with amazement is the superiority of this country, in matters of technics and organization. Objects of everyday use are more solid than in Europe, houses infinitely more convenient in arrangement. Everything is designed to save human labour. Labour is expensive, because the country is sparsely inhabited in comparison with its natural resources. The high price of labour was the stimulus which evoked the marvelous development of technical devices and methods of work."

Guillermo endorses this as his favorite trait of the country: the high cost of labor drove technical devices and methods that save human effort.

His closing logic: if it is hard to part ways with a more efficient web browser, "imagine how hard it is to leave behind a more efficient country!"
