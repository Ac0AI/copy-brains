## Windows-compatible iPods as a distribution move

- Dalton calls Apple's 2002 decision to make iPods Windows-compatible one of its two favorite tactical moves, one he only recently came to fully appreciate.
- It looked un-Apple at the time: exposing beautiful hardware to Windows users "must have been controversial inside of the company."
- His own story: in summer 2002 he ran Linux and bought a Creative Nomad, but its Windows-only sync software was "terrible" and he returned it. He considered Archos (software reported worse) and bought an iPod. The software wasn't great, but it was "great by comparison."
- That iPod was his first Apple device, and the first for many people.
- Core argument: by co-opting the Windows userbase, Apple made "Windows desktop marketshare irrelevant in the mobile-centric world that would soon come into being."
- Once everyone had an iPod, whether they used Windows or Mac stopped mattering. Buying an iPhone or iPad isn't seen as "switching," and iPad buyers' legacy desktops run Windows.
- The move "paved the way for the iPhone. For iOS. For the iPad."
- Takeaway: a product that meets users on the platform they already use can become the entry point to a later ecosystem. Competitors' bad software on that platform makes a merely good product win.

## Market segmentation off a single product line

- Dalton's second favorite Apple move: "Apple is doing market segmentation off of a single product line!"
- Typical hardware companies treat the product cycle as a replacement cycle. New models launch, the previous model is quietly dumped, and car dealers do the same with closeout sales.
- Apple instead keeps some manufacturing capacity on the previous iPhone. The old device is a fully debugged process with increasingly cheaper components.
- Result: Apple lets Moore's law turn today's great product into tomorrow's entry-level device, with no re-tooling and no "polluting your own channel."
- He cites pricing at the time: the flagship iPhone from 3 years earlier for $0 (with contract), the one from 2 years earlier for $99.

## One product, one team

- In Apple's org chart the iPhone is a single product with a single product team, software team and marketing team, all focused on "the single greatest product they can" build.
- Low-end buyers get the same great product, only older, instead of a separate cheap product.
- Dalton's contrast: at a large Android manufacturer he dealt with at a previous company, separate teams built phones for separate segments (low end, business users, etc.).
  - Each had its own product manager, requirements, budget, and marketing and rollout schedule.
  - The devices shipped with different Android versions; he recalls the low-end phone on 1.5 and the others on 1.6.

## Why price and segment matter: the WebOS tablet example

- Dalton uses HP's 2011 Palm WebOS tablet liquidation to show how powerful price is.
- HP killed WebOS and liquidated all tablets in the channel at once.
- The product "they couldn't sell to save their lives" "flew off the shelves" at $99.
- Lesson: demand at a lower price point can be huge for a product the market ignored at the original price.

## Why competitors don't copy it

- Nothing about the strategy is specific to Apple or to mobile, so Dalton asks why Dell, Samsung or HP haven't done it. He allows he may not understand the hardware supply chain.
- His best guess is corporate inertia:
  - A single flagship phone means one person in charge, one head of marketing and one head of engineering. That would require moving, demoting or firing many executives and managers from their fiefdoms.
  - It would kill large existing profitable revenue lines, which is hard to explain to public-company investors.
  - A change this radical at that scale has "overwhelming odds" of becoming a disaster "of the Yahoo (or RIM) order."
- He also wonders how much of Apple's strategy was fully understood in advance versus "smart people doing the incremental right thing at thousands of micro-decision points." His answer: "Probably both."
