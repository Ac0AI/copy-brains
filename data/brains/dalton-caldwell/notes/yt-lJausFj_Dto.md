## Magical thinking: the founder failure mode Dalton warns about

Dalton defines "magical thinking" as his own founder version of the psychology term. It is believing you live in a world with magical causes and effects, where things not rooted in reality are true for you. He calls it a poor grasp of how the world actually works.

- Example: believing that if you pitch a famous investor in an airport, he will instantly see your brilliance, "get out of his checkbook" and fly you away to become the next Google.
- Another version: treating life like a romantic comedy where you are the main character and a dramatic, showy act will make things work out.
- Dalton's contrast: the real way to meet an investor is to apply to YC on the website. "We're just normal people," and the path is "actually pretty straightforward."
- Founders who think this way often assume funded companies "got lucky like that," so they copy the luck instead of studying how the world really works.

## How magical thinking shows up in founders' behavior

- **Excuses:** Direct questions get long, complicated answers about why they can't grow or get customers. The story always casts the founder as the hero and external parties as the villains.
- **"If only I had money":** They tell stories about other companies getting breaks, or believe the next break will take them from zero to 100 in one step. Dalton finds this "extremely frustrating" because it misunderstands how the game works.
- **Product:** A founder with no tech co-founder who has never built a product and given it to anyone, yet thinks things are going well, is too optimistic. Dalton's blunt line: "it's not going well."
- **Idea worship:** Many founders pitch a social network that "is like Facebook and TikTok" with no code and no product. They are frustrated when YC isn't excited, because they assume the idea itself has inherent value.

## Over-optimism after raising money or hiring

- **Money as a savior:** Raising from famous investors does not mean problems stop existing. Dalton sees founders turn off their brains after a raise: "we're on easy street, we nailed it."
- **Investors aren't magical:** Founders assume their investors have powers that will save the company. They don't.
- **The magic-hire fantasy:** Founders with a big, hairy problem they don't understand (usually pre-product-market-fit) assume a marketing or sales hire will come in and solve it. The belief is that they will "do it," not that you'll figure it out together.
- This is over-optimistic on two fronts: employees won't hand you the answer, and the best marketer in the world wouldn't join a pre-MVP startup anyway.

## Chasing hot categories

- Dalton's pattern: founders assume an idea will work because it is trending or investors are talking about it on Twitter.
- Example: the flood of remote-work and video-conferencing startups when COVID hit. Zoom had been working for years and had a head start in technology and position. Starting from scratch after the moment arrives means optimizing for something that happens later.
- Rule: get in before everyone thinks it's cool, not at the peak.
- Signal an idea has "jumped the shark": it becomes one of the most common ideas people apply to YC with. His example is chatbots, which everyone built for a couple of years.

## Too pessimistic: what it looks like and how to diagnose it

Dalton's rule: you need to be both optimistic and pessimistic, and if you are too far toward either extreme you will fail.

- **Core cause:** poor expectation setting. Founders don't realize the work is hard, and that even when it works you mostly get "no's."
- **Typical trigger:** a founder sends an unpersonalized cold email to 100 prospects, gets five replies and decides the startup doesn't work. Dalton notes YC hears this "every batch" and has to say "it's going to be fine."
- **Anything short of unmitigated success** right out of the gate makes them want to quit.
- **Giving god-like predictive power to one opinion:** a single investor, friend or customer saying it won't work.
- **Missing the point of aggressive goals:** founders treat missing a Demo Day goal as proof the company is dead. The goal exists to make you move fast. If you learned a lot pursuing it, you got the value even if you missed it.
- **Other signals:** giving up too early, expecting it to be easy, believing everyone else has it easier, and leaving people deflated after talking to you.
- Dalton says pessimism is curable, because the fix is usually to set realistic expectations.

## Holding both at once: the balance

- Dalton invokes cognitive dissonance: holding two conflicting ideas at the same time, which most people find uncomfortable.
- The best founders can do it. Example: "My star employee just quit and says my startup is horrible and I don't know what I'm going to do, but I'm going to get on the sales call and sell my heart out and close this customer."
- Every day brings crises and victories. Founders who fail resolve the dissonance by going too far in one direction: freaking out over bad things or losing contact with reality.
- Many strong founders don't enjoy this but learn to thrive and execute in it, and the risk sharpens them. Many don't bring their true A-game until things are pretty bad.
- Blueprint in Dalton's words: be optimistic, just don't be magically optimistic, and stay "squarely rooted in real reality."
