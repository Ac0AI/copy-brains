## Unique startup ideas come from the discard bin, not from what's currently cool

Dalton's core claim: the bottleneck has moved from building to having an idea, and most ideas are not unique.

- Tools are now so good that "you can crank out anything." The scarce thing is a real idea.
- Many founders are pushed toward ideas that "sound good to investors" or toward the current Twitter trend. His example: cloning whatever is hot at the moment of recording (he names OpenClaw). He calls this "pretty low effort."
- Everyone has a similar filter for startup ideas. If your process is to find something that is both good and that no one has thought of, "you're going to have a bad time."
- His alternative: find an idea that has already been discarded by others for some reason. Instead of looking for what no one has thought of, "find something that everyone's thought of and thinks is bad."
- Instacart is the classic example of an idea that sat in the discard bin.

## Why copying funded companies is not validation

Dalton speaks from 12 years of funding companies at YC.

- Founders interviewing at YC would say a company from two batches earlier doing the same thing "validates the market."
- His reaction: he funded that company, and he did not fund it for that idea. It pivoted during the batch. "There's no signal here."
- Reading the tea leaves of what launches in YC batches is "not a marketplace of picking ideas that have been de-risked."
- Seeing that something raised money at a billion-dollar valuation does not de-risk your idea either.
- Deciding what to spend the next 10 to 20 years on by looking at a list of what others are working on is a weak basis for a decision.

## Move away from consensus, including the VC content you consume

- Non-consensus is the point. Move away from the consensus of startup founders, investors, and friends and family.
- Many successful YC companies had every friend and family member think the idea was bad (the Airbnb parents story came up in the conversation).
- Dalton's examples of deeply weird starts: OpenAI began as a non-profit research lab during the rise of SaaS and had no real product for a long time. Anthropic was "super weird," and a lot of its early money came from FTX. Both are now consensus winners, but they began as "the definition of non-consensus things."
- His caution: these are not recipes. Copying what worked for others will not give you a truly unique idea.
- VC content (including his own podcasts) is part of the problem. He says they have tried not to tell founders what to think, only to tell stories and give advice, like office hours for a wider group. But "if you're making all of your life decisions based on podcasts, that is a definition of coming up with ideas that are not unique."
- Before TechCrunch, startups had almost no coverage. Early-2000s founders had no VC content to anchor on.

## Big-TAM thinking filters out the best ideas

- A common talking point is that only $100 billion or trillion-dollar outcomes matter. Dalton says this kills truly unique ideas, since they would never fit that framework.
- Whatnot, one of his better YC investments, is "a marketplace for authenticated Funko Pops." He asks "What's the TAM on that?" and says you would discard it.
- Applying late-stage investor advice to idea selection means only a small set of ideas pass, and those end up "extremely derivative repetitive ideas."

## Weird people have an easier time generating unique ideas

- Non-conformist or quirky people constantly generate strange ideas and do not need to ask permission. Society tells them to stop, but they tend to do well in startups because they have no trouble with unique ideas. Their bigger problem is deciding which idea is worth their time.
- Systems are "consensus machines." People good at calibrating and triangulating consensus often find it hardest to come up with unique ideas, because it has been trained out of them.
- Y Combinator itself is his example. Most fund founders have investment careers and MBAs. Paul Graham built a Lisp website, got rich, and made up a summer program for young founders from first principles. He had no investor training, and the normal VCs now look up to him.

## If you can't come up with unique ideas

- Dalton says it is "totally okay to go work somewhere." Not everyone has to be "a weird shaman that comes up with strange ideas," and society would not work if everyone were.
- You can also join a founder who is weird and let them come up with the idea.
- If you do start something, brace for it: working on something many people think is a bad idea is "100% the experience of everyone that's succeeded."

## What he looks for as an investor, and his stance on judging ideas

- He is excited when a founder seems willing to stay for a couple of years. When he gets the vibe that a founder will move on if it doesn't work in a couple of months, that is a turn-off. His phrase: "you're 10% in, but you want me to be 100% in?"
- Don't seek approval from authority figures, including the hosts: if your filter is whether the people in these videos would approve of your idea, "we're saying no."
- He says they regularly funded ideas and worked with founders whose ideas they thought were bad. He compares his job to a doctor treating whoever walks in: "who am I to know whether this is going to work?"
