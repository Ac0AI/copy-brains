## Orkut and the limits of critical mass

- Dalton uses Google's Orkut as the cautionary tale. It launched invite-only a few months before TheFacebook, and an invite was a hot commodity in Silicon Valley the day it launched. He was one of the first few thousand users.
- Within months the community changed. As it grew, he got several new friend requests a day written in Portuguese, and many early users, including him, stopped using it.
- US traffic and usage "dropped like a rock", and Orkut became an embarrassment to Google. He thinks Google stayed out of social networking for as long as it did because of how poorly Orkut fared. He wonders whether Facebook would exist in its current form if Google had managed its social strategy differently.
- His takeaway: the conventional wisdom that more users always makes a community more valuable is wrong in this case.

## Critical mass and the "growth above all else" dogma

- Dalton agrees an online community needs critical mass to be useful, and calls it a high bar. Most consumer internet startups fail because no one cares about their product, leaving a ghost town.
- He sees "grow your userbase as fast as possible at all costs" as VC dogma for consumer internet. He understands the reasoning but says "dogma is still dogma."
- One reason for the dogma is the assumption that consumer internet companies are free and ad-supported. A free service that can't reach a large enough userbase can't sustain itself on advertising.
- He calls this reasoning circular: a free service must scale because it is ad-supported, and it is ad-supported because it is free.

## The "Suggested Users" mirage: big follower counts are not value

- He knows a reporter with 429,000 Facebook subscribers. The comments and likes on her news posts were poor, which suggests the followers were passive, scared to participate, or not seeing her posts.
- He suspects Facebook pointed a ton of random users at high-profile influencers, and that those users were "profoundly confused/looking for a date". He questions whether that is the value advertisers and Wall Street want.
- He had a similar experience on Google+, where he was added to over 5,000 Circles within weeks of launch. When he asked why, the responses suggested people had no idea who he was or how he ended up in their stream.
- He calls such big numbers "a superficial numbers-game" rather than quality.
- He compares social networking tech press to The Three Stooges, with Facebook, Google+ and Twitter slapping each other with growth and engagement numbers. The discourse centers on who can build the most profitable-looking vanity metrics for Wall Street and advertisers.

## Anti-network effects

- Dalton defines anti-network effects as what happens when a community that has already reached critical mass starts to lose value with each additional signup.
- The cause is that the core community that created the value in the first place gets marginalized and leaves.
- His example: Quora is more valuable than Yahoo Answers, but could easily become Yahoo Answers if, in pursuit of "network effects", it diluted community quality. The most interesting and value-adding users would then vacate.
- He notes the Quora team is aware of this risk and is apparently steering to avoid it.

## Twitter's blueprint: asymmetric follows plus a global feed

- Dalton calls Twitter's growth model a good blueprint for reaching critical mass and then scaling globally.
- Step 1: launch with a small, dedicated group of interesting people who can follow each other asymmetrically.
- Step 2: add a global feed of all content posted. This makes you feel like a member of an interesting, vibrant community.
- Step 3: as the site scales, early users depend less on the global feed and use their own following list to turn the amount of information up or down.
- The asymmetric follow model also avoids the strange behavior on Orkut, Facebook and Google+. Strangers can follow or @-reply, but it doesn't feel like they are "putting" troubling messages on your content.
- He says Pinterest and Instagram followed the same blueprint, going from a small critical mass of interesting people to a massive global community. Both were fun and useful for early adopters at a small userbase and kept their community mostly solid through massive growth.

## App.net: picking early adopters and treating social as infrastructure

- Dalton likes that the backers of join.app.net overlap heavily with early adopters of Quora and Instagram. What they share is an interest in quality, novel approaches to old problems, the business models of third-party "indie" developers, and a willingness to be part of something "new and strange".
- His belief is that a critical mass of users and developers can take the basic plumbing of the API and webapp and build a vibrant social ecosystem.
- He wants the official webapp to be a proof of concept that the API is sound, and a bootstrapping medium for novel integrations built by others. He points out that Twitter community members, not employees, invented hashtags and retweeting.
- He says Twitter had the chance to abandon its official apps and let the entire service be the API, and instead chose to control and destroy its ecosystem in the name of advertising. He frames App.net as a do-over with a radically different business model that aligns user and financial incentives.
- His historical argument: infrastructure innovations such as electricity, cellular service and the Internet are judged by the long-term value enabled and circulated through their ecosystem, not the value corporations directly captured. If social platforms are as important as many believe, they should behave more like infrastructure and less like the entertainment industry.
