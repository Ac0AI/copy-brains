## Investors can't fix your company, and some founders don't want to hear it

- Dalton says telling founders this is "like telling them Santa Claus isn't real."
- Founders get time with him and ask what to do because no one wants their product. His honest answer is often "I have no idea."
- As a founder he believed that once he raised from top investors, they would hand over the secrets the world was hiding. He says that belief was wrong.
- Many investors have never been founders, so they haven't "been humbled by having a crappy startup" the way he and Michael were. It is easy for them to think their advice can make companies work, as if they were the magician.
- Over the years he has seen YC founders follow such advice and make very common errors. He can trace the mistake back to the type of person who gave the advice.

## The finance-background investor: "when all you have is a hammer"

- Dalton describes this investor as someone who has never run or operated inside a company.
- If what you know is money, the solution usually involves money: raise more, spend more, "throw money at the problem."
- Example: being asked pre-product-market-fit for five-year financial projections. He says his reaction was "what's a financial projection?"
- Financial projections can make sense at a later stage. Pre-PMF, the real problem is that the company makes no money and spends money every month.
- He says the pattern shows up as founders who treat product as an afterthought and do financial engineering instead.

## The big-company exec investor

- People with big-tech experience are great at taking a successful product and scaling or refining it.
- Being employee 100 or 200 at a big company is a very different job from being employee 5. Scaling a product looks like scaling a process, such as hiring.
- That mindset can take for granted that getting any users at all is hard. Failure for many YC companies means getting zero real customers, and a big-company PM mindset doesn't consider that you can fail that badly.
- Their typical advice is to hire executives before product-market fit, such as a marketing exec because "marketing doesn't work" or a VP of engineering because the team isn't productive.
- Pre-PMF the mistake is more often the wrong person in the company, not a missing department. Examples are an engineer no one wants to work with, or no one measuring marketing results.
- After product-market fit, hiring more people becomes essential.

## The non-tech entrepreneur, the junior investor and the influencer

**Non-tech entrepreneur**
- Someone who made money in real estate, strip malls or franchises may treat a startup like a franchise store. They ask for crazy terms and more control, and stress that you'll lose their money.
- Dalton says you can't be mad at them. They are applying principles that worked for them in a different industry, and software companies look different from most other companies.

**Junior investor**
- His mental model is to put yourself in their shoes. New investors aren't in a position of power, and a few bad early investments can end a career.
- Their KPI is that you raise a follow-on round at a higher valuation so they can mark up the investment.
- So they push you to fundraise constantly and tell you everything is working, that you have product-market fit and should scale faster. They aren't trying to hurt you.

**Influencer or famous person**
- Influencers understand distribution because building it is their business. They treat startups like ad deals: advisor shares in exchange for promotion.
- It is tempting to think a celebrity tweet will fix distribution. In his experience, both personally and across YC, founders mostly feel they got a raw deal. The clicks were less helpful than hoped.

## The extremely young investor

- These people are sweet and exciting to talk to.
- Dalton compares them to founders who learn how to "play the part" of a founder from movies and books. These investors read what other investors say and do, then repeat the hot trend or the latest essay.

## YC partners give bad advice too

- Dalton names the common YC-partner advice: the lean-startup path of not hiring, not spending much, talking to users and shipping a rough MVP.
- Sometimes a team builds in a cave for two years without talking to a single user, and it works. The data on YC companies makes it hard to recommend that strategy.
- The best partners are careful how forcefully they give advice. Over time you build a record of times you told someone to do X and the opposite worked.
- His takeaway to Michael is that the founders who made it work believed in themselves. They took bits of outside information but took personal accountability for making it work, and didn't blindly follow anyone, YC included.

## The best investor advice Dalton ever got

- As a founder he was "swimming in his head" with too many ideas and too much data.
- Someone from the outside looked at everything and synthesized it into something simple: "this is working, you should do more of this," or the opposite, "this is bad, you're failing."
- His brain was full of excuses and a thousand threads going in different directions. Having someone boil it down was hugely valuable.
- Looking back, the founder advice that helped most, for both him and Michael, came when people were hard on them. That was more hopeful than being told everything was great.
