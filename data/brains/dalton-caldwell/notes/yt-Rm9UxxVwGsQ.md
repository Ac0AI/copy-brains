## Why there is "no secret" in YC advice

- Dalton says "the secret is there is no secret." Office hours test a founder's own thinking, like a coach who sharpens a craft the founder already has, rather than handing over hidden knowledge.
- The big trap is the "midwit" end of the bell curve. Founders overthink and stress instead of taking the simplest approach.
- The core advice still holds, but the details need updating as the ideas change. Early YC ideas were mostly web versions of desktop software, and today's are different.

## Why startup ideas have gotten narrower

- Early YC had no content machine about startups. Paul Graham's blog took off because nothing like it existed, and Dalton read Marc Andreessen's pmarca blog as a founder.
- In the 2000s, founders mostly picked ideas that resembled VC-funded ones, ideas advisors endorsed, or totally zany ones. He recalls a YC applicant with a truck that baked muffins on the way to deliver them fresh.
- Today so much startup content exists that the "aperture" of conceivable ideas is smaller. This raises the floor, because the worst ideas disappear, but it also screens out truly unusual ones.
- He says OpenAI would have been very hard to pitch the year it started, since it was a research project and not a business.
- He cites space and nuclear companies as ideas that were hard to explain a decade ago and are now legible. Fintech was also outside the aperture: banks wouldn't partner, and Bank Simple took about 3 years to launch a prepaid debit card and sold for roughly $100 million.
- The best time to invest in an idea is before it fits the aperture. Investors told him fintech was a warning story.

## Favor weird, obsessive, non-conformist founders

- Unconventional people tend to be unconventional in everything, including their ideas. His examples are Marc Andreessen, Paul Graham and Elon Musk.
- When he met Elon around 2008, Elon talked at him for 2 hours about SpaceX before it existed. Elon's opening line was "my plan is to die on Mars, hopefully not on impact."
- Career-optimizing "leveling up" types (valedictorian, elite college, MBA, fancy job) are very smart but struggle most at ideation, because everything they've done sits inside the aperture.
- Wanting recognition and praise makes it hard to come up with a truly weird idea.

## Pivot early and pick ideas by unfair advantage

- He encourages pivoting during YC: "If you're going to spend the next decade of your life on something and you're not 100% sure you're working on the right idea, it's much easier to fix it today than tomorrow." Pivoting inside a batch costs nothing, and 5 years in it is brutal.
- Founders often ask for a list of ideas. His method is to start from the founder's unfair advantages, find areas of context and advantage, and apply them to the emerging technology.
- Obvious ideas built on a new platform rarely win big. The cloud era made a CRUD app easy for everyone to build, and the iPhone App Store era rewarded Uber and DoorDash more than the obvious apps.
- For AI, the most obvious ideas, with thousands of startups doing the same thing, are unlikely to yield big outcomes. The winners will use AI to do something that wasn't possible before.
- Anyone can write code with AI, and "that is not enough." It has to combine with something inspired that delivers real value to users.

## Build the MVP: one clear feature, not many

- Paul Graham's advice to use AWS instead of racking servers sped up YC startups. Dropbox was derided as a thin wrapper on AWS, and the cloud was a competitive edge against slow legacy companies.
- "Do things that don't scale" originally meant skipping scalability work and building the pure essence of what delivers value. Google's impressive part was a search engine users loved, not its back end.
- With coding tools, features are cheap, but a product with 80 unfinished or half-finished features won't get adopted, and you may never get a second shot. A low-quality product is hard to fix by adding features.
- Examples of starting simple and widening: PostHog started with product analytics, then added rings around it. Robinhood started with free stock trading, and Coinbase with buying Bitcoin in a non-sketchy way.
- To compete with Robinhood, he would not clone its features. He would pick one distinctive feature people can explain and add more around it later.
- Customers should be able to explain back what the product is and why they want it.

## Launch now and build in public

- Most founders overestimate how much people care about their startup. Talking about what you're building is a form of marketing and community-building, and for developer tools, posting on Twitter or Hacker News is how you get early customers.
- "Most startups die not because someone copied your idea, it's just because no one ever heard that you existed."
- Posting a lot creates luck. Open source and network-effect businesses have little to fear from copying, though some easily clonable businesses might stay quiet.
- In his portfolio, he can think of only one company that doesn't want to talk much. Even then, some public presence helps recruit engineers, since a no-footprint startup with non-famous founders is hard to hire for.
- A secrecy-dependent idea worries him, because "eventually you're going to be attacked by the clone army."

## Talk to users, especially now

- Paul Graham's advice was counterintuitive in the 2000s because founders built websites and used viral flows, SEO and ad buys. Talking to users one at a time seemed unscalable, like digging a hole with a spoon when an excavator is available.
- Talking in person gives context a dashboard doesn't: nuance, body language and what users are doing.
- It's now easier to hide behind the keyboard with ads, viral loops, paid influencers and AI agents that deliver reports. That loses the nuance, so talking to users matters more.
- "Sitting down and talking to 20 or 30 people is going to give you more information about if you're on the right track than signing up 10,000 randos from Google Ads." Scale ads and viral flows later, once things are dialed in on small numbers.
- Example: his co-founder Paul built Gmail first for himself, then sat with users at the Google office until he had 100 happy users, asking what features to add next.
- Talking to users keeps the humanity in view and gives the product soul. Products built by someone who never talked to users feel like features glued together. He cites Gusto as a boring product with soul.

## Fundraising and selling the company

- Fundraising has become a status game (amount, valuation, investors) because it's legible to parents and LinkedIn. Some founders love it, others find it awkward, and YC was built for the latter by putting the process on rails.
- Pre-seed: less is better because constraints force focus. Giving a pre-idea company $5M leads to hiring and feature sprawl that doesn't help build an MVP. YC invests half a million dollars, which he calls enough for a two-person startup for 2 years with no revenue.
- Raise more only with a hard constraint, such as an AI training run, FDA approval or a banking license. Investors "neg" founders with "you want to be ambitious, right?", which he calls an effective trick.
- Seed rule of thumb in YC: stay below 15% dilution. Raising a few million for 10-15% is "a pretty good trade," and the band to avoid is something like 20 on 40.
- His new firm, Standard Capital (a Series A fund), copies the YC process:
  - Application on the website with no warm intro, and founders state their own valuation.
  - 10% dilution rounds at Series A.
  - No board seat, as with PG at YC.
  - Group office hours, an idea Paul invented. Founders learn more from respected peers doing the same thing than from a professional investor at board meetings. Groups are reshuffled yearly by traction, so the highest-traction companies end up together.
- Selling the company: the best way is to grow so fast you destroy competitors, who then want to buy you. Examples: Google bought YouTube because it was beating Google Video, and Facebook bought Instagram because it was a frightening threat. Time spent networking with corp dev doesn't help.
- If you're that successful you probably don't want to sell, like Facebook. "Be so successful that you don't need to be acquired."

## Hiring, and sleep and exercise

- Hiring: Anthropic keeps hiring engineers, so there is still incremental value in hiring across sales, marketing, growth and engineering. Dalton says to revisit when the labs stop hiring engineers.
- Middle management seems to be shrinking, because its main job of tracking work and synthesizing reports can now be done with AI.
- Sleep and exercise: burnout kills many startups, and it may be a bigger risk now. He has seen founders burn out, and says vibe coding with Claude is so fun that founders stay up all night, feeling the window is closing ("AI psychosis").
- YC opened every batch with a talk on sleep and exercise. He still stands by it.
