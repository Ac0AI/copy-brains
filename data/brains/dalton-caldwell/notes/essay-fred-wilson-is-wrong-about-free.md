## Platforms should not be ad-supported

- Dalton's core claim: platforms should not be ad-supported. He says explicitly he never argued "free" is wrong for everyone, only that it is wrong for platforms.
- He defines a Web2.0 service as one with an API that is ultimately a platform. Facebook and Twitter encourage companies to build on their APIs, and Dalton treats those APIs like an operating system (Linux), a hosting platform (AWS) or a programming platform (Ruby on Rails). Platforms let you get benefits from work done by others, which is "one of the key reasons that Web2.0 actually did meet its promise of widescale global adoption and technical innovation."
- The problem is business models. Building on a platform is "a foundational risk." If the platform dislikes what you do, or likes it enough to compete with you, it can damage your quality of service or shut you down, and "your business is dead."
- He calls this a flawed premise underneath Web2.0 and proposes a better alternative (join.app.net, the paid platform he was building at the time).

## Why the TV analogy fails

- Fred Wilson defended "free" by pointing to Pay TV. Dalton replies that TV is not a platform.
- HBO did not build its audience by offering an API for developers to extend, then acquire or destroy the developers who drove its early success.
- His point on debate: a real defense of free should address what he actually said (platforms specifically), not argue "this will never work, people have tried it, it's a waste of time."

## "Be your own bitch" and the platform-risk hedge

- Wilson had said: "Don't be a Google Bitch, don't be a Facebook Bitch, and Don't be a Twitter Bitch. Be your own Bitch." He also said Twitter had no set strategy at the start and would have launched with all platform clients if it were planned.
- Dalton notes that strategy sounds like join.app.net.
- Wilson's reply was that "be your own bitch" means building for everything, including Twitter. Dalton reads this as an admission that big platforms treat their partners as their "bitch."
- Under that view, the developer's best defense is to hedge by playing platforms against each other, and hope to be acquired (Twitter buying Tweetie) before being put out of business.
- Dalton wants a world where no developer is anyone's "bitch." His goal is a platform whose core values and business model mean no third-party developer needs to hedge against the platform turning on them.

## Why media business models trap platforms

- Dalton spent most of his 20s building a large, ultimately unsuccessful digital music business (imeem). His takeaway: the people in the music industry were essentially good, but the control- and scarcity-based business model was "intractably flawed."
- Digital music startup innovations get crushed because labels have a fundamental financial incentive to do so.
- He applies the same view to most media business models: "Attempting to create a huge platform business that is at its core about controlling and monetizing 'bits' is a fool's errand."
- He argues Twitter treats its content (tweets, the social graph, the "intention stream") as its core asset. Once a company adopts a "let's control our content" mindset, "no matter how fundamentally good your employees, management team, or board members are, you are screwed."

## Open vs closed, and services vs content

- Wilson implied a paid model is more "closed" than an ad-supported "open" one. Dalton says he is "dead wrong."
- Media models depend on control of content. The paid proposal depends on providing a service to users and developers.
- In his model, monetizing around other people's content is not the reason the company exists. Content passing through is a side effect of a useful service: "content is not king – it's just bits passing through our system, at the behest of our customers."
- His thesis: digital services are valuable and easy to monetize, digital content is not.
- Frame to use: treat Twitter and Facebook as communications platforms, not media/entertainment sites. Seen that way, their ad-supported business models are on "the wrong side of history."
