## What Dalton wants founders to do after a Series A

Dalton frames the Series A as a starting line, not a finish line. His definition: raise it when you have product-market fit. From that point the job is to scale, grow and hire, but carefully.

- "Congratulations, you won the prize. Your award is more work."
- He compares it to getting into YC: founders expect easy street, and instead it "was like, oh, no, this is really hard." He says he levels with founders that it is only the beginning of the pain.
- The practical takeaway is to treat the round as an arbitrary checkpoint. Take it in stride, use the money and resources to reach the next level, and enjoy hard things. The game only gets harder from here.

## Hiring after the Series A: where it helps and where it hurts

Dalton's view is that hiring should accelerate something that already works.

- **Seed vs. Series A.** He does not give seed companies the same hiring advice. At seed, you may still pivot, and hiring can accelerate you in the wrong direction. At Series A, you are hopefully already moving in the right direction, so hiring helps you move faster.
- **Sales and engineering.** The typical Series A hires are salespeople (the founder has already sold successfully) and engineers added to an existing team. The aim is "adding more fuel to the fire."
- **Anti-pattern 1: hiring too fast.** He uses a rowboat image. One person climbing in won't tip it, but ten jumping in at once will. Going from 5 people to 20 can wreck culture, so ease hires in gradually.
- **Anti-pattern 2: under-hiring.** Some founders have read too many tweets about building a "decacorn with no employees." His answer: "Seems like Anthropic and OpenAI are still hiring... check back in with me when Anthropic stops hiring people."
- The goal is not to avoid hiring. The goal is to build a great company.
- **Compensation.** Startups often pay under market before the A and promise employees that things improve after it. At the A stage, it is appropriate to move closer to market pay.

## Founders should do the job first, then hire someone to replace themselves

This is a core rule at Standard Capital.

- Hire into roles where you already know how the work is done. A common failure is a founder dropping someone whose whole career was at a big company into a startup as, say, VP of sales, when the founder has never done sales.
- If you have done the job successfully and the new hire is failing, you can coach them or let them go. If you have never done it, you cannot tell whether the job is simply hard.
- This is why Dalton wants at least one technical founder. It is easy for engineers to "snow" non-technical people, and you cannot manage what you do not understand.
- His advice: understand every level of your startup and be able to do the work at every level, because debugging is far easier when you do.

## Setting 6, 12 and 18 month goals after the A

After investing at the Series A, Standard Capital asks each company for 6-month, 12-month and 18-month goals.

- Dalton's main point: "To me, the most important thing is just having the goal." For many founders the goal was to get to the Series A, so afterward the question is "okay, you made it. Now what?"
- A typical 12-month goal for portfolio companies is going from roughly $2M ARR to $10M. He calls $10M "the magic number."
- Helping founders visualize what it takes to reach $10M means asking which tactics can actually get there. He notes it is hard to reach $10M ARR buying Facebook ads, and that some seed-stage tactics will not carry you through.
- Goals should be ambitious but achievable. You should be able to work backward from the goal to today and name the steps along the way.
- He says the mistake of slowing down after raising is "exactly wrong."

## Runway and burn: spend only when you have a channel that pays back

Dalton's position is that it depends, but with a clear test.

- **If you have a very effective growth channel you can spend into, spend aggressively.** His example: a startup finds that salespeople each carry a quota and pay for themselves in about six months. In that case, do it a lot, don't be shy about runway, and keep tracking that it works.
- **If you don't have such a channel, it is often smart to bank the money** rather than burn much of it yet.
- He recalls a YC company whose advice to the batch was "only burn the prior round": when you raise a seed, burn your pre-seed; when you raise an A, burn your seed. You then always have the last round in the bank, which means you always have leverage.
- He concedes this is not universal. Hard-tech companies "kind of got to burn the money," and some great companies have come within weeks of running out.
- The real question is "what are you getting for the burn?" Bringing in high-value customers is good burn. He has been disappointed by investments that incinerated money with nothing to show for it.

## Why Dalton, as an investor, tells founders not to burn everything fast

Dalton describes a pattern he saw as a seed investor and YC partner.

- He often heard that a founder's Series A or B board member pushed them to burn all the money within a year. He allows that this may have been garbled in retelling, or that those investors had reasons he did not know.
- His worry was about alignment. The investor's incentive can be that the company either works, raises the next round and gets a markup, or dies fast so they can leave the board and make another investment. The founder has their whole livelihood in it, and the investor has no skin in that game.
- As a YC partner he found himself disagreeing with later-stage investors' advice and telling founders: "Maybe don't do that... only start burning once you really know it works."
- He tells founders directly that he is an investor and does not want them to burn all the money. Standard Capital is banking on the long-term success of the company.
- He says this was easier to reason about in the era of physical hardware: Google raised to buy servers, the servers earned ad revenue, and that funded more servers. Once everything became software, it felt easier to lose sight of where the money goes.

## Avoid building a startup on the greater fool theory

Dalton's rule: "Never run a startup that is implicitly betting on the greater fool theory."

- Flip the assumption. Your most foolish investors should be your earliest ones, and each later round should be smarter. If your late-stage investors look like geniuses, you are on the right track.
- His test for founders is to imagine a smarter person investing in the next round. That person is unlikely to be excited by "we blew all the money on the Super Bowl ad." They would respond to "we bought really valuable assets" or "we did something very effective with the money."
- The alternative is "just hoping there's some other schmuck that will continue to invest in the company."
- **Theranos.** He notes most Silicon Valley investors steered clear because when asked how it worked, the answer was "it's secret." The backers were people sophisticated in politics, not in investing in technology companies. He also recalls privately describing another company years earlier as "Theranos, but real," and that company turned out to be fraudulent too.
- **Fake graphs.** He agrees that selling a dollar for 50 cents makes growth easy, and that you can produce those charts without having a real business. He links this to the Uber-era habit of investors pressing startups to burn faster.
