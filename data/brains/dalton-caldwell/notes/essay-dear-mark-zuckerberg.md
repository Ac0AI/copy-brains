## Platform risk and the Facebook M&A "formula" (June 13, 2012 meeting)

- Dalton was building an iOS app and service on the Facebook Platform and Open Graph. Facebook developer-relations staff had reassured him it was an interesting, valuable use of the platform.
- He went into a meeting at Facebook HQ (Menlo Park, 4:30 p.m., June 13, 2012) hoping for executive-level support for his launch. Present were a VP Engineering & Products, a VP Partnerships, a VP Corporate & Business Development, and the Director of Developer Relations/Open Graph.
- Facebook's executives told him his product competed with their newly announced App Center. They said they would hate to compete with his "interesting product" and wanted to acquire his company to help build App Center.
- He refused an acqui-hire. He said he would only entertain a serious conversation about acquiring his team and product, and that otherwise he would "rather reboot my company" than see the product shut down and join Facebook.
- The platform executive did not defend his position. He explained that new ad units gave him responsibility for over $1B/year in ad revenue, and that Dalton's product's success would be an impediment to those revenue goals. Offering an acquisition was framed as a kind gesture.
- Dalton's lesson: building on an "open platform" owned by a company with conflicting ad-revenue goals carries huge platform risk. He concluded he got what he deserved for taking "this foolhardy risk."

## Intimidation as negotiation

- Dalton says other startup founders and Facebook employees told him his experience was not isolated and was part of a systematic M&A "formula."
- He distinguishes being "good negotiators" from implying you will destroy someone's business built on your "open platform." He says these are "not the same thing."
- He draws on years of experience with intimidation-based negotiation tactics from dealing with the music industry (his imeem experience), and calls bad-faith negotiations "inexcusable."
- After the meeting he gave direct feedback about how unhappy he was to one of Facebook's executives, who apologized and said he would take it under consideration.

## Why he vowed to stop building on ad-supported platforms

- He resolved "to never write another line of code" for ad-supported "platforms" like Facebook or Twitter. He says he had taken the Facebook risk because the Twitter platform was "even more of a joke."
- His motivation: he wants to build quality social software that does not force users to re-create their friends list or avoid OAuth, and that required enduring platform risk on someone else's network.
- He does not think the people at Facebook or Twitter are bad. He argues employees watching a falling stock price "freak out," and that both companies have shown they will mess with users and third-party developer ecosystems in the name of ad revenue.
- His prediction: once a company starts down the slippery slope of messing with developers and users, he has no confidence it will stop.

## Misaligned incentives between platforms, users and developers

- He argues Facebook built a business whose financial motivations are not in line with users and developers.
- Facebook once called itself a "social utility." Dalton notes the company had stopped using that term, and implies the reason is the conflict between that promise and its ad-driven model.
- He ends by saying the public markets and employees may give Facebook time to fix the "obvious structural flaws" in its platform business, but that those flaws are structural.

## What he thinks replaces them: platforms as infrastructure

- He believes future social platforms will behave more like infrastructure and less like media companies.
- He expects a number of smaller, interoperable social platforms with clear, sustainable business models to usurp Facebook and Twitter. These companies would be valued at "a small fraction" of what Facebook and Twitter are, and he is fine with that.
- His principle: "Platforms are judged by the value generated by their ecosystem, not by the value the platforms directly capture."
- He says the change will happen even if his own project (App.net, the paid, ad-free alternative he was launching) is not the mechanism that triggers it.
