## Twitter's fork: API platform vs. ad business

Dalton remembers early Twitter as having almost no traffic, with a global firehose on the front page that was "mostly employees and their friends talking to each other." Once it gained traction, he decided Twitter was "the Best Thing Ever" and told reporters and investors it was the most exciting company in the valley.

- His reason: Twitter's API made it a real-time protocol connecting services in a new way.
- He debated tech friends on whether Twitter could become "one of the fundamental building blocks of the Internet" through that API.

## The two camps inside Twitter

Dalton understands that a deeply divisive internal debate happened as Twitter gained traction.

- **API camp:** build the whole business around the realtime API. Twitter would have become a realtime cloud API company.
- **Ad camp:** copy Google's advertising model and build its own version of AdWords.
- The advertising group won, and many of the open-API people left.

He sees the ad camp's reasoning but calls the outcome "a tragic mistake."

## Why an ad business forces a closed platform

Dalton argues the ad strategy has a logical chain of consequences:

- An advertising/media business needs to own all the screen real-estate users see.
- The next step is to kill third-party clients.
- Then lock down the data in the global firehose so the company controls the "content."

In his view, the business model dictates the platform's openness, and an ad model pushes toward closure.

## The upside he believes was forfeited

Dalton grants that today's Twitter is cool and powerful, but says "that doesn't mean that it couldn't have been much, much more."

- Claim: an API-centric Twitter "could have enabled an ecosystem far more powerful than what Facebook is today."
- He acknowledges the counterargument that the API model might never have worked and Twitter might not be alive today. His response is to ask whether today's Twitter is really the early Twitter living up to its full potential.
- He questions whether all the engineering, product and design man-hours and "hundreds of millions of VC dollars" should have produced the product users see now.

## Developer trust as a cost of the ad model

Dalton points to concrete symptoms that make him wish the pro-API side had won:

- A K-Mart ad appearing in his feed.
- "Wonky behavior in the official clients."
- Twitter "drop[ping] another bomb on their developer ecosystem."

The recurring pattern he flags is a platform that repeatedly changes terms on the developers who built on it.
