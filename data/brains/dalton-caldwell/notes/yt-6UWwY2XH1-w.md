## Why most startups die from giving up, not from competitors

- Dalton says most startups die of suicide rather than homicide: running out of hope is what makes founders quit.
- His logic: if you give up, there is a 100% chance you will not succeed.
- He says the natural state of startups is that they are failing.
- He warns of a "dysmorphia" where everyone else's startup seems to be going better than yours. It isn't.
- If it doesn't feel like it's working, you are probably right, and that is what you should expect.
- Part of his aim in this talk is to help founders feel fired up, and to back that up with logic rather than just adding fuel.

## Successful companies usually took longer than founders think

- Founders get in their heads that successful companies succeed quickly, because their examples come from Twitter or TechCrunch.
- Dalton suggests using AI to get the real facts. List 10 companies you like, or the 10 apps on your phone, and ask how long each took to do the thing it is known for.
- He says some will have taken years to figure out their business.
- What he tries to comfort founders with: there are many roads to success, and most are longer than you think.
- If it's year one and you are not Cursor yet, you don't have enough evidence to know whether it will work.

## Don't repeat year one over and over

- A common failure Dalton sees: founders repeat year one repeatedly. They pivot or start something new, but never get past year one and never actually learn something or build a good product.
- Quitting or switching because it isn't working quickly enough means you never reach the point of knowing.
- Legitimate reasons to stop: you are literally out of money, or your health is suffering.
- If the reason is "why aren't we taking over the world yet?" or embarrassment that expectations aren't met, he says to reframe your thinking.
- He says to give yourself enough surface area to be lucky.

## Be careful with the voice in your head

- Don't be your own worst critic. Negative self-talk about your startup is self-defeating.
- He frames giving up early as validating the bad kind of authority figures: "Don't you want to stick it to the man?"
- He notes that this period is so common it has a name, the "trough of sorrow". Being named means almost everyone goes through it, which is a sign you are not alone.
- Founders usually only admit how bad it was after they succeed. Investors don't advertise their bad investments either, so you rarely hear it.

## What the bad stretch looks like, and why advice won't fix it

- Dalton describes the typical state: you work hard but aren't sure what to build, and ideas shift every week ("what if we added agents?", "let's put in an MCP").
- You read tweets and tech press about everyone else doing better, which makes you want to pivot to copy what's working.
- You are probably not talking to your users.
- People in this state want authority figures to make them feel better.
- In office hours, founders would ask, "Dalton, what should we pivot to?" and he would answer, "I don't know, man."
- His advice: learn to enjoy the process, realize it's normal, and dig deep. The answer comes from talking to users and shipping product, not from getting into a bad mental state.
