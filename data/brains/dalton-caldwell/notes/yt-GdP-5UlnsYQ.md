## Why Dalton started Standard Capital

- He gives pivot advice by asking where a founder has the most unfair advantages and the best network. He believes "every person has a perfect idea for them" and that a partner's job is to pull it out of the founder.
- Standard Capital is his own version of that idea, designed for the founding team of Dalton, Paul Buchheit (PB) and Brian Berg.
- The thesis is to take what makes YC work at the seed stage and apply it to the Series A.
- His reasoning is that seed rounds changed completely over 20 years (the safe, a deep angel ecosystem, YC), while Series A has not changed since the 1980s.

## The Series A process he is trying to replace

- When he raised a seed round 20 years ago, he needed a lead investor and a priced-round term sheet. He sold 25% of the company, gave up a board seat, and it took seven months. He calls getting it done "a miracle."
- His Series A was the same. He warmed up intros, kept investors in a CRM and went on coffee dates.
- Investors would spend weeks or months on him and say they were "definitely coming in," then pass at the very end, often silently.
- The fundraising meant he wasn't working on his startup. Paul Graham was the only one honest about it: do it in as little time as possible.
- Founders raising an A today describe the same experience.
- Decoding VC-speak is "so much of our job at YC":
  - Anything other than a clear yes is a no.
  - A long email full of words is a no.
  - He compares it to "a plate full of garnishes and just a no in the middle."
- He is sympathetic to VCs. They have to raise money from their own investors, so doing something different is the riskiest thing they can do.
- His diagnosis of the classic Series A investor: the business is relationship- and reputation-based. They invest where trusted people tell them to.

## Standard Capital's product: apply, standard terms, you set the price

1. **A real application.** Anyone in the world can apply on a website, with no warm intro. It should take an hour or two, like a YC application. The point is to keep the founder's time cost and downside minimal. No one knows you applied, and the upside is getting money fast.
2. **A standard Series A term sheet, public on the website.**
   - That is where the name comes from.
   - Like the YC safe, anyone can read the terms before applying.
   - This replaces the usual pattern where the real terms show up at the end of the process, when the founder has no leverage.
3. **A fixed 10% for the lead check, with the founder naming the amount.** The application is a text box, for example "$6M for 10%." He says valuation has no equation: "Everyone just makes up numbers," and negotiation is like haggling at a flea market. This mirrors YC demo day, where founders set their cap and investors are in or out.
4. **A promised turnaround.**
   - The site will tell applicants how many days until a firm yes or no.
   - He admits this is hard to promise (vacations, hot deals to chase).
   - He argues a clear answer, even a no, beats being stretched out with "one more meeting" and "the partner meeting."
- He is explicit that he designed the process to be intuitive for YC founders.
- He also dislikes decks. Founders can spend a month on one and it is often still weak, and it is not the job. YC applications are all in one format, which makes them easy to read.

## Software at the center of the fund

- His view is that YC was "a software company created by a person that loved software" and didn't love a lot of human process. Whenever there was a problem, PG built software for it, and much of YC's software traces back to that.
- Brian Berg ran a company with Dalton for over a decade, and Dalton introduced him to Patrick Collison when he started at YC. Brian became employee 70 at Stripe and has been an engineer there since. He is the software person on the Standard Capital team.
- Dalton says it was unusual to build software into an investing firm. A pitch deck in an inbox is not software-centric.
- Standard Capital is using AI in every decision it makes. He describes the firm as treating itself as "guinea pigs" to build an organization of the future and to be self-similar with its portfolio companies.
- He describes the launch as an MVP to improve over time, in the spirit of the YC goal of earning its 7% each year.

## No board seats, and a peer group instead

- Standard Capital will not take board seats. His reasoning:
  - Founders don't want someone who might fire them.
  - It is awkward to ask for money from someone who might fire you.
  - There is no time to get to know them or pressure-test the relationship first.
- Instead, it will run quarterly subgroups, like YC group office hours.
  - Founders present their company metrics, board-update-style, and their biggest problems to each other.
  - He calls it a "group therapy thing."
- His argument: founders respect other operators in the trenches more than investors repeating stories they heard elsewhere. His example is Zuckerberg, who could call anyone. He has founders like Tony from DoorDash, Brian from Airbnb and Drew from Dropbox as directors. Dalton says that is who Zuck consults on running Facebook and on AI strategy, all YC people.
- The bar to join the peer group is product-market fit among AI builders. He says that is rare, since most batches mix companies at very different stages.
- He thinks investors overvalue both the money and their own advice. Standard Capital is built so value comes from the peer group instead. It will give advice, but "the value prop is not we're the best advice givers," and the biggest check does not always win.

## Non-zero-sum thinking about startups

- He holds that PG's thinking is deeply anti-zero-sum. The zero-sum view is that only one good startup begins each year, and you are bad if you miss it.
- When YC started, many people predicted it would fund the worst startups, "a baseball team with all the worst baseball players."
- Dalton says PG was right that wealth and growth are not fixed and feed on each other. Many early YC companies, such as Twitch and Reddit, would not exist otherwise.
- His thesis is that there will be many more big companies, not one or two a year. Standard Capital funding more great founders creates more abundance and does not hurt other VC firms.
- He calls the zero-sum mindset common in the industry and "disappointing." Positive-sum thinking is "why tech exists."
- He notes that AI may lower how much capital companies need. His example is a profitable company at $8M in revenue wondering whether to raise at all.
