## Low ambition is the secret killer of startups

- Dalton frames the topic as "the thing that I believe is the secret killer of startups. And it's not what you expect. It is low ambition."
- His basis is that he and his co-host work with a lot of startups, and he says he has seen what happens when a company has low ambition.
- He calls the failure mode counterintuitive. Founders hear how hard startups are and how few succeed, so they try to raise their odds by aiming lower. A small startup seems easier than "the next Google, which sounds very hard."
- He says the evidence suggests that is not the case.

## Ambitious ideas often come from curiosity, not insider access

- Dalton argues that most people watching can be curious about an area that "seems perhaps far-fetched, but if it went the right way could change the world."
- He says this is less hard than it sounds:
  - Ten years ago AI was not a buzzword, but working out who the leading AI researchers and the leading papers were was only a question of curiosity.
  - Understanding how broken NASA was is described the same way.
- He says the right area might not be in the zeitgeist, and that this "might actually be a good thing" because it could be the opportunity.
- His contrast: "building the 17th AI SDR might raise you your $2 million seed round", but that is not the same as picking something that could matter.

## Ambition is for venture-backed companies

- Dalton's framing is for companies raising venture capital. A low-ambition idea can still be funded in a hot market, so funding is a poor way to judge whether an idea is ambitious.
- Ambition here means a mission that could change the world, not a small optimization.
