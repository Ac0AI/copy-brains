## AOL vs. the music industry: two ways to defend a dying cash cow

Dalton compares AOL's dial-up access business with the recorded music industry. Both were among the most lucrative businesses ever and both peaked in the late 90s. AOL peaked at 35 million subscribers, each paying a monthly fee. The music industry peaked grossing $20B annually from CDs.

He rejects the usual straw man that the music industry failed to embrace new technology-based business models and "missed the boat". The real difference is how each handled risk.

## AOL's strategy: make longshot bets and keep the risk on management

Dalton says AOL management knew the legacy "access" business would shrink every year until it reached zero. They used what was left of that cash cow to swing for the fences:

- Bought Bebo.
- Hired Tim Armstrong to build a next-generation advertising business.
- Bought The Huffington Post.
- Put a great deal of money into Patch.

He notes these bold moves drew more criticism than praise from investors and press, because they "could easily fail". His read: "The jury is still out." The bets keep the risk and reward on management's shoulders.

## The music industry's strategy: license, don't build

With a few exceptions, the music industry joins new business models through licensing relationships rather than directly creating and marketing products to consumers.

- Dalton describes the implicit philosophy of music licensing as "every deal is wired for success".
- A typical licensing deal is built in a way "that would make a Goldman Sachs partner smile".
- Deal terms include minimum payments, upfront payments, and greater-of calculations between 3 different variables.
- He says he once heard that labels now ask for, and get, "non-dilutable" equity in startups.

## Beyond Oblivion as the ideal licensing outcome

Dalton calls Beyond Oblivion the case study for the most successful music licensing deal ever done:

- It paid out a great deal of up-front money to work out licensing deals, then promptly shut down.
- The service never launched, so none of the alleged non-refundable payments were tied to music usage, and no money went to artists.
- He compares this to breakage.

## Why labels shouldn't care if Spotify fails

Dalton disagrees with the claim that labels want Spotify to fail. If the deals are constructed properly, labels "should have no real preference" whether Spotify fails.

- "If every deal is wired, it doesn't matter how successful any individual license-holder is."
- What matters is that more deals remain to be done and that new licensees keep arriving willing to pay cash.
- His analogy: the industry cares which music service "wins" about as much as ExxonMobil cares which gas station chain wins.

## Dalton's takeaway: financial engineering vs. execution risk

He sums up the contrast:

- AOL made longshot bets that keep the risk and rewards on management's shoulders.
- The music industry used financial engineering to push product and execution risk as far from its core business as possible.

On which is better, he says it depends on what outcome you are optimizing for.
