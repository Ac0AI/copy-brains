## Goals before product-market fit: measure love, not growth graphs

Dalton argues that the tools and habits from post-PMF companies (Meta-style analytics, AB tests, weekly growth targets) have no relation to tracking progress in an early-stage company. His point is that graphs are a waste of time when you have zero users.

- With three users who all churned, he says you effectively have zero users, so weekly growth is the wrong thing to talk about.
- Founders who learned analytics at big companies sometimes have to unlearn "all this crap" first.
- He notes that growing 7% week over week is the result of doing many things right, but founders treat it as the cause of the company working. Mixing those up "really screw[s] you up".
- His advice: "Don't even worry about graphs and charts and weekly growth. Just get something that someone loves."

## Founders cheat metrics, and it only hurts them

Dalton describes a pattern where founders pick metrics that let them hit a target without reflecting reality.

- Example: using cumulative users who ever signed up instead of daily active users, ignoring whether anyone actually uses the product.
- He calls this Goodhart's law in practice (founders "doctor the metric so that they hit it"), and says "all you're doing is screwing yourself."
- He asks founders a gotcha question: to get value out of the product in a week, how much time should a user spend in it? Then: how much time are they actually spending?
- The answers he expects are "we're not measuring" or "an hour a week" when they claimed an hour a day. He then asks whether the product is helping users in their business or goals at all.
- Following that line of questioning usually ends at "we actually have zero users", and he says that is fine: "I'm glad we had that little chat."

## Build something a few people love (the Gmail example)

Dalton says "it's almost impossible" for something a hundred people love to not be growing. Either the love is exaggerated or nobody is looking at the growth.

- He cites Paul Buchheit's Gmail as the model. Paul built it for himself and was the first user.
- He then gave it to people inside Google, adding only one or two a week and asking what to add.
- It stayed under 100 users for months, because he wanted people to love it. By the time it was in beta, hundreds of people "ran their life off it."
- Dalton's takeaway: "This is how you build great products." The most popular consumer product in the world did not start by chasing a hockey-stick graph.
- He admits it sometimes takes time to get someone to love a product, and time to work out what to build.

## Don't build your startup for investors

Dalton pushes back on the "I have to fundraise in n months and investors love graphs" mentality.

- His view: building to impress investors means your actual customer is an investor, and "nothing good will come of that."
- You can win a seed round that way if you are smart and capable, but he notes only about a 2% chance of ultimately winning even after raising a seed round, so you have just taken your odds to roughly 98% failure.
- The alternative is to spend that same time with customers, testing hypotheses and learning how to improve their businesses and lives.
- He says he has seen many founders who fundraised poorly at demo day, had about half a million dollars from YC, powered through, and a year later were doing far better than batchmates who had built for investors.
- It is also habit-forming in a bad way. A "we'll worry about investors now and talk to users after we raise" culture sticks.

## Real company stories: Stripe and early customer obsession

Dalton uses Stripe as the counterexample to the fast-growth narrative.

- Stripe is a company that YC talks about a lot. It is a hundred-billion-dollar company that didn't launch publicly for over two years.
- It was invite-only and beta-only, built first for the founders' own side product, with customers added very slowly.
- The Collison installation: they would sit down at your computer and help you implement Stripe into your payments flow.
- Dalton was an early Stripe customer at his own startup (among the first couple hundred customers). Patrick Collison messaged him on Google Talk nearly daily asking how it was going, which made him feel like an important customer who could reach the CEO directly.
- He calls that closeness a superpower founders don't talk about.
- His challenge: use AI to research how your top 10 favorite companies actually started. How long until launch, until 100 users, until $1M in revenue? "You're going to be shocked" that the real stories differ from the pop version.
- He says the fastest-to-$100M graphs are not the real race. The goal is enduring value and enduring businesses, not flash-in-the-pan growth.

## Dalton's goal ladder for pre-PMF companies

Dalton's answer to "how do I set goals before PMF" is the progression used at YC, based on counts of users who love the product.

1. Do you have more than zero users?
2. Do you have one real user, with no asterisks?
3. Do you have 10?
4. Do you have 50?
5. Do you have 100?

- If you can get 100 users or customers who love you, "that is an awesome goal," and that is the point where he'd start graduating to graphs.
- He tells founders to be honest with themselves and use the word "love": how many users love my product? If none, what would cause them to?
- Staring at graphs all day is dehumanizing to your users. If you aren't thinking about the people you serve and whether they love you, "you're hurting yourself."
- On Whatnot, he says value was easy to see: sellers were making so much money that it was life-changing, an obvious sign the product provided value.
