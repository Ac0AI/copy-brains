## Info hazards are mostly about getting sucked in, and the fix is staying grounded

Dalton's view on this topic is only partly recoverable from this transcript: PB does most of the talking on feeds, the like button and the "jar of sand" analogy, so those are left out. What follows are the points that clearly come from Dalton.

## Media history: shared TV, cable bubbles, and online communities

- Dalton frames the history of info hazards as: television, then cable, then online communities, then social feeds, then LLMs.
- Early TV had two or three channels. His view is that there was not much info hazard then, because everyone consumed the same content. He jokes that "everyone got brainwashed the same."
- Cable created filter bubbles. A kid in the US watching Nickelodeon, Comedy Central and MTV shared more culture with a kid a thousand miles away watching the same channels than with neighbors watching different ones. He grew up on Nickelodeon, Comedy Central and music-video-era MTV, and says it affected his state of mind.
- His mother was afraid of this, so his house had no cable.
- Online forums let you find people just like you, which is great. But you can fool yourself into thinking niche or fringe opinions are widely held when everyone in your forum agrees. He notes that if you're watching this video, you're probably in a YouTube filter bubble that recommended it.

## Social media: it isn't real life, and it feeds you more of what you click

- Dalton still uses x.com and says he has an "okay relationship" with it and isn't overly addicted.
- His core reminder: it's not real life. You can close the window and almost nothing that happens there matters (an earthquake would be the exception).
- He tells his kids that Instagram feels real and kind of isn't. His advice is to remind yourself over and over that entertaining content is fine, but it isn't your actual life.
- Viral content skews negative. His view is that the biggest ramification of staying in feeds too long is that you get depressed, and the usual fix is to "close the window and go exercise."

## Immune systems for new media

- Dalton's mental model: when a new form of social media appears, people have no immune system, like a new virus where everyone catches it. Young people who grew up with it develop better natural immunity.
- Example: boomers on Facebook believing AI slop, versus young people who are much better at spotting it.
- On LLMs, he says we don't yet have an immune system. Nobody has defined a healthy amount of usage, and there are many shades of gray between a power user and "AI psychosis."
- Is it appropriate to ask an LLM about every micro-decision in your life? He says the jury is out.

## LLM filter bubbles and validation

- Dalton warns about the model training incentive: you train models to get a thumbs up from the user, so they affirm you. There is a fine line between wanting to talk to the model all the time and unhealthy dependence.
- His advice: be especially wary of an LLM validating big life decisions, or fantastical claims about your abilities that aren't backed by reality.
- He notes LLMs have created a new class of crackpots who believe they've solved physics.
- **Never use these tools as a mirror of yourself.** People get in trouble when they judge who they are by the reflection technology gives back, e.g. asking Claude "Who am I? Do you understand me? My friends don't get me." His reaction: "Don't do that, man."
- Have a firm idea of who you are outside of these things.

## The positive case: these tools make you smarter, if you can disconnect

- Dalton is convinced YouTube made him smarter because he watches videos that teach him things.
- He says TikTok made him smarter too. He learned about exercise, personal health, running and swimming tips, and music theory. In a parallel universe without TikTok, he thinks he'd know much less about all of it.
- The downside is spending all day on it until you "couldn't hold down a job."
- Same for LLMs: they're already making him smarter (e.g. posting a photo of something and asking questions). The test is whether they fill a crippling place in your life. His benchmark: "I can function without talking to Claude every 20 minutes."
- Practical tests and habits: can you disconnect, and can you stay away over the weekend.
- Go outdoors without earbuds. You don't have to listen to podcasts all the time either. Just walk or run.

## Staying grounded: in-person peers and real customers

- Dalton's keyword is "grounded." Staying grounded in physical reality comes from real people you know, from going outdoors, and from customers.
- Standard Capital focuses on in-person time. Its group office hours format forces founders to come in person, away from screens.
- He values real peers you know in real life, plus a Slack channel with all the founders, over only having X followers. If all the startup people you know are anonymous accounts with anime profile pictures, "you could get in a pretty weird spot."
- On customers: as a founder you can convince yourself everyone wants your product. But "when you ask people for money, they say no." He calls actually trying to sell a product a form of grounding. Claude will tell you your idea for a french fry salad is awesome, but when you try to sell it you find out for real.
