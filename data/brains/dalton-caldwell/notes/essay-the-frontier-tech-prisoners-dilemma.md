## The frontier tech prisoner's dilemma

Dalton frames new-technology launches as a prisoner's dilemma, an adjunct to the Innovator's Dilemma. Incumbents implicitly cooperate to keep a powerful, potentially disruptive technology in the lab, for various reasons. Once one participant "defects" and releases it, the others are forced to change strategy and defect too.

- His current example is AI, where one company's public release forced the incumbents to respond.
- Other examples he lists: smartphones and mobile apps before the iPhone, the Xerox PARC GUI, open source software, publishing news for free on the Internet, Napster and music streaming, and selling computers to consumers.

## How the incumbent objection sounds

Dalton says defection is seen as controversial at the time. Many agree the technology is interesting, but others, including market participants, argue it would be bad or dangerous to make it widely available.

The incumbent line he quotes is roughly: "This isn't a new thing and we think it's dumb that this defector is getting so much hype. We have been working on this forever. We just don't think it's ready."

- The pattern: incumbents claim long prior work on the technology and say the market timing is wrong.
- He links the RIM CEO's quotes about the iPhone as an example of this response.

## Critics are sometimes right, but the defector still matters

Dalton concedes the critics can be correct. Most new technologies aren't ready, and there are "innumerable historical examples of half-baked hype cycles" where the skeptics were proven right.

His argument is that this doesn't change who brings new technology to market. Whatever anyone thinks of a particular technology, the participant who defects and brings something controversial to market is the vector by which powerful new technologies are introduced to the world.

## Why waiting for consensus fails

Dalton's core point: "If we all had to wait until there was full consensus a new technology was 'ready' we would be waiting a very very long time."

- Readiness is never agreed on in advance, so someone has to ship before consensus exists.
- Incumbents with strong existing businesses have reasons to keep holding the technology back.
- The first to defect forces everyone else to move.

## Takeaways for builders

- Treat "it's not ready" from incumbents as expected behavior, not proof the opportunity is empty.
- Being the one willing to release something controversial is the position that sets the market's direction.
- Incumbent claims of having worked on it "forever" don't prevent a defector from winning attention and forcing a response.
