## The "RIP Good Times" meeting: what Dalton saw in the room

- Dalton was Founder/CEO of a Sequoia portfolio company when Sequoia emailed asking for his personal presence (not a VP) at a vaguely described mandatory meeting.
- The meeting was held on October 9, 2008, at a conference center on Sand Hill Road. Roughly 60 people attended, and it was nicely catered.
- He did not expect the deck to leak. He felt he was being briefed into proprietary knowledge.
- The later leak of the PowerPoint turned the meeting into the "RIP Good Times" cultural touchstone of modern-era Silicon Valley. He wrote the post because he thinks some of the context has been lost.

## The financial backdrop in October 2008

- The meeting took place about three weeks after Lehman declared bankruptcy on September 15, 2008, while the US financial system was "literally melting down."
- CNBC coverage was "tantamount to a deathwatch," with scared, disoriented pundits trying to work out who was exposed and who would fail.
- Failures, bailouts and back-room deals were happening in real time, over the course of days.
- After the presentation, one of the most important people in the room told him things that "made my head swim." One example was that GE Capital might default.
- He says it is hard to imagine the chain of events if GE Capital had defaulted, but suffice it to say "I don't think it would have been good."

## Rebutting the "ulterior motives" narrative

- At the time the press read ulterior motives into the deck. Some said Sequoia was trying to scare people, drive down prices, or hurt competitors.
- He calls that "bullshit." He was there, "looked in the partners' eyes," and says they weren't bluffing. They were trying to help the founders.
- Present-day commentators mock the deck as a "boy who cried wolf" moment that "ultimately did more harm than good." He rejects that framing.
- His view is that Sequoia gave portfolio CEOs a somewhat sanitized version of what it knew, plus actionable tactical instructions, and that this can only be read as good faith.

## The Cuban Missile Crisis analogy

- He compares the moment to the Cuban Missile Crisis. Most normal people were oblivious to what was actually going on.
- He believes the financial system was minutes away from a disaster at least one, possibly two, orders of magnitude worse than what actually happened.
- He is glad the presentation turned out to be wrong, just as he is glad the Cuban Missile Crisis did not become a full-blown nuclear war.
- His lesson: a warning that proves wrong in hindsight is not evidence it was a bad call or made in bad faith. Judging it by the outcome that was avoided misses the risk that was real at the time.

## Takeaways for founders on trusting investor information

- A prepared investor who convenes portfolio CEOs in person and shares sensitive knowledge, with concrete tactical guidance, is acting in the founders' interest.
- Leaders should take the information seriously even when most people around them are oblivious to the danger.
- His personal reaction shows the weight of it: after the meeting he walked to his car, closed the door, and sat in silence for ten minutes trying to catch his breath.
