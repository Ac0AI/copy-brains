## Dalton's framing: what growth doesn't solve

Dalton opens by saying the advice to "grow, grow, grow" stays, but with nuance. Growth fixes a lot: burnout, not being able to raise money, motivation.

- He is not knocking growth. His point is that fast growth does not automatically fix everything.
- The trigger for the topic is AI-related companies seeing "shocking growth" whose other problems may remain.
- At YC, the standard advice is a graph that goes up and to the right. To convince an investor, you convince them the existing trend will continue. He still thinks that is good advice.
- He wants founders who are experiencing growth to ask themselves some harder questions.

## Unit economics don't fix themselves with scale

Dalton's first question for a growing company is about unit economics.

- His thought experiment is a "free money website" that pays you a dollar for every 75 cents of revenue. It would grow reliably while losing money.
- Losing money to get something off the ground can be a sophisticated, valid strategy.
- But "just growing faster doesn't mean you're going to solve uneconomic issues."
- He says more companies die by assuming economies of scale exist, without probing whether they ever arise in their own business.
- Classic example: growth bought with heavy ad spend, with a hoped-for payback period and no retention data yet. Ad-supported growth does not mean the problems are solved.
- On CAC: he has "never seen" a company whose cost to acquire a customer stays the same if it 100x's its marketing budget.
- Unit economics assumptions made at small scale need to be reassessed at every order of magnitude of scale, because they often change.
- Internet businesses often get worse economics as they scale because of hidden costs. Customer service and fraud are the ones that bite and that you never see early.
- Once fraud rings spend time attacking you, it is a "high class problem", but it is still hard.

## Moats: fast growth is not a moat

Dalton's second question is about defensibility.

- He has seen people grow very fast with no idea how to make it defensible.
- His thought experiment: why won't a YC company in the next batch see that your thing is working and do exactly the same thing? What stops them taking your customers?
- Historical example: ad-tech style businesses that are a thin layer between many customers and a very large social media site. The large site may decide it wants a direct relationship with the customer.
- He does not demand an A+ answer all the time. His point is only that "it is not self-evident that because you are growing fast, you have a moat."
- The advice is to probe yourself and ask the hard question before a future competitor does.

## Be your own critic when things go well

Dalton argues the meta-reason to ask these questions is that no one else will.

- Investors seeing revenue go up and to the right, employees, and the press will all say "great."
- At an inflection point, "you have to be your biggest critic," because no one else will do that work.
- He contrasts the two states of mind: when things aren't going well you have to be your own biggest fan, and when things are going well you have to be your own biggest critic. They are very different.
- It is hard to play that role without becoming "deputy downer" in your own company, especially while everyone is celebrating mega valuations.

## Vision: a hockey stick doesn't give you a picture of the company

Dalton says growth does not force you to have a clear picture of what the company should become.

- He sees positive correlations between founders who have a very clear picture in their head of what the company is supposed to be and success. Others, when asked about far-future expectations, draw a blank.
- On his own work at Standard Capital: "I have a very clear picture about what I want it to be. I don't know if it'll work or not, but I can promise you, I can see it." His job is to manifest what he sees in his head.
- Example: Tony Xu of DoorDash. In office hours, when DoorDash was two months old, he could explain what it was supposed to be, and that is what it became. He kept referring to Amazon, and over the years he did it. At one point he said DoorDash was a given percentage of Amazon's daily deliveries, which was large.
- Founders with a vision have almost a philosophy: a somewhat odd explanation of what customers want and how to serve them over a long time, and they can "nerd out" about the company in depth.
- He says he truly had that experience at YC. He saw YC as a "100-year institution", a combination of a university, a VC fund and a software company. Paul Graham had a clear picture; many investors just see a well-paid job.
- Advice to a fast-growing startup: assume you have a competitor who thinks like that. The folks with the clearest picture are probably going to win.
- As you hockey-stick, you stop competing with little startups and start competing with the companies that make up the economy, and those have in-depth visions. If you have no vision, your work may just further their business model, for example when they bundle the best startup ideas in a space into their product.

## Hiring and customer value under fast growth

Dalton flags two traps that come with growth.

**Hiring**
- Companies growing fast make bad hiring decisions, which he says comes from unclear thinking.
- Advising a company in a crisis, people want to hear that hiring one specific person or doing steps one, two and three will put out the fire. His reply is that "there are no firemen": the goal was to live in the fire, and that is what winning looks like.
- His examples: Tony (DoorDash) and Brian (Airbnb) are "in the fire."
- Growth makes hiring harder, not easier. When you aren't growing you mostly can't hire, and when you are, impressive-looking people suddenly want to join.
- Story: at a friend's high-flying company, an employee who didn't know who he was said they had picked five winning companies and planned to spend 18 months at each to pass the vesting threshold. His reaction: a taker, not a contributor, and easy to miss when growing fast.

**Not actually solving the customer's problem**
- Growing fast does not mean you are solving the customer's problem. The longer you grow, the more evidence accumulates that you are, but it is not proof.
- B2B SaaS example: a company sold a number of seats, but how many of the people with seats actually use the product? The innocent answer is "how would we know?" The darker answer is "we're working on retention."
- Software is often purchased in a company that no one wants or uses, and later un-purchased. He says this is common and rarely talked about, especially when a company is riding the zeitgeist.

## The founder's personal life isn't fixed by growth

Dalton's last item: growth does not fix, and can worsen, a founder's day-to-day life.

- Health or personal problems are not solved by growth. A big stressor in your life stays.
- Growth demands more of your day-to-day resources for the company, so if you were just holding on, things get harder.
- He recalls a conversation with the former YC batch director, who said growth is an emotional struggle. Looking back, he realized that his now-wife had been with him through almost his whole startup journey, and he took that support for granted.
- "Your life gets exposed a little bit when you're growing and when your company's demanding more."
- His closing note: growth is still good and still the main thing. These are issues to flag if you are growing well. They are all fixable if you look at them, but "one of them is going to get you" if you put blinders on, because there are "too many stray bullets around."
