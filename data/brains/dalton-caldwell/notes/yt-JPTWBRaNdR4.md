## Standard Capital: YC for the Series A

- Dalton calls Standard Capital "YC for series A", built with co-founder PB (the inventor of group office hours at YC). Fund one is $425 million, started in summer 2026, with 23 Series A deals led so far.
- As a YC partner, helping the best companies graduate to a Series A was a core part of his job. As a founder who raised a Series A twice, he noticed the product had barely changed, while YC had reinvented the seed stage (the SAFE, demo day).
- He copied YC's format for the A:
  - An application, with no warm intros and no coffee meetings.
  - Standardized docs published on the website, like the YC SAFE. He says Standard is the only Series A firm doing this.
  - A fast decision: two meetings (about half an hour each) to a yes, for checks of up to $10 million.
- His rationale: YC alumni already know this process, so it feels intuitive. A YC founder whose only fundraising experience is demo day will find it natural.
- He sees YC culture as self-reinforcing: YC alumni prefer buying from, hiring and raising from YC-culture people. He tells his own companies to pick investors who share that culture.
- He could imagine doing the same at later stages someday. He notes YC alumni already include S&P 500 companies (Airbnb, DoorDash, Reddit), so the culture can scale.

## No board seats, and peer board meetings instead

- Founders constantly asked him how to raise a Series A without giving a board seat, or how to give the lead no more than 10%. As a YC partner he had to say it wasn't possible, because nobody offered that deal. Standard is built to supply what founders ask for.
- Standard takes no board seat. Instead, founders attend "peer board meetings" once a quarter, in groups of five or six post-PMF Series A companies, modeled on group office hours.
- His logic: "who has better advice on how to run a fast growing AI company than another founder of a fast growing AI company?" Investors mostly sit in other boards' meetings and repeat what they hear, so Standard cuts out the middleman.
- He compares this to Paul Graham's innovation. Every seed investor took a board seat when YC started, and PG was ridiculed for investing without control. Dalton's view is that the best founders don't need to be bossed around.

## Founders name the price; no negotiation

- At Standard the founder states the valuation and the check size, and Standard is either in or out. If the price looks off, Standard asks the founder to reconsider and come back, so it stays the founder's decision rather than a haggle.
- Why he does it:
  - The worst part of investing was negotiating against founders. At YC he never negotiated: it was a standard deal for about a thousand companies, and he could say everyone got the same terms.
  - His advice to founders raising an A used to be "make up a number", which leads to a counter-offer and invented stories to meet in the middle. He compares it to bartering at a swap meet and says it makes you dislike the person across the table.
  - Professional investors negotiate constantly, while a founder does it once, so the process is stacked against the founder. He cites the story of an investor who halved the price right before closing.
- At YC demo day founders already state a cap and the amount, and investors are in or out. Countering with "half your cap" would be insulting in that ecosystem, and Standard follows the same norm.
- His analogy: negotiating with a car dealer versus buying a Tesla in the app and hitting "buy". Standard is designed for founders who dislike dealing with car dealers. He accepts it isn't the best fit for founders who enjoy negotiating.

## Will AI clone every SaaS company? What he looks for

- Standard's application asks what prevents AI from replicating what you build and selling it cheaper. It replaced the older question about big companies copying you.
- His position is that LLMs cloning any piece of software, faster and cheaper, is inevitable: "I don't know how you could take the other side of that bet." The old moat of "we built this and it was hard" is gone. His analogy is digital music breaking the legacy music business after Napster, and he says coding agents "break the business model of a lot of SaaS businesses."
- What he wants is founders who can say "you could clone our product and we'd still have a business." Answers that work:
  - Marketplaces and other network effects. You could tell an agent to clone Airbnb.com, but you still wouldn't have the supply.
  - Data network effects.
  - Economies of scale, where the business only works if you own the whole thing or operate at huge scale.
  - Hard tech or hardware components, which can't be cloned.
- He doesn't supply the answer; he wants the founder to impress him. His YC lesson: the best investments were founders much smarter than the partners who just described the future. At DoorDash's batch, the founders laid out the vision (suburbs city by city, food first, then other items, then global) and "all you had to do was listen."
- Tip: don't have an LLM write the answer, since it's less likely to be unique. YC applications are now noticeably longer, and AI-written slop is a signal that someone isn't serious. He treats AI as best for human augmentation, which is why Standard still has humans doing interviews.
- Will AI replace human founders? He says it's hard to see that far ahead. His short-term signal is that Anthropic and OpenAI keep hiring humans: as long as a human's incremental value exceeds their compensation, headcount keeps growing. He would watch for headcount dropping at those labs.

## Flash-in-the-pan revenue versus enduring value

- Fast early AI revenue that later drops to zero is not new. In the 2000s and 2010s, many YC companies raised money, bought ads to build a big user base, and evaporated when they turned the ads off. Facebook apps were once a whole VC category that disappeared.
- Nothing is rock solid at any stage: OpenAI versus Anthropic, AWS ending up bigger than Amazon's e-commerce business, Nokia and BlackBerry.
- Enduring value comes from durably giving customers more value than you take from them, "forever". The honest test is whether the price you charge is a tiny fraction of the value the customer gets.
- Cohort retention, revenue retention and NPS are angles on the business rather than the end-all, but bad retention means you aren't creating value. Founders often see bad numbers and say "we'll fix it later" or "hire people", and that is how flame-outs happen. He agrees with the "don't fill a leaky bucket" framing.
- On investing, he says the smartest move is to back the best businesses and never sell, as Buffett did. For the top of the power law you should keep riding it. He still holds DoorDash shares from his batch and sold only some.

## Raising at demo day: dilution and PMF

- Some companies should raise an A at demo day or within six months of it. Examples he cites: Scale AI and Lora raised at demo day, and DoorDash within six months.
- Revenue alone isn't the test. Some batch companies show big headline revenue without real PMF, and they may be better off raising a normal round and letting it bake. His advice would be a nuanced discussion of PMF signals and use of capital.
- A hockey stick is not the same as PMF. Look at the sales cycle and the quality of retention. Some companies have stronger PMF signals with less of a hockey stick.
- Risk of raising an A too early: you get a board member and the "ready for Series A" expectation. If growth slows, the investor is hard on you, and you may need two years in the wilderness to figure out the business with that pressure.
- Dilution math: raising a large seed and then a Series A six months later from a traditional VC can sell 15-20% twice, around 40% in under a year, which he calls "dumb". One company Standard invested in at demo day took 10% from Standard plus some angels, and ended up with its entire seed plus Series A at about 15% dilution.
- Demo day money is hard to turn down, but he says to be sophisticated and give bespoke advice per company rather than "everyone should do their demo day funds this way". Standard accepts applications from companies at demo day, but they're still rare because strong proof of PMF is unusual.

## Tarpit ideas in the AI era

- Tarpit definition: an idea that seems good and looks like nobody else is doing it, so it feels like an opportunity. The real reason nobody has done it is that many people have tried and it is structurally hard. Founders crawl in and get stuck. He's been in one himself, and he doesn't call it dumb.
- Classic example: apps to find out what your friends are doing so you can coordinate hanging out.
- Current tarpit-ish ideas he sees:
  - Generic "Claw"-type products, like OpenClaw but subtly different. You compete directly with OpenAI, Anthropic and Google, and need a strong distribution story.
  - B2B workflow orchestration and automation tools ("workflow automation for dentists").
- He isn't saying nobody can win in these. His advice is to research who else is in the space and be able to explain why you win, ideally backed by proof, rather than being "the 5,000th company doing it". The bar is higher if you're in a crowded space, compared with something unusual like a space hotel company.
