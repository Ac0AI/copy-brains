## Leverage is the frame behind fundraising, sales, hiring and marketing

Dalton's core idea from his YC years is that most founder questions ("how do I raise a good round", "how do I hire good people", "what should be in my pitch deck") are really questions about leverage. When a founder asked, he would stop them and ask "how much leverage do you have?", because the answer carried far more weight than a better deck or a better process.

- Leverage means: take whatever audience you are dealing with and ask whether you deliver more value than you ask for.
- A related definition is your ability to walk away. The more badly you need a thing to happen, the less leverage you have.
- He says negotiating is "10 times easier" when you have leverage. The advice is to ask in every negotiation how much leverage you have and what you can do to bring more to the table.
- Many young founders carry over their school experience, where teachers and institutions invest time and attention in you with little expectation of return. They expect investors and customers to behave the same way. Dalton says the startup world is a different one, where you must bring something of value.

## Fundraising: grow first, and a bad fundraiser with traction beats a great one without

Dalton says one of the most misunderstood aspects of Y Combinator is that people think it is fundraising school, where partners teach you to pitch for three months and build hype. He says he would never have worked a decade at a place that was that.

- The real model, which he credits to Paul Graham: the way to be effective at fundraising is to grow so quickly and have so many proof points that you could be bad at fundraising, put very little effort in, and do it in a short time frame.
- Counterintuitively, doing it "seat of your pants" at the very end beats doing a lot of prep.
- His line: it is "way better to be like a very bad fundraiser with something that's working than someone that's a very good fundraiser with something that's not working at all."
- Investors see so many pitches that they are expert at spotting polished language. A founder's time is better spent making a good product, launching and talking to users.
- If you are not growing and not launched, working on the deck can be the biggest waste of your time. He says the deck isn't doing the work.
- Titles of slides are the wrong frame. The real question is whether you are growing, and how fast.
- He asks founders to estimate how many hours they spent raising a seed round, and how much more value they would have created spending those hours on the startup.

## Wasted time in fundraising, and why Standard Capital caps founder effort

Dalton describes long fundraising processes as a drag on the industry. Low-leverage founders pitch for months with almost no chance of it working, and would have been better off not fundraising at all.

- He gives a "dark game theory" explanation for investors stringing founders along: an investor keeps meeting a founder to hold an option, so that if the company takes off they are well positioned. That process harms the founder's ability to reach the growth inflection point. He says he is "so not aligned with that."
- At Standard Capital he cannot fund everyone, and most applicants will not be funded. But he can calculate how much time they spent on the application and the interview, and he is comfortable that it is capped. He wishes more investors were judicious with founders' time.
- He sees this as an advantage YC has demonstrated in public for 20-plus years that has not been copied. He suggests both sides want to believe that spending lots of time with a non-growing company reveals subtlety a short set of meetings cannot, and says "that's certainly not the bet I would take."
- He describes long diligence on non-growing companies as "torturing everyone to find the diamond in the rough", a dead-weight loss on the industry. Most of the time, he says, a VC is investing on traction and chasing.

## Sales: you can't need any one deal

Dalton says first-time founders treat an impressive inbound email (his example is someone from Google) as "we made it" and think they only need to close one deal. His position is that you can't need any single deal.

- High leverage is having so much inbound that you can be picky and choosy. Example: launch a product, get 1,000 signups, segment them by customer type, and decide whom you will serve (for example, skip enterprise).
- With no signups and one possible customer, you put all your eggs in that basket. Bad second-order effects follow: you can't tell whether you are near product-market fit or whether this one customer is weird, you get pushed into custom work, and the customer knows they have you. You may move further from PMF without realizing it.
- He notices that founders with a hot deal often stop doing outreach, telling themselves that landing it makes everything easy, or that they are too busy. In reality they are taking about one meeting a week.
- A test for leverage: if any deal dies, how much do you care? With leverage the reaction is "oh well, we've got eight others in the pipeline."
- Customers dislike feeling the company needs them more than they need the company.
- With leverage you can be aggressive on pricing, avoid discounts, and get a signed contract rather than an LOI. Strong companies even fire customers outside their ICP, saying in effect "you're paying us a lot, but we can't service you anymore."
- His conclusion: you become great at sales negotiation by not needing any one deal, not by learning "corny negotiation tactics" or redlining contracts.

## Hiring: build a pipeline and run it like a sales process

When Dalton debugs a founder's hiring, the biggest issue is usually that there is no pipeline. Too few candidates at every stage means that if a good one appears, the founder has no leverage and no choices.

- Ideal shape: a thousand possible candidates, 50 passed to a first screen, and so on. Then it doesn't matter if one candidate declines or asks for something you can't give.
- He argues founders often think they compete with Google, but the person who takes a job at a company of tens of thousands is probably not the same person who becomes employee number 10 at a startup. The overlap is smaller than people think, so you are usually competing with other early-stage startups, which is easier.
- Pitch the adventure: tell candidates about the responsibility they will have and the hardest challenges (his example is the website going down over the weekend). If a candidate looks at you weirdly, they aren't right. You have more leverage than you think with people excited by that, and far less with someone who wants a pre-IPO spot at OpenAI with the least work for the most money.
- If hiring is a big issue post-PMF, put as much effort into it as into sales. He says the emotional energy needed is on par with getting customers, because you are selling a job at your company. All the same advice about sales applies, and "I put up a job listing, why is no one applying" is not a process.
- Winning is the pre-work, not ninja tricks at the end of negotiation.

## Marketing and PR: tell your own story

Dalton says founders over-index on getting press (his examples: The New York Times, TechCrunch), thinking "if I can get TechCrunch to write about me, I made it."

- At YC they tracked how many clicks, signups or customers founders got from press, and the result was shockingly small in directly measurable terms. Press is nice because your parents see it, but founders were disappointed when they looked at their dashboards.
- Needing someone else to tell your story is low leverage. High leverage is telling your own story by posting constantly, over and over, with no gatekeepers.
- His test for content: would you want to consume it yourself? The same goes for outbound email: would you respond to it, or want to kill the company that sent it?
- He calls press the anti-best-practice for the last five to ten years. This video series exists for that reason: they didn't think their stories would be expressed adequately unless they got on camera themselves, rather than being guests on someone else's show, possibly for a "fake controversy."
- It takes time but creates value. The same muscles apply to fundraising and recruiting, and a direct channel lets potential users know you are the person who will help them.

## Closing principle: win through pre-work

Dalton's summary is that winning is about how much leverage you have, not how much people will give you. Founders can feel they have little leverage, but there is often a lot they can do, especially against other startups.

- It is "usually the pre-work", not the final negotiation. By the time you arrive at the negotiation, you have already won.
- Analogy: Usain Bolt's 10-second race involves more than zero work, but it is the years of work beforehand that matter.
