## Organizational complexity is a model of the outside world

Dalton frames a company's internal structure as a simplified model of the external world. The more external states it must represent, the more complex it is. He borrows the idea from AI knowledge representation: building a good enough model matters as much as the algorithm that runs on it.

- Simple models give false confidence that success "scales up". The SHRDLU demo led AI researchers to "excessive optimism which was soon lost" once systems met real-world ambiguity.
- His summary: don't assume a success on a simple model will seamlessly carry over to more complex circumstances.
- Applied to companies: what worked at five people will not automatically work in a messier, larger setting.

## Why large companies are complex, and what it costs

A large company's job is to learn what the outside world wants to buy and mobilize its infrastructure to deliver the right product, at the right place, at the right time.

- Using Apple as the stand-in: no single person understands every piece of work behind a new iPhone, and none needs to. Well-run subsystems each own a piece. "That complexity is what allows them to ship very complex products."
- The downside: "Complexity tends to breed more complexity." Employees describe a world of byzantine internal politics with little relation to customers, with much of each day spent navigating the shifting org chart.
- He observes that the larger a company gets, the more resources go to inward-facing rather than outward-facing issues.
- Org complexity leaks into products. His example is the Microsoft Surface offering two very different OSes (Windows RT and Windows 8 Pro), which he suggests is Microsoft deciding to "ship their org chart".
- He points to the 2006 "Peanut Butter Manifesto" by a Yahoo SVP as an excellent first-hand insider account.

## Complexity inside early-stage startups

Even a one- or two-person startup has an internal model of the world: what people want, when something is ready to ship, which technology to use. With near-zero bureaucracy it is still easy to get stuck on inward-facing projects that never matter in the long run.

- He treats "focus", "simplicity" and "doing just one thing right" as complexity management.
- If a company tries to do too many things at once, its org chart and workflow will naturally model that sprawl.
- Tools that remove work keep the founders' mental model small. His examples: AWS, outsourced HR, and streamlined billing providers like Stripe.
- "Simple models are the easiest to understand and modify on the fly."

## When adding complexity is the right move

As a startup grows, its internal model must grow to serve the demands of the outside world. Dalton explicitly says some complexity is good.

- Traffic example: if traffic jumps 100x, the team faces an overwhelming amount of work to keep the service running. It builds a more sophisticated hosting setup, implements database sharding, or hires a scaling expert. What was once rolling out code to Heroku now has far more moving parts. The faster the startup recognizes and adapts to the new external complexity, the better.
- Revenue example: rising sales force the company to create internal process for customer support, sales support, refunds, taxes, billing and accounting. Even if all of it is outsourced with zero added headcount, someone is still responsible for those functions being performed and managed.
- He calls out the failure mode directly: a founder shouldn't say "I believe in simplicity and focus, therefore we will have no systems in place to handle accounting or customer support."
- The growth loop: better product-market fit plus a matching increase in organizational complexity to handle demand "can feed on itself to create a virtuous cycle of growth". This is how, "once in a blue moon", a startup becomes a large company.

## "More companies die of indigestion than from starvation"

When a problem feels overwhelming, or Dalton hears of companies struggling (founder falling out, lack of focus, employee infighting, bad hires, can't hire fast enough, hired too fast), he thinks of David Packard's line:

> "More companies die of indigestion than from starvation"

- He first dismissed it as a useless platitude, but appreciates it more as he gets older.
- Takeaway: the risk is often taking on more than the organization can absorb, not a lack of opportunity or resources. Growth, hiring and scope have to be digested at a pace the org's model can handle.
