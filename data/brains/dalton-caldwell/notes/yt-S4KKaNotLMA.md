## Hard tech at YC was widely ridiculed at the time

- Dalton frames the topic as what he and PB learned funding hard tech companies at YC "way before that was a thing". The first one he recalls Sam recruiting was Ginkgo Bioworks.
- He describes the reaction to the early hard tech batches as ridicule on all sides.
- Investors asked what "YC bozos" know about hard problems. Sam was "the guy from Loopt", YC had "the Gmail guy", and he quotes the line "what does Paul Graham know, he was like an artist".
- Investors also mocked the founders for taking "such bad terms": a big ownership stake for a small amount of money, with no board seat.
- The wider market thought "none of this stuff is ever going to work". In biotech, companies that chose YC were ridiculed by their peers.
- Dalton says he could never have imagined how many of these companies would succeed. He calls the hit rate "incredible".

## Why hard tech worked: few qualified founders act as a moat

- Dalton finds it counterintuitive that hard ideas had such a high hit rate. In a typical software idea space, 100 people work on the same thing or pivot into it.
- A quantum computer company is different, because very few people are qualified to build one.
- His example is Chad Rigetti's demo day slide. It was a Venn diagram: the set of people qualified to start a quantum computer company, and the set who want to. Chad was the intersection, "cuz it was such an academic field".
- His conclusion: "there were so few people that could start companies like that that it inadvertently was a moat."
- On space, he says to take out the cranks and count only real attempts at space launch. Twenty years ago it "was just pure insanity", though it is cool now.

## Raise capital in stages, with each stage proving a "miracle"

- Dalton's first tidbit for hard tech founders: "financing risk is real".
- Software companies usually raise on revenue, growth and momentum. Hard tech companies have none of those.
- Their milestones are therefore "what miracles you performed". The founder has to keep performing miracles and choose the right ones to argue they have made enough progress to raise again.
- This requires a sophisticated view of how much capital is needed to reach the next miracle.
- The intuitive approach is wrong. Many founders think they can say "I want to build a lunar moon base, let's put up a hotel, give me a billion dollars", and he stresses "that is not how it works".
- Instead, separate each stage of capital and decide what that raise will prove. He says YC saw this "over and over again" with its companies.
- His advice on investors: don't expect them to see the brilliance of the idea and fund the end state at once. Keep setting yourself up to succeed by doing amazing things, and raise at each stage to reach the next level.
