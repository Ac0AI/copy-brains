## Why "all investors are the same" is a founder's naive mistake
- Dalton says that as a first-time founder he assumed investors were interchangeable: "people that give you money." You talk, they wire, you move on.
- He only understood the gap between good and bad investors after being a founder for a long time.
- His view is that the investing process itself differs hugely between a good and a bad investor, and the gap is largest at the extremes.
- The advice is aimed at angel and pre-seed investing. Dalton notes that losing everything on a deal is the normal outcome there. It is the opposite of stocks or real estate, where going to zero means something went badly wrong.

## A good investor's process is simple, and sophistication shows in it
- Dalton says a great process is a sign of a sophisticated investor.
- The least experienced angels often come from real estate or public-market investing. He calls their approach "super midwit" process:
  - Meet us three times.
  - Have four people on the call.
  - Send three-year financial projections.
- Asking all this of a two-person startup is "silly crap," in his words.
- He finds it telling that people with no angel experience are the ones full of bad ideas about how to run a process.

## Investors with no real money, or a tiny budget
- Some "investors" simply have no money.
  - Their plan is to get a startup to accept their terms, then raise the money from someone else and act as a middleman between capital and the startup.
  - Dalton calls this classic bad behavior.
- Others have a tiny budget but still take hundreds of founder meetings to make one investment.
- A good investor decides how much they will devote to startups and then invests it. Making a decision and moving on is straightforward for them.
- Dalton agrees that "not caring as much" about the individual dollars is a good thing.

## The screening test: ask about recent investments
- When founders ask how to tell a good angel or pre-seed investor from a bad one, Dalton gives this concrete test. Ask:
  - How many other investments have you made in the past year?
  - How big were the checks?
  - Which companies were they?
- Then contact those founders and ask whether this person really invested in them.
- He thinks this simple check screens out about 80% of bad investors.
- Green flags: roughly 20 deals in the past year, similar check sizes, and founders who say "they're cool."
- Red flag: he feels bad for founders who spend months and months with investors who, in truth, haven't invested in anyone in the last year. The whole thing is a waste of time.

## Check size and number of bets
- Dalton recounts a party conversation with a doctor who wanted angel investing tips. The doctor planned to put his money into one large investment in a single company.
- Dalton suggested splitting it into about 10 much smaller checks. He explained portfolio theory and the expected value logic, and says the doctor was convinced.
- His framing is that you get to roll the dice, and he calls 10 rolls "way better" than one.
  - Putting everything on one roll reduces nothing about the upside if you hit.
  - Ten rolls lowers the upside slightly but raises the odds that you hit a very good investment.
  - He suggests running a Monte Carlo simulation, or asking ChatGPT, to see this.

## Inexperienced investors become a constant source of problems after you take their money
- Dalton says the unsophisticated or inexperienced ones "suck up a lot of time" once they are on your cap table.
- Examples of what they send founders:
  - An email every week saying they read an article and are really worried.
  - Intros to totally random people, such as "my neighbor knows about computers."
- In his words, they become a "vector for annoying junk."

## Slow processes are a bad sign at every stage
- For pre-seed, he says to ask questions that test how sophisticated an investor is, and how many investments they have made.
- At later stages the same logic holds: an "inappropriately prolonged process" is a bad sign.
  - His examples are a six-month or a three-month fundraise process.
  - He says he doesn't know anyone good who does that.
  - If a founder tolerates it, they are selecting for bad investors.
- On returns, Dalton's argument is that the slowest investors have the worst returns.
  - No good founder would tolerate a very slow process. They would talk to someone with a faster one.
  - That creates selection bias. The struggling startups with the hardest fundraises are the only ones who put up with slow investors, so they end up with the saddest stories.
- From his time as a YC partner: his strongest companies said fundraising was "pretty straightforward." The insane stories, which he says he could never repeat on video, came from companies having a hard raise.
- His conclusion is that there is a market matching effect, and "you don't want to be on the bottom part of the market."
