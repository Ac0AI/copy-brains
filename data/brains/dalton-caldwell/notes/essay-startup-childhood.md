## Early-stage startups are "childhood," not Google in miniature

- Dalton says early-stage founders often hold two mental models: the unglamorous current state of their startup, and the dream end-state once it is hugely successful. The steps between are hazy, so founders imagine them as "running their dream endstate company but in miniaturized form."
- He calls this "Google-in-miniature" thinking, a form of "startup dysmorphia" that "can be very counterproductive."
- It happens because successful companies founders know are already huge, and it's easiest to picture them in their post-product-market-fit state rather than the developmental stages it took to get there.
- His analogy: the concept of "childhood" is relatively new in human history. Before it was invented, children were treated as "miniature adults." A startup shouldn't be treated as a miniature version of a mature company either.

## Prioritize differently pre-PMF versus "startup adulthood"

- Dalton argues that what to prioritize in the earliest stages of a company is different from what makes sense during "startup adulthood."
- Examples of things that are questionable at a very early stage but expected at a late-stage company:
  - Hiring a full org chart with every function a late-stage company would have.
  - Shipping multiple products at the same time under different names.
  - Many tiers of management and job titles.
- Just as the concept of childhood helps us understand young people through developmental stages, he wants founders to understand their company through its stage.

## Product-market fit is the dividing line

- Dalton believes the most helpful way to separate the "childhood" and "adulthood" stages of a startup is whether it has achieved product-market fit (PMF).
- He notes that most startup milestones are communicated through fundraising announcements, where big numbers act as a proxy for success and maturity.
- Conflating fundraising success, valuation and PMF status "can be value-destructive to all of the stakeholders involved."

## Why big raises make the pre/post-PMF line harder to see

- The more money a company has raised, the trickier the pre/post-PMF delineation becomes. Raising large amounts of capital ahead of traction creates an "emperor-wears-no-clothes impasse":
  1. The founder doesn't want to admit to investors or employees that they lack PMF.
  2. Investors don't want to admit they put a great deal of money into a company without PMF.
  3. Employees generally don't want to admit they work at a pre-PMF startup.
- The result is that all key stakeholders become "willing participants in an elaborate fiction that the startup has PMF."
- He thinks they only get honest once the company runs low on money or fails to fundraise; until then, "everyone keeps pretending."

## Be intellectually honest about your stage

- Dalton wishes admitting a startup is pre-PMF didn't feel like something people around it needed to lie about. "Dishonesty about facts is not particularly healthy or helpful."
- He calls building a pre-PMF startup "hugely rewarding" and the time "when the most important insights and innovation occur." It is just different from operating a startup post-PMF.
- Being intellectually honest about where the company is in its developmental lifecycle increases the chances of it becoming a post-PMF company.
- His summary: "Children are great! But they need to be treated differently than adults. Pre-PMF companies are also great!" All stakeholders should be rigorous and honest that the company is pre-PMF and act accordingly.
