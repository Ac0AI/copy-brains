## Why advertising can't fund services users depend on

- Dalton argues the services people rely on most are "too important to its users to be ad-supported."
- Case study: SourceForge was ad-ridden and user-hostile, "designed to squeeze every last advertising penny out of you." Getting anything done took extra pageviews and clicks. Two months into his first programming job there, 25% of the company was laid off.
- Github came later with no advertising, charged for certain features, and became profitable quickly because the service was so good and important that people paid. Its team spends time making the best service possible, "as opposed to spending their time trying to extract additional pennies out of their users."
- Thought experiment: a purely ad-supported Dropbox would mean ads in personal documents, a music file-listing sold to the music industry, or API access (e.g. IFTTT) shut off. He would happily pay to avoid that.
- He frames the question as trust: "if an online service doesn't have the trust of its users and developers, then what does it have?"

## Paid, for-profit, centralized services built on open protocols

- Both SourceForge and Github were hosting platforms plus collaboration services on open source tools and documented, open protocols (CVS and Git).
- Because the protocols are open, anyone can use them without the centralized service.
- He believes for-profit, centralized companies innovating and operating these services is "a Very Good Thing."
- He believes a paid, profitable steward is far better than an ad-supported one.
- He says SourceForge lost to Github not because its team lacked talent or understanding. Venture capital and centralization aren't inherently evil. The ad model was the problem.
- He rejects both camps in the Twitter debate: the open-source federation camp, and the camp that says everyone must "swallow our bitter ad-supported medicine."

## The ad-supported monoculture and the case for a paid alternative

- Consumers effectively choose among Facebook, Twitter or Google+, plus startups with "hand-wavy future advertising business models." All are in the business of selling your clickstream to advertisers.
- He quotes Jeff Hammerbacher: "The best minds of my generation are thinking about how to make people click ads. That sucks."
- His question: why can't you pay for an ad-free feed from a company whose product is something you pay for, not you? He compares it to choosing a Mac over a crapware-infested PC.
- He is glad ad-supported options exist, but objects to them being the only option.
- His stated demand signal: "I would gladly pay for a service that treats me better."

## What went wrong in Web 2.0, from his own failures

- Pattern he describes: a free service with a vague business model wins users and becomes vitally important. Hosting bills and payroll balloon as it grows. Founders then face three bad choices: sell the company, cram the site with ads, or keep raising money to delay the problem.
- imeem: users loved it, but he couldn't make it profitable. He raised more than $50MM, had 100 employees, and at peak sold $2MM of advertising per month. He expresses regret and apologizes to users and employees.
- picplz: he made monetization a first-class focus from the start, but gave up fairly early and spun the product off because the space was following the same Web 2.0 cycle.
- His conclusion: running an important service on "hand-wavy platitudes about someday launching a 'new form of advertising'" is unfair to users and employees.

## What App.net already had

- App.net was a paid service for mobile app developers, with public developer tools.
- For about 8 months most engineering went into a secret project: a consumer iOS app and service built around realtime feeds and developer APIs. He calls it the highest-quality software his team had built.
- Assets he cites for the refocus:
  - a polished native iOS app
  - infrastructure able to handle about 200MM API calls per day with no code changes
  - a developer-facing API provisioning, documentation and analytics system
- His point: "This isn't vaporware."

## The proposal: fund it first, minimum critical mass of $500,000

- He refocuses App.net into a financially sustainable, ad-free realtime feed API and service.
- Launch mechanism: a Kickstarter-style campaign. App.net accepts money only if it hits critical mass, defined as $500,000, roughly 10,000 backers.
- Why 10,000 is enough, citing Paul Graham on a hypothetical new search engine: a product used by "the top 10,000 hackers and no one else would be in a very powerful position despite its small size, just as Google was when it was that size."
- Graham's advice, which Dalton endorses: build what you yourself want, even "excessively hackerish." "Anything that gets you those 10,000 users is ipso facto good."
- Demand evidence: his earlier post, "What Twitter could have been," drew 80K pageviews and strong response.
- He accepts the risk of public failure and criticism of his past failures because he believes in the project.
