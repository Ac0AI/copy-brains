## Existing products existed, but were bad enough that a 10x version was possible

- Dalton notes that people assume Airbnb, Coinbase and Stripe invented their categories. None did.
- Airbnb: he used VRBO before Airbnb existed. CouchSurfing was big and had mind share, so Airbnb felt like a "third-rate entrant to a crowded space".
- Coinbase: buying bitcoin around 2011 was miserable. One path meant sending money orders to a foreign country, which he skipped because it "seemed too much like a total scam".
- Stripe: accepting cards online was painful, and Stripe was a clear 10x improvement.
- His takeaway: in all three cases the founders had the problem themselves, had real experience with it, and saw an obvious opportunity for something 10x better.

## Airbnb: the contrarian takes that made it look like a bad investment

- Dalton, speaking as an investor of that era, says there were two "bad idea" signals.
- Renting a room in a home where the host is present was seen as unappealing and scary. Older, wealthier people would not choose a stranger's Manhattan apartment over a nice hotel.
- Charging money was seen as uncool and impure. CouchSurfing's ethos was communal and pay-it-forward, and being commercial was "extremely in poor taste".
- The advice founders of that era were given: get huge first, with 100 million people, then monetize.
- His summary: "build something like couchsurfing but with no users... and you're going to charge money for it. Good luck." It sounded like a suicide mission.

## Coinbase: a simple fix for a terrible market, doubted even by its founder

- Dalton lost some money (about one bitcoin) on Mt. Gox, which was the biggest exchange, kept going down, and got hacked. Money went in but didn't reliably come out.
- Buying bitcoin was something people wanted to do, but every option was bad. Coinbase wasn't the first mover, but the incumbent product was so poor that a 10x version was within reach.
- The objections at the time:
  - Bitcoin was seen as a tiny market, a bubble and a crash risk.
  - It was synonymous with fraud, and investors disliked it.
  - A US bank deal was seemingly impossible, which is why exchanges were offshore.
- Brian's own YC application leaned on a P2P-transfer idea, "something like PayPal". Dalton says even he under-weighted the core promise.
- That core promise was buying bitcoin in the US with a debit card, without being sketchy or getting hacked. In Dalton's words, "that was enough of an insight to be the basis for like a hundred billion dollar company".

## Stripe: a painful experience of his own, and a product built for developers

- Dalton set up card acceptance twice before Stripe, in about 2002 and again in 2007.
- A merchant account was "like applying for a mortgage": lots of paperwork, faxing, personal guarantees, fraud checks, minimums, and expense. He calls it "gnarly and the opposite of easy in every way".
- He was among Stripe's first hundred beta customers, for his third company. When it appeared on Hacker News he wanted it before he had even seen it.
- Why Stripe won on go-to-market:
  - They built a beautiful website and beautiful documentation, and asked what a programmer would want.
  - Competitors targeted business buyers. Whoever implemented authorize.net was just someone paid to wire up code.
  - Developers freaked out about Stripe, and everyone copied its docs and site.
  - Dalton says "if Steve Jobs had to design a payments company it would be Stripe".
  - Stripe ran a beta invite program, which created buzz because access felt like a favor.
- The objections were that it was a crowded market with PayPal as an obvious threat, that regulation made it hard, and that it needed a bank deal.

## YC's early reputation and the lesson on being contrarian

- Dalton recalls that the knock against YC at the time was that it "would just fund crappy versions of existing companies", meaning third-rate knock-offs of something good. He finds it hilarious in hindsight.
- His overarching theme is that all three founders had to be contrarian. Asking experts, investors or fellow founders would have mostly produced "that's stupid".
- The signals that mattered were:
  - the founders had the problem themselves
  - their smart friends complained about the same thing
  - the existing product was bad
  - the 10x improvement was within reach
