## Resilience: the core job is not dying

- Dalton's mantra is "just don't die. Just keep your startup going." He says it over and over, and founders often tell him it was the most impactful thing he said, more than any clever move.
- He compares it to coaches miked up in the huddle: even elite performers are coached on mundane fundamentals, because being reminded of the basics puts them in the right mindset.
- In YC and non-YC stories alike, the founders rationally should have given up at some point. Airbnb should have shut down three or four times before YC. Keeping going was an irrational act.
- Nearly every founder goes through a near-death moment where "I guess we're done." Even among those who hit truly hard situations, he guesses around 50% keep it going through sheer will, then look like overnight successes.
- The most common cause of death is not running out of money. Founders usually still have some money but are out of ideas, fight with a co-founder, can't agree on what to work on, and quit. He says you can see in their eyes whether they feel they have "one more try" left.

## When to keep going and when it is fine to stop

- First test: are you still having fun? Do you enjoy your co-founders, your customers and the problem? If yes, lean toward continuing.
- If the startup is badly hurting you and your relationships, or you don't want to work with your co-founder anymore, lean toward stopping.
- Founders who turn things around tend to love their customers and product. Airbnb's founders liked each other and knew their first hosts by name even while it was going badly.
- Shutting down costs little: "no one will remember that you shut down your company in 10 years," as long as you act with integrity. Life is short, and staying in misery only to avoid losing face is a big opportunity cost.
- His life motto: check in with yourself that you're having fun and value what you spend time on, and if not, change something. As a founder you are in control and can change your own company.

## Pivoting: how to tell when, and what makes a good pivot

- When to pivot: count how many more ideas you have for making it grow. With a dozen untried growth ideas, try them (Airbnb tried cereal and conventions and didn't run out of zany ideas). If the best idea left is "maybe pay influencers," that is a better signal to pivot.
- A successful pivot gets warmer, closer to what you are an expert at, and builds on what you learned from the prior idea. "A good pivot is like going home."
- Examples he gives:
  - Brex began as a VR headset company run by Stanford dropouts who knew nothing about optics. They had earlier worked on a fintech company in Brazil, so he told them to work on what they know.
  - Retool came from cashew, a UK peer-to-peer payments app. The founders had built many internal dashboards to run it and in earlier internships.
  - PostHog knew analytics and had strong opinions on it.
  - Segment started as software for telling a professor you were confused in class, then became a Mixpanel competitor, then a JavaScript snippet that sends events to multiple endpoints. They could not have started with the final idea because they learned how analytics works by grinding on earlier ones.
- Sometimes founders resist the right idea because they are burnt out on it. They have to get over that barrier.
- In the winter 2017 batch, Brex and cashew looked like the two worst companies in his group of about 35-40. Both turned into Brex and Retool, the most successful in the group.

## Finding ideas: avoid the same information diet and tarpits

- Founders share the same information diet (same podcasts, same Twitter accounts, same blog posts, same friends), so they land on the same ideas. His prompt: go off the beaten path, using your own experience or interests, so your exact person would come up with something others wouldn't.
- Trends become fashionable fast. Trucking startups were fresh because nobody did them and worked well, then became conventional wisdom.
- Zip's approach (his bespoke advice to Rul): find large public or private-equity-owned incumbents that are hated by their customers, where the software is horrible, and look for a knowable big market with a low-NPS incumbent. Rul had also run a marketplace, Flight Car, and was a strong executor. Zip went through about six pivots before landing there, and Dalton says his co-founder Lou deserves credit too.
- Tarpit ideas: ideas many people come up with, that seem like an unsolved problem, and that get lots of positive validation. That is different from a bad idea. Examples: an app to coordinate with friends where to go out at night; Foursquare clones (for years "Foursquare is going to own this," and now Foursquare is a B2B business); music discovery, which he worked on himself in his first startup.
- Founders get stuck because early validation is genuinely positive, and people have been starting these startups since the 90s.
- Market size: he says it matters more the later the stage. For pre-seed and YC applicants it matters little. Uber, Airbnb, and Razorpay (the largest payment processor in India, whose 2015 market was tiny because few used credit cards) all had tiny market sizes at the start. He cares more about "how do you get users, how do you grow, are you making something people want." He notes many investors still care about Tam, and says it is hard to win that debate.

## Talking to customers

- Most founders know to talk to customers, but when he asks how many they talk to, "they get really quiet." The cause is mostly social anxiety and fear of looking stupid. Power through the awkwardness until it stops feeling bad.
- It has to be in person, not hiding behind a keyboard. Landing pages and Instagram ads to validate an idea are not talking to customers.
- Heuristic: look at your calendar. Roughly 20-30% of your time should say customer meeting or call (it varies by idea space), and the past month should show real physical meetings.
- Examples:
  - Brex and Retool sold inside the YC network.
  - Zip's founders cold-DMed people on LinkedIn asking how they felt about their procurement products, did hundreds of these, pre-sold before building, and the contacts became early beta testers.
  - PostHog launched open source, drew excitement on Hacker News, and had calendars full of people wanting to talk.
- The Collison install: when a customer says yes but goes quiet, don't let it churn. The Collisons would say "I'm in the neighborhood," show up, ask to drive the keyboard, and install Stripe into the customer's site, not leaving until it was implemented. His takeaway: even after a yes you are not done with sales; finish the last mile. Patrick also messaged him weekly on Google Talk when Dalton was an early Stripe customer.
- Getting started: do customer validation first, before a PowerPoint deck or fundraising. Pre-sell something before writing code. Build once you think at least one person would use it. Finding excited customers is "a great green light" to do the startup.

## Don't over-delegate, stay close to product

- Common mistake: investors push founders to hire senior people with shiny big-tech resumes, or hire a PM or senior salesperson too early, or to "spend the money you raised." Two years later they realize they over-delegated and must clean it up. It is nobody's fault; the founder took their eye off the ball.
- His best product advice: founders who are great at product stay deep in the weeds, still care a lot, and still talk to customers at late stage. "You can't delegate caring about your users" or about the product being great.
- Time management: if you care about customers and product, your instincts on priorities are usually good. He would cut time spent hanging out with investors and networking first.
- Sales: founders afraid of sales think they need an expensive coach. His cheaper path is to read the most popular basic sales books on Amazon, such as Getting to Yes, plus Founding Sales (he recommends it for founders doing sales). He says that gets you about 80% of the way.

## Growth hacking is a waste of time very early

- His contrarian view: growth hacking, analytics and A/B testing are a total waste of time for very early startups.
- Much online startup advice is aimed at Series A/B companies, and seed founders consume it and get confused. Split testing and feature flags make sense for a Facebook employee shipping small features on a product that already has scale, not for someone with no users.
- Imagine the original Airbnb founders, with four users whose names they knew, running complicated split-test growth theory.
- Instead, know your archetype, then look at what comparable companies did from zero to one and ignore what they do today. Examples:
  - Whatnot (a consumer marketplace) focused on the buy side and was intellectually honest about marketplace metrics rather than dumping money into Instagram ads.
  - Facebook got 100% penetration at Harvard first rather than launching broadly.

## What big founders have in common, and investor behavior

- He doesn't think personality type matters. Very quiet and very extroverted founders, such as Tony from DoorDash, Grant from Whatnot, Patrick from Stripe and Ryan from Flexport, are all very different people.
- What they share: they really want it and believe in themselves, with something deep that says "I'm the one" and won't accept it not working despite employees and executives wanting to quit. It works like an internal gravitational force that bends the world to their will.
- Conviction is built, not innate at the start. Patrick wasn't sure Stripe would work until a year or two in. Conviction grows as customers and data reflect back that you're on track, creating a virtuous cycle.
- Why investors say no: put yourself in their shoes. They make few investments per year, so most "pretty good" deals get a no. There is usually no hidden truth, such as your Zoom setup or shirt color. They pick what they are personally most excited about or think can be phenomenally big.
- Careers are long and people reinvent themselves. He met Reid Hoffman when LinkedIn had about 12 employees, and knew Sam Altman as a young Stanford dropout working on Loopt. His own career went engineer, founder, product manager, then YC partner.
- YC's 2024 request for startups (a nudge to widen information diets, not a prescription) included ERPs (few applications, usually good ones), open source companies, space companies, ending cancer, spatial computing, defense technology, bringing manufacturing back to America, better enterprise glue (software connecting brittle business systems, which LLMs may improve), and small fine-tuned models as alternatives to giant generic ones.
