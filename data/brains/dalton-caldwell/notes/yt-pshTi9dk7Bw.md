## Why Dalton warns founders off music startups

- Dalton built imeem, a music startup, and spent six years on it. He now calls the opportunity cost real: "if I would have spent that time building something else, probably better for me."
- Founders are drawn to music because everyone feels like an expert on taste. Many also think they will fix the labels single-handedly, or that "social music" is an aha idea.
- Traffic is easy to get. If you put up free unlicensed MP3s, users show up even if the product is bad. Dalton says this makes you feel smart, but it is a trap.
- His example is the original Napster: it "crashed a lot," but people kept using it because the music was free.

## Tools for artists: low margins, little money

- Dalton says the "tools for artists" startup (Radiohead going direct to fans, "we don't need labels") is a very common and saturated category. Artists generally don't have money to pay.
- He points to Tunecore as a disruptive model: it charges a flat fee to put music in stores and takes no percentage of sales.
- He points to Topspin as strong, since its CEO manages the Beastie Boys and already has distribution for artist-to-consumer sales.
- The margin math on a store:
  - Best case is about 20% margins.
  - To make $50K a month to run a startup, you must sell about $250K of an unknown band's content every month.
  - Selling that much unknown-band MP3 content is, in his words, "the hardest thing I can possibly imagine."
- His advice: this is the one startup everyone does, so "don't do it."

## Download stores: advances and incumbents

- Licensing deals with labels require advances, which Dalton puts at about $3-4 million up front. Elsewhere in the talk he says $5 million in with 20% margins means you'd need to sell about $25 million to pay back the capital.
- iTunes already holds 90%+ market share, and it is very hard to get people to switch where they buy music.
- Amazon is selling music for less, and Google was about to launch a music store.
- His verdict: "this is a really hard business."

## Ad-supported licensing: per-play and quarterly minimums

- This is what Dalton did at imeem. His vision was to take the traffic that lived in peer-to-peer land and monetize it so everyone gets paid.
- Per-play minimums are widely criticized, and he agrees they are hard. You owe the labels on every play whether or not you showed an ad, so you can get upside down fast.
- The deadlier problem, which "no one writes about," is quarterly minimum advances:
  - You owe the labels millions every quarter no matter what, even if your site goes down.
  - You can't pivot out of deals like this. "You're boned."
- Labels may stop licensing ad-supported music at all. Free music on mobile devices removes the reason to own music, and cloud music further breaks their economics.

## Subscription startups: the unit economics

Dalton's sarcastic recipe: spend two years in stealth doing deals, raise $5-6 million, and give labels 10-30% of the company. Then the business model:

- Charge $10 a month.
- Expect about 10% monthly churn, which he calls "the AOL effect."
- You keep roughly $3-5 of the $10, and he says he is being generous.
- That has to cover headcount, bandwidth, IT and customer acquisition, plus the quarterly minimums.
- International licensing is separate: each country has a different body to license with, so launching in more than one country is "insanely difficult." A social site limited to one country struggles to get network effects.
- Competition includes Rhapsody, Spotify, Audio, MOG, and the roughly $300 million of ads Rhapsody ran on MTV and VH1.

## Unlicensed or "DMCA-compliant" sites, and Pandora

- Dalton does not recommend running an unlicensed site that takes down tracks on request. He has been sued himself.
- The human cost: when someone declares jihad on you in the press, you "don't want to come to the office." A process server knocking at 6 a.m. is "not badass, not cool."
- He says he has talked to founders who chose to shut down rather than face more litigation.
- DMCA radio, which is Pandora's route, looks decent:
  - They had to lobby Congress to keep it going and succeeded.
  - They make about $100 million in annual revenue and are close to profitable.
  - He still does not recommend it to very small startups.

## Why it is hard: structure, not people

- Dalton says the problem is the legal structure, not the people. He compares it to software patent trolls.
- There are no clear rules for running a music startup, especially internationally. Lawyers tell you "you can do it or not, your call... do you feel lucky today?"
- This applies to mashups and to publishing: on top of paying the labels you also pay publishing, which he says he could talk about for three hours.
- "If you mess with music you're going to get sued. It's just a fact of life."
