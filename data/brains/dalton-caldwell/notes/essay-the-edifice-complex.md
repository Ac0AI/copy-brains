## The Industry Standard and real estate as a company killer

Dalton read "Starving to Death on 200 Million," the book about the two-year rise and fall of The Industry Standard. His main takeaway was that bad real estate deals made the company unfixable. The company had a great brand and could plausibly have survived in some form, but bankruptcy was the outcome.

He notes that when he looks at the behind-the-scenes details of what killed successful companies, it often boils down to terrible real estate deals.

## Real estate as the dividing line in the dotcom shakeout

Dalton argues that one of the clear dividing lines between who lived and who died in the dotcom shakeout was who could most effectively wind down their real estate deals.

- WebVan, Kozmo and Garden.com were all stuck with massive physical distribution infrastructure.
- Those commitments could not be unwound when revenue fell.
- He says he has several more anecdotes along these lines, but "the stories are all the same."

## Leasing buildings as a vanity growth signal

A real estate broker once told Dalton about a publicly traded dotcom that leased two or three complete buildings in San Francisco, with nowhere near enough employees to fill them.

- The company did this to show growth in leased square footage as a leading indicator of revenue and headcount growth.
- The leases were treated as "proof" that it would hit its earnings projections.
- Dalton's reaction is to ask the reader to "think about this for a minute": the lease obligation was being used to signal growth rather than to serve growth that had already happened.

## Frugal real estate at Google and Facebook

Dalton contrasts the dotcom excess with how Google and Facebook handled space.

- Google leased cheap, crappy real estate next to the freeway in Mountain View. It eventually moved into nicer offices originally built by the once high-flying SGI.
- Facebook was once spread across a bunch of small offices in downtown Palo Alto. It later moved into the former headquarters of Sun Microsystems.
- In both cases the company took space that a previous high-flier had built, after it had grown into it, rather than building or buying ahead of its growth.

## Long-term real estate obligations are the one lever you cannot pull

Dalton's core rule: when the "sht hits the fan," there are several levers you can pull to manage cash burn, but "significant long-term real estate obligations are a one-way ticket to bankruptcy court."

- Most costs can be cut when revenue disappears.
- Long-term lease or purchase commitments cannot be cut in the same way, so they turn a survivable downturn into a terminal one.

## The "edifice complex"

Dalton asks why this mistake gets made "time and time again." He cites a dotcom CEO he knew who called the phenomenon of hot companies building or buying huge buildings and decking them with expensive furnishings and signage "the edifice complex."

- He treats the phrase as a glib thing to say in conversation, but says there is "biting truth" in it.
- The pattern is that a hot, well-funded company spends on a showpiece headquarters at exactly the moment it is least able to judge what it will need or afford later.
