## Success is what triggers lawsuits in music and streaming content

- Dalton says that if you work with music, "you're going to get sued. It's just a fact of life." Streaming content also draws patent trolls, as it does for everyone else.
- Founders often assume that no label email so far means the labels are fine with what they're doing, or that they like the "discovery platform." Dalton says the labels won't come talk to you until you have money and are successful.
- Labels keep a queue of startups they track, and they take screenshots. He says: "you just never want to get to the top of that queue," because once you do, they focus on you.
- Raising money and getting traffic are the moments the suit arrives.

## M&A is basically impossible for music startups

- Without label deals, no acquirer will buy you. The huge legal liabilities would pass to the buyer, so the big company would be on the hook for a lot of money.
- With label deals, no one will buy you either, because the deals aren't transferable.
- Every label deal he has seen gives the labels a right of first refusal to pull the deal if they dislike the acquisition.
- He compares it to needing approval from all four major labels on top of your own board. The labels would look at the economics of the deal and decide whether they are fairly compensated.
- His conclusion: either way, an exit is tough.

## If you depend on someone else's content, you don't control the asset

- This applies beyond music, to any large content company.
- If your survival depends on content someone else controls, "you effectively are not in control of the asset. It's not actually your company." You are a proxy through which they hold the asset.
- If the people who control the content don't like what you're doing, they can take you down or destroy you. He says he would not recommend that position to anyone.

## Why the labels' behavior is rational

- Dalton says he didn't understand the labels until he spent a lot of time with them.
- Their industry went from about $15 billion in annual gross revenue and has been cut roughly in half. Executives (SVPs/EVPs) are paid to "fix" it while it shrinks every year.
- They are emotional about it: their friends keep getting laid off, and the labels have layoffs every few months. He describes real people with families whose entire industry is being burnt down.
- Their equation requires bringing in several billion dollars a year. A ramen-profitable startup that might make a few thousand dollars next year, pitching "get big first, focus on users, monetize later, Google AdWords in the meantime," isn't worth the salary cost of the conversation.
- So they hunt for the big win, "the touchdown pass, the buzzer beater," rather than licensing many small companies. He says he doesn't want their job and has sympathy for them.

## Structural incentives behind the "money transfer scheme"

- For several years, music startups (and video content sites) have effectively been money transfer schemes. Money flows from VC funds (who take their fees) to startups to labels.
- It's good for the labels' bottom line. The margins on these cash deals are very good, and they don't have to pay the artists out of the advances.
- Label staff are paid for short-term cash, much like Wall Street. He compares it to the real estate bubble, where people optimized for their own short-term bonuses with no incentive to think long term.
- His view is that this is why labels now chase billion-dollar deals instead of licensing three-person startups, and he says he agrees with that choice at this point.

## His proposed fix: legal music APIs and statutory licensing

- Developers need broadly available, legal music APIs to build on.
- He tried to provide one at imeem: he got a license and shipped a music/video API. It wasn't successful and is no longer on, and "people can't really build a company when something doesn't exist."
- He believes Congress has to get involved, as it did for Pandora and webcasters, with statutory licensing: a clear checklist, published rates, and a claim of the statutory license.
- This would also stop long-tail rights holders (not owned by the labels, like the Beatles) from suing even after you've done label deals. They want their own publishing and masters, so they need to be in the statutory system too.
- It has to work internationally. Every European country has a different licensing body, each wanting its own advances and deal structures.
- His summary: the solution is similar to software, where the government has to get involved.
