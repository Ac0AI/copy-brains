## Pivoting is part of the earliest stage of a startup

- Dalton defines a pivot as simply changing your idea. Strictly, a "true" pivot is a real company with users and funding shutting down one product to build another. His example is Slack, which had raised money and had about 100 employees on the video game Glitch before shutting it down.
- He says that pre-launch or just after launch, changing ideas should feel lightweight. "If you're not in a state where you're rapidly changing ideas or assumptions over and over and over again in quick succession, you are likely doing it wrong. You are likely moving too slow."
- He argues most founders take too long to pivot, not too short.
- He says founders should own the decision. They should not ask a YC partner or other authority figure to tell them when to give up. "It's your life and if it works out good or bad, that's on you." Advisors can only give guidance.

## Why pivot: opportunity cost and more shots on goal

- Dalton's main reason to pivot is opportunity cost: you can only really work on one thing at a time, so working on something with evidence it isn't working costs you the chance to work on something else.
- He wrote a joke formula: (how well things are working ÷ months of concerted full-time effort). If that is lower than (excitement to work on something else + confidence you can find something better), pivot. The driver is months spent without it working.
- Months of effort matter. A couple of weeks in, the signal is weak. Many months with nothing is a strong signal.
- Pivoting gives more shots on goal at product market fit. Dalton says most people never get PMF. You know you have it "when growth is not your biggest problem. It's other stuff."
- He says it is much easier to be lucky with half a dozen shots than one, so multiple shots "create luck for yourself". The shots must be high quality: make something, complete it, ship it, and get it to people. Pivoting through ideas without really launching them doesn't count.

## Good and bad reasons to pivot

Good reasons:
- You hate working on it.
- It isn't growing and nothing is happening.
- You are not a good fit for the idea.
- You depend on an external factor you don't control, such as mainstream VR headset adoption or mainstream crypto adoption.
- You are out of ideas on how to make it work.
- You know in your heart it isn't working. Dalton says many founders secretly know and keep up a front. "You know more than anyone else about your business."
- The idea is impossible to start, such as "once we raise $100 million, then we will build a prototype".
- You have launched and spent weeks or months trying to get users and it feels hopeless.

Bad reasons:
- Running away from hard work. Some founders build a product and pivot right when it is time to sell, repeatedly.
- Being a chronic idea-changer. He recommends following something through all the way.
- Chasing a hot new thing because of a TechCrunch article or someone else's funding round.

## Why founders take too long to pivot

- Loss aversion: once you have invested, it is hard to let go.
- A little traction, such as a few users or one customer, makes you think "maybe it'll work".
- Politeness mistaken for traction. People rarely say they will never be a customer. They say "this is great, add a few more features and come back." Founders can loop on this for years without getting customers.
- Fear of admitting weakness or defeat.
- Blaming customers or investors, with the belief that "the world is wrong" and the idea is ahead of its time.
- Inspirational messages that say believe hard enough and keep going.

**The "uncanny valley of product market fit":** Dalton noticed at YC that a company whose idea totally fails immediately has a weird advantage over one with a little traction. The first can write off the idea, with no second-guessing, and move on. A little traction can trap talented people for long periods.

**Anecdotes:** He says stories of founders who persisted for 5 years and then won are true but not actionable, like lottery winners. He prefers advice that plays the statistics and takes accountability.

## How to find a better idea to pivot to

- Pick something you are more excited about, that makes you more optimistic about the world and more excited to wake up and work on.
- A counterintuitive corollary: a perceived harder idea is often better. Boring ideas like ad networks, ad tech and affiliate or ad targeting "never work" because nobody is inspired by them. His example is a batch company that moved from ad tech to a "Robin Hood for India". "The founders lit up" and the excitement was contagious. It was the same founders; the change was a real idea they cared about.
- Make an honest, self-aware assessment of what you are good and bad at, and play to your strengths.
- Choose something you can build and validate very quickly, not a year or two of R&D. Pivoting from one thing that is impossible to ship to another is a bad sign.
- It is fine to build a business that won't raise venture capital, since most businesses don't need it. If you do want VC, the idea matters a lot, and founders get frustrated trying to raise for things that aren't fundable.

**Rules of thumb for VC-fundable ideas** (he says there is no official guidebook):
- You can imagine hundreds of millions or billions in net revenue per year.
- You can imagine getting there in less than 10 years, or 5, and fast.
- You can picture it as a publicly traded company someday.
- Technology is a key component and the founders build it, at least early on.
- High margins, such as 70-80% gross margins.
- He adds that Shark Tank-style businesses are mostly not venture fundable.

## When to pivot, and the cost of pivoting too much

- Pivot as soon as you have launched and spent weeks or months trying to get users and it feels hopeless, or the idea can't be started without huge capital, or you know in your heart it isn't working.
- Whiplash from pivoting over and over is bad because it makes founders sad and want to quit, which kills the company. Dalton says this is more deadly than working on a mediocre idea you enjoy: "If you're having fun working on a bad idea, you won't give up." If you hate your life working on your startup, you will not succeed. Aim for a happy medium between pivoting daily for six weeks and working on the same idea for five years.
- Avoid having employees while pivoting. It slows you down and makes them sad. Dalton would add people only after the idea is working and you have confidence. Scaling a team while unsure of the idea is not best practice.

## Scoring ideas: Dalton's 1-10 quality score, with YC examples

He made up a subjective score for the lecture: rate four criteria 1-10 and average them.

1. **How big it seems.** Is it an obvious publicly traded company (a car company, a bank) or at the Subway-franchise or eBay-import end of the spectrum?
2. **Founder-market fit.** 10 means you worked on self-driving cars through undergrad and your career and now start a self-driving car company. 0 means a startup in advanced AI when you don't know how to program.
3. **How easy it is to get started.** Dalton thinks this is undersold. Many good ideas never work because founders can't find a way to start, then someone later has a better way in and wins. Finding an idea that is easy to start is as important as the idea itself.
4. **Early market feedback.** Do people want it immediately and is the sale straightforward, or is it impossible?

**Brex (YC17)** (named in the transcript only as "BS"; the details match the credit card for startups pivot, and the figures below are as Dalton gave them):
- Before: a VR hardware headset for doing work. Big 6/medium-big, founder-market fit 1/10 (fintech software people with no hardware or optics knowledge), ease of start 2 (needed hardware hires, millions of dollars for a prototype, years of manufacturing), market feedback bad because no one wanted it. Overall 2.5/10.
- After: a credit card for startups. Size 10/10 (many public fintech comps like Square and Stripe), founder-market fit 10 (they had built and sold a fintech company in Brazil for about $20-30 million, and could code and ship it themselves), ease of start 3 (launching a credit card is hard, but the fit made it manageable), market feedback 8 (in the batch, people said yes to being customers).
- Result as Dalton states it: raised hundreds of millions and worth billions in two years. He calls it an outlier and "the most epic pivot of all time", with him having a front-row seat.

**Retool (W17):**
- Before: Venmo for the UK. Seemed big, founder-market fit 3, ease of start 7 (already launched with users), market feedback 3 (no one wanted to pay and they lost money on every transaction). Dalton says this was the insidious kind of traction that made it hard to give up.
- After: a tool for building internal tools. Size 10 (he says 80% of software built is for internal consumption), founder-market fit 10 (one founder had built something similar in a college internship), ease of start 10 (built in two weeks and got the first customers), market feedback 5 (interest but people were unsure about trusting a startup, and it did not fly off the shelves).

**Magic (W15):**
- Before: a blood pressure coach app. Size 2, founder-market fit 2 (no health knowledge), ease of start 8 (built quickly and got users quickly), market feedback poor. People were polite and usage was horrible.
- After: built a prototype in a weekend, posted it on Hacker News where it hit number one with about 2,000 upvotes, went viral overnight and got lots of press. They had about five ideas, and Dalton says nobody could have predicted which would capture the imagination. He credits it as a "shots on goal" example and an inadvertent inspiration for chatbot clones. They built a profitable, sustainable company.

**Segment (S11):**
- Before: a classroom feedback tool where students push a button to tell the professor they are confused. Founder-market fit about 5 (they were students), easy to build, and decent feedback with a bunch of schools adopting it. It took years to discard.
- After: the analytics data collection tool. At the time it wasn't obviously a future public company. Founder-market fit was great because they had built so much analytics and were experts. They open sourced it, gave it away for free, and the market begged them to support it. "If the market's begging for something and you don't even think it's good and they have to convince you it's good, that's a pretty good sign." Segment pivoted multiple times, including years after the batch, and is now worth billions.
