## Why most startup advice contradicts itself, and how Dalton filters it

Dalton argues that the dirty secret of startup advice blogposts is that, aside from the placebo effect, the advice is "not terribly useful." If you tried to synthesize all available advice into one coherent framework, you would find the aggregate "advice framework" deeply self-contradictory.

- He compares this to cold-reading techniques used by "mind-readers," especially the "Rainbow Ruse": a statement that awards someone a trait and its opposite at once (e.g. "mostly shy and quiet, but when the mood strikes you, you can easily become the center of attention").
- Using such techniques he says he could make believable-sounding statements about entrepreneurship, hiring, fundraising or big companies.
- He does not think advice writers do this intentionally. He reads it as a sign of a real truth about startups and life.

## Comfort with contradiction as a core founder skill

Dalton's central claim: "nothing is black-and-white, and in fact, much of succeeding in life consists of being comfortable sitting with contradictory information."

- The discomfort of holding conflicting information is called Cognitive Dissonance.
- He notes that some psychologists see most personality disorders as symptoms of coping strategies for dissonance.
- A highly functioning person resolves it constructively: acquiring knowledge and experience, expecting a fair world to reward hard work.
- Every founder feels the "squishy and complicated" uncertainty. In his words, "everyone feels this way, it's just that some work harder than others to cover it up."

## Why people crave linear narratives and simple rules

Dalton describes why founders consume startup advice: to quiet the fear that they are not sure what they are doing and that things look shinier and easier when others do them.

- People build linear narratives that promise three comforts:
  1. There is a reason things happened the way they did.
  2. The reason is knowable and easily understandable.
  3. The learnings can be digested from a 1000-word blogpost.
- The implied promise is that success is easily attainable: follow 5 simple rules, find a co-founder, or raise an angel round, and everything gets "way easier."
- He says anxious people "eat this stuff up" because it cures the uncertainty in the pit of their stomachs.

## How to keep your advice filter in good working order

His practical rules for reading advice:

- Beware of black-and-white thinkers.
- Beware of people who present very simple narratives as the explanation of incredibly complex events.
- Don't take anything you read too literally.
- Accept that much of what you feel will be squishy and complicated.

He points to two sources that capture the real, conflicted experience of building a company:

- Ben Horowitz's post "The Struggle," on his conflicted internal monologue as a CEO.
- Peter Thiel's Stanford CS183 class notes (class 18), on the extreme and contradictory traits founders exhibit.

## The mantra for smoothing out the emotional roller-coaster

Dalton keeps a self-contradicting mantra in the back of his mind to remind himself of the shades of grey and the inherent contradictions in any situation:

> "Things are much worse than they seem, also, things are much better than they seem."

- Its purpose is to smooth the emotional roller-coaster.
- He stresses that emotions "are just as dangerous when things are going well as they are when things are going poorly."
- Applied in practice: don't over-celebrate good stretches or over-panic in bad ones, because both readings are partly true.
