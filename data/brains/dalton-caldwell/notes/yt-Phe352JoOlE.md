## "What if Google does this?" and the turkey-graph risk

- Dalton recalls being a founder and rolling his eyes when investors asked "Oh, what if Google does this?" He calls it the cop-out question every investor asks, and admits this line of thinking can annoy founders.
- He argues AI changes the answer: models keep getting smarter and faster, so some startups are implicitly betting the models won't improve.
- He asks whether a founder's business is "turkey graph" shaped: customers and revenue look great until a lab moves into the space.

## Building around model weaknesses is a short bet on the model

- Dalton's example: building "bread middleware" to make models work in use cases where they're deficient. In his words, that is "the definition of a turkey startup", because a new model drops and it's game over.
- His analogy: building "googledogs.com" because Google Search is bad at searching for kinds of dogs. That is a bet that Google won't get better.
- He summarizes it as making a short bet on the model provider, and calls that a bad bet.
- He frames the diagnostic question for founders: how do you know whether you're exposed or can feel okay?

## Which businesses are safest

- Dalton asks which businesses are safest from the labs and from clones, and who needs to worry least.
- He asks what a founder should do who is early enough to pivot.
- He asks what a larger company should do, using the example of a Series B SaaS company. When told "panic", he pushes: "You got to do more than that."

## The internet as the best comparison for AI

- Dalton calls the internet his best comp. He allows that AI may be bigger than the internet, "we'll see".
- Incumbents hit by the internet were slow to react. His examples are Barnes & Noble and Blockbuster, which faced internet-native competitors.
- Others adapted and survived. His example is the big banks (Bank of America, Wells Fargo), which stayed okay despite online banking changing their business.
- He asks which categories were totally hosed by the internet, offering music stores as a first guess.
- He concludes that businesses selling media had no move other than pivoting into a different line of business.

## Pure software moats

- Dalton's conclusion: pure software businesses whose moat is "that it's hard to build" will have a bad time.
- He also asks which businesses did fine when the internet arrived. Car companies are the example in the discussion, where the internet's distribution effects didn't touch them.

## Energy and compute as the bottleneck

- Dalton argues the bottleneck to doing interesting things is energy. Early computers were limited by processing power and RAM.
- His example: Bill Gates and his peers were trying to do almost impossible things by laying out every single byte. Nobody worries about that now.
- As energy and compute become more abundant, the scale of what you can do easily gets bigger. He says this runs all the way to colonizing space and building worlds on Mars.
- He asks the discussion to paint a picture of limitless free energy, and ends on "I like that" when the "every problem is a physics problem" framing comes up.
