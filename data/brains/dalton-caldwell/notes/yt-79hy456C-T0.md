## Revenue multiples are driven by expected future cash flows, not current revenue

Dalton explains that valuation comes from discounted cash flows: what a company will earn in 5, 10 and 15 years, not just today.
- A business with strong cash today but a declining market deserves a low multiple, because its future cash flows are low.
- A high-growth company with little profit today can deserve a high multiple if it is believed to become hugely valuable.
- He frames the practical question for a founder as: how much money will this business make in 10 or 20 years?

## Why startups should care about public-market multiples

Dalton says everyone in startup land is affected by the stock market, even if public prices feel far away.
- Public companies in a startup's category are the comparables it gets valued against.
- Right now many SaaS companies trade at very low revenue multiples. He says this hurts SaaS startups through a "downwind effect" on their valuations.
- The opposite held in the 2021 stock market bubble, when high public multiples led startups to raise at very high valuations.
- Public companies show the spread: one may trade at 1x revenue and another at a thousand times revenue.

## The Tesla lesson: valuing a market, not a product

Dalton uses Tesla to show why multiples diverge.
- Viewed only as a car company, it should trade like one. Car companies get low multiples because they face competition, have limited margins, and are not expected to grow 10x. GM will not sell 100 times as many cars.
- For a long time Tesla was worth more than every other car company combined, which drove many experienced short sellers "crazy".
- He argues the market may have been rational. The upside is autonomy (robotaxis) and humanoid robots for labor replacement, a market he calls effectively unlimited in size.
- The stock acts like a call option on those outcomes. If Tesla were only a niche car maker, he says it would be a terrible investment.

## AI-enabled services companies: why they get valued like the business they replace

Dalton flags a risk in the hot new startup category: AI-native versions of traditional service businesses, such as an AI-native law firm that looks like a law firm to the customer but uses AI models underneath.
- Legacy service companies trade at low multiples of revenue or cash flow, perhaps 1x or 2x depending on the business.
- At a 2x multiple, a company with $1 million in annual revenue is worth $2 million.
- Founders must be aware of this and bring proof points and a story for why they should be valued as a technology company with technology multiples, not as the service company they are disrupting.

## Finite market versus infinite market

Dalton's core test for whether a startup deserves a high multiple is whether it is growing into a finite or an unlimited market.
- A market that is flat or shrinking is unexciting for investors. A market that may be 10x or 100x larger in future has unlimited growth potential.
- Dentistry example: great products exist, but he would not expect dental spend to grow 10x in 10 years.
- Security example: AI makes securing systems more urgent, so he can imagine security growing 10x in 10 years, which is a far more exciting market.
- Healthcare: fighting over a fixed pie, such as squeezing money from insurance claims, is a capped game. Creating something new, like longevity, taps near-infinite budget because people will pay almost anything to live longer with higher quality of life.
- Advice to founders: ask how your market grows and whether AI and related trends will make it larger or smaller. If it is shrinking, consider a new direction.

## SpaceX: turning a finite market into an infinite one

Dalton uses SpaceX to show how a founder can change the market-size answer.
- Early on, people were bearish because the global launch market was small, and that was true.
- Elon fixed this by creating his own demand with Starlink, which gives him a reason to launch rockets over and over. Dalton says the majority of satellites in space are now SpaceX satellites.
- The market became effectively everything off Earth, including data centers in space, and only SpaceX has the rockets.
- By contrast, he says you would never want to invest in ULA (the traditional launch business) at a high multiple, since it is a fixed-size business now being destroyed.
- He notes it did not "hit people over the head" at first. Many said SpaceX was not different, so it takes creative insight to articulate the difference.

## Practical checklist for founders raising money

- Look at the big players in your space and ask whether any are worth $100 billion or $200 billion. If the incumbents you hope to displace are worth single-digit billions, that is a red flag, and you must explain why you will be worth 100x as much.
- Ask ChatGPT for the comparables of your company and see how they are valued. He notes that for many startups the comps that come up are a company just acquired by private equity for about $400 million, or a very small-cap company. That may sound like a lot but "that's not great".
- Do this research on comparables and how they are valued before you pitch.
- If the comps are not great, build a really good story for why you will be worth far more than them.
- Revenue alone does not decide the outcome. Standard Capital sometimes passes on a company with $5 million of revenue and funds one with almost none, because market size and growth matter more.
