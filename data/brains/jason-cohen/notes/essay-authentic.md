## Authenticity beats polish: why customers forgive humans and refund corporations

Jason argues the expectation of customers has shifted. They no longer demand "those assholes better not ever screw up." They expect a company to try hard, care, and treat them well when it inevitably does screw up.

- His example: a customer said of a hosting company, "I want someone else making sure the server doesn't go down. Or, if it does go down, I want someone to apologize to me."
- Ten years earlier, he says, the same sentence would have ended "someone to scream at" or "give me a refund."
- His summary: "People readily forgive sincere human error, but become adversarial and distrustful with the typical, sterile customer/provider relationship."
- He says small startups are especially suited to exploit this, and incumbents cannot.

## What earns forgiveness (and what doesn't)

Jason says this is not a free pass to abuse customers and then publish a faux-heartfelt "open letter from the CEO." Three things have to be true:

- **You're doing your best by customers**, shown continuously in all communication (blog, tech support, website), not just after a crisis.
- **You're learning from mistakes**, shown by problems trending toward the esoteric and by apologies that explain what steps prevent this and similar classes of error.
- **You're doing everything in your power to be the best**, shown by a culture of awesome employees and new ways to make customers successful, so mistakes read as ordinary human error, not negligence or indifference.

He adds that the apology itself isn't the point. No one is convinced by an insincere, legally-vetted "official apology" that fixes nothing. What customers really want is to work with people who behave like real people, who are obviously trying, and who respond to problems earnestly and quickly.

## Why "be human" is hard advice to follow

Jason jokes that "be human" and "be authentic" are standard advice that nobody explains how to apply ("Compared to what, being feline?"). "Just be yourself" is equally vague, since Thales said the hardest thing is "Know Thyself" (and the easiest is "To Give Advice").

He rejects the heavyweight route of synthesizing your life experience and ten-year goals into a four-word home page philosophy ("I can't even decide what to have for lunch"). He offers three practical ways to discover your point of view instead.

## Three practical ways to find your voice

- **Criticize others.** When you love someone's slogan, ask why: funny, clever, specific, unwavering, simple, confident, ballsy? When you loathe an "About Us" page, ask why: too personal, not personal enough, too formal, childish, arrogant? Complete the sentence: "I absolutely [love|hate] that because ...."
- **Decide what you are not.** Corporate behavior that annoys you tells you what to avoid and what to do instead. Examples he gives: "I hate companies who use formal language," or the opposite, "I hate companies who think it's funny and clever to use informal language; I'm going to instill confidence by showing that we behave like grown-ups."
- **Copy something you love.** You love it because you identify with it, so copying gives a head start. Treat the original as "a rough draft that needs extensive editing but whose heart is in the right place." The goal is mimicry, not theft; influence, not carbon-copy. Over time it morphs into something unique.

## Strong choices alienate some people, and that's required

Jason says every strong decision will alienate many people while thrilling others. His examples:

- Informal style: some find it refreshing, others find you untrustworthy.
- Proactively announcing bugs: some gracefully tolerate problems, others are "shocked" and tweet that you sell shoddy software.
- Admitting the company is two people: some expect primo customer service, others flee because you might not be around next year.
- Cursing on your blog: many click "Back," others laugh and click "Subscribe."

His conclusion: be "strong, specific, and honest." Turning off some people is the price, and the remainder will love you more and tell their friends. He says this wins far more customers than people think.

## The case against having no persona

Jason frames the alternative bluntly: with no persona, "why would anyone get excited about you? Why would they put up with your faults? Why would they tell their friends about you?"

- A persona is what makes customers tolerate faults and spread word of mouth.
- His closing line: "Is your goal to become a soulless corporation? No? Well then, do whatever it takes to be soulful."
