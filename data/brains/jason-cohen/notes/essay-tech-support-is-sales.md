## Tech support as the face of the company

Jason argues tech support is the only human interaction most customers ever have with a company, so it is the brand, the positioning and a sales channel. He rejects the view of support as the bottom of the food chain (angry customers, answering phones, no power to fix things) and says that attitude means missing channels for marketing, product development and sales. His analogy: David Beckham looks like the "quintessential picture of manly sportif" but his voice is a surprise nobody forgets. A home page is the public face only until someone "opens their mouth", and the question is what happens when the company's bullet points collide with its behavior.
- "Are you really going to leave that up to your worst-treated, least-paid, least-qualified employees?"

## Tech support is sales: how Smart Bear sold without "sales"

At Smart Bear, Jason's company, he says they made millions of dollars in both individual and enterprise sales, including six-figure deals, without "sales" in the usual sense. By that he means the processes, personalities and management single-mindedly focused on hauling in revenue on a quarterly schedule. There was no golf and no Salesforce. Their tech support was the sales function.
- His definition of the purpose of tech support: "to make your customers fantastic at their jobs, which happen to involve your product." He credits the idea to Kathy Sierra.
- Make the customer awesome and she gives you money so she can keep being awesome. "That's sales."

## How to run support so customers become more successful

Jason's tactics for turning support into customer enablement:
- Don't just locate a menu command. Find out what the customer is trying to accomplish and help with that.
- Don't just explain a feature. Help them use the result to impress their boss.
- Don't just apologize for a missing feature. Help them work around it and succeed anyway.
- Support staff know the product and problem space better than customers, so making customers far more successful than they would be on their own is "not that hard."
- Enabling customers is about the entire company, not only the product.

## Low expectations make support a cheap way to delight customers

Everyone's stereotype of support is negative: a reboot as the answer to a font question, up-sells during a billing-address change, phone menu mazes that ask for the account number three times. Jason's view is that when customers expect "a turd sandwich" and get "a turkey club with chipotle aioli", they earn the company outsized credit.
- Observed results: users tweeting about the service, people switching to a service because of its support, and customers following a company's tweets and telling their own followers to do the same.
- He calls this a better "social media outreach" program than hiring interns to spray comments on random blogs.
- Support has "under-promise" built in. Even acting like a human being puts a company ahead, and answering email with a non-automated response is "killing it."
- A pleasant surprise is rare in business, so being the company where it happens every day is a cheap opportunity.

## Support as the closest thing to getting "outside the building"

Steve Blank's advice is that everything you need to know about customers is outside the building: talking face to face and watching them react. Jason agrees, but notes that a bootstrapper or "work-a-preneur" with no travel budget is at the desk most of the time (he says 94%). For them, tech support is the next-best source of customer development, because it is where people complain about what is broken, missing or confusing.
- Cataloging problems is not enough. The insight is in the meta-questions.
- To answer them you have to go back and forth with customers to reach the root cause.
- You need to read hundreds of emails to get a gut feel for what customers experience, which he says you cannot get from an "Incident Summary Report" or similar automation.

## Meta-questions to ask about every support confusion

When a customer is confused, the immediate job is to set them straight, but Jason lists questions that turn it into product development.
- Tactical:
  - What caused the confusion?
  - Is the customer's world-view different from mine?
  - Is our terminology wrong?
  - Are we using the wrong metaphors?
  - Do I need to optimize the new-user experience instead of the expert-user experience?
- Strategic:
  - Does this hiccup reveal a customer pain point I didn't know existed but can solve?
  - Is there enough evidence of a conceptual mismatch that I should pivot?
  - Is there a new product idea here?
  - If customers are abusing my product to get what they really want, can I provide that from the start?
- Example: the last line of questioning is how Smart Bear came to be a company about peer code review and not "version control data mining." Jason says that without it "you wouldn't be reading this right now."
- His conclusion: tech support is "the closest, most honest chance for product development", more straightforward than going through traditional sales, because it is where real users discover and report on the product.
