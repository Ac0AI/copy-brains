## Jason's Needs Stack framework

- Jason says every customer action sits in a "stack" of needs. Each lower item is a means to a higher end.
- Example: Charlie buys AWS infrastructure only to get a WordPress site, which is only for a personal website. That website is for a book deal, which is for becoming a famous speaker.
- He notes that Charlie "never wanted to set up infrastructure, and still doesn't."
- Format: "★ top need <br/> → next need down → ... → bottom need."
- He distinguishes it from Jobs to Be Done and Five Whys. His point is the competitive insight: a product at a higher level makes the levels below it obsolete.
- Moral: "You need to understand the Needs Stack of your target customers", not just your market, product, or immediate job.

## Competitors you don't see: higher-level products make lower levels vanish

- WP Engine sets up a WordPress site in about one minute, with no infrastructure to think about. It handles monitoring and WordPress-specific support. Charlie buys it and "disappears from the cloud infrastructure market."
- AWS would name GCP, Azure and Digital Ocean as competitors. Jason argues WP Engine is also a competitor for Charlie's business, even though it buys infrastructure from AWS, GCP and Azure.
- The same applies up the stack:
  - Wix and Squarespace skip "get WordPress."
  - Substack skips "build a website" with tools for long-form writing, a mailing list and social promotion.
  - Speaker bureaus and services like SpeakerMatch skip the website, years of writing and the book deal.
- You always want to stop at the level you operate on, "but there's always another level."
- Lower-level businesses survive. WP Engine is 16 years old and healthy, and AWS posted $5.2B profit in one quarter. Jason says higher-level products only win customers who value speed over flexibility.
- Data he cites: 43% of the top 10M websites use WordPress, with Shopify next at 4%. Over one million sites run on WP Engine.

## The core tradeoff: speed up the stack, flexibility down the stack

- "Up the stack, customers achieve their end-goal faster; down the stack, customers have more flexibility and customization."
- Substack vs WordPress example:
  - WordPress is far more configurable, so sites can look unique, while Substack newsletters all look alike.
  - WordPress has far better long-form tools, such as drag-and-drop layouts that differ for phone, tablet and laptop. Email newsletters can't do that.
  - If the customer values those things, WordPress is still right.
- Rule: if ease of reaching the need matters more than customization, the higher-level product makes all lower levels obsolete.
- WP Engine doesn't let you customize infrastructure, so customers who need that still buy from AWS.

## Messaging: features at the customer's level, benefits one level up

- Jason resolves "sell benefits, not features" with the stack. A customer thinks at one level at a time, so an engineer shopping for a database wants specs, and someone shopping for a socket wrench wants "a 5/16" lock nut" and "has a mounted light."
- Google searches are "a good proxy for 'what level of the stack are potential customers operating at right now?'" Charlie might search "best way to publish online," then later "how to get a WordPress site."
- Tactic:
  - Meet the customer where they are and talk features there. For "get a WordPress site," that means showing how you are the best way to get one.
  - Sell the benefit one level higher: "Become famous online with our turn-key, customizable WordPress website."
  - Substack can't say "customizable," but can say "Grow your own audience with our turn-key newsletter platform."
- Jason notes Substack's homepage leads with higher-level benefits ("be independent," "get paid for writing") and lists same-level features lower down.

## Positioning: make the levels below you irrelevant

- Fight at the level where you win. Substack would lose a "get a website" fight, so it fights at "build an audience."
- Message: "While others just give you a website, we help you build a following." It won't work on businesses that need a website, but it can suit the aspirational speaker.
- WP Engine does this to cloud services. They say "click 17 times to make a server," then install server software, configure WordPress, set up a paid caching network, add plugins, patch security and learn SSH. WP Engine says "You don't want infrastructure, you want a WordPress site, and in less than one minute, you'll have exactly that."
- WP Engine adds that it is faster and more secure than doing it yourself, and has a support line cloud services don't.
- Summary: "make 'infrastructure' irrelevant by focusing on the next level up."

## Moving up the stack yourself: barriers and partial strategies

- Jason says you can disrupt yourself and competitors by moving up, but companies "almost never" overcome the barriers:
  - **Different product and business.** You must rebrand, build expertise and build the product. Pricing may change, such as from per-gigabyte to per-site monthly, pay-for-performance subscribers, or a percentage of speaking fees. Mature companies may buy the product to trade cash for speed.
  - **The next level may not be possible.** Software can't guarantee an audience or force people to subscribe.
  - **A smaller market.** Few people share Charlie's exact stack. WordPress sites serve many personas (individuals, brands, media, ecommerce, communities, non-profits, governments), each with a different level above. Narrowing is a tried-and-true strategy but may not suit an established broad brand.
- Partial strategies:
  - **Sub-brands or products:** keep the original business, launch vertical or niche products that use the parent's scale, and treat each as its own startup.
  - **Add-on features:** an extension aimed at personas sharing that next level, without fully committing to it.
  - **Better marketing:** educate without promising results, with field guides, content ideas, partner promotion and customer success stories.
- Successful full transitions he cites are the "Emotional vs Functional" case studies in Blue Ocean Strategy: Cemex selling "build an addition to your house, for your family" instead of cement, and Starbucks selling a lifestyle experience.

## Metrics and purpose from the levels above you

- Measure three layers:
  - The bottom of the stack tells you whether you're operating well.
  - The level the customer is actively thinking about tells you whether the product delivers its direct promise.
  - The very next level up is strategic for predicting long-run happiness. Not too far up, or it's hard to measure and external factors dominate.
- Shopify example:
  - A customer can create a store (the product works) but get no sales and churn.
  - Jason cites only 34% sticking around for even one year, with huge cancellation rates that "would sink most companies." Shopify survives because inbound volume is high and survivors grow a lot.
  - Shopify's primary success metric is GMV (gross merchandise value, the total customers sell through their stores). It reports GMV to Wall Street right after revenue, in the same large font.
  - GMV measures the next level up, which Shopify can't control but can measure and help with, via marketing articles and features like ShopPay to reduce cart abandonment.
- Purpose: direct goals like signups, activation, churn and costs create no higher purpose. Purpose lives in the upper stack: helping someone build a career, win respect, be a hero in their organization, raise money for a cause, or speak truth to power.
- Jason's advice is to tell those stories on the website and internally, and celebrate when it happens for a customer.
