## Hiring employee #1 means choosing between a lifestyle business and trying to get big

Jason says the first hire is a big decision, because what you're really deciding is whether to keep a lifestyle business or attempt to "cross the chasm" and maybe get rich. He treats employee #1 as a culture-setter, not "any old programmer or salesman".

- Employee #1 helps set the company culture and has to mesh with the founder's personality "100%".
- You'll put in long hours together. If they don't get your jokes, "it's not going to work".
- He compares it to "getting married" more than hiring an underling. The rules of HR don't apply, except the law.
- His closing advice is that going with your gut is not wrong.

## Hire for comfort with startup chaos, not for big-company habits

Jason argues startups are chaotic, rules change, and there is no "job description". The founder already lives by this "Code of Turmoil" because they have no choice. Most people abhor chaos and are used to big companies, so the hire must be comfortable with the bedlam.

- Someone leaving IBM is a fit only if they left over bureaucracy, tolerated incompetence and no visibility into what customers want.
- Red flags in an interview: asking how often you do performance reviews, or saying "I thrive in environments with clear requirements, written expectations, and defined processes". His advice is to run away.
- It's better to make a strong decision that turns out wrong, and admit it, than to plan ahead or wait for instructions.
- Prefer people who favor potential earnings (stock, performance bonuses) over guaranteed earnings (salary, benefits).

## Put your personality in the job description to filter candidates early

Jason says not to wait for the interview to test personality fit. Make the job description funny and reflective of your company's uniqueness.

- He points to the WP Engine jobs page for examples, covering culture, code quality and customers. Quoted lines:
  - "Being transparent about our strengths and weaknesses wins us sales"
  - "You think using a profiler is fun, like a treasure hunt"
  - "Whether or not you sleep at night is directly proportional to whether you've made something thrilled or pissed off that day"
- The test shows up in the cover letters. If someone still sends a generic cover letter after a posting like that, they're not for you. If they respond in kind, it's a good sign.

## Don't use recruiters on a bounty; their incentives are opposite to yours

Jason quotes Bryan Menell: "If you find yourself wanting to hire a recruiter, hit yourself in the head with a frying pan until the feeling goes away." He says you need an absolute superstar, and recruiters aren't in the business of finding superstars.

- Recruiters work like real estate salesmen. They earn the same amount whether it takes four days or four months.
- Every extra day, resume or interview lowers their income per hour. So the longer you take to find the right person, the more they push you to settle for someone you already rejected.
- The exception is a recruiter who works by the hour instead of a hiring bounty. These are hard to find, and he's had luck only in that case.

## Treat resumes as conversation starters, and require clear communication from everyone

Jason says resumes are useless for judging fit, since your own resume likely has nothing that qualifies you to run a company. They're useful only as talking points on a phone call: probe past experience and test claimed technology knowledge.

- Trick: dig deep on one detail. Pick the weirdest technology or an odd bullet from two jobs ago. Don't accept "It's been a while"; if they can't talk about it, they can't claim to bring that experience.
- Everyone must communicate clearly, even someone who will spend 60 hours a week writing code or solving differential equations.
- Reasons: in a modern startup everyone writes blog entries, tweets and posts on whatever comes next, and there's no layer between employees and customers.
- Everyone needs to be able to talk to customers and even sell to them. He reminds readers that "tech support is sales".
- Because everyone must decipher each other's nuances, part of the interview must include free-form writing.

## Screen applicants with a short writing test instead of reading resumes

When you post on large sites like Monster or Craigslist, expect a torrent of resumes (100 in a day isn't unusual). Jason says screening resumes is not an option, so he sends an email template asking for a few paragraphs on each question.

- His example questions:
  1. Why do you want to work at [company]?
  2. Describe a situation in your work-life where you failed.
  3. Describe a time when you accomplished something you thought was impossible (work or personal).
- Expected results: most people never respond ("Good riddance!"). Some give lazy answers like "I want to work at your company because I saw you are hiring" or "I have never failed at anything".
- He says to resist replying "You just did", because that's what assholes do.
- Roughly 10% actually answer. In two minutes you can tell whether the person can communicate, and whether they seem fun, intelligent or interesting.
- Exception: if a cover letter is truly wonderful, it's a rare great sign and you can skip to the phone interview.

## Hiring a good person takes 3-6 months, so hire constantly and be a place good people seek out

Jason's rule of thumb is that it takes 3-6 months to hire a really good person. If you start when you really need someone, it's too late.

- Why so long:
  - Good people are rare.
  - They change jobs at most about once a year, more likely every 3-4 years, so you must catch their window.
  - They get lots of offers, so by the time you finish the writing test, phone interview, in-person interview, compensation talk and written offer, they may have accepted elsewhere. He says this happened to him all the time at Smart Bear, and was happening at WP Engine.
- Good people choose where they work: they hear about a cool company and call you. So the company must be a place they seek out.
- His ideas for that:
  - Build a blog and Twitter following with a steady stream of readers.
  - Attend local meet-ups and user groups, and meet the organizer, who knows everyone worth knowing.
  - Sponsor a meet-up at your office, or co-sponsor with a company or co-working space if you have no office.
  - Example: OtherInbox sponsors the monthly Austin on Rails group and the annual Lone Star Ruby Conference, so the best Ruby developers in Austin already want to work there.
  - Ask friends for resumes of people they liked but didn't hire.
  - Make your Jobs page rank in local search, e.g. "java programmer job in austin tx", not "java programmer".
  - Take everyone you know to lunch and ask about candidates. In person brings out more than email, and sometimes the person you ask is interested himself (it's happened to him a few times).
