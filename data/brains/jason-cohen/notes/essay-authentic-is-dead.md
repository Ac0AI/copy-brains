## Jason's rule: say something specific, not a dead marketing phrase

Jason argues that stock marketing language is lazy, undifferentiated and should be retired. His list of phrases to stop using (except to mock them):
- "The Holy Grail of", "Designed with you in mind", "Putting customers first", "All new", "Win-win", "Proven track record"
- "Fast and easy", "The leading provider of", "And more", "For everyone", "The go-to choice for ___"
- "Transforming the way you ___", "The future of ___ is here", "___ is broken", "___ is dead", "___ sucks"
- "Saves you time, so you can get back to doing what you do best."

His point: "Say something meaningful, specific, evocative, so your website wins, and you can be proud of it."

## Why overused words stop working

- He lists words he says are "devoid of meaning": authentic, solution, turnkey solution, genuine, powerful, secure, simple, innovative, insight, revolutionary, unsurpassed, unparalleled, cutting-edge, world-class, state-of-the-art, game-changing, ground-breaking, value-add, disruptive.
- Words get corrupted when companies claim them without acting accordingly. A company that "puts customers first" but uses "Level 1 support" as a shield against customers shows that talk is cheap. A company claiming "secure" payments after 100,000 credit card numbers are stolen shows you don't need a permit to claim security.
- A company that calls itself "innovative and disruptive" but pitches an idea you've heard ten times that month reminds us that if you have to say it, it's probably not true.
- He cites that 78% of "About Us" pages claim to be "the leading provider" of something, so the claim no longer impresses.
- Quote: "repetition of even powerful, wonderful phrases will kill them." He compares it to an over-played song or an over-hyped restaurant.
- He concedes that "authentic" is theoretically good and that its champions drove a better give-first-sell-later ethic. But theory is "for people who don't need to sell $2,600 more software by next Friday so they can make rent." The misuse is too widespread, so retire the word.

## Tactic 1: Be specific

Many dead words never carried information. "Solution" just means product and/or service. Replace adjectives with concrete claims:
- Instead of "easy": "so straightforward, you won't need a manual."
- Instead of "inexpensive": "just a dollar a day."
- Instead of "powerful": "processes 6,253,427 requests daily."
- Instead of "secure": "blocks 96 million attacks daily."
- Instead of "disruptive": "72% of our customers say they'll never go back to a normal email client."
- Instead of "beloved": show your NPS, CSAT or G2 rating, or a massive wall of real, unsolicited testimonials like tweets.

## Tactic 2: Show, don't tell

Some dead words describe but don't paint a picture ("powerful" doesn't say how it changes my life). Showing beats describing:
- Fast: show a speed test, especially against competitors.
- Easy: a video of your tool solving someone's problem in 60 seconds flat.
- Responsive, intelligent tech support: put a "chat now" bar on every page of the website.
- Customers love you: show testimonials from 100 customers. No one will read them all, but you impress with sheer quantity.
- Innovative: show your change-log to demonstrate product velocity.
- Benefit bullet lists: quote actual customers describing your impact on their lives.
- Generic testimonials: reproduce unsolicited tweets that show genuine love and gratitude.
- Valuing customers: tell them to call you to test it, and answer the phone on the second ring.

## Tactic 3: Name and embrace the cliché

Jason's favorite way to start a sales pitch is to make fun of typical sales pitches. At Smart Bear he said:

"I know you were hoping for a 22-slide PowerPoint deck with our mission statement and company history. I'm really sorry to disappoint! 'Cause I'm just going to start the demo and let you interrupt me with questions."

And, on peer code review tools:

"People claim that peer code review tools will do magic things like make your developers smarter or fix existing social problems with the team. Actually, if anything, code review can magnify latent social issues! However, I do believe our tool will save you time and aggravation in these 4 specific ways [...], so as we go through the demo, see if you agree."

Why it works: saying the quiet part out loud, what others hide, especially when everyone knows the truth, earns credibility (and often laughs). Prospects then become more open to your claims, even well-worn ones.

## Tactic 4: Own the abused word completely

An overused word can still be used if you totally own the concept. Example: you can claim "legendary customer service" if you back it with:
- first-ring, human phone service
- online chat from your home page
- quick-response Twitter monitoring
- 15-minute turnaround on tech support emails, even at 3am on a Sunday

Communicate all of that too. If you lead with the dead phrase, the visitor leaves before you get to prove it.

## Tactic 5: Create fresh ideas by synthesis

When an idea becomes cliché, that is an implicit call for new ideas, so try to lead instead of follow. Jason admits this is hard but notes great ideas often aren't "ground-breaking, innovative, out-of-the-box, Earth-shattering epiphanies." Instead:
- "Often great ideas are a synthesis of other ideas" with a smidge of novel insight.
- Or they put into words what others sense but cannot articulate.

He calls this the hardest and most time-consuming way to break out of the mundane, but the most rewarding: customers who love you for it help you grow. His sign-off: "You're a little company, so act like one."
