## Genuine enthusiasm from the messenger beats polished marketing copy in a live relationship

- Jason's story: at a Korean restaurant, he asked the waiter the difference between bibimbap and dolsot bibimbap. The answer was only "Dolsot bibimbap better!" He ordered the dolsot, a dish served in a hot stone pot, which was $1 more and, in his words, "2x better."
- The rice gets crispy, the egg cooks, and the veggies and meat stay extra hot.
- Jason says the vague answer was "perfect," and that his own usual advice (be specific, differentiate, make every phrase meaningful) did not apply here.
- The reason: the answer was not in an ad, a datasheet or a "30-second elevator pitch mechanically regurgitated on a tradeshow floor." It came from a hard-working person who barely spoke the language but did his best to steer Jason to the right choice.
- His conclusion: "It's the messenger, not the message, that made the experience wonderful."

## Obsessive excitement is contagious and sells

- Jason states the principle: "Being obsessively and genuinely excited about something is contagious. And good sales. In any industry, with any product."
- A salesperson's real belief in the product persuades more than the exact words used.
- The waiter had skin in the game: he worked at the restaurant constantly, was probably related to the owner, and wanted the customer to pick the better, pricier option.
- The upsell worked because it was sincere: a customer who trusts that the person wants them to enjoy the meal will pay the extra $1 for a dish that is twice as good.

## Where specificity matters and where passion matters more

- Jason distinguishes channels. Websites are not relationships; they are "attention-getters and information-distributers." There, marketing copy must be specific and differentiated, as he argues in his other writing on specificity and differentiation.
- Once a human relationship begins, he says the most important thing is to be genuine in passion, knowledge and desire to make customers successful.
- He lists the situations where this applies: sales pitches, tech support and tradeshow booths.
- Practical takeaway: write precise, differentiated copy for the web, but staff live conversations with people who truly care about the product and the customer's success, rather than scripted pitch-reciters.

## Hire and prepare customer-facing people for authenticity, not scripts

- Implied tactic from Jason's example: scripts and memorized elevator pitches are weaker than a person who knows the product well and believes in it.
- The three traits he names for the human side of selling are passion, knowledge, and a desire to make customers successful.
- Even imperfect language skills did not matter, because the waiter's care came through and pushed the customer to the right choice.
