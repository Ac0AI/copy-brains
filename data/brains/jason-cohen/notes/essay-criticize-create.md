## Criticism is cheap, but dismissing it is just as costly

Jason argues that explaining why any business will fail is easy, and that being "right" is the wrong goal for an entrepreneur. He takes a hard stance against reflexive skepticism and a softer one against ignoring critics. In practice, that means building with humility and listening with "half an ear".

He also shows how easily a good business can be torn apart. His Netflix example, written in 2011 when Netflix still shipped DVDs, makes the point.

## Why armchair criticism is easy and useless

- Jason says he can "rip any business idea to shreds."
- His Netflix takedown: inventory logistics costs, millions of non-technical customers, the Postal Service, wear and delivery losses, no browsing gratification, movie duplication angering publishers, and Blockbuster copying the model and undercutting on price.
- The outcome: Blockbuster, which did copy the model, went bankrupt. Netflix turned a profit of $60m in the latest quarter, more than Blockbuster earned at its peak.
- If your goal is to be *right*, you should bet against every startup and every novel idea. You will be right far more often than wrong.
- He calls this "Right, but useless": it guarantees nothing new or great gets created.

## What the entrepreneur's goal actually is

- "The goal of the entrepreneur is not to be 'right.'" At least not about everything, all the time, from the start.
- The job is to construct with humility, hold strong opinions while seeking better ones, and infect customers and employees with your "peculiar disease."
- Live to fight another day, and acknowledge being wrong before the fault turns fatal.
- Even a "failed" venture never really fails. Jason has never met a "failed" entrepreneur who didn't say they were better off: new skills, clarity about what excites them each morning, a view of their limits, and more confidence in what they can do.

## Learning over being right: experiments and Francis Crick

- He ties this to the Lean Startup movement (the book came out the same year), which values learning and experience over being right and frames the work as "running experiments."
- He quotes Francis Crick, Nobel winner for the discovery of DNA: "The dangerous man is the one who has only one idea, because then he'll fight and die for it. The way real science goes is that you come up with lots of ideas, and most of them will be wrong."
- Real science is mostly being wrong while staying vigilant and honest enough to keep going until you aren't. Dating and startups work the same way.

## Every craft wants to be "right" and has to temper it

- **Designers** want to be right but must temper that with analytics. The true masters treat metrics as an incontrovertible goal while keeping the aesthetics, creativity and philosophy that make design an art.
- **Programmers** want to be right but must temper that with building and shipping, even before it's ready and even with bugs. They should build what customers actually want, including features customers truly love rather than ones that are rationally calculated.
- **Advisors and pundits** (he includes himself) want to be right but should aim to inspire people to be a better version of themselves rather than indoctrinate them with their own biases, since their experience contains "unknown quantities of luck."

## Listen to critics with half an ear, and don't dismiss them either

- "As a founder, you still need to listen to critics with half an ear." Sometimes you have been blind, and critics are showing you the truth.
- Jason admits he resists opposition to his ideas more than he should: "I still hate being wrong."
- A founder must be negatable. Otherwise you are pig-headed.
- As founder of WP Engine, he says most days hold more contradictions than construction: balancing customer needs with internal costs, following your nose against what competitors do or say, and staying true to the vision while flexing as you learn what the market and you want.
- Dismissing criticism without consideration is "just as easy" as criticizing, and "equally hazardous."

## A test for criticism that stings

- Ask whether it stings exactly because it contains an element of truth you don't want to face.
- His rule: ignore the messenger, ignore the emotion, and see whether it's "a gift of truth, poorly wrapped."
