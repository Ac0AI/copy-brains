## Making something people want beats being "disruptive"

- Jason is wary of founders who pitch "disruptive" products. Disruption is "occasionally a consequence of innovation," but when it becomes the primary purpose, "you've lost sight of why you should be doing any of this in the first place."
- He says to worry about making something useful and delightful, and less about disrupting everything.
- "Disruptive" is the modern word for what used to be called a "paradigm shift."
- Disruptive products can generate billions, which is why venture capitalists love them, and he says their goals make that rational.
- Disruption is rare and usually expensive. It is hard to name a disruptive technology that didn't need hundreds of millions or billions of dollars of investment ahead of revenue.
- Most founders don't have access to that capital. Many would rather build an idea they understand, one that makes a living and that people want and need.

## Incremental improvement is a legitimate strategy

- "There's nothing wrong with incremental improvement." He asks what is wrong with doing something interesting, useful and new but not transcendental.
- His preferred approach is to take a known problem in a known market and do it better, or with a fresh, unique perspective.
- You don't have to create a new market or turn everyone's assumptions upside down to succeed, and he questions whether you should try.
- Simple, modest goals are the most likely to succeed and the most likely to make you happy.
- "It's not 'aiming low' to attempt modest success." Making a nice living for yourself is not failure.
- He argues you are more likely to change the world if you don't try to change everything at once.
- Modesty applies both to what you count as success and to what you try to achieve each day.
- He quotes Kathy Sierra: insisting something be "Deeply Meaningful With Purpose can sometimes suck the joy from it."
- He doesn't say disruptive pursuits are wrong. Modest goals are simply much more likely to be achieved.

## Disruptive benefits are hard to explain, which makes them a poor sales pitch

- Using Twitter circa 2010 as the example, he notes that explaining why it is a fundamental shift in how you meet and interact with people is hard. Listeners typically respond with "I don't need to know what everyone had for lunch."
- Good explanations exist, but they are lengthy and need listeners with patience and open minds, "two attributes in short supply."
- His rule: "'It's hard to explain' means it's a terrible sales pitch." "You just need to try it" and "trust me" don't work.
- Twitter could get away with that, but 100 other social-networking and link-sharing networks that didn't survive couldn't. Ask them, he says, about selling intangible benefits.
- A 2024 editor's note says the point held up: most people who became obsessed with social media didn't become obsessed with Twitter.

## People don't want to be disrupted

- Founders and readers of startup blogs are more open to new ideas than most people. Most people are creatures of habit.
- They "don't want their lives turned upside down." They complain even when a toolbar is rearranged, and cringe at a new social media craze, hoping it is a passing fad.
- Change is hard, so a person has to be experiencing real pain before they want to change.
- It is easier to sell a point-solution for a point-problem than to get people to change how they live.
- Identifying specific pain points and showing how your software addresses them is easier than tapping into a general malaise and promising a better world.

## Famous disruptors usually started as "just better"

- Google was roughly the 11th major search engine (he cites a count of 18 in a linked history), not the first. "A better search engine" was hardly a new idea.
- Google is seen in hindsight as transforming how people find information and how internet advertising works, "but the genesis was just 'better'" than the engines before it.
- Citing Scott Berkun, he points to the iPod. It was not the first of its kind. Earlier crappy devices sold well enough to prove a market existed but had no clear winner.
- The iPod won on design alone. It had no new market, no innovative features and no better sound quality or battery life, and it wasn't concerned with "disrupting" everything else.
- His conclusion: setting out to be disruptive isn't how quality, sustainable companies are built. "Disruption, like expertise, is a side-effect of great success, not a goal unto itself."

## Disruptors often don't capture the money

- Companies that built high-speed internet fiber backbones and large data centers changed how business is done worldwide, but many went bankrupt in the 2000 tech bubble. Survivors still haven't recovered the infrastructure cost.
- Research labs show the same pattern. Xerox PARC invented the mouse, Ethernet, laser printers, windowing user interfaces and the fax machine (as the essay states), and made no money on them. AT&T Bell Labs invented Unix, C, wireless Ethernet and the laser, and also made no money.
- He offers possible reasons without settling on one:
  - Disruptors may be before their time and unable to hold out until others appreciate the innovation.
  - Innovation and business sense may be decoupled.
  - "Version 1" of anything is inferior to "version 3." By version 2 there are new competitors who didn't pay to invent version 1, watched its failures, and can jump straight to version 3.
- His bottom line: "Disruption is often unprofitable."

## His own examples of modest, profitable companies

- **Smart Bear:** it took an existing practice, peer code review, and solved five specific pain points (annoyances and time-wasters). He made millions of dollars from it.
- It "wasn't worth a hundred million dollars" at the time and didn't turn anyone's world inside-out. He found it fulfilling that people were happier doing their jobs with the product.
- A 2024 note says Smart Bear kept growing for ten years afterward, acquired other profitable bootstrapped software-quality companies, and was sold for nearly $2B. None of its products were "disruptive," but all were well executed.
- He is sure that trying to fundamentally change how everyone writes software would have failed.
- **ITWatchDogs:** the problem was simple, since server-room gear fails when rooms get hot. The solution was a thermometer with a web page that emails or pages you if it's too hot.
- It had many competitors: APC (about a $1.5 billion market cap), NetBotz (millions in revenue and funding) and small operations of about $2M a year like his own.
- Its edge was an inexpensive product with 80% of competitors' features, "but nothing disruptive." The company was profitable and sold for millions of dollars, though he made less money personally than at Smart Bear.
- He is sure that trying to change how IT departments monitor server rooms would have failed.
- **WP Engine (2024 note):** it is still growing and profitable at hundreds of millions of dollars in revenue and powers 2.5% of the largest websites in the world.
- It wasn't built to "fundamentally change how websites are created." The founders saw how to dramatically improve something people already did by the millions, running WordPress sites.
