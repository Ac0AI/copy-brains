## Strategy is "how we will win," and most documents titled "Strategy" aren't

Jason defines strategy as "How we will win." The format does not matter (a four-sentence master plan, a few dozen bullets, a six-pager, an eighty-pager, Salesforce's V2MOM, a Lean Canvas). Its only job is to communicate how the company wins. He says most so-called strategies are "vague, wishful thinking, written once and never seen again."

He lists six traits of great strategy:
- **Simple:** reshapes complexity to be manageable and actionable.
- **Candid:** spotlights the most difficult truths.
- **Decisive:** asserts clear decisions and accepts their consequences.
- **Leveraged:** magnifies strengths into durable competitive advantage.
- **Asymmetric:** higher upside than downside, so it beats uncertainty.
- **Futuristic:** solves for the long term.

Without these, the "strategy" is at best a plan and at worst "wishful thinking masquerading as 'vision.'"

## Simple: omit detail so people can remember and use it

- "No one reads anything... No one remembers anything. Certainly not an 86-slide PowerPoint." A strategy has to be simple, even "simplistic," to be read and remembered.
- Strategy lives in weekly meetings and prioritization sessions. A simple narrative lets teams move quickly and independently with little coordination while still supporting one theory of how the company wins.
- Long supporting documents are useful as optional reading. They explain and justify, and they inspire conviction even in people who don't read them. Tesla's 39-word Master Plan was preceded by 1,200 words of justification.
- A simple strategy may look obvious, and obvious may look uninsightful. Jason treats obviousness as a sign it is easy to communicate, execute and believe. Its job is to specify what matters most. X, Y and Z may all be "obvious," but choosing X and not Y or Z creates focus.
- Simplicity can become reductive, overlooking complexity and landing on wrong conclusions. The process of creating strategy must tackle real complexity. The output is like a street map, which omits detail to show the important routes. Simplicity that comes from having processed the messy world is elegant.
- Signs of a strategy that is not simple: pages of detail, slides with more than 20 words, a litany of numbers with no narrative, diagrams with 20 boxes, too many points to recall from memory, and key concepts with no short phrase people can use as daily shorthand.

## Candid: expose the scariest facts, as present-tense threats

- Strategy must "lay bare the most frightening, embarrassing realities." If it doesn't expose and then solve the most brutal facts, "the strategy is wrong." Existential threats matter most.
- Lazy threats could apply to any company: "Google could copy us," "a new startup could get huge funding," "a security breach could cause half our customers to leave."
- Real threats are happening now or are at least 70% likely. They are specific and backed by data, such as a shrinking market, a competitor with accelerating share, or cancellation rates showing even paying customers don't value the product. They are written in the present tense, not with "could" or "might."
- If someone might leave when they hear how scary things are, "maybe they should leave the company." He quotes Nick Saban: "Great players want the coach to tell them the truth."
- Example from Jim Collins's *Good to Great*: A&P and Kroger both had data showing their 1960s grocery model was becoming obsolete. Only Kroger faced it and changed strategy. Though half the size of A&P, Kroger increased its value 100-fold while A&P shrank and then went bankrupt.
- Sign of a non-candid strategy: nothing that makes the reader say "Oh wow, what are we going to do about that?", and a reader who finishes thinking "we're still ignoring the elephant in the room."

## Decisive: choose, and accept the negative consequences up front

A strategy asserts justified, self-consistent decisions on:
- Which market subsets to target, and which to ignore.
- Which customer personas to delight, and which will dislike the product (their feature requests get a quick "won't do").
- How to position against competition, including where competitors are stronger.
- What the company values (quality, service, speed, design, compatibility, lock-in) and what it gives up.
- What to build and what not to build.

Key arguments:
- Customers outside the target segment will buy anyway, then complain about features, UX, integrations and price, drag down NPS and star ratings, and cancel at high rates. Without a strategy that names these consequences, teams log the complaints and start prioritizing them. With one, the team can say "we expected that."
- Fluffy language like "leverage synergies to create unique solutions" is a hallmark of indecision.
- The **Opposite Test:** the opposite of a real decision must also be a rational choice made by successful companies. Open source versus closed source passes. "Customer-first" fails, because no successful company is "customer-last."
- Decisions must at least be self-consistent. A low price conflicts with white-glove service, and top design conflicts with out-shipping competitors, unless you accept extra constraints that resolve the conflict.
- Better than consistent is **mutually reinforcing**. Few features plus amazing design works because customers forgive less functionality when the design is delightful, and great design is easier to deliver with a simple product.
- Strong decisions make you say "no" to most things, including reasonable ones. He cites David Allen: "You can do anything, but not everything."
- Signs of non-decisive: "for everyone" targeting, headings that use "and" to expand scope, unclear what you're not doing, and conflicting decisions.
- Example, Craigslist: it values a consistent experience over great design and accessibility over monetization. Pundits have predicted its demise for 25 years, yet it remains one of the most-visited sites in America, with revenue of $1,000,000,000 in 2020 and a staff of about fifty.

## Leveraged: play to strengths and build moats

- "If Sandra is a great singer, and Peter is a great pianist, then... Sandra [should] sing and Peter... play piano." Great strategy leverages the organization's strengths. Bad strategy asks it to act unnaturally, often "under the cover of 'overcoming weaknesses.'"
- Leverage means a large effect from small effort, and you know you have it when others are amazed at how much you did in so little time.
- Most organizations spend their energy making weaknesses "less weak," which never makes them strengths. Putting the same energy into strengths has a much larger effect, and it is more fun.
- Strengths are better when differentiated from competitors, and better still when durable. Software and business models are easy to copy, so decide which one or two moats to build and name them in the strategy.
- Example, Zappos (1999): when few people bought shoes online, it combined postage-paid returns even 364 days after purchase, legendary support (including buying pizzas for customers) and a culture of employee empowerment. Each depends on the others. Competitors laughed, press was constant, and it reached $1B annual revenue before its tenth birthday.
- Signs of non-leveraged: it would apply equally to a competitor or another industry, no moats are being built, and it demands overcoming more than one or two major deficiencies.

## Asymmetric: bets where upside far exceeds downside

- Jason's metaphor is a sailboat, which always moves forward because its shape resists moving backward and eases moving forward. Strategy should amplify the positive and mute the negative, so setbacks and bad luck don't stop progress.
- Activities should have upside far above cost, so that even if you "took 2x longer to achieve 50% of what you expected," you still win.
- A bad sign is when success requires everything to go right. A portfolio of asymmetric bets (VC-like convexity) lets the successes make the failures moot.
- Forms of asymmetry he names:
  - **Large, growing markets:** budgets, pricing and channels already exist, and optionality means many niches and many ways to succeed.
  - **Compounding processes:** retention, upgrades, employee retention, word of mouth and viral products. Most don't grow exponentially, which is still fine.
  - **Reseller channels:** growing resellers by N brings N × M potential customers.
- Intuit example: it made QuickBooks easy for CPAs, who then required their clients to buy QuickBooks, so few CPAs produced many sales.
- Threats are asymmetric too. With ten competitors, only a few need to break out. He cites the IRA's line after the Thatcher bombing: "we only have to be lucky once. You will have to be lucky always." A portfolio of bets means you don't need to be lucky always.
- Example, Amazon: it kept taking bets (electronics, warehouse and logistics products, Kindle, phones, cloud). Many failed and failure was expected. Bezos is quoted: "A few big successes compensate for dozens and dozens of things that didn't work." Per Andy Johns, Bezos assigned the Kindle to the executive running physical books: "Your job is to kill your old business with a Kindle."
- Signs of non-asymmetric: upside less than 10x cost, linear risk/reward, and several hard things that must all go right at once.

## Futuristic: choose the mountain, aim three years out

- Agility is good for climbing a mountain shrouded in fog, but strategy picks which mountain. An agile team climbing the wrong mountain wastes everything.
- Strategy looks further out than anything else at the company, and data tells you about the past, not the future.
- If a problem can be solved in a month, solve it, but it is not strategic. Anything buildable in three months is not a moat.
- Distinctions he draws:
  - Features or timelines make a **roadmap**.
  - Teams, roles, hiring or processes make an **operational plan**.
  - A strategy specifies market and business outcomes, primary decisions and secondary consequences, and what takes the whole company together.
- Strategy is where you pick the "high-hanging fruit" (important but not urgent, in the Eisenhower Matrix), which are the Rocks in Rocks/Pebbles/Sand.
- The **vision** describes what the world looks like when you win. Examples he lists: Stripe's "Increase the GDP of the internet," YouTube's "Give everyone a voice and show them the world," WP Engine's "Power the freedom to create online." It is often the first sentence of the document but the last thing written, because you can only summarize the future after understanding challenges, courses of action and desired results.
- Example: after Google bought YouTube, it set a goal of a billion daily views. This drove architecture demands, debates about licensing TV and film, and billions invested in fiber and data centers.
- Signs of non-futuristic: the "vision" describes what the company already does, it specifies teams or timelines, it solves quarter-sized problems, and it relies primarily on data to predict the future.

## Bad-strategy checklist and what is not strategy

- Generic statements that fit almost any company are not strategy: grow faster, lower attrition, hire the best talent, delight customers, beat the competition.
- Also not strategy: aspirations without a "how," financial goals instead of how to win competitive markets, plans that live only in someone's head, and written documents not referenced when making plans.
- Jason's closing: a sailboat always moves forward, but without a destination it ends up somewhere you didn't want. "A mediocre strategy is still better than no strategy," so don't wait to write one.
