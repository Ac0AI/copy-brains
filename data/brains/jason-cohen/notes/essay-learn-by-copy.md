## Copying is a legitimate way to learn a craft, as long as it's a means to an end

- Jason argues that American culture treats all copying as evil because it prizes creativity, uniqueness and "innovation". Plagiarism (using someone else's words) is bad, but he thinks the culture throws out the baby with the bathwater.
- His position: "copying is one of the best ways of learning, growing, evaluating, and exploring." It is "a valid tool so long as we regard it as a means to an end."
- The goal is not to match the person you copy. After he imitated James Altucher's style, he said the point was not "being as good as James at James' style" but to "learn and grow as a writer."

## Copy style, not content

- Jason separates plagiarism (lifting someone's words) from copying style: structure, pacing, tone, how an essay opens and closes.
- His examples:
  - **Seth Godin style:** the 160-word post "Hello, I'm 1074018628" (2008). One simple point, a specific example that runs straight to an overarching lesson, and an inspiring ending.
  - **Steve Almond:** a satirical piece about Joel Spolsky whose construction was "100% lifted" from the first essay of Almond's *Rants, Exploits, and Obsessions (not that you asked)*.
  - **James Altucher:** a personal story up front, short sentences, admitting embarrassing things about himself, and an almost trivial conclusion that still leaves a sense of the larger point.
- He still does this after finding his voice, with 65,000 subscribers at the time of writing.

## What copying teaches you: what you like and what you can actually do

- Imitating a style shows you what you like and what you have some ability to do.
- Lessons from copying Seth Godin:
  - He likes evocative examples.
  - He dislikes leaving the reader with no framework for finding a solution. A post that says "be the opposite of shitty" gives inspiration but no tools, like "full of expectation and no answers."
  - He can't explore an interesting thought in only 160 words.
  - He likes leaving the reader on an up-note.
- He applied these lessons directly in a later article, "Be Yourself", which exemplifies each of them.
- Lesson from copying Almond: the structure was fun to do, but inventing structures like that is beyond his ability.

## The same technique works outside writing

- Racquetball: Jason was the inter-mural racquetball champion at the University of Texas. It took lots of practice, but he learned by watching the good players and copying their swing, where they ran and where they placed the ball.
- Chefs: he asks rhetorically whether any great chef did not, at points in her career, intentionally learn styles from various cultures.
- Art: even Picasso, "one of the most creative, innovating artists in history," first learned his craft by copying the styles of the Great Masters.
- He calls the technique "useful in almost any pursuit."

## Copying helps you find your own voice

- Jason's claim: "Copying ironically helps you discover yourself."
- The method is to try things on like clothes and see what fits: "Bits and pieces of you are already reflected in the things around you, so pick up handfuls of other creations and see which bits you want to keep."
- This is how you learn what you value, what you're good at and what you believe.

## Why school writing instruction didn't teach him

- He says High School English III "doesn't teach you how to write prose in the real world any more than a college CS degree teaches you how to write code in the real world. English III teaches you how to write things that English III teachers want to read."
- In class, nobody understood the Eudora Welty story. When he honestly said he didn't know what it meant, the teacher was disgusted and never told them the answer.
- The class ended up memorizing a textbook commentary in case it was on the test, which taught nothing about writing persuasively or with entertainment and interest.
- Having never learned to write well that way, he taught himself by copying.
