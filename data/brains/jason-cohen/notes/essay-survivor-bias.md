## Survivor bias makes most business advice unreliable

- Jason argues that learning only from successful founders is a deeper problem than people realize. Success stories are the "survivors," and the failures that hold equally valid (and perhaps more instructive) lessons go unseen.
- He asks whether "failure could contain more lessons than success."
- His working test for any advice: what would the failures who did the same things look like?

## The bullet-hole planes: the data is in what's missing

- In WWII, English engineers mapped bullet holes on returning bombers. Hits clustered on the wings, tail and rear gunner's station, with few in the cockpit or fuel tanks.
- The tempting conclusion is to armor where the holes are. Jason says that is wrong: planes hit in the cockpit or fuel tanks never made it home. Holes on returning planes mark spots that are "by definition relatively benign."
- The real data is in the planes that were shot down.
- Definition he gives: survivor bias is "drawing conclusions only from data that is available or convenient and thus systematically biasing your results."
- Business application: you read about successes, but the businesses that "never made it home" are invisible.

## The file-drawer effect: invisible evidence

- Journals publish extraordinary results, so statistically insignificant studies get abandoned or stowed away. This is called the "file-drawer effect," and it is insidious because it is invisible.
- He quotes Peter Norvig: when a paper says "this could only happen by chance one in twenty times," similar experiments may have been run twenty times without being published.
- Pharma example: more than two-thirds of studies of anti-depressants given to depressed children found no better than sugar pills, but companies published only the positive trials.
- Business application: Harvard Business School case studies include only success stories.
- His Starbucks test: "what if twenty other coffee shops had the same ideas, same product, and same dedication as Starbucks, but failed?" That would change what we can learn from Starbucks.

## The Margaret coin-flip: finding a winner in a crowd proves nothing

- Rhine's 1930s ESP experiments screened 500 people on a 25-card Zener deck, re-tested the high scorers, and treated the one repeat passer as evidence of genuine ESP.
- Jason's analogy: put 1000 people in a room to flip a coin ten times. One woman, Margaret, gets ten heads, a 1-in-1024 chance for her specifically.
- But the experiment was "let 1000 people flip and find Margaret in the crowd." The chance that somebody flips ten heads is about 62%.
- When many people attempt a feat, normally unlikely events will happen, so Margaret's result says nothing about her abilities.
- Applied to himself: three consecutive successful companies might just mean he is the one in the crowd who guessed right three times, with no reason to believe a fourth would succeed.
- 2023 note: he has since done it a fourth time with WP Engine (200,000 customers, hundreds of millions in ARR, profitable). He says he "still wouldn't change a word" and may still be Margaret. He allows that he may have earned "additional benefit of the doubt," but the warning remains.

## Evidence from business books: Good to Great and In Search of Excellence

- Steven Levitt (Freakonomics) tested this directly. *Good to Great* analyzed eleven companies that went from mediocre to stock-market stars, concluding the common trait was a "culture of discipline."
- Fannie Mae, one of the eleven, collapsed as Levitt read the book. Circuit City, also extolled, went bankrupt.
- An investment in the eleven companies in 2001, when the book came out, would have underperformed the S&P 500.
- Jason's critique of the method: the author combed 1435 companies and picked eleven, which is "the ESP experiment all over again." The book also never asked whether the other 1424 companies showed a "culture of discipline" too, since that may be common regardless of performance.
- Levitt found the same effect in *In Search of Excellence* (1980s).
- Levitt's point, which Jason endorses: these books are backward-looking but implicitly promise the principles will keep working. To the extent that doesn't hold, it "calls into question the basic premise of these books."
- Jason still loves *Good to Great* because he thinks its ideas are "timeless and excellent."

## How to treat advice, including his own

- Jason admits his own advice may suffer from this: he did not start Smart Bear with a fixed philosophy, a fixed customer or even fixed products. His philosophy is post-hoc and might be "survivor bias."
- He raises the possibility that he is like Rhine, convinced of "objective measures of success" while living in "an egotistical, possibly even narcissistic dream world."
- Readers cannot fully verify whether any advice suffers from this. Simply being aware of survivor bias makes you less likely to be taken in.
- Practical filter: prefer advice that
  - makes you think rather than giving answers,
  - forces you to answer tough questions,
  - helps you extend your existing strengths,
  - helps you become a better version of the person you already are.
