## Striving is part of who Jason is, so "be yourself" does not mean accepting your flaws

Jason takes Lady Gaga's "be yourself" ("I was born this way") as sound advice. The upside is fulfillment, happiness and often success. Natural excitement leads to "merry obsession", which leads to hard work and long hours in a field that fits your talents. Even total failure was fun and was your own life. He adds that being yourself is necessary but not sufficient: many other things also have to be true for a venture to succeed.

His tension is self-improvement. It is sensible to work around your faults, but should you also tackle them directly? A person afraid of public speaking could pick a career that avoids stages, or take a stand-up comedy class to beat the fear.

## How Jason's relationship to being "the best" changed over time

- As a kid he needed the blue ribbon, the gold medal and the first-place trophy in everything.
- In high school he accepted there will always be people smarter and better. His new rule had two parts: (1) be the best at one thing (telling a computer what to do), and (2) keep improving at everything else.
- He measured against himself, not others. He didn't need to beat the top student on the Math Team, but he beat his own scores in national competitions year over year. He didn't need to outpace piano prodigies, but he played harder pieces as well as he could.
- Over time, responsibilities pile up and even "improving" is too much work. For hobbies he dropped it: he runs a few 5ks a week untimed and learns new piano pieces for enjoyment, not progression.
- That relaxed stance doesn't work for a career or startup. You can't say "I suck at selling stuff, and I don't care!"

## Procrastination: why the usual excuses are bullshit

Jason says he struggles constantly with procrastination, and that everyone does to some degree. You invent logical-sounding reasons to avoid something. His list, with his rebuttals:

- "I don't have time for X right now." Time always exists; you're choosing to spend it on something else.
- "It's more efficient to do X with Y and Z." Someone is waiting on X, and you've been delaying Y and Z too.
- "I'm not in the mood, and it'll go twice as fast when I am." If you dislike the task you'll never be in the mood, or you haven't been for weeks.
- "I forgot because I don't have a good system." There are 100 organization systems, plus blogs, books and videos.
- "I have better things to do." Then delegate or delete it instead of leaving it on the to-do list.

He rolls deadlines over to "tomorrow" and so out-wits his own to-do software. He has used GTD for years ("yes, it works") and been inbox-zero for years ("it changed my life"). He still has "temporary surges of success and guilt-ridden lapses of ineptitude". He even wrote a post arguing procrastination is useful for running a startup, and half jokes that this was a coping mechanism.

## Jason's decision: keep fighting, because "I'm a person who strives"

He chose to keep fighting procrastination because it means getting more done in less time with less stress, and optimizing for the long run instead of the easy moment. The deciding factor is another fact about who he is: he is a person for whom "good enough" isn't.

Examples he gives:
- He won't let a 5k stay at 26:06.
- He lectures that blogging success is about content and time, not looks and plugins, yet wrote his own plugin to control how the "retweet" link behaves in the RSS feed.
- He couldn't just make peer code review software. He made it a "real company", created the modern theory around it, wrote a book, and pushed it into 70,000 people's hands.
- He couldn't just read Joel Spolsky and Jason Fried. He wrote his own blog, got better at writing and promoted it, "for no reason or reward other than pure ego".
- After two Mixergy interviews with Andrew Warner (already an honor), he pushed for a third so he could interview Andrew on Andrew's own show.
- After selling Smart Bear he couldn't stay retired. New ideas "foisted themselves" on him, so he ran through bad ones until he found a good one and started that company.

## The "motor" and why serial entrepreneurs repeat

Jason sees this attitude in most successful entrepreneurs. He cites Adam Carolla's term "motor": an internal, unstoppable force that keeps you going from wake to sleep for decades. It explains serial entrepreneurship. His line: "You do the third one for the same reason you did the first one, because you are compelled to."

## The four problems the motor creates, and how to manage them

The motor causes problems for most entrepreneurs regardless of age or success:

- **"Spread too thin":** you're interested in and good at many things, so you join too much. Nothing gets enough time, and most things end up worse off than if you'd stayed out, because others could have planned around your absence instead of relying on you.
- **"Shiny new thing":** you get bored when a project moves from the "mostly creative" childhood to the "mostly execution" adolescence, so projects don't get the lasting attention they need.
- **"Work all the time":** it works better when young, but you burn out. Jason did, and says many famous workaholics have, though they rarely admit it. Andrew Warner publicly admits he sold his company for less than he should have because of burnout. Jason's verdict: "Pulling 70-hour weeks catches up to you. Period."
- **"Not good enough":** perfectionism or control-freak tendencies make nothing ever feel done. If you also believe others will find out you're inept, you have real impostor-syndrome problems.

His remedies:
- Acknowledge each syndrome and act accordingly.
- Learn to say "no" to new projects, which lets you stay healthily obsessed with a few.
- Take a trip without the laptop so you have no choice but to be "unproductive". Do it once for a week and you'll be so struck by the gain in productivity, energy and happiness on return that you'll never doubt the tool again. He says this as a workaholic.

## Being yourself in business, and the closing principle

- Don't "vomit out some stupid marketing prose" on your website. Decide what you believe, then run your company accordingly and publicly, and you'll be more proud, more fulfilled and likely more successful.
- You can't fight every foible, nor should you.
- Never compromise your ethics or your sense of taste and awesomeness, because those are among the few things that differentiate you and your company.
- Don't stop striving, improving, learning and developing "who you are". His closing line: "Feed your motor."
