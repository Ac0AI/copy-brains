## How Jason decides whether to sell, and why he sold SmartBear

- Jason told every employee who joined SmartBear that the goal was to sell someday. He likes starting things and making things, and he wanted to make money. He calls this "just as mercenary as that" while stressing it was never at the expense of customers.
- He says most people start companies for two reasons: they hate having a boss, and they want money. He argues it's fine to admit the second one.
- An earlier offer for SmartBear was not good, so he didn't consider it. The Insight Partners offer was a strong revenue multiple, all cash, with no performance-based second bite, and it moved fast: first email to close in four months, closing December 20th. He notes that WP Engine's $250 million round also closed at the end of December, so "no deals are done in December" is false.
- The deciding moment was at home. After the first in-person meeting with Insight in Austin, his wife told him "you have to sell" because she could see how unhappy he was: always in a bad mood, even when things went well. He calls it classic burnout.
- He thinks selling was a good decision but says you can't judge a decision by its outcome (the poker point). He had treated it as binary, sell or stay miserable, when there were many other options.
- Burnout and feeling stuck are, in his experience, the most common reason founders of 5-10 year-old companies want out. He says there are few people to complain to about it, so peers and podcasts are where it gets talked about.

## Options Jason missed besides a full sale

- **Take some money without selling the whole company.** Financing or a secondary can create breathing room.
- **Step back and let the team run it.** Become part-time or take a smaller role, vest more equity into the people who step up as CEO or CTO, and accept dilution. A smaller percentage of a much bigger company is worth more in dollars. "The percentage of the company is not important, the dollars are important."
- **Example (Bluefish Group, an Austin consulting firm):** the founder handed it to the CTO after 12 years and took a small stipend while keeping ownership. The company had been growing about 5% a year and doubled the next year. The founder's reaction: "I should have left sooner, I was holding it back."
- Jason says founders can't believe they're holding the company back because they think they're the smartest. Often they're mostly right, but hungry superstars with no room to move can unlock growth.
- **Change the business model.** SmartBear sold one-time licenses and tried to get roughly 20% a year in support contracts. Revenue reset toward zero each month, which was stressful. Recurring revenue would have lowered the stress and made the business more predictable.
- **Find a real coach or therapist.** Founders dismiss them, but Jason says good ones exist (he cites Jerry Colonna) and finding one is worth the time. His analogy: Federer's tennis coach isn't as good at tennis as Federer, because a coach "holds up a mirror" and offers insight, not skill.

## The freedom line and how much money is enough

- Wealth isn't linear. Jason points to the St. Petersburg paradox, where people value money roughly logarithmically: you need to add a zero before it changes your life. Beyond some personal line, even another zero doesn't change whether you're happy.
- Below the line you have money worries and conflict. Above it, many problems disappear (no debt, a car that works, a comfortable house). Offers that push you over the line are the interesting ones.
- After SmartBear sold, he didn't buy a new house or car. At WP Engine, secondary sales have paid him orders of magnitude more than the SmartBear sale, and his car is nine years old.
- If a company already throws off $1M a year in profit, you're probably past the line, so selling isn't how you get there. That opens options like letting employees run it.
- He warns founders to be realistic. $5M in the bank in America does not mean you never work again: taxes, inflation and low real returns mean you need far more.
- His back-of-envelope math: if you could earn $200K a year in a job, and you expect a 2-3% real return, you divide $200K by 0.02 to get about $10M. Adding inflation and conservatism, he guesses "20 is right." Don't sell for less than the income a job would give you.
- Secondaries (selling some of your own shares, which doesn't make the pie bigger) can go either way. Founders may disengage, or they may take bigger risks because the downside no longer feels like zero.

## The box game for thinking about risk

- Box A has $5M. Box B is 50/50 between $0 and $10M. You pick once.
- Economists say expected value is identical ($5M), but Jason says no one treats them as identical and expected value is useless here. The problem is non-ergodic: you run it once, not a million times. His example: Russian roulette played repeatedly ends in death, not "one in six."
- Founders sell once, so the group average doesn't matter. The point of the game is to find your own risk and reward preference.
- Change the numbers (box B is $0 or $50M, or $0 to a billion) to find whether any upside would make you give up a sure amount near your freedom line. He says there may be no number you'd take.
- Real decisions like "will the company grow over three years" have invented probabilities, and only one outcome will happen, and it could be bad.

## Advice, blowback and employees in a sale

- Jason made index cards after calling everyone he could find for advice, about 17 in total. Opinions split evenly between "never sell" and "take it, deals often fall through." He recommends getting people who help intensely during the sale, not just one-off conversations.
- He got blowback from people who think you should never raise money or sell (he alludes to the 37signals camp). He notes people who have actually gone through it tend to be supportive: "God bless, I hope it worked out well for you."
- One critic outweighs 19 kind comments emotionally. His only tip is to name the feeling, which sometimes diffuses it.
- Employees didn't want to sell even though selling was the stated goal. Some resented it, some understood. They were generous in the payout, so there was no resentment about wealth.
- He believes founders should share proceeds with people who contributed, even if nothing was promised. He doesn't call it immoral to pay only salaries. He says employee one can deserve something close to a third or fourth of what the founder has, and a late joiner can also be valuable. Zero "seems like a small number" to him.
- Do it with a system, not ad hoc. A lawyer will warn that after-the-fact gifts invite lawsuits. His example: a consultant who got a $20,000 thank-you check after a sale and sued for more.
- SmartBear used a non-option system where people got paid at sale, modeled on vesting. He describes other ideas:
  - Set aside a pool and issue everyone a unit each quarter, so long-tenured people hold more.
  - Hiring more people dilutes everyone's units, which discourages unnecessary hiring.
  - Optionally let each person give one unit per quarter to someone else, though it could become cliquey.
- Cash-bonus-style payments are taxed as regular income, not capital gains, and rules differ by country. WP Engine operates where employees can't hold stock.
- This kind of spreadsheet system works for small companies (up to about 30 people, a 50-60 person consultancy in Austin tried it), not WP Engine's 1,200.

## Two unicorns and why the company is not your child

- Jason is a founder of two unicorns (SmartBear, still a 21-year-old brand under a different owner, and WP Engine, with over 1,200 employees and a round of roughly $300M). He thinks it says he has good product taste and intuition about business models and customers, not design.
- About 3.5 to 4 years into WP Engine, he hired Heather Brunner as CEO and became CTO. He says the company is a unicorn because Heather is the CEO and that he wouldn't have been nearly as good.
- He takes full credit for the early work: the idea, the market choice, the product, all the customer interviews, and no first hire for over a year.
- He cites an MRI study: a person's brain responds to pictures of their startup's logo the way it does to pictures of their kids. That is why identity gets tangled with the company.
- But he says the child analogy is wrong. You never sell a child, yet sometimes selling or shutting down a company is right (e.g., indie hackers boasting about $1,600 MRR after three years). Likewise "team is a family" is wrong because you fire people who aren't right and you don't fire family. A healthy team is more like a sports team.
- Knowing your brain is doing this to you lets you be more intellectual about the decision.
- Unlike a parent, a founder keeps credit forever and can use it on a Twitter bio, so he feels no resentment when the company succeeds without him.

## The fulfillment framework: joy, skill and need

- Jason built a three-circle Venn diagram to decide whether to hire a CEO and to stay at WP Engine. He wrote it up as "Fulfillment" on his blog (asmartbear.com). The circles:
  - **Joy:** what you love doing.
  - **Skill:** what you're good at.
  - **Need:** what the company needs done right now.
- You want the center. Two out of three is a trap:
  - **Joy + skill but the company doesn't need it:** the developer happily coding features while the company needs sales. "You're running very fast but in the wrong direction," and as a founder nobody tells you.
  - **Skill + need but no joy:** he did sales at SmartBear, closing 50% of demos, and burned out. Fine short term, bad long term.
  - **Joy + need but not skill:** founders think they're best at everything, which proves they don't know what good looks like. Example: trying affiliates, failing, and concluding "affiliates don't work." Jason says competitors said that publicly while affiliates work well for WP Engine.
- Applied at 60-70 people: the company needed a CEO to scale from 60 to 500, run the board, hire an executive team, and build a global sales org, real marketing, scaled support, finance and HR. Jason didn't love or excel at much of it, so he stepped aside.
- He showed the framework to the whole company to explain the change (so nobody thought he was on his way out) and suggested people use it for their own careers.
- He keeps re-asking the question periodically, and moved from CTO to smaller product roles. He briefly took all of R&D (250 people) temporarily, knowing it wasn't forever.
- Founders have an extra role that doesn't fit the framework. A VP of sales who came from Rackspace (employee 16) told him the thing people missed most when founders left was them walking around and talking to people with no agenda. Jason says that's real and special, but floating around with no real responsibility isn't healthy enough.
