## Pivot Points: the core concept

- Jason's "Pivot Point" is a non-judgmental fact about a person or company that is neither a strength nor a weakness. It is a strength in some situations, a hindrance in others, and irrelevant in still others.
- He dislikes "strengths" and "weaknesses" (in personality tests, SWOTs and similar tools) because they assume the context for judging is already known, and often it isn't.
- Example: his own third-ranked StrengthsFinder trait is "Competitive". It helps in sales calls and in closing a candidate who has other offers. It hurts in team meetings meant to help each other, and when self-worth is based on others' accomplishments.
- The name comes from the "pivot foot" in basketball, lacrosse and Ultimate Frisbee. One foot is stuck, and the rest of the body can go anywhere subject to that constraint. Pivot Points are the constraints you build around.
- Example: if you hate managing people, don't become a manager, and don't build a company that will eventually require a team (or at least don't be its CEO).
- Pivot Points can change, but not often and not capriciously. Short-term planning should treat them as fixed. Long-term planning, especially about where to invest time and money, can ask "What new Pivot Points do we want?"
- This matches the Lean Startup "pivot": you keep what is correct or immutable and pivot the rest of the company around it.
- He doesn't like the term "enabling constraints" because people get hung up on "constraint". Pivot Points are facts with consequences that you leverage or design around.

## Finding your personal Pivot Points

Jason says honest self-knowledge is hard because we are biased, carry baggage, and can't get outside our own heads. His approach uses fill-in-the-blank prompts plus outside input.

**What drives you** (sample prompts):
- "Even when I was a kid, I would ______, and I still find myself drawn to it."
- "When I'm on an extended vacation, I get itchy to ______."
- "Whenever I ______, I get lost in the work, and feel energized (not exhausted!) when the work ends."
- "I asked a few people who know me, and who I trust to be thoughtful and observant, and they said my special strengths are ______."

**What hinders you.** Jason notes it is sometimes easier to avoid what you hate than to construct a life of what you love ("always invert"). Sample prompts:
- "When I'm faced with ______ I physically feel the 'pit of my stomach' falling."
- "If my job starts requiring me to ______ with even 10% of my time, I would at least think about changing jobs."
- "Whenever I ______ all day long, I know I do great work, but I'm absolutely exhausted."

He says to be honest even if "society" won't like the answers. Wanting money or fame is a legitimate motive, and he quotes Orwell's "Why I Write" on desire to seem clever.

## Outside-in questions for personal Pivot Points

Jason asks others about "strengths and weaknesses" because that language helps them generate answers, then untwists the answers into non-judgmental Pivot Points. People are rarely fully honest, even anonymously.

His favorite reference-check question, because candidates only refer friendlies who give sanitized praise:
- "What is the ideal scenario, under which this person thrives?" Construct the perfect set of circumstances (subject matter, goal, team or lack of one, direction or lack of it, incentives) where they kill it, are efficient, productive, extremely happy, and make everyone around them happy.
- The opposite: "Construct the scenario where they die inside, mess everything up, and piss everyone off." It's funny, easy to answer, and doesn't feel negative.

Example of extracting Pivot Points: "give them a goal so they know what success looks like, then get out of their way because they explore quickly" yields "goal-oriented" and "craves exploration". That means R&D (not goal-oriented) or a mature product (needs execution, not exploration) is a poor fit, while an early-stage startup is great.

A third question: "What are my strengths that I'm unaware of because they come so naturally to me? Where I don't realize other people aren't like that too?" These are the most natural, powerful Pivot Points and constitute your "edge". You can't see them yourself, so ask others.

## Finding your company's Pivot Points

- Pretend you are an outside consultant hired to reverse-engineer the business: not what people inside think it is like, but what is actually going on. Observe behaviors and results.
- Don't interview people about why they took actions, because they will rationalize or defend. The goal is discovering, not judging.
- Start by "write-storming" (brainstorming in writing, in isolation, then pooling) observations about what is special or peculiar about the product and company.
- Write-storming avoids bias toward the fastest, loudest voice, supports slower thinkers and non-native speakers, and uses background processing. He cites Leigh Thompson's *Creative Conspiracy*: "I was shocked to find there's not a single published study in which a face-to-face brainstorming group outperforms a [write-storming] group". His optimal process is write-storming to maximize creativity, then meeting to synthesize.

## Prompts that surface company Pivot Points

A vague prompt like "what's special about us" won't be comprehensive, so Jason uses specific prompts. Each applies regardless of whether the answer seems good, copyable, or what customers care about.

- **Undeniable comparative strength:** What customers buy despite our foibles; what even competitors admit in their own sales calls; what our team can do that most can't.
- **Consistent complaint:** Complaints heard so often you don't need data; where a competitor instantly wins; what makes people leave even when they give 10/10 NPS and apologize as they cancel.
- **Customers advocate for:** What genuine advocates brag about; what disgruntled employees admit on Glassdoor; what one-star reviewers concede.
- **Best customers:** Those who pay the most, never call support, score 10/10 NPS, never leave, upgrade, and advocate. Look for characteristics they share that other customers don't (shared by <10% of the rest of the population).
- **Proud of:** Craftsmanship, "who we are", difficulty, or fun. Or the opposite: what you hate that another company does.
- **Talking and walking:** How much of what the website claims is true, and what else you are great at but don't brag about (the Talk/Walk Framework).
- **Clear and present existential threats:** Only things happening now or with at least a 70% chance in the next few years, so the list stays short. Otherwise every company has twenty theoretical disasters.
- **Structural attributes:** What the technical architecture or organizational structure makes possible, easy, natural, unstable, manual, or unscalable.
- **Philosophy and values:** Things you'd honor even if it loses customers, money, speed, or means firing people. Examples: is great design critical or overrated? Is "ship fast with bugs" best (fastest path to product/market fit) or "ship fewer things with high quality" (lifelong loyal customers through craftsmanship, not treating them as "unwitting lab rats")?
- **Great ideas:** Recurring ideas driven by conviction from the areas above, whether or not customers ask for them.

## The condensation workshop

Jason turns the raw observations into fewer, clearer Pivot Points in a six-step workshop:

1. **Rotate through observations.** Go around the room and ask each person for just one of their better observations. This is a facilitation trick he learned from Jonathan Slain; it stops the first person from unloading dozens of items.
2. **Clarify.** Ask clarifying questions only, not arguments. Boil each observation to a few words on a board, in one color.
3. **Convert to Pivot Points.** Ask what Pivot Point the observation translates into, in another color, with the observation placed next to it. Example: "Customers post screenshots on Twitter of long wait-times in tech support" becomes "Slow tech support"; side-by-side screenshots against a competitor become "Delightful, remarkable design". If one observation maps to several Pivot Points, duplicate the card.
4. **Don't evaluate; don't act.** "Slow support means hire more people" is premature. Fast support may not be strategically important, so you might de-emphasize it in marketing instead. Even if you fix it, options include documentation, AI chatbots, chat instead of tickets, better UX, or a different market segment.
5. **Merge as you go.** Fewer, stronger Pivot Points are easier to hold and act on. Don't merge into something generic like "We love our customers"; use the Opposite Test. When in doubt, don't merge. Don't set a target count like 3 or 10: "There are as many as there are."
6. **Loop until exhaustion.** Skip people as their lists run out. It starts slowly and people fear eight hours, but it accelerates as duplicates and weaker items drop out.

Capture side ideas and actions visibly elsewhere so people know they aren't lost, but stay on task.

## Applying Pivot Points to strategy

Jason's questions for using a Pivot Point list:

1. Where does the current or proposed strategy reinforce the Pivot Points (go deeper) or contradict them (pivot)?
2. How do we do more things that leverage multiple Pivot Points? Those are automatically differentiating, because competitors have different Pivot Points and can't copy them easily.
3. What strategy changes design around the negative consequences, so we aren't playing a game others are better suited to win?
4. What additional Pivot Points must also be true, as a downstream consequence or for a self-consistent strategy? This is recursive ("turtles all the way down"), which is what a mutually reinforcing strategy looks like.
5. Is there a singular concept leveraging or avoiding multiple Pivot Points around which to build positioning and strategy?
6. What customer (demographic, firmographic, job-to-be-done, budget, location) would find the Pivot Points attractive? Is that the current ICP?
7. Are we making promises we can't keep, or failing to make ones we'd naturally win? Does marketing match behavior, and what level of the Needs Stack applies?
8. Are there contradictions, such as "Instant 24/7 support" versus "Can work only 4 hours/day" because of a day job and family? Some must be resolved by changing a Pivot Point, but synthesizing a new idea that makes both work is often the most valuable and differentiating outcome.
9. What if a Pivot Point is wrong? Challenge each one and ask what else would change.

## Pivot Points in practice and changing them

**Examples by founder type:**
- Jason, being Competitive, favors entering a large existing market with stagnant competitors, building "a 10x better product" and then winning in marketing and sales.
- Someone who hates "winning" at sales could build a niche product simple enough to explain without a sales team, with a one-person-boutique brand voice ("IYKYK").
- Someone who loves people but hates sales could run a consulting company that builds long-lasting client relationships by taking over a whole department (marketing, support, engineering, finance).
- All are good strategies if they match the founder's Pivot Points and the needs of the chosen market.

**Changing Pivot Points:**
- You can invest in changing one (new skill, new industry), but it may be too difficult, too expensive, or unsuccessful. Change disrupts the organization, and employees or customers may leave.
- Change one only when the upside is so large that achieving only 30% of the transformation is still worthwhile. Don't change too many at once; maybe just one.
- He cites Tim Ferriss's test for writing a book: "If the rewards are 1/4 of what you think they will be, and it takes 2x longer than you think it will take, is it still a *no-brainer* to do it?"

**Payoff:** Designing a life or strategy around Pivot Points means more efficient and effective work, better quality, better competition against those who don't share them, and more fun. His closing line: "Don't fight Pivot Points. Embrace them."
