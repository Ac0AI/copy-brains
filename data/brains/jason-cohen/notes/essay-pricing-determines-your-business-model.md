## Price is business design, not an afterthought

Jason argues that pricing "cannot be 'figured out later,' because it determines your business model today." He sees price as "inextricably linked to brand, product, and purchasing decisions" (by whom, why, how, and when). It is not a supply/demand curve slapped on after the product exists. It shapes the nature of the product and the structure of the company that builds it.

- Price is as important as any feature in deciding product/market fit.
- Typical failures he lists: people say "it would be great if someone did X" but won't buy at $700; app reviews hung up on $0.99 vs $1.99; users stuck on an inferior product they can't afford to upgrade; users stuck on freemium who refuse to pay anything; buyers stuck with an expensive, inferior product bought on salesmanship ("expensive must mean it's better") rather than craftsmanship.

## Bring price into customer development interviews

Jason disagrees with the common advice not to mention price in customer interviews. That advice says price diverts talk to budgets and away from the customer's day-to-day pain. He wants price discussed like any other feature.

Questions he suggests asking:
- What do they expect it to cost, and why?
- Who would write the check, and who would need to approve it?
- Would an ROI argument be welcomed or scoffed at?
- What would need to change (if anything) to justify doubling your price? If there is an answer, maybe you should double it.

He closes with: "Price is not an afterthought, it is essential business design."

## $0/mo and $1/mo: growth over revenue

**$0/mo**
- The goal is to maximize growth (trust and usage), not revenue.
- The product has natural tripwires that trigger other pricing (freemium), or the business model is "left as an exercise to your future self."
- Typically needs venture funding because there is no income, and success demands lots of people and tech.
- Even efficient outliers raised tens of millions: WhatsApp (sold for $19B with 55 employees) and Instagram (sold for $1B with 13 employees).
- Often B2C, since value is in quantity and there are 100x more consumers than businesses.

**$1/mo**
- You can't afford customer service, and running the technology must be nearly free. So the product must be simple enough to be self-service.
- Marketing and sales spend is nil, at least initially. It needs a reason to spread by word of mouth, ideally virally as a natural result of using it, or via vocally passionate early users, perhaps galvanized around a mission.

## $10/mo: the slow bootstrapped slog

- Customers see you as a cheap version of something else, yet still expect a phone number (his example: GoDaddy).
- Bootstrapped businesses can make it work (e.g. most shared hosting companies), but only make interesting money at scale. By his math, it takes over 8,000 customers to make just $1m/yr.
- You can reach tens of millions in revenue, but it takes about 10 years.
- To scale faster you need venture funding, because revenue is anemic and you can't afford to advertise.
- Bootstrapped companies at this price often boast of having no marketing or sales departments. Jason says the truth is they can't afford them, so they grow slowly and are often eclipsed by competitors who can grow 10x faster. In a huge market there is still room for everyone.
- Upside: people forget they have a $10/mo service even if they don't use it. That "free revenue" can be substantial at scale, as with shared hosting.

## $100/mo: the sweet spot for bootstrappers

- Customers expect to be able to call support. If a competitor is substantially better, switching is probably worth the effort.
- Almost exclusively B2B unless it's a "luxury" item.
- Big companies buy it with little consideration. Small companies need to understand the value, so you may need sales material and a demo.
- Jason says he loves this zone for bootstrapped companies: low enough to address a broad business market, high enough to become "nicely profitable" with a reasonable, achievable number of customers (e.g. 200-300), and you can afford to spend money acquiring them.

## $1,000/mo: mid-market and the sales burden

- Only medium to large companies can afford it. They have buying processes: annual budgets, approvals, ROIs, demos.
- You'll be compared to alternatives and weighed, and you'll be asked for a discount.
- You need to be part of that conversation: a real sales force, sales materials, impressive logos, case studies, referenceable customers.
- Features may need to include big-company requirements like role-based access privileges and LDAP integration.
- He calls it a "surprisingly difficult zone to become profitable in," because the sales, marketing, and engineering costs match those of much larger deals without the attendant revenue.

## $10,000/mo and $100,000/mo: enterprise

**$10,000/mo**
- Larger companies only. The product is unlikely to be sufficient out of the box, so you may need in-house professional services or consulting partners to build the "Whole Product" (Geoffrey Moore's term).
- If something goes wrong, they'll cancel and won't pay out the rest, "no matter what your contract says; what are you going to do, sue your customers?"
- Mid-sized companies can reach this price if it's usage-based (his examples: ZenDesk, JIRA, Box, Slack) or performance-based (e.g. marketing optimization). Then the product must create and demonstrate value to earn it.

**$100,000/mo**
- Global 2000 only. Large projects need multiple departments to decide and approve.
- Sales cycles run 9-18 months, which requires a massive cash outlay while you wait.
- Likely starts as a pilot or proof of concept, ideally paid, and it must succeed convincingly, perhaps against another vendor's pilot.
- Needs on-site visits even in the video-conferencing era.
- Examples: WorkDay (much of its revenue is consulting) and IBM.
