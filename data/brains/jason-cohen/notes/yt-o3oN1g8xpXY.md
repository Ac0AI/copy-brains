## Why Jason questions a new-segment push that only shows 5% of traffic

- Jesse's big-company visitors were about 5% of traffic and converted at zero on the existing product. Jason reads zero conversions as "I don't want it", not as a hidden 5% pool.
- Capturing all of them on the current offer would add only about 5% growth. The bet only works if they pay roughly 10x more for a different package.
- His test for any new segment: what did you learn about what they want, and what would delight them, beyond covering their table stakes?
- He asks what made the early demos convert. Invoicing and POs, audit logs, and white-glove migration are table stakes. They remove fear but don't excite anyone.

## Delight versus table stakes, and what to say to marketers

- Jason separates "I just have to have this" features (SSO, roles, audit logs, POs) from features that make a buyer tell others. Both matter, but only the second drives word of mouth.
- His example of delight is Linear: people rave about the design and speed and the product feels better than Jira. He says "there's no such thing as a product that shouldn't delight a customer."
- Enterprise users are still human. Keep the simple, polished UI that wins end users, and only add enterprise checkboxes where there is no choice. Don't let enterprise features degrade the design.
- For marketers at big companies, he'd position around keeping their job. A marketer who can tell the boss "I'm getting us into LLMs" is "saving my job", which is stronger than "you'll hate this less".
- He wants a pitch a marketer repeats in a Slack channel or alumni list ("dude, I just did this"). He says you don't need 100% of buyers to feel it. Even partial success lifts everything.
- Utility is the easy zone: "you need this and I make that" is true but weak.

## Find out who the buyer is, then test messaging with ads

- Jesse's demos skew technical, but the marketer may hold the budget. Jason says the real answer may be "don't know", and finding out is critical.
- The marketer and the engineer need different messages. Engineers care about drop-in, migration and ongoing hassle. Marketers care about job security and AI visibility.
- Run separate ads and landing pages (even separate home pages) for each. Demos several clicks downstream won't reveal who was drawn in.
- Jesse's earlier $5-figure ad tests targeted SMB-style bottom-of-funnel intent with an unthoughtful message. Jason says that doesn't test this idea.
- Two ways ads can pay at the higher price point:
  - ROI: if the plan is ~10x the monthly price and lifetime value is higher, a campaign can pay even if SMB ads never would.
  - Learning: a fixed budget used to find the best 4-word hook and landing-page message in days. SEO takes too long and attribution is murky.
- The channel can be AdWords, LinkedIn or podcasts. Ask where the marketers actually are.
- Don't spend conference or sponsorship time on a fringe community (Laravel) when the fish are elsewhere (React). Budget it as fun and culture time, not growth.

## Pricing page: avoid a huge visible range

- A single page showing $5-$20/month and $10,000/month leaves visitors unsure who the company is. Jason says buyers think "I don't want either one."
- His rough ladder rule with no other data: each tier about 2x the previous (sometimes 3x). At the time Jesse's plans were around $49, $199, $499 and $750.
- He called $500 and $750 too close to justify two tiers. The top tier mainly adds SSO.
- A $50 to $750 jump is segmentation, not growth. Nobody moves up because of a little more usage. He notes brand customers come in and stay flat.

## Pick the segments that scale: Big Commerce and agencies

- Jesse's data: Shopify has more customers but is a worse cohort (high churn, support burden, app-store slop, many not real businesses). Big Commerce stores are real, old-school businesses, and the cohort loves the product. Jason praised this analysis.
- Big Commerce math from Jesse: 1/7 of 37,000 stores do over $400K a year. 5% converting to the team plan is about +$40K MRR. 5% on the business plan is about +$100K MRR.
- Jason's focus: "agencies and Big Commerce" over also chasing big brands generally.
- Why agencies: each averages about 3 customers over time. A direct brand pays once and stays flat, while an agency compounds. Jason calls it a parabola versus a flat line.
- Agency tactics:
  - Run a deliberate motion, not passive inbound.
  - Go back to existing agencies and ask about other clients who need a blog (content marketing, LLM visibility). The agency makes money on a new blog project.
  - Send agencies a monthly newsletter to stay top of mind.
  - Tier agencies: nurture the top ones regularly, treat the long tail differently.
- Some agencies want kickbacks, others are offended by them. He told WP Engine agencies "whatever you want."
- Where to meet them: events and podcasts, referral channels and online communities. At WP Engine he met agencies by going to WordCamps and chatting online.
- Look for existing podcasts rather than starting one. Hosts want good stories, and the AI visibility topic is current.

## Run it like a company initiative: owner, budget, separate metrics

- Jason would not announce a "new strategy". It makes staff nervous. Frame it as expansion: "and, not instead of", the mission is unchanged, here are the green shoots, here's how every role contributes.
- Each initiative needs a single owner whose only thought is that area (an agency lead, a Big Commerce lead). Otherwise the existing business always wins every competing priority.
- Cordon off the budget and revenue for each so you can see its numbers and decide when to shift money between them.
- Metrics differ per track (SMB newsletter is for retention, agency newsletter is for growth), so measure them separately.
- If you've never done account-based marketing, hire or consult someone who has built it. Setting up the system is a different person than executing it.
- A ~$700/month plan can fund a lot; ~$400/month funds demos but not full ABM. He distinguishes enterprise-group selling from true enterprise sales like a $10M Workday install.
- Idea for a stronger presence: an online conference. At WP Engine in-person events were very effective but costly. When they moved online with pre-recorded talks, speakers chatted live in the room and live Q&A followed. It positioned them as the experts and gave a reason to reach agencies. Use 2-3 topics (e.g. "blogging in 2026").
- First-three-month plan: use ads and calls to learn the positioning that really lands with marketers (the scared, old-school marketer at a Big Commerce company), then scale.

## Not every plateau needs this playbook (Rob's framing, Jason's contribution)

- Jason cites his own essay on the "elephant curve": everything plateaus, and at some point you change strategy (new product, new customer type, new big channel).
- A plateau at $5K MRR is a different problem from one at $1M+. Optimising existing channels has already been tried here, so the next lever is a new segment or channel.
- Jason's own book is Hidden Multipliers (hiddenmultipliers.com).
