## The adjacency matrix: how Jason decides where to expand after PMF

Jason separates expansion from two things it is often confused with. A pivot is for a company staring death in the face. Incremental product work (bug-fixes, features) is for protecting what already works after PMF. Expansion keeps what works and sprouts new shoots in new directions. It should leverage existing assets to attack a valuable opportunity with asymmetric upside.

A great strategy that doesn't align with your strengths "is a bad strategy _for you_." The test is "adjacency": how close the new idea sits to what the company already does.

## When it is the right moment to expand

Jason lists four conditions. Meeting one is enough to consider expanding.

- **Bottlenecked growth:** spending more time or money on existing marketing and sales channels no longer yields cost-efficient output. A new channel, geography or market segment can accelerate growth.
- **Target market saturation:** you have won about 5% or more of your ICP (the perfect-in-every-way customer). 5% is not saturation, but if you got there you can presumably reach 10% with similar methods. That lets you turn attention to segments that are similar enough to be relevant but different enough to need effort.
- **Diversification:** new channels, markets, geographies or pricing options add growth and make the company robust to disruption.
- **You can afford to invest:** a company throwing off more than $1M a year in profit can try something new without a failure being fatal. Expansions are costly and risky, and extra money covers both. Normal investment rules apply.

## WP Engine's diversification example

Expanding upmarket at WP Engine changed the value proposition, not just growth.

- Small businesses saw WP Engine as "**expensive**, but the best."
- Large companies saw it as "**low cost**, yet still enterprise-grade."

During COVID, smaller companies were going out of business (so "expensive" was bad), while larger companies were looking to save money (so "low cost" was good). When one segment struggles, the other grows, and the net effect is stability. At scale, Jason calls this "nirvana."

## Generating expansion ideas

Expansion options are easy to think of:

- a new geography
- a different ICP, niche, vertical or use-case
- up- or down-market moves, including the customer's Needs Stack
- a freemium tier, a higher pricing tier, a lifetime plan or a new product line
- iOS-only to Android, web-only to mobile app, one integration to ten
- shifting between SEO/social (invest in the future) and paid marketing (pay for today)

To be systematic, fill each box of Ash Maurya's Lean Canvas with the existing business, then brainstorm ways to expand or improve each box. LLMs help generate possibilities, but not select among them.

## Building the Adjacency Matrix

List the major functional areas of the business and tune the list to your company. The default rows are Marketing, Sales, Service, Product, Engineering and Business Model. A product-led company with no Sales department but a heavy design culture would drop Sales and add Design.

For each area, rate how much it must change to support the idea. There are only three choices:

- **✓ Trivial:** almost no change. Training is one email or Zoom session, and sales and marketing material gets a few new bullets.
- **⚠ Adjustment:** manageable within current processes and org structure, like a large new feature release. This means training, new sales slides, a new web page, a non-trivial pricing change, or new subject-matter experts in Support.
- **✗ Overhaul:** hiring for new skills or capacity, significant retraining, new specializations, or structural and management changes, especially to the org chart. Often you can't identify every challenge, so unknown unknowns await.

This is another use of his Fermi-estimation approach. Limiting the choices avoids arguing over predictions you aren't qualified to make anyway, so sorting into a bucket should be easy without extensive analysis.

## Worked example: WP Engine in 2013

The ICP was small to mid-size companies' home pages and small to mid-size blogs/media, often built by freelancers and small agencies. Jason compares two Enterprise ideas.

| Area | Enterprise campaigns | Enterprise full site |
|---|---|---|
| Marketing | Adjustment (new audience, "campaign" message instead of "home page") | Overhaul (new competitors and channels, brand from scratch) |
| Sales | Overhaul (drastically new process and cycle times) | Overhaul |
| Service | Trivial | Overhaul (account management, white-glove onboarding) |
| Product | Trivial | Adjustment (compliance requirements, few new features) |
| Engineering | Trivial | Trivial (enterprise sites get similar traffic to popular small-company sites) |
| Business Model | Trivial | Adjustment (new plans, same model) |

Campaigns need one Overhaul, while the full site needs three. Jason's takeaways:

- Selling to the Enterprise is far more different than people realize.
- Startups at only $500k ARR often claim Enterprise as their growth path. At that moment it is "definitely the wrong strategy," yet many conclude the opposite.
- They should instead pursue simple use-cases at larger companies, or ignore large-company complexity and keep winning where they are strong.
- The matrix evaluates a decision, it doesn't dictate it. A company at $60M ARR with steady dollar growth but slowing percentage growth could reasonably spend $10M adding an Enterprise-focused business model.

## Rules for reading the matrix

- More than one Overhaul is a deal-breaker for an incremental, sustaining innovation.
- Multiple Overhauls are acceptable only if all three hold: (a) you will make a huge investment, (b) you accept a large risk it won't pay off, and (c) the potential upside is enormous. This restates the first rule of investments.
- At some point an idea is so non-adjacent that it is "definitionally a bad strategy to attempt it, regardless of upside." Strategy should leverage existing assets.

## Choosing among options and communicating the decision

**Selecting:**
- Value divided by cost is tempting. If you use it, Jason recommends his ROI rubric, which forces rough estimates on both variables.
- For real investments (substantial cost, time and risk with only hopeful upside), he recommends solving first for maximal impact and only secondarily for cost. Binstack can identify the highest-value items, with cost and risk breaking ties.
- The matrix is mainly for ruling out ideas that are clearly too far afield and for spotting particularly low-risk ones.

**Planning:**
- The matrix outlines the cost and risk analysis by naming the areas that must be considered.
- For changes you understand, describe what needs doing, the budget and the remaining risks.
- For complex changes with Overhauls, add:
  - mitigations
  - objective measures or milestones to catch problems early
  - attacking the high-risk, high-uncertainty areas first
  - specialized hires "who have seen this movie before"

**Communicating:** Leaders perennially undervalue communicating the decision, and it needs to feel natural, intelligent and clear to everyone. Jason's narrative template:
- Acknowledge the organic pull, for example from larger enterprise customers.
- Name the options considered and the one chosen.
- Say which department takes on the complex, risky work and why it is easy for everyone else.
- Frame it as the least-risky, highest-chance-for-success way to open a new growth area and learn.
- Ask the company to give that department "support and grace" as it transforms.
