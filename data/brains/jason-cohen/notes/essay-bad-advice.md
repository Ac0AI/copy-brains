## Don't let advice change who you are

- Jason (founder of Smart Bear, which he later sold; "it's worth over $2,000,000,000" now) argues that advice should help you become a better version of yourself, not change who you are.
- He tells the "Frank vs. Gerry" story as the core lesson. Frank, a silver-haired ex-VP of Sales, joined 18 months after Smart Bear started. Gerry, Jason's co-founder in ITWatchDogs and mentor, sold companies for $100 million without setting foot on a golf course.
- Frank's demands:
  - Rename to "Software Test and Deployment Systems, Inc" (STDS), because "Big companies like acronyms."
  - Get patents "to keep competitors at bay."
  - Hire a sales team, because "software doesn't sell itself."
  - Lighten up and knock off early on Fridays.
  - 50% of the company, a salary, 35 hours a week, and no investment.
- Jason said no. Two weeks later he landed a $50,000 deal with Intuit. A few years later Smart Bear did millions in revenue with big companies, still called Smart Bear, with no patents and no sales guys.
- Gerry's reaction to Frank's pitch was a list of facts about the deal. Jason says Gerry framed his view as "pointed questions."
- Saying no to Frank was hard, because Frank had the expertise and Jason felt he was the one who was wrong. Without Gerry he "would have gone through with it."

## There is no objective "best" advice, only what works for you

- Jason compares startup advice to dieting. Every approach has been touted as healthy and as poison, so the only question is "What works *for me*?"
- For nearly every "X is right" success story there is an equally compelling "X is wrong" story:
  - Prices low vs. prices high.
  - A big Twitter following with no ads vs. paid ads and no social media.
  - Scratching your own itch vs. researching an underserved niche.
  - A founder who is an expert in the field vs. one who brought a new perspective.
- He says the right answer depends on your market, competitors, customers, price point, business model, team, strengths, goals, financing and culture.
- Stories like Steve Jobs, Bill Gates, Mark Zuckerberg, Jason Fried or Pieter Levels are outliers even among their contemporaries. "No one should attempt to follow their unique paths."
- In diets, half the answer is physiology (how your body reacts) and half is sustainability. In business he maps these to company fit and whether you can keep doing it long-term.
- Sustainability examples:
  - If you find Twitter insipid, Facebook unserious and Instagram fake, forcing yourself to post there won't work. Most companies' marketing isn't primarily powered by social media.
  - Thousands of successful companies have crappy design. If you're not a designer and don't care to invest in one, ask what makes companies succeed despite poor design.
- Things that need fresh willpower every day don't last.

## Test whether it's insight or an excuse

- Ask whether you like the advice because it justifies behavior you know is wrong, or because it clearly articulates a ground truth.
- Example: if you inherently dislike social media, you can find advice saying it doesn't matter. Did you enjoy it because it feels correct, or because it lets you avoid the truth that social media is critical in your chosen market?
- Asking yourself proactively makes the answer "often" clear, even though these are feelings.
- The reverse also applies: pay attention to advice that triggers an immediate, automatic revulsion. Often the feeling is right because it signals a context mismatch. But if you are wrong about something, "this is what it feels like to discover that. This is an opportunity; don't waste it!"

## Check the advisor's context

- Rule of thumb: "Advisors give advice to themselves." Within their own goals, world-view, markets, products, customers and competitors they can give great advice. Outside that, "who knows?"
- Ask whether the advisor matches you on all those dimensions. If so, it may be relevant wisdom, even if hard to hear.
- If not, either ignore it completely ("Focus!") or run it through a substantial mental filter.
- The boundary of an advice's context is often unknown to both advisor and advisee.

## Prefer questions to commands, and constructive to merely critical

- The best advice comes as a series of questions from a devil's advocate, not a barrage of commands. Pointed questions make you defend your choices and challenge assumptions without implying they're false.
- Rarely will an advisor know more than you about your domain, but an outside voice still helps. He compares it to Roger Federer's coach, who is "a mirror and a provocateur," not someone who is "better at tennis."
- Frank stated axioms. Gerry asked questions that required rebuttal or agreement.
- Jason suggests playing devil's advocate periodically: find an intelligent foe, take her to lunch, and follow Scott Berkun's "Rude Q&A."
- "It's easy to cut down ideas; it's hard to create and execute them." He can find someone who thinks any idea is dumb. Constructive means constructing, so look for advice with a clear method for implementation and a clear way to know whether it's working.
- Look for an advisor who volunteers a clear way to measure the idea's success. Then the idea is self-evaluating and you can change course if it doesn't work for you. He says this is the guiding principle behind Smart Bear's marketing.

## Tradition, relationships and gut feel

- "This is how it's done" is almost never a good reason by itself. It's just momentum.
- Example: Smart Bear posts its prices on the internet rather than negotiating, even though enterprise software is rarely sold that way. It's honest and makes business sense.
- He also warns that many founders treat tradition as something to contradict. Much of tradition is hard-won wisdom, so traditional answers carry neither positive nor negative baggage and should be investigated like any other idea.
- Actively build a network of trusted advisors: local entrepreneurs, online forums, even bloggers you like. "Everyone needs a Gerry or two." Advisors won't always agree, but nothing beats running ideas past people you respect who have your interests at heart, even if their advice turns out wrong.
- Your gut often knows the answer. When both "X" and "Not-X" are convincingly peddled as The One True Way, you may need something outside pure logic. If you find yourself vigorously agreeing with a new idea, "that might be all the evidence you need."
- He adds that "no one knows what's right, not even me."
- Closing logic: being yourself is the only thing you'll be good at, and that gives you the leverage you need to win.
