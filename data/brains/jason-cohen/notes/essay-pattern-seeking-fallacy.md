## Pattern-seeking in noisy data produces false insights

Jason argues that humans are bad at telling patterns from randomness, and that marketing data is full of "insights" that are actually noise.

- Gilovich's 1985 study recorded basketball hits and misses as strings like `HMHHMMMMHMMHH` and found players were not "streaky": a coin flip produces the same runs. Pundits and fans stayed unconvinced.
- When shown strings with varying real streakiness, pundits and fans could not classify which were streaky and which were random.
- He notes that a later critical analysis suggested the hot hand might exist and the debate is inconclusive. His takeaway is that it is extremely hard to know whether something is random, even for statisticians.
- Humans also fail at the dot-field test: asked which dot set was placed randomly, we pick the wrong one.

## How picking the sample window hides data

Jason uses baseball announcer stats to show how a framing choice inflates an observation.

- "This pitcher has retired 5 of the last 7 batters" implies that batter 8 got on base, and so did the one before the window. Honestly, it is at least "5 of 9," which sounds less impressive though it is the same data.
- Choosing the cutoff after seeing the data builds the pattern into the claim.

## Seeking combinations until something shows up

The core fallacy is combing through a mountain of data until a pattern appears.

- Announcers cite things like "Rodriguez is 7 for 8 against left-handed pitchers in asymmetric ballparks when the tide is going out during El Niño." Someone examined millions of combinations and reported the one that sounded impressive.
- Jason says this "is the epitome of Pattern-Seeking."
- The Bible Code: strip out non-letters, arrange the letters in a grid, and look for words at regular row and column intervals. With any grid size and any interval, a huge set of letters automatically yields words you recognize.
- The same method finds coded messages in _War and Peace_. It does not matter which translation or language is used. All you need is lots of letters and lots of combinations.
- He compares this directly to A/B tests and combinations of "patterns" in web analytics software.

## The coin-flip math: rare events become common when you look often enough

Jason uses a fair-coin experiment to show why searching for interesting results is not the same as running an experiment.

- One 10-flip trial: 10 heads in a row is a 1 in 1024 event, so a coin that does it once is suspicious.
- Run 1000 ten-flip trials: a fair coin still lands 3-7 heads per trial, but in 1000 trials there is a 62% chance of seeing 10 heads at least once.
- Key line: "Those events are rare, but I'm flipping so much that rare events will naturally occur."
- In a real experiment, a very unlikely result suggests something systematic. When you are "seeking interesting results" instead, highly unlikely events necessarily happen, yet you still conclude something systematic is at work.
- He ties this to scaling: scale makes rare things common, which is one reason scaling a business is so hard.

## How the fallacy shows up in marketing and sales data

Jason maps the coin-flip lesson onto Google Analytics and similar tools.

- There are a hundred ways to slice and dice data. Compare enough variables enough ways and some correlation will appear, for example "when we use landing page variation C along with AdWord text F, our conversion rate is really high on Monday mornings."
- He says this makes you:
  - like the baseball announcer, tumbling combinations of factors until something "significant" falls out;
  - like someone running 1000 coin-flip trials and looking only at the all-heads one to declare the coin "biased";
  - someone seeing streaks and hoping an extra-high conversion rate is evidence of a controllable, systematic force.
- Examples he gives: "We tried 10 AdWords variants and combination D is the clear winner," and "We sliced our Google Analytics data every which way, and these 4 patterns emerged."

## The fix: form a theory, then test it

Jason says apparent patterns are fine for forming a theory. The error is skipping the test, which turns science into baseball announcing.

- Ad copy: do not use a thesaurus to generate 10 ad variants. Decide which pain-points or language you think will grab potential customers and test that theory specifically.
- Analytics: do not root around hoping to find a combination with a good conversion rate. Decide beforehand which conversion rates matter for which cohorts, then measure and track only those.
- Customer research: do not ask customers leading questions or collect scattered thoughts. Form hypotheses and test them intentionally.
- Advice: do not blindly follow the startup founder who dramatically succeeded (the "1-in-1000 coin flip" of survivorship). Gather advice and observations that align with your style and goals.
- Keep testing your theories, because the environment changes, you change, or you may find something better.

## Quotes he uses

- Asimov: "The most exciting phrase in science isn't 'eureka,' but rather, 'that's funny.'" Jason's use: a surprising pattern is a prompt to form a hypothesis, not a conclusion.
- Fermi: "There are two possible outcomes: if the result confirms the hypothesis, then you've made a measurement. If the result is contrary to the hypothesis, then you've made a discovery." This only works if you had a hypothesis beforehand.
