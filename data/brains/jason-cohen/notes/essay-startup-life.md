## Startups as a crucible, not a roller coaster

- Jason calls a startup "a crucible -- a fiery place that tests your limits, not by probing them but by violently exceeding them, all of the time."
- He says the same is true of raising a baby. His daughter had colic: she screamed 12 hours a day for three months, and a newborn feeds every three hours.
- He says the startup is one of the few times in his life he has been taken to the brink, physically and mentally. Colic was the other.
- He argues that the pithy tweets about the "roller coaster o' emotions", the thought of quitting your job, and books like The Dip are "only words". They "don't help much, not really. *You* have to *live* it."
- He argues that you can't understand it until you do it.

## What a startup does to the founder

- Jason says that even if you "fail", you come out "stronger, wiser, ready for the next thing, never able to go back to being a cog, eyes opened."
- He says the change "can never be undone, forever changing who you are and how you interact with the world."
- He has "never met a person who tried a startup, failed, and said they wouldn't have done it all over again. Never."
- He treats that regret-free pattern as a signal worth thinking about: "What does that mean?"

## Startups and children: where the comparison holds and where it breaks

- Jason lists the similarities:
  - Both spend the first two years of life "trying to kill themselves" while you frantically work to prevent it.
  - Both are a mix of your own creation and their own direction. They are malleable in some ways and stubborn in others.
  - Each one is different, even when made by the same parents, and needs the freedom to find its own way with loving guidance.
  - Both pass through predictable macro-level stages, which are obvious to anyone who has walked the path before. Both also have micro-level stages unique to each one.
  - Both take all of your time and all of your patience.
- He says they are not actually that similar. A kid grows up pretty much no matter what. A startup usually dies regardless of the founders' actions, and often because of them.
- He thinks drawing such comparisons is "not only unnecessary but useless". His test: even if the two were similar, "so what? What would you do differently tomorrow?"
- The one thing he says both share is the crucible effect.

## Needing to improvise without knowing what to do

- Jason describes bringing his daughter home with no training, and says it matters less than you'd think that you don't know what to do next.
- He says each day can feel like "an interminable mixture of necessary drudgery and constant invention", and that the same is true of a startup.
- The work is "boring and inevitable and yet precious and impossible".
- He says eventually they "don't need you anymore, or so they claim."

## Finding your own crucible

- He asks where your "child-like combination of wonder and determination" is. He means the thing that is so difficult you're sure you can't do it, "and yet, you do it".
- He argues that this is "how you really live a life, instead of watching life elapse."
- He closes with a quote from Omar Khayyám: "Awake my little ones, and fill the cup, before life's liquor in its cup be dry."
