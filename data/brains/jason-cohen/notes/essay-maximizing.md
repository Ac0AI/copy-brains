## Maximizing vs. satisficing: pick the goal per decision

Jason defines two decision-making goals.

- **Maximizing:** finding the best solution. It takes exploration and analysis to make sure "the best" option wasn't overlooked, and confidence in how the options were evaluated.
- **Satisficing:** picking the first, easiest or cheapest option that meets the requirements. It prefers a faster decision to the best one, avoids paralysis from chasing "perfect," and rarely yields the very best outcome.
- People lean one way or the other, but it depends on the subject. His example: you might maximize your career and satisfice your diet.
- Studies he cites show maximizers make better choices, but satisficers enjoy their choices more, and they spend less time and feel less stress deciding.
- His core instruction: "You must choose when to Maximize, and when to Satisfice." Pick the right goal for each decision and communicate it clearly.

## Why speed usually means satisficing

- Jason calls speed "one of the greatest competitive advantages," and says it partly explains why small upstarts beat large incumbents.
- Speed requires satisficing, not maximizing. It can seem that maximizing is best, especially when smart teams are available to do it, but he says that is often the wrong move.
- Startup strength comes from shipping. Benefits arrive today instead of theoretical benefits in the future.
- Use customer reaction and real data to decide your next move, rather than planning many moves ahead, which he says you can't do anyway because the future can't be predicted.
- Satisficing is also the right mode for "sand" in his Rocks, Pebbles, Sand analogy, where deliberation is the enemy.

## When to maximize: product and business strategy

- Maximizing is best for decisions that should be made slowly.
- His main example is Product & Business Strategy, which he calls "a slow obsession." Approach it from many angles:
  - Pound it with devil's advocacy.
  - Question every assumption.
  - "Sleep on it" repeatedly.
  - Repeat pieces of it to customers, strangers, and anyone who might have a useful reaction.
- Reason: strategy isn't something you want to change often, since whole teams and careers get built on top of it.
- For the most important decisions, he recommends maximizing with his Binstack method.
- For almost anything else, he says satisficing is likely wiser.

## The perfectionist trap

- Jason warns that the same perfectionism that makes you "a force to be reckoned with" also drives impostor syndrome.
- That same trait will lead natural perfectionists to maximize too often.
- Default to satisficing, and reserve maximizing for the few high-stakes, slow decisions like strategy.

## Set an objective threshold for "satisfactory"

- For satisficing activities, he says to define an objective threshold for "what is satisfactory."
- Tie it to a metric. When a metric is below its threshold, investment is warranted.
- Once it is at or above the threshold, you "must not" keep investing in it. By definition it isn't valuable enough to deserve more attention.
- Redirect that investment to areas that are below their own thresholds, or to things worth maximizing.
- This turns "good enough" from a feeling into a rule that stops over-investment.
