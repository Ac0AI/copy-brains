## Cash needs are always higher than the plan says, and deviation in either direction is the reason

Jason argues that a business always takes more money than you expect, even when you account for that fact. He frames it as a version of Hofstadter's Law: "It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law." His version: "A business always takes more money than you expect, even when you take this fact into account."

- He says the standard investor advice, "Always raise more money than you think you need," is correct, and that it is not a ruse by greedy investors chasing extra equity.
- The only way you need exactly the amount you planned for is if everything over the next 18 months goes exactly to plan.
- A deviation in either direction, better or worse, means you need more money.

## How the original funding number is built, and why it is a floor

Jason describes what a founder has on day one: a business plan with a plausible growth trajectory (neither too conservative nor too optimistic) and a cost structure for driving signups and serving customers.

- It costs money to acquire a customer, but the monthly revenue takes time to cover that cost. Costs therefore rise faster than revenue, and a cash investment funds the gap.
- The plan yields a projected cash low-point of $X. You then raise $Y, which covers the operational cash requirement and also de-risks the company with additional investments you couldn't otherwise afford.
- That $Y is built on one plausible scenario. It is a floor, not a buffer.

## Scenario 1: growth is slower than planned, so you need more money

Jason walks through what happens if revenue lags the plan nine months in.

- You have already signed a 5-year office lease and hired support, sales and engineering. You have also brought in training, a comptroller and a business operations person.
- Marketing is over-spending to try to kick-start growth, so you burn more than expected.
- His position is that the correct move is to forge ahead and let revenue catch up, "not fire half your team and retreat."
- Holding that course costs more cash than the plan assumed.

## Scenario 2: growth is faster than planned, so you also need more money

Success also consumes cash, which Jason treats as the counterintuitive half of the argument.

- You scramble to hire ahead in support, sales and engineering. You trade money for speed through recruiting, contractors, office space and finance.
- Customer on-boarding costs arrive faster than planned. Over the long run the revenue more than makes up for them, but right now these unexpected new customers cost much more than their revenue contributions, and cash reserves plummet.
- If sales and marketing is working, you want to spend more on it too (he links this to his annual prepay essay).
- Both outcomes end in the same place: "you'll need more money than you planned."

## Why under-raising is irresponsible

Jason's point is that no one can predict a startup's finances 18 months out with precision, and that this is fine because founders aren't supposed to.

- The failure is denying that uncertainty. Not raising "more than enough" money means "you're denying that reality."
- The result is a time-pressured cash crunch for yourself 9-12 months from now, which he calls irresponsible.
- Practical takeaway: size the raise to cover plan variance in both directions (slower growth and faster growth), not just the plan's projected cash low-point.
