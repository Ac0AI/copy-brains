## Platform dependence is a business-model risk, so pick stable platforms or diversify

Jason argues that building a startup on a volatile platform can kill the company overnight, and that bootstrapped companies have no cushion to survive it. His two defenses: choose platforms with this risk in mind, or diversify so no single platform can sink you.

- Platform risk applies to the *entire-company* case. Using a platform as one part of the business is fine.
- "All technology depends on 'platforms'" (operating systems, browsers, libraries, even customer prejudices). The danger is a platform still "undergoing tumultuous change", especially when the whole company rests on it.
- He closes with: "At least don't pick a rug that's already moved around the house three times."

## Twitter's 2010 ad ban killed the paid-tweet startups

Jason uses TweetUp and Ad.ly as the cautionary case.

- Twitter once allowed unlabeled advertising tweets. TweetUp and Ad.ly acted as liaisons between advertisers and popular tweeters, e.g. an advertiser paying about $3 for someone with 10,000 followers to tweet about a widget. He calls it "AdSense for Twitter".
- In May 2010 Twitter launched its own ad platform and barred "any third party" from injecting paid tweets into a timeline on any service using the Twitter API. The Terms of Service now explicitly prohibit it.
- TweetUp renamed itself PostUp and ended up with vague marketing-buzzword copy and no clear product.
- Ad.ly survived by pivoting to matching celebrities (e.g. Mandy Moore) with major brands (e.g. NBC).
- Both had millions in funding and could afford to flounder. "Bootstrapped companies have no such luxury."

## Apple's App Store and SEO as unpredictable platforms

Jason treats these as two examples of platforms whose rules shift without notice.

- **App Store:** famous for "screwing innovators without notice", with scandals almost weekly about apps inexplicably banned or allowed (one telephony app banned, the next sanctioned). Flurry went from awesome analytics platform to being called out by name by Steve Jobs.
- Rule of thumb: an iPhone app as a useful addition that wouldn't hurt if it vanished is fine. A company built entirely on an iPhone app is in critical danger.
- **SEO:** in his opinion it is "an ever-shifting landscape of shit you chase without ever really knowing what the rules are."
- SEO as one of ten tactics for getting people to your site is fine. Betting the entire business on search traffic is not, since "at any moment a competitor or the platform itself could oust you."

## Platforms that look like lock-in but aren't: the Heroku test

Jason offers a test for whether a platform dependency is truly dangerous: can you leave and keep the business alive?

- Heroku (managed Ruby on Rails hosting: "git push" deploys across redundant servers) looks like scary black-box lock-in.
- It isn't, because the stack is still Rails and Postgres, with free tools you already run on your laptop.
- If you had to leave: get a server, "git clone" the code, install a database, restore from backup. It would be painful, with downtime and refactoring, but that's true wherever you host, and the company survives.
- His conclusion: "you're exploiting Heroku as a tool; your business's success does not depend on them."

## Ecosystems like WordPress and Salesforce are safer than single-owner platforms

Jason distinguishes an ecosystem from a platform controlled by one company. His company WP Engine depends on WordPress.

- An ecosystem is a guild of hundreds of thousands of agencies, products, companies and power users who depend on each other for revenue, attention and credibility.
- WordPress is open source, so Automattic couldn't kill the community "if they tried". He also cites five years of evidence that Automattic supports it, including a deal with WP Engine that landed both on TechCrunch.
- Contrast: Twitter doesn't care which clients are popular, and Apple "rule[s] with an iron fist no matter the consequences".
- Salesforce is a model ecosystem curator. It intentionally leaves "obvious" features out of the base product so as not to harm companies that make money adding them. It is the shark that knows the remora fish must survive.
- Positioning aside, people call WP Engine "The Heroku of WordPress", which he loves, though most WordPress people don't know Heroku, so it isn't a good home-page message.

## The fix: avoid dynamic platforms or diversify your channels

Jason gives two ways to avoid platform dependence.

- **Don't base the company on something dynamic and outside your control.**
- **Diversify.** Keep doing SEO for lead generation, but not as the only source. If it disappears, "you still have nine other ways to get people to your website."
- If you've tried nine things and none work, "the problem is with you, not the ten platforms!"
- In the end you have to depend on *something*, and the rug can always be pulled. The goal is to pick a stable one.
