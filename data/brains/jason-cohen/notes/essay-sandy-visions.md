## The first customer as "magic" you can't fully explain

- Jason describes a new customer signing up as an almost impossible chain of improbable events: a frustrated person searching Google, landing on a site at position #7, being caught by a headline "for reasons he himself could not articulate", hovering over screenshots, weighing a missing "hard requirement" against a clever feature, and sizing up the "professional, yet approachable" About Us page.
- The buyer is often too annoyed with his current provider to worry much about pricing. He then has to pry a company credit card from a gatekeeper and get through a long payment form that "assumes he's guilty of fraud until proven innocent".
- Jason says that at WP Engine events like this happen "with the constancy of the sand-falls of the beach", and each is as magical as the first.
- He quotes Arthur C. Clarke's idea that magic is what you call something until you understand it. His point is that no amount of preparation or effort removes the unbelievable, inscrutable feeling of the first customer.

## Why founders get hooked on growth metrics

- Jason argues the first sale is the most exhilarating moment, more than the first feature. Even tech founders who refuse to think of themselves as "sales people" feel it.
- He compares each new customer to another "hit off that pipe", with none as sweet as the first.
- Single hits stop being enough and you need a "biggest ever" to feel the rush. Founders then become "slaves to the mantra of %MoM growth", push more sophisticated metrics into acceptable ranges, and chase ever more abstract calculations that together get called "scale".

## Obsess over customer and employee happiness, not just financial ratios

- Jason says obsessing over metrics is not wrong, as long as founders obsess over customer happiness and employee happiness at least as much as the "self-serving financial ratios".
- He trusts that customer and employee happiness are the most efficient and personally fulfilling path to the financial results. He links this to his essay on legacy.

## Metric optimization can block bigger opportunities

- Jason says that even a "right-minded" obsession with metrics too often blocks an even greater calling.
- To fulfill the promise of entrepreneurship, meaning what founders can do for customers, communities, employees and the top and bottom lines, visions must "lift beyond the incremental metric and the hourly order rate". Those are "necessary and just, but incomplete".
- He names the cost of staying inside optimization: there is little room for true innovation and exploration in "a land of metrics optimization". He asks how you realize you can move vertically when "your compass measures only two dimensions".

## The crab metaphor: fighting over scraps while the ocean goes unexplored

- Jason uses the image of three crabs fighting over a scrap of seaweed on a beach full of it, which is effectively infinite to a crab.
- He says he goes back to the office the same way, "fighting the other crabs for seaweed", forgetting the wider beach and the ocean and foreign lands beyond it that are "begging to be discovered, explored, won".
- The takeaway is that day-to-day competition over incremental gains hides much larger, unexplored opportunities beyond the current market.
