## Competitors are mostly unknowable, so build for your own customers

Jason uses the Rock, Paper, Scissors literature as an analogy for second-guessing competitors. A new study (via the Telegraph and New Scientist) says rock is the most popular first throw. That produces an endless chain of counter-moves, and he draws a startup lesson from it.

- If neither player knows the study, an opponent throws rock too often, so you should throw paper.
- If the opponent has read the study, he expects you to throw paper and will favor paper himself, so you should throw scissors.
- If he knows that you know that he read it, he flips back to rock. "Double-fake!"
- If you know all of that, you can double-fake too, and the chain has no stable end.
- His takeaway on the game: people with more information win more often.
- Another game tactic he describes is to announce what you will throw and then throw it, on the theory that the other person won't believe you. If A says "rock" and B disbelieves, B's best reply is scissors and second best is rock, so A ties or wins.
- That tactic also stops working once B knows the strategy.

## Don't try to outwit competitors; focus inward

Jason compares the guessing game to trying to work out what your competitors think, what they know, what they think they know, and whether they will react to your knowledge of that guess.

- His conclusion: "screw it, just build the best company you can, for you."
- He argues you are wrong about most of what you think about competitors anyway, "both the good and bad".
- So stop reacting to them and focus inward. Build something you're proud of.
- His framing of the ideal: "Even better than trying to outwit the competition, is to not react to them at all, and just do your best, directly for your customers."

## When information advantage matters, and why it's a trap in business

Jason notes that in the game, more information wins. He does not carry that over to competitive strategy.

- In RPS the payoff structure is symmetric and the counter-move chain is endless, so each extra level of reasoning only helps until the other side matches it.
- A company that spends effort modeling competitor reactions enters the same regress, with worse data than a game player has.
- His alternative is to put that effort into customers and the product, where the work compounds and does not depend on guessing the other side's next move.
