## Starting WP Engine from customer interviews

- In the late 2000s Jason's blog kept crashing when it hit Hacker News each Monday. He asked other bloggers how they kept WordPress running under that load. The answer was always "I don't know, but if you find it tell me."
- That gap became the seed for WP Engine. He ran about 50 customer interviews, and the same exercise showed him his other idea was "a fine idea" but "wasn't a good business."
- The interviews led to the pitch: if hosting were fast, scalable, secure and came with good service, people would pay about 10x what they paid GoDaddy or Bluehost for WordPress hosting.
- Those four points were literally bullet points on the first website, and they are still what people sign up for 13 years later.
- His takeaway is that "customer development and figure out the pain" is still the core work.
- He doubts the Lean Startup book works as a manual. It got founders to "get outside the building," but he sees no evidence that people who follow it fail less.
- Asking "do you like this feature?" teaches nothing because people say yes. The questions have to be directed.
- He recommends Teresa Torres's "Continuous Discovery Habits." Nearly every chapter is usable, including what to do with interview data afterward.
- From that book he also likes "right-storming." Everyone first understands the problem together. Then each person brainstorms alone, in whatever rhythm suits them. Then the group meets to discuss ideas already written down in a Miro board or Google Doc. He says this works on Zoom, where live brainstorming does not.

## How Jason knew WP Engine had product-market fit

- He dates it to January 2012, about two years in, when the team was 4-5 people.
- The signal was that all he could do was keep up with demand. Orders and support tickets flew in, and he couldn't think about new features.
- Hiring was hard because the team was too busy and had no hiring practice.
- Revenue went from roughly $1M to $5M that year, a hockey stick.
- He credits a pricing change as the cause, though he admits it is hard to rerun the experiment. He also notes a first-quarter seasonality effect, since people build websites then, but says growth "never ended" so it wasn't only that.
- The trial had about a 90% conversion rate, which he reads as product-market fit in itself.

## The pricing changes that unlocked growth at WP Engine

All of these came from customer conversations.

- **More sites on higher plans.** Customers said they would happily pay $100/month for one big site, but would not keep a second account elsewhere for a tiny site, such as one for their mom's art studio getting two hits a month. Higher plans should allow more sites. Freelancers also need a place to host a site before launch.
- **Lowest tier from $50 to $29.** Customers on GoDaddy at $5/month could be moved to $20-30, but "mentally" not to $50, even if it was worth $50. His point is that this isn't generic willingness to pay or anchoring. People can't make the jump in one shot.
- **Plug-in products anchor on the host product.** Products that sit inside another ecosystem (WordPress, Salesforce) have a price ceiling set by the main product. People ask how they could pay something similar to a component.
- **A top tier called "Business."** He added the most expensive tier. He says he has "no clue" if that helped.
- **Money-back guarantee instead of free trial.** Hosting costs money immediately, so the two-week free trial lost two weeks of revenue on customers who stayed anyway (90% converted).
  - He switched to charging up front with a 60-day money-back guarantee.
  - He expected it to hurt, but sales went up.
  - Customers said two weeks wasn't enough to check the product out, but two months was.
- A cheap way to test this is to apply it to new customers only, since you can undo it at will.
- He points to his 2012-13 talk "building the ideal bootstrap business" for more on the money-back guarantee and charging up front.

## Picking the key metric: a moment of customer value, not MRR

- Early on, before hypergrowth, his only metric beyond MRR was keeping customers happy. Churn looks good early because there hasn't been time for people to decide they don't like the product. After product-market fit, "who can even think about the metrics," because you have to operate.
- Any number you wouldn't react to if it changed a lot (like raw traffic) is a vanity metric, not a key metric.
- MRR and retention are the most important numbers, because without them you starve. But they are poor to operate on: they lag, and they have many inputs (sales, marketing, upgrades, downgrades, product, service).
- His preferred key metric is the moment in the customer journey where they get value and are likely to succeed and stay. You'll know revenue follows if it happens, and it happens daily, so you can act on it and see results within a week.
- Netflix example: the number of people who put 3 or more DVDs in their queue on day one. This correlated, not necessarily causally, with being about 7x more likely to become a customer and stay at least 6 months. Obvious tactics follow: ask their favorite genre and fill the queue, or suggest the 10 most popular titles.
- Instacart example: total cart value as the Northstar. Its inputs are the number of customers, average cart size and items per cart. The inputs overlap, and "this is not an equation."
- The key metric can and should change over time, especially early.
- Treat the supporting metrics like a car dashboard, not goals: "it's just what's going on." At any moment you can decide to focus on one for a few months.
- A single central metric also helps at scale. Engineers don't own revenue, and sales has quotas. MRR is the output of everyone doing well, so it isn't any one team's primary metric. This avoids "who owns what" fights.
- A central metric lets you ask a directed question like "how do we raise average cart size?" instead of the undirected "how do we make MRR go up?"

## Using excitement as a tiebreaker for prioritization

- Excitement alone is a bad reason to do something. But when several projects all sound good and nobody can tell which will work, he says the tie should go to the one people are excited about.
- His reason is that people "work on it harder and care more and just want to do it more," which is a huge advantage. It also gives that project the best chance of success.
- He says WP Engine does this at scale.
- If one option is clearly better, do that one.

## Whether to raise venture money

- His default answer for traditional venture is "you shouldn't accept it," unless a set of conditions hold. Like any tool, it has a time and place.
- At WP Engine he didn't expect VC to be the right tool at first. Then it became clear, two years in, that the market was huge and growing, with tailwinds, and that this could be a billion-dollar-plus company.
- Personally, he was a bootstrapper by default. He wanted a different journey this time: the constraint shifts from profit to growth, the mandate becomes growth, and money is less of a constraint. He frames it as different, not better.
- He calls it "simply a fact" that if you haven't quit your day job, the company has a lower chance of success. Taking a little money to work on it full time clearly reduces risk.
- Other reasonable uses of money:
  - Hiring one person (the gap between one and two people is big, even if the second isn't a cofounder).
  - Paying for design if design matters and you aren't a designer.
  - Funding marketing experiments.
- You should know exactly what the money is for. "So I can quit my job and buy ads" isn't a plan.
- A good plan looks like this: the next phase (a version, a partnership, a customer count) is blocked only by money. You have a theory backed by customer conversations, competitor observation and people asking you to go in that direction.

## Stepping aside as CEO: the joy, skill, need framework

- Jason gave up the CEO role at WP Engine himself, a few years after the VC round. No investor forced it. He became CTO and brought in Heather Brunner as CEO. Heather is still CEO nine years later, and he calls her the "late joining co-founder," a phrase he borrowed from Reid Hoffman about Jeff Weiner at LinkedIn.
- The framework is three circles: **joy** (what you love), **skill** (what you're good at), and **need** (what the company needs done). It was synthesized from other sources and tweaked by him.
- Each two-circle overlap is a trap, and founders are especially exposed because nobody can tell them otherwise:
  - **Joy + skill, not need.** You love it and you're good at it, so you stay in flow. Classic case: the engineer-founder building the next feature that two people asked for, when they should be doing sales and marketing.
  - **Skill + need, not joy.** This is burnout land. His own example was accounting: Smart Bear's acquirers called its books "the cleanest" they had seen. Bootstrappers do this work because they can't afford to hire. They think they're protecting their team, but the team wouldn't want them burned out, and he says you should ask them.
  - **Joy + need, not skill.** The engineer doing AdWords badly for three months, becoming a self-declared expert, and still not knowing how to manage a hire. He asks who tells the founder they're bad at marketing, and says the answer is a cofounder.
- The goal is not mediocrity but excellence everywhere.
- He applied it to the CEO role at about 70-80 people. Managing people and the board, and building a global sales team, were things he didn't enjoy and wasn't best at. Engineering and product were joy, skill and need.
- It took about four months of struggle. His board member, a repeat founder, told him: "You're thinking one day maybe you'll ring the bell on the NASDAQ and you want the credit." The clincher was "you're the founder, you'll always get the credit."
- That freed him to keep the credit and the ego forever, so he had no reason to run the board meetings. He says the move has been "fantastic."
