## How Jason tells a truly great startup idea from polite "sounds good" feedback

Jason's test is whether the answers you gather converge or diverge as you dig. He borrows the idea from Feynman's Lectures on Physics, where the same contrast separates a real law from a coincidence. The aim is to know when an idea is real and when to give up.

## The problem: people say nice things but aren't buying

- Jason's complaint is that people "say nice things to your face, but really they're not buying", even after twenty systematic customer interviews.
- The usual advice is contradictory. He lists "trust your gut", "trust data", "don't give up", "if it's hard it's not a fit", "don't listen to the haters", "haters have something to teach us".
- He says no formula settles it, because the formula "doesn't know what to do with the answers".
- His answer is a way of reading the answers you already have, not a new set of questions.

## The physics analogy: a coincidence versus a fundamental law

- The drag force on an airplane is approximated as F = Cv², proportional to the square of velocity. That is astonishing given how chaotic the airflow is.
- Jason says it is "not a law at all". It is a "tenuous coincidence".
- It breaks in new situations. It is inaccurate at slow speeds and at very fast speeds. The constant C changes dramatically with small wing perturbations. Removing one wing changes the drag on the other.
- Conservation of Energy behaves differently. Friction seemed to break it, but the lost energy showed up as heat, in exactly the predicted amount. Heat was then explained as the kinetic energy of jiggling particles, and the predictions held again. It survived Relativity and Quantum Mechanics.
- His rule: with the drag equation, "the deeper we dug the more we discovered that the 'law' doesn't encompass much truth". With energy, "the closer we look the more powerful the law becomes". The second case is the mark of a real fundamental law.

## "Truth in startups converges or diverges in the same way"

- A real idea gets stronger as you probe it from more angles, and answers from different people point to the same place.
- A false idea gets blurrier. Each new conversation sends you in a different direction.
- Jason says to watch for "multiple areas of inquisition lead[ing] to a common destination instead of leading to different planets".
- He admits it is not an immutable law. It is "something that behaves like truth".

## Divergent example: the startup idea before WP Engine

- During customer development for an earlier idea, everyone said "That's a great idea, you should do it!"
- Digging into each person's answer, the "truth" diverged:
  - One said to target enterprises, charging $1000/mo and selling through consultants.
  - One said to go freemium and convert 5% to $5/mo.
  - One said to charge a minimum of $50/mo to cut out moochers who email support but don't pay.
  - One said the small-to-mid-sized business market was the untapped niche.
  - Use cases split three ways: measure online ads and forget about leads; feed leads into Salesforce and forget about ads; or reveal general marketing efficacy without closing leads.
- He describes the result as a "blotchy Venn Diagram with twenty lobes of dissonance and no discernible center", with no clear steps forward. It was intriguing and exciting but not fundamental.

## Convergent example: WP Engine

- The more people he spoke with, the more they agreed on the pain, on whether his solution was acceptable, and on what they would spend.
- The converged offer was $50/mo for a WordPress site that is fast, scalable and secure. Tech support should answer the phone and be knowledgeable about WordPress. Add a staging area and backups and "it's a done deal".
- Thirty of forty people agreed to sign up during their interview. Twenty of those thirty later did, and WP Engine launched with a total of thirty customers.

## Use the test honestly, and don't delay the answer

- There is "no rubric" for deciding whether an idea is tenable or when to give up. But the feeling of convergence or divergence is "very strong" if you are introspective and honest with yourself.
- He says "your hardest battle is indeed with yourself". You are tempted to bias the evidence toward validation, and you fear learning that your cool pet idea "is not a business", meaning people won't give you money for it.
- The cost of staying ignorant: "You will meet the same, bitter end, only after a significantly larger investment in time, money, heart, and reputation."
