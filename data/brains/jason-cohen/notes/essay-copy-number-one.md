## Why "undefeated" and "#1" prove less than we think

Jason argues that finishing as the #1 team or company is partly a product of the tournament system itself, not clean proof of superior skill.

- A single-elimination tournament always produces exactly one undefeated team, even if every match is a coin flip.
- The number of teams reaching each round, and the fact that one goes undefeated, is dictated by the system regardless of how random each contest is.
- He says being "undefeated" is "merely a result of the system itself, not necessarily proof that the winner is in fact better than other top contenders."

## Skill versus luck is unknowable, in sports and in business

Sporting results mix skill and luck in "unknowable proportions", which is why underdogs can win on any given day.

- Statistically replaying a match until a significance threshold is reached would reduce luck, but it is neither fun nor practical.
- Business works the same way: a mix of skill and luck in unknowable proportions.
- The market and economy act as a tournament that decides how results are distributed, and it is an experiment that can be run only once.
- Long-tail distributions are common across industries, which Jason takes as evidence of systematic effects outside the usual "luck versus skill" debate.

## Why fixating on the leader misleads

People fixate on whoever is #1 and tacitly assume the contest (marketing, sales, product) is mostly skill, so the marketplace must have picked the rightful leader.

- Jason says he is not sure the skill/luck proportion is known.
- He does not assume the market picks #1, or #2 or anyone else, according to a "repeatable, codify-able law."
- He asks which decisions actually caused success, and notes that this is hard to know.

Questions he poses to challenge the assumption:
- Does a pitch contest winner have a better shot than the #7 finisher who learned from it and is redoubling his efforts?
- Does McDonald's have all the answers, or can the #12 largest fast-food chain also teach something?
- Is the #4253 largest company in the world not still a success story with its own lessons?
- If the Google founders had published the PageRank paper at MIT instead of Stanford, without being plugged into the Silicon Valley system, would Google have existed?

## How to respond when told to "be more like" a famous company

Jason lists reasons to be skeptical of advice that points at a famous example.

- "More like Google": the person may be citing the only company they know to compare to.
- "More like 37signals": almost no successful companies are like 37signals.
- "More like Apple": the person probably does not know what really goes on inside Apple or whether it resembles your company. He also asks which Apple is meant: today's, or Apple at 3 years old doing hardware with a late-1970s mindset.

## A better kind of role model

The more interesting comparison, in Jason's view, is a little-known company that someone says you resemble.

- You visit its website and try the product, and it resonates.
- It feels like a "finer, more mature version" of yourself.
- It reinvigorates you because it inspires you to become a better version of what you already are, not because of its revenue or celebrity status.
- His conclusion: it is fine to muse about being #1, but not everyone should strive to become just like #1.
