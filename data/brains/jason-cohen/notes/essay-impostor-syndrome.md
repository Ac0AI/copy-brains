## Feeling like a fraud is normal, and Jason says the cause explains the cure

Jason felt like a fraud every day in Smart Bear's early years. He sold a "wobbly, buggy tool" and called himself an expert in a field that didn't exist, since Smart Bear made the first commercial tool for peer code review. His friend Jason Young, 16 years in, told him: "I've been at this for 16 years and I still feel like a fraud." Jason's takeaways:
- Looking back, the feelings were misplaced. The tool saved customers time and headache, and he had seen more teams in more situations than anyone doing the day job.
- "Emotions don't respond to logic." The feelings don't go away even when they ought to.
- He cites Mike Meyers, Jodie Foster and Fields Medalist June Huh as people with the same doubt.
- Cited figures: "40% of successful people consider themselves frauds", and 84% of surveyed entrepreneurs and small business owners scored as feeling like impostors at moderate, frequent or intense levels.

## The specific doubts a founder carries early on

Jason lists the doubts he had while building Smart Bear:
- Did the claim that the tool "cuts code review time in half" hold up, or had he just repeated it so often he stopped questioning it?
- Should he handle large purchases with no knowledge of accounting, cash flow, invoicing or purchase orders, while Accounts Payable belittled him?
- Who was he to teach "best practices" for code review, even in a published book?
- Was he too young, the tool too crappy to charge for, or himself too inexperienced without an MBA or sales training?
- Was Smart Bear a "real company"?

Sales, he found, "isn't as mystical and unknowable as I feared."

## Signs you have it

Jason's checklist:
- You dismiss compliments and awards as "no big deal."
- Mild, constructive criticism crushes you.
- You think you're less capable than others believe, and fear being found out.
- You think peers are more "adult" and "have their shit together."
- You credit successes to luck and failures to ability.
- You struggle to take credit for accomplishments.
- You feel like the embodiment of "fake it until you make it."

## Why founders feel this: perfectionism, not confidence

Jason argues the entrepreneur-as-confident-genius stereotype is wrong.
- A perfectionist sees flaws in everyone's work, so she wants to do it her way and struggles to delegate.
- She also sees an endless stream of flaws in her own work, and can't accept that others are impressed by what she sees as mediocre works-in-progress.
- "Our entrepreneurial motivation is not confidence, it's an insatiable desire to do it our way."
- Doing it your own way is not the same as being confident it is the correct way. It means never accepting any idea as best, even if you prefer yours to everyone else's.

## Telling constructive doubt from harmful doubt

Jason says the feeling can be useful if it pushes you to learn. He quotes Andy Wibbels: "If I don't feel like a fraud at least once a day then I'm not reaching far enough."

**Constructive:**
- Doubt your "expert" title, so read, write and immerse yourself in the field daily.
- Doubt your software quality, so fix bugs fast, write unit tests proactively and thank customers for their patience.
- Doubt you deserve your reputation, so work to earn it.
- Notice you're not yet as good as you want to be, and see yourself slowly improving.
- Not a "real company" yet, so focus on making customers successful until they stop caring about company size.

**Harmful:**
- Worrying nightly about being exposed and scanning for trivially easy "escape-hatch" jobs.
- Covering software flaws with graphic design and heavy sales pitches.
- Losing sleep over fear of exposure.
- Avoiding speaking, writing or programming, and feeling like a trapped animal when forced to do them.
- Feeling guilty whenever someone pays you or believes you.

His advice is to recognize which mode you're in and deal with it logically.

## Four questions to answer the impostor voice

- **Add "yet."** "I can't do this job... yet." "I don't deserve the title I have... yet."
- **"Am I a curious student?"** If you saw a student humbly and energetically pursuing growth, you'd think "good for them, how can I help," not "impostor for not being an expert." Acting as a curious student is being a student, "which is awesome."
- **"Do I deserve to be peers with my peers?"** Yes. Neil Gaiman and Neil Armstrong feel the same way, and you know both are wrong.
- **"Do I deserve the title or responsibility I have?"** Did "some idiot" give you a hand-out for nothing? If not, you earned it.
- **"I feel stretched and don't know what I'm doing."** Jason calls that "the human condition... for humans who strive." Only the ignorant and foolhardy fail to see there's more to learn. Discomfort while striving is normal, not evidence you shouldn't be doing it.

## The last-resort argument when logic fails

Jason's closing argument:
- You believe Mike Meyers, Jodie Foster and June Huh are talented, and might believe Jason is an expert in peer code review and startups.
- Yet they and he doubt themselves daily, "and we're wrong." So "if we're wrong, you're wrong too."
- Don't stop striving. Stop holding yourself to an impossible standard and stop worrying what others think: "They're not thinking about you at all."
