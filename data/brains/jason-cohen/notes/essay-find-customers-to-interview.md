## Interview ground rules before any outreach

- Jason says to interview only the ICP. Family and friends will say "that sounds cool" but cannot say whether real potential customers will pay. "Go where the ICPs are."
- "Good enough is good enough." An hour-long interview is best, but a partial one is fine. If the person seems interested, always ask for a follow-up, because that is "10x more valuable."
- Frame the request as asking for advice. People hate being surveyed but love being asked their opinion. Flatter them honestly ("because you're an expert", "I've really loved your article on ...").
- Make scheduling trivial: send a calendar link with many open slots and minimize back-and-forth.
- Expect a low success rate. Cold outreach is "soul-crushing" for you and annoying for them. A 10% response rate would be fantastic. Don't take rejection personally.
- He does not need many methods: "You only need one or two methods to work," especially if each interview yields new referrals. The goal is to either invalidate the idea or change it until it is validated.
- What won't help: building software, adding features, and "yapping about nothing on social media."

## Referrals: the compounding source

- Ask your whole network to introduce you to ICPs. The network has almost no ICPs itself, but it probably knows some. Use a tight ICP description so people immediately think of the right person.
- At the end of every interview, ask who else you should talk to. Try to get two or more.
- Recruit them into the journey instead of just asking for names: "I'm trying to have 20 conversations like this, to give this product a real shot. It's my dream to create something people genuinely love. It would mean the world to me if you could help me by intro'ing me to a few other people who are [my ICP]."
- If you average one referral per interview, "you have an infinite supply of interviews."

## LinkedIn outreach (the WP Engine playbook)

- For WP Engine, Jason used LinkedIn to find people with the target title and industry (web developers using WordPress) and asked for an hour to discuss a new startup concept for which they were the ideal customer.
- Result: out of 50 requests, 40 agreed, and 30 eventually became customers.
- Why the hit rate was so high:
  1. He offered payment: "I will pay any fee you feel is fair for an hour of your time. I'm not asking for a donation; I'm genuinely interested in your expertise and opinion." Only 1 of 40 asked to be paid. "Reciprocity works."
  2. He researched each person briefly (who their business serves, location, anything in recent blog posts to comment on), so the outreach was personal.
  3. He tried to get an intro from a mutual connection, and wrote the InMail for the connector. It was polite, did not over-sell, and sounded like a normal person, not a robotic marketer.

## Communities, complaints and people asking for help

- **Forums (Reddit, Discord, Slack, Facebook Groups):** Selling is a no-no, but customer development is not selling; it is learning about people's lives. Post one question per post and don't flood. Answers are disconnected, but good for surveying and honing hypotheses.
- Lurk to learn when the community is most active. Give high-quality answers to others first, because communities are "give-and-take, not just take." Treat it as a long-term relationship, not hit-and-run.
- Hacker News, Quora and Craigslist are possible, but they are general forums where ICPs are hard to target and long-term relationships are nearly impossible. Small independent community sites may welcome a new member or sell cheap, tasteful ads. Don't dismiss old-school Facebook Groups, even for modern software.
- **Complainers:** Set up searches for your problem area and competitors across social media (including YouTube comments), forums, and review sites (G2, TrustRadius, Capterra). When someone complains, find them on LinkedIn and reach out, referencing their review.
- Best signal: people who have taken action (evaluated other products, switched, or built workarounds). "It's free to complain, but expensive to act, so they must really mean it."
- Feedback and voting sites are informative for trends, but caution applies: your product, segment, goals and maturity differ. Verbose posters are more likely to want to talk.
- **Help seekers:** Use keyword monitoring for "How do I ______?" posts, actually help them, then ask for a call.

## Landing pages, influencers and content

- **Buy traffic to a one-page landing page** asking for interested people. Bid on low-competition keywords; you want a few targeted visitors, not a scalable channel. A/B test ads and page. AdWords is the most sophisticated for targeting and testing, but Twitter, LinkedIn or Facebook may fit where the audience is. Tim Ferriss used AdWords to test titles and positioning for The 4-Hour Work Week.
- Clicks are a positive signal on positioning but not an interview, so collect emails from people willing to talk. Offer something (an Amazon gift card, a charity donation, "first year free").
- Bonuses: add web chat so a visitor sees a real human; offer a fee to those unconvinced; gate content the ICP cares about behind an email; put a scheduler link on the page, or make the scheduler itself the landing page, including the participant fee (as John-Paul Anderson did).
- **People who already talk about the problem:** Bloggers, newsletter writers and social posters like discussing it. Engage thoughtfully first, then offer a chat and to share your aggregate findings. "It doesn't matter how many followers they have"; smaller ones are more likely to engage and later promote you, including via affiliate links.
- Guest posts work only if significantly better than their median post, and better on multi-author sites than single-author blogs.
- **Influencers:** Don't ask them to spam their audience. Offer value: share your findings summary for a post, a revenue split on promotion later, a useful report from your prototype, or a truly great guest piece.

## Paid sources and investors

- **Pay for meetings:** User Interviews (and maybe Fiverr or Upwork) has people who talk for money. Use a strong ICP filter; your ICP may not be there.
- **Expert networks** (GLG, Third Bridge) connect you with verified operators, including Directors, VPs and C-level people, for candid talk about budget, decision-making and competitive dynamics. They cost more than most options.
- **Angels and VCs:** They talk to founders for a living and have broad industry perspective. Treat their input as "ideation" not "validation," because "ultimately they don't know any more than you do. If they did, they wouldn't have such a large failure rate." Their real value is introductions to actual ICPs.
- **Professionally engaged people:** Job postings reveal pain points (read the requirements and responsibilities patterns); contact hiring managers. Look for backers of similar products (Kickstarter prepayers, ProductHunt voters). Newly funded startups (via Crunchbase, AngelList) may enjoy talking to another founder.

## Build in public, micro-tools, podcasts and cold outreach

- **#buildinpublic:** Twitter was the main place; Bluesky is growing, with some on Threads. LinkedIn "isn't very good for this." Comment usefully on others' posts, then ask for referrals. Beware that other early-stage founders are almost certainly not your ICP.
- **Free micro-tool:** Build something SLC (simple, lovable, complete). It serves both early conversations and a permanent inbound funnel. HubSpot pioneered this; at $2B revenue it still works, with 30+ tools.
- **Podcast:** Pitch an episode where you interview the podcaster on the topic (they must be an ICP). Or run your own: Steve McLeod's podcast, described as being for his ICP, now has a waiting list of people wanting to be interviewed.
- **Cold outreach (email, DMs):** Low conversion but cheap. A/B test a tight message and offer a reward.

## Physical places, PR and events

- **Waiting people:** Approach people in lines, airport terminals, between conference sessions, or in live-stream comment sections. Gifts help: Nick Basile used free donuts with university students; Karsten Duus Wetteland found people in his own building (future encounters make them friendlier). If you work in the target industry, interview coworkers.
- **Early PR:** Usually late-stage, but if you are an expert with interesting insights and good at interviews, it builds credibility and inbound conversations. "You have 'given first,' and now you can 'take.'" Stunts count: Brian Chesky's "Obama O's" and "Cap'n McCain's" cereal at the 2008 DNC; Alexis Ohanian's personalized Reddit luggage tags to the first few hundred people mentioning Reddit.
- **Conferences and networking events:** The event must attract your ICP. People love talking about themselves; prepare 3-5 questions. Bonus: earn a speaking slot for instant credibility. Noah Kagan gave his speaker fee to the audience ($50 each) at FinCon.
- **Make your own events:** Giff Constable's example: a founder targeting mothers of young children failed at pickup zones, got a few minutes at playgrounds, then succeeded by hosting evening events at a spa with pedicures and wine. Jitpal ran an industry panel with experts and C-suite; costly, but it led to many ICP conversations and initial sales. A local Meetup.com group takes time and works best in a large city, but can last for years and become a brand fixture, and you need no existing following.
