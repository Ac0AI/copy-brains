## Honesty about being small wins early customers

- Jason argues customers forgive honest mistakes from earnest people, not from "stolid, cold, inhuman corporations."
- In Smart Bear's first year his code review software was buggy, yet early customers were fiercely loyal.
- He ties that loyalty to honesty about who he really was: a company of one.
- "Truth is such a rare thing, it is delightful to tell it." (Emily Dickinson, which he quotes approvingly)

## The Tom story: a customer who knew Jason was a one-man shop

- Tom, a customer at a large company with thousands of employees and billions in revenue, phoned Jason. Jason notes software developers rarely call anyone.
- Tom had spent six weeks sending real bug reports. He debugged a lot of Jason's code himself, with help from his own employees.
- Tom then listed 20 new features he wanted. On timing he said: "Oh, I know you're just a one-man shop, so just do your best. If you get through this half as fast as you get through bugs, we'll be fine."
- Jason always wrote "we" and used support@smartbear.com, never his own name. Tom still figured it out from the writing style and from always reaching only Jason on the main line.
- Jason had been told big companies don't buy from one-man shops. He nearly gave the defensive line that he used consultants when workload rose (which wasn't true) and nearly started pitching service quality.
- He decided that would insult Tom's intelligence and set the relationship back. Tom was effectively saying: "I know who you really are, and I accept it. I still want to do this."
- Jason chose to match that honesty.

## Why Tom put up with so much: customers who become heroes

- Jason visited Tom in Ottawa to meet two of Tom's bosses about a widespread purchase and roll-out of the peer code review tool.
- Tom introduced Jason as a small Austin company he found half a year earlier and had been guiding so the tool "now works perfectly for our environment." Tom also claimed he did sixty code reviews every day.
- The bosses checked Tom's evidence and approved the roll-out, which became a big order for Jason.
- Jason's conclusion: "Tom was a hero." Tom got fewer bugs in delivered code and trained new hires faster, not by buying from IBM or adopting a process from Dr. Dobbs, but by finding a small company and directing its product.
- Tom put up with screens locking up, reviews disappearing, installers installing the wrong files and occasional out-of-memory crashes.
- Jason says Tom tolerated it because:
  - It was just him and Jason.
  - He knew Jason always kept his word.
  - He could stick his neck out for Smart Bear without being let down.
  - Jason would keep the product solving his problems as it changed.
- Tom pinned his reputation on the product and won, and lived vicariously through Smart Bear as a product designer.
- Jason adds that if he hadn't owned up and behaved honestly, none of this might have happened.

## Who your first hundred customers really are

- Jason says the first hundred won't be big companies with bureaucratic purchasing that you bluff through, or well-known consumer-advocacy bloggers.
- They will be early adopters: people who like trying new things, like working with new companies that still have spark and something to prove, want to be part of the creative process, and want to say they were there at the beginning.
- If you pretend to be something you're not, they'll see through it.
- The cost: "You've lied to those who would have loved you for who you are," which is not how you build a relationship.
- He links this to targeting your ideal customer persona.

## Honesty is a small-company advantage

- Jason is clear it doesn't mean never telling a lie. Authenticity doesn't mean abandoning social white lies; there is a business equivalent of "No those pants don't make you look fat."
- He says honesty should in theory be easier, quoting Mark Twain: "if you tell the truth you don't have to remember anything."
- In practice, businesses are accustomed to "fluffing your feathers and putting on a show," so acting like a normal human being takes effort.
- "Be yourself" is as hard in business as in personal life, but worth it.
- He calls this one of the few advantages small companies have over big ones: "Use it."
- Context he gives: this happened before "relationship" became a marketing buzzword, before blogs and Twitter.
