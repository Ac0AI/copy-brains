## Strategic planning with the fairytale structure: why it works

- Jason says fairytale structure is "a cheat code for effective communication" because "our brains are pre-wired for fairy-tales".
- The structure: once upon a time there was an unsuspecting hero; every day, the way things are; but then, one day, a change that motivates action; adventure begins; it was almost impossible because of obstacles; until finally victory; and forever after, the world is different.
- He uses it for sales calls at WP Engine. A under-a-minute case study of a food blogger whose site crashed on Thanksgiving, their busiest day, costing thousands of dollars in revenue and hundreds of newsletter sign-ups. They moved to WP Engine, it never happened again, and they now just focus on their business.
- He also applies it to presentations, fundraising and product proposals.
- His less obvious use: a strategic plan, meaning analyzing the current situation and deciding what to do. For a single team, this means being in command of the most critical things to do right now.
- He builds WP Engine quarterly department plans this way. Over about two years, he and a few product teams refined the "Fairytale Plan". The process resembles the Double-Diamond design process, and the final result matches the fairytale structure.

## The five-part Fairytale Plan

1. **Objectives:** what we're trying to get, and what happens if we don't.
2. **Obstacles:** what stands in our way.
3. **Actions:** how we overcome the obstacles while advancing the objectives.
4. **Measures:** how we'll know we've been successful, and when we're finished.
5. **Not Doing:** the important things we've chosen not to do so we can achieve the above.

- Guiding question for objectives: "If we weren't encumbered by roadblocks, with no distractions and no interruptions, how would we advance our strategy right now?"
- Guiding question for actions: how do we overcome the obstacles while advancing the objectives, with the people and capabilities we currently have?
- Unlike a real fairytale, where surprising the hero with obstacles is fun, the plan must clarify obstacles up front.
- The final plan is clear and simple, and hides the complexity of the process that created it.
- Every team and the company needs one. Without it you're probably not working on the right things, and not communicating what must be done in a way everyone can implement.

## Objectives: strategy vs. plan

- "If you run very fast in the wrong direction, you fail." Objectives name the right direction.
- Objectives shouldn't summarize the whole strategy. Link to the strategy document and highlight 1-3 objectives that make sense to advance right now.
- "Right now" is the difference between plan and strategy. Strategy is the long-term vision and main ways to achieve it. The plan selects specific next steps that materially advance the strategy while delivering results needed today, typically revenue growth, cost reduction or an internal transformation.
- Expect fluidity between exploring the problem space and solution space. If you can't invent a working solution later, you may need different objectives, or decide that "eliminating obstacles" is all you can do right now.
- It's fine for objectives to stay the same for a few quarters; it gives stability and focus. To test whether you're sticking too long, ask how the world is different. If there are good answers, you're making progress.

## Obstacles: the most important section

- Jason cites Einstein: "If I had an hour to solve a problem, I'd spend 55 minutes thinking about the problem and 5 minutes thinking about solutions." This is where most time goes.
- Resist jumping to solutions or saying "we've already talked about this." Without fully exploring the problem space you don't know the goal of the solution space, you repeat mistakes and avoid difficult truths.
- Generate many options first. The team benefits from clarity on what to tackle and what they're allowed to leave alone "even though it's on fire".
- Obstacle types to explore:
  - Difficulty executing the strategy (novel builds, algorithms, scale, pivots, repositioning).
  - Team challenges: skills, experience, motivation, burnout, headcount.
  - Competitive pressure.
  - Customer retention.
  - Interruptions (interrupt-driven vs. planned work; reduce via automation or rejecting work types).
  - Too many bugs.
  - Dependencies and coordination.
  - Process bottlenecks (step-changes in productivity, not nice-to-haves).
  - Market evolution.
- Narrow to the 1-3 most critical. The test: which few issues, if you took a huge bite out of them and let all others fester, would substantially improve velocity, competitiveness, team dynamic or retention? Versus issues that, left untreated, would block the objectives even if five others were fully solved.
- His order of precedence when several serious problems exist, picking whichever comes first:
  1. Retention (if customers are leaving, the business model and product aren't working)
  2. People (must have the right team)
  3. Throughput (can't do enough strategic work per month)
  4. Growth
  5. Competition
  6. Health of the code base
  7. Execution risk
- His rule of thumb for a "good" monthly cancellation rate: 3%/mo consumer, 2%/mo small business, 1%/mo enterprise.

## Choosing main activities

- Finding the crux is the emotionally hardest part; deciding what to do is the intellectually hardest. No framework makes you creative.
- Start in "generation mode," coming up with as many ideas as possible, then switch to "selection mode". Draw on idea queues from around the company, and use Extreme Brainstorming if stuck.
- Select a few large, impactful things ("Rocks"). Otherwise you chip away at incremental work, believing you're progressing because you're "agile", without tearing down obstacles or achieving objectives fast enough.
- The Herbie parable, from Goldratt's *The Goal*: a hiker who keeps falling behind blocks the group reaching the summit. Three options, which map to solutions:
  1. Leave Herbie behind: let that problem burn or ignore work you assumed "must" be done.
  2. Help Herbie get better: do extra work that mitigates the problem.
  3. Decide you won't reach the goal: change the objectives to something achievable.
- Best solutions often synthesize several ideas. At WP Engine, an idea that speeds customer websites often also cuts costs, since it takes less CPU time or fewer bytes transferred. Even a bonkers idea may combine with a practical one into a great solution.
- For final selection he recommends the Binstack method. Objectives and obstacles act as natural filters, eliminating anything that doesn't address them, with a few secondary prioritization dimensions allowed.
- He says not to use ROI analysis for the critical work. ROI suits smaller activities where the goal is the "best use of time". For critical work the goal is "to maximize your impact on the most critical things".
- He points to his Rocks, Pebbles, and Sand framework for covering all work types, including medium-sized work where ROI is appropriate.

## Measures and indicators

- It's easier to avoid accountability by believing hard work and high throughput equal impact. "Focus on inputs, not outputs" is true when executing but false if it means you don't get the outputs you need.
- Don't measure against metrics you don't believe in, and don't insist everything important reduces to a number.
- Ideal numeric measures are objective, well-defined, measured daily, with a clear stopping point where you can declare victory. Use his KPI framework as a checklist for a good metric.
- For non-numeric concepts, capture the essence in crisp writing understandable inside and outside the team. He notes AI can turn a rambling audio description into a pithy statement. Examples: "Be revered as the most innovative product in the category"; "Have the best social media content."
- Such statements are powerful even if people later disagree on progress; that debate is the point of metrics, since it sorts out how things are going and whether to change.
- Test for sticking with objectives too long: the "how will the world be different" answers show progress even when objectives repeat.

## Not Doing: saying no out loud

- Guiding question: what do we really want to do but must wait on so more important or urgent things can be done?
- Decisions are about saying "no" even more than "yes". Without articulating the no's, the team is less focused, decisions less clear, and stakeholders wonder whether you know about the problems you skipped.
- Deciding on a few things means deciding against dozens. "Not for now" is mostly "not ever", since you always have 10x more ideas than time, as every Jira backlog and prioritization spreadsheet shows.
- Unstated, it looks like you don't know those things exist. "Perception is reality." Saying it out loud also steels your team to stand firm on "no" when a big customer complains about something you intentionally ignored.
- Revisit the list next quarter. Many items will seem less important; some will now be right.
- At WP Engine it is a simple one-line-per-bullet list of topics, without reasons. You could add reasons, or a Now / Next / Later roadmap.
- His preference is "Now / Likely / Maybe", which signals confidence the ideas are even right. If you get to under 50% of "Later" items, they're "merely a possibility".
- It's not a random bucket of 100 feature requests. It's the important, even urgent, things that barely missed the cut, the difficult decisions.
- Quotes he uses: Buffett ("really successful people say no to almost everything"), Derek Sivers ("You can do anything, but not everything"), Steve Jobs ("Innovation is saying no to 1000 things").

## Worked example: Tesla

- **Objectives:** accelerate the world's transition to sustainable energy via EVs. Invent desirable, high-performance, safe battery-powered vehicles; create scalable, cost-effective production lines to be profitable; generate excitement and loyalty by bucking the view that EVs are impractical and low-performance.
- **Obstacles:** intrinsic difficulty of inventing new batteries and production lines; market skepticism; manufacturing scale; lack of charging infrastructure causing range anxiety.
- **Activities:** battery innovation ("the insight is that the battery is the key"); direct-to-consumer sales to bypass dealerships; Gigafactories ("factories are not just a necessary step but strategically vital"); Supercharger network.
- **Metrics:** battery cost per kWh and discharge rate; units sold and EV market share; fully completed cars per day per Gigafactory; new Supercharger stations; non-numeric consumer delight ("never wanting a gas-powered car again").
- **Not doing:** safety and practicality (solve batteries and performance first); affordable models (start as a luxury brand until scale and costs improve); expanding to China (needs a popular working car first).
