## Why "I had the problem myself" is only a starting point

Jason argues that the most common startup origin story, "I had the problem myself, so I built a product," is also the most common origin story of startups that fail. It is a start, not a justification. For a company to succeed, a market must exist at an acceptable price, matching customers' willingness and ability to buy. Often left out of that list is the founder: the company has to be right for you, personally. His simplest test is "Why are *you* the right person to start and grow this company?"

He says the claim implies seven things: (1) I understand the problem, (2) I have an attachment and passion for the problem, (3) I know how to solve it, (4) I have the ability to execute it, (5) I understand my customers because I am one, (6) others have this problem and will choose my solution, and (7) I have what it takes. Some of these are true, and he calls that the trap: "Intermittent reinforcement is the strongest mechanism for conditioning," so because some are true, founders can't see why others are false. That blindness "consistently results in failure." Most startups may begin this way; most of those startups fail.

He backs this with others' warnings:
- Eric Ries: "You are not your customer."
- Evan Williams: "Be a user of your own product... But don't trick yourself into thinking you are your user."
- Cranky Product Manager: if the VP of Engineering or the VP of Marketing thinks the target customer is just like them, "you're doomed."

## You understand your version of the problem, not your customers'

Jason says you do understand the problem, but only your version of it. Getting 1000 customers means solving a variety of related problems, even inside one niche. Sometimes the broader understanding is real: a consultant who has seen 13 variants, or an engineer with ten years across three companies. Even then, being an "expert" is its own form of blindness.

What to check through your customers' eyes:
- **Language.** Discord used to say "get a free community space," but kids were used to "setting up servers," so "get a free chat server" worked better. Their community spaces are still called "servers."
- **Variants.** Onboarding pain looks different by segment: Enterprise needs multi-phase, white-glove processes and training; small businesses need templates and ready access to tech support; consumer products need self-service options and wizards. Pick some to solve, or use features and language to show you address all of them.
- **Integrations** with systems you haven't heard of.
- **Budget.** Theirs might be 1/10th or 10x yours.

His fix: treat your initial understanding as a starting point and interview customers purposefully to get the full answer.

## Passion is real and necessary, but it won't carry you alone

Jason says attachment to the problem is the item most likely to be true, and it matters. Passion gets founders through the hard times:
- constant rejection from customers, hires and investors
- friends, family, Twitter and Hacker News dismissing you
- bad reviews and cancellations
- engineering that takes longer than expected
- customer acquisition slower than dreamed
- watching others succeed while you struggle, especially a direct competitor

He notes that the day before a competitor goes out of business, all their social media and press is positive: hiring, growing, new features, happy customers. "Do not believe what you read."

Founders need some personal connection, loving the work or the problem, ideally both, because the company becomes an extension of who you are. "I solved my own problem" is a good motivator, but you must be strong enough to face what is coming, which means doing the other things he describes.

## Knowing how to solve it for one case is not solving it for the market

Jason says you know how to solve one facet of the problem: the exact configuration and constraints you had. Most things with computers can be solved, so he believes you can handle the other cases too. But there are cases where this is false, especially in AI, where demos look great and then immediately fail, not good enough often enough, in the real world.

## "It" is the whole company, not the code

When founders say they have the ability to execute "it," Jason points out that "it" is everything a company must do to succeed, and most of that is not writing code. Questions he asks:
- Can you write a home page headline that attracts the right person in three seconds, or does it just say "______ doesn't have to suck" because you're a lazy writer who has never written a high-performing hook?
- Can you get dozens of qualified leads onto the pricing page, or have you never created a single successful marketing campaign?
- Can you interview twenty potential customers before they buy, or do you not know how to find them?

This doesn't mean you will fail. It means failing is likely if you ignore all this and "go back to writing code," assuming that because you have something working, the rest of the company will unfold naturally.

## You are not like your customers

Jason is blunt: "You are absolutely not like your customers." You were so frustrated with current solutions that you built your own instead of buying and implementing something that existed. So you are like "exactly none of your customers."

Customers have day jobs. You're lucky if this problem is in their top three right now, and those top three are what they have budget and time to solve. His prescription is to find and interview potential customers to learn what they are actually like, using your own experience as the initial theory, and iterate until you actually understand them.

## The #1 failure: assuming others have the problem and will buy your solution

Jason calls this fallacy "the #1 reason why companies fail when they start with 'I solved the problem for myself.'" The problem and solution may be real, but many other things must go right before there is a market that can sustain even a solo entrepreneur. Enough people must:
- have the problem, and
- know they have it, so they search for a solution, and
- have a budget for solving it, and
- prefer buying from you over a competitor, and
- stay longer than a couple of months.

Existing solutions may be "good enough" to customers, who won't risk switching to a new product from a new company that lacks features, doesn't lack bugs, and might not exist in two years. His line: "There are a hundred times more legitimate problems in the world than there are markets to buy solutions to those problems." Believing "where there's a problem, there's a company" will almost always disappoint.

## You don't have what it takes yet, and neither did any successful founder

Jason encourages founders: you don't know what you're capable of until you try. Today you probably don't have what it takes, but that was true of every successful first-time founder. Don't give in to impostor syndrome, but expect an emotional gauntlet. Almost everything you need, you lack yet: knowledge of customer, market, marketing, sales, finance and skills.

He uses himself as evidence: even second-time founders typically fail, and after four successful startups, two unicorns, both bootstrapped and funded, "still most of my initial ideas about most things are wrong." So everyone needs a Beginner's Mind and should use their pivot points to win in their own way. His closing line: "Your current idea is the spark; the right idea is still out there. You have to go get it."
