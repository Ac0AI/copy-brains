## Brittle points: single dependencies that can kill an otherwise healthy company

Jason Cohen defines "brittle points" as places where a sudden change makes the company fail catastrophically, "regardless how wonderful everything else is." The product works, customers pay, sales are picking up, and then one component breaks. His examples:
- **Platform changes.** An app drops to zero downloads after Apple builds the feature into iOS. A Google Workspace add-on drops to zero sales after Google builds it into Docs. A Twitter tool breaks when Twitter removes API functionality.
- **Channel saturation.** The first marketing channel saturates fast, and growth stalls at a low but non-zero rate.
- **Channel wipeout.** Large bidders triple cost per click, Google's SEO algorithm changes, the big industry event stops, the hot blog loses its traffic, or the magazine running the ads closes.
- **Customer loss.** One big customer representing 80% of revenue leaves. Signing that customer wasn't a mistake, because it funded the company.
- **Key-person loss.** An early 10x person can mint the company but may be irreplaceable. Finding, convincing and ramping a replacement takes too long when pay is almost nothing.

He notes that all young companies, and some mature ones, have dependencies like these. You have to rely on outside technology, vendors and people.

## The engineering analogy: redundancy buys robustness, at real cost

Jason borrows how engineers harden a single server.
- A power supply, strip or cord can fail, so data centers add a second set of each. "In short: redundancy"; "It's twice as expensive, but it buys robustness." He says airplanes do the same.
- City power can fail, so data centers keep gas generators, stockpile gasoline, and test and maintain the units weekly. They often run several generators.
- The whole data center is itself a brittle point, so clouds add identical servers in a physically separate data center with different power and network vendors.
- That requires a smart traffic-routing system, which "becomes--you guessed it--a Brittle Point."

His takeaway: fixing brittle points adds cost and complexity and sometimes creates new brittle points. "Reliability is expensive." The same pattern applies to every business failure above.

## Neutralizing each type of brittle point

**One platform**
- Be multi-platform. At WP Engine, they run on all three major clouds (AWS, Google, Azure).
- A Twitter listening tool could add LinkedIn, Threads and Bluesky. Extra features then double as risk mitigation.
- Or build only on platforms whose owners are committed to never competing with their ecosystem. Ideally the platform promotes the ecosystem so it becomes a growth vector. He says Salesforce is currently the best at this.

**One marketing channel**
- Find additional channels so variation in any one isn't fatal.
- This is a "double-win" of growth plus risk mitigation, which makes it especially worth investing in.

**One big customer**
- Get a long-term contract with a serious breakup clause, as insurance while you win replacements.
- Prioritize sales until that customer is a smaller share of revenue.
- Take up-front payments, so you have cash to invest in that growth.
- Charge more than you originally calculated, for the same cash-flow cushion.
- Wrong attitude: "We now have a large customer, so pour extra money into development to make sure we don't lose it." Right attitude: use that money to win other customers.

**One key employee**
- They may leave, and they will inevitably get sick or take vacation.
- He pushes back on the startup refrain that everyone must work 70+ hours a week to the exclusion of everything else: "Talk about brittle!"

## How to prioritize: keep a list, attack one at a time

- "Solving these things takes time and money." You can't just hire three great developers or snap your fingers and find three new marketing channels.
- Maintain a list of these risks and periodically ask: "Which single one is best to attack right now?"
- Example: experimenting with new marketing channels is cheaper and easier than finding, interviewing, convincing and managing a second developer. A second channel also generates revenue, which lets you afford the second developer. So put all your energy into getting a second channel working.
- Manage risk rather than trying to eliminate all of it at once. Tackle one or two items at a time.

## Brittleness scales up with the company: the HubSpot example

- As you grow, the "chunks" that create brittleness grow too, creating new risks and new investments.
- In 2016 HubSpot had $260M revenue, was growing about 60% a year, and had about a thousand employees. It was not brittle in any of the ways above.
- But Jason says it was a single-product company, which at that scale is a brittle point. A sea-change in the inbound marketing software market could be fatal, and a maturing, saturating market limits long-term growth.
- The redundancy was to become a multi-product company. The second product had to scale at least as well as the first, because it's still brittle if one product is 95% of revenue.
- In his words, HubSpot attacked that problem, and "in 2024 it's clear that they succeeded."

## The biggest brittle point: the founder's whole identity

- Jason says almost all founders, himself included, suffer from the largest chunk: "the entire company."
- This is one reason founders are often sad, sometimes permanently depressed, after a successful sale. The company was their identity and life for years. They may have no hobbies or close friends left, and may have sacrificed family or health.
- The fix is not two companies or two jobs. That's burnout, and a lack of singular focus creates worse outcomes.
- Instead, realize there were things you loved before and there will be things after, perhaps different ones. "You are inside a chapter in the book of your life."
- He frames it as robustness "not in many things simultaneously, but in things serially."
- Rediscovering who you are is rarely easy, simple or fast. He hopes the successful sale "has literally bought you time."
