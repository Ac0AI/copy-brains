## Valuing a long-term investment: why expected value fails

Jason defines an "investment" as a big commitment of time, money and reputation over months or years, for a large payoff in the far future, under real uncertainty. You can only do 1-2 at once because you are "all in." This is different from short experiments like a poker bet, an A/B test or a brochure to test demand.

His framework for judging one has these parts:
- Mandate an outsized payoff.
- Value must accumulate over time.
- Success conditions must stay stable for ten years.
- Align with global trends.
- Have an edge.
- Match your long-term vision.

## Why "expected value" is the wrong tool

Jason says expected value (payoff times probability) is almost always the wrong tool for evaluating investments. His example:

- Investment A: spend $50,000 and a year. The payoff is $1,000,000 with 100% probability, like a signed contract with cash in escrow. Expected value is $1M, a "20x."
- Investment B: spend the same $50,000 and year. The payoff is $10M at 10% probability and $0 otherwise. Expected value is also $1M, also "20x."

The math calls them equivalent, but B almost certainly wastes a year and $50k, while A is a guaranteed life-changing sum. Unless you already have many millions, you would not pick B.

It is worse in practice because you rarely know either number. Even experts can't predict probability or outcome, so you end up multiplying garbage numbers together.

Expected value does work for a portfolio of bets: a VC with twenty companies, 100 newsletters and 1000 tweets, or 1000 poker hands. Winners and losers roughly balance. The bets must be uncorrelated ("as goes one, so very likely goes the other" breaks it). Single big commitments like your next job or the startup you spend your savings on are not like this.

## Mandate an outsized payoff, estimated to a power of ten

In the success case the payoff must be enormous, because the risk is unknown, both whether it pays and how much. If it can pay 10x or 100x, or be life-altering, then falling short (3x, or merely life-improving) is still worthwhile.

How to size it:
- Use Fermi estimation: estimate to the nearest power of ten. It is easy and needs little research, unlike detailed estimates that tend to be wrong.
- Example: a career path earning 50% more for ten years is about 10x more earning power over the career. A startup in a small, stagnant niche might be worth $1M, versus $10M for a small software company in a larger, growing space.
- Don't round up. Errors typically run toward the investment being less valuable. If your goal is to maximize earning power, "50% more" is not good enough; look for a route that potentially doubles your trajectory. Jason notes 2x for 10 years is 20x, but he rounds down to 10x since it may not be exactly 2x and may take years to ramp.
- Large personal investments are the "Rocks" in his Rocks, Pebbles, Sand prioritization framework.

## Value must accumulate, ideally with a feedback loop

For a large payoff, value has to be reliably addable and must not erode quickly. At minimum it accretes; ideally growth begets growth.

- Social media and SEO: more writing brings more interaction, links, attention and traffic, which makes more attention easier. Writing consistently also improves your writing.
- Recurring-revenue product companies: revenue grows as long as more customers start each month than stop, which holds if people truly want and love the product.
- Consulting, accounting and law firms often don't accumulate. Work for one client doesn't automatically apply to others. Jason says such firms are often valued around 1x revenue or less, while SaaS companies with similar growth rates are valued at 5x-10x revenue.

He warns the investment doesn't need to be exponential. People often think "viral products" are exponential when they are not, and exponential is probably a red herring for most ventures.

## Bet on what stays stable for ten years

If you spend 3-5 years investing and expect payoff in the following 5, the world must still value the thing in years 5-10.

Jason endorses Jeff Bezos's 2012 point that people ask what will change in ten years but rarely what will not, and that "you can build a business strategy around the things that are stable in time." Customers will always want low prices, fast delivery and vast selection, so effort there keeps paying off. "When you have something that you know is true, even over the long term, you can afford to put a lot of energy into it." Jason notes that ten years later this analysis proved correct.

## Align with a global trend

If a stable thing isn't available, pick something aligned with a trend that has enough momentum to not change soon. A dependable trend is either:
- (a) so large today it would take a decade to change significantly, or
- (b) growing so fast (at least 5x in the next 5 years) that it stays strong even if your growth estimate is badly wrong.

For startups, he favors a large, growing market:
- More niches where you might find traction, without triggering a large incumbent.
- More marketing channels you can use.
- People already spend money, so you answer "why buy from me" rather than "why allocate budget at all," "that's a problem but not a priority," or "I never heard of that problem."
- The pie grows even if your slice is small.

Trends he cites: distributed workforces, aging populations, physical delivery and online shopping, global demand for health care, education and energy, and AI unlocking new product capabilities.

## Have an edge, and a vision

An edge answers "why should you make this investment, not someone else?" A good investment you can't execute with excellence isn't good for you.

- Edge in investing often means evaluating risk better, but that is rare. Jason notes the majority of VC funds lose money and angels do worse.
- More likely your edge is in execution. You don't need to be unique.
- Two routes: be top 0.1% in the world at one thing (rare), or be top 25% at a few uncorrelated things, which together make a special edge. Math plus programming is correlated and not special; artistic plus great at programming is a much more elite group.
- Example: Scott Adams was a decent illustrator, a decent humorist and held tech jobs, and the intersection of those is what is unique.
- Passion is part of an edge, not enough. It gives motivation through hard times, but doesn't mean the risk is low, the market good, or the career lucrative.

He also says the payoff must match your long-term vision. If it won't make you proud, happy, fulfilled or rich (whatever your goals are), it's not a good investment. "If you run quickly in the wrong direction, that's not progress." He admits he often lacked a vision and wonders how much of his path was luck: "Best not to leave it to luck."

## Good investments are rare

Jason says few investments pass all these filters. Most startups fail, most VC portfolios lose money, and 80% of day-traders lose money. That is why adhering to the criteria matters: most investments aren't right for you, and you don't want to spend five years on the wrong one.

You'll never know if you made the best choice, even in hindsight, so the goal is an excellent choice: "To invest wisely, with the information you have."
