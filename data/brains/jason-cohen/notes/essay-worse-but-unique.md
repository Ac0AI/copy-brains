## Positioning against incumbents: pick a "worse but unique" strategy

Jason argues that an objectively "worse" strategy can win if it leverages something unique or unexpected. Startups can use this to beat incumbents.

- The chess analogy: the Queen's Gambit is popular and objectively good, but beginners are better off playing a refuted opening like the King's Gambit.
- At beginner level the King's Gambit isn't refuted, because neither player has memorized the branching sequences of moves and counter-moves, and nobody can find the best move in the moment.
- Games at that level are decided by "stupid blunders," not brilliant tactics.
- His conclusion: "Worse, but unique," is in fact "better."

## Why fighting on popular ground backfires

- Because the Queen's Gambit is popular, your opponent has likely studied it. They may know the defense better than you know the attack.
- As you progress, this gets more likely. The question becomes whether your preparation beats your opponent's preparation.
- Jason says that is not "the sort of 'luck' you want to depend on" and "not a good strategy."
- The fix: learn a refuted-but-not-completely-atrocious opening. Almost no one has prepared a defense, because "no one plays that." The opponent reacts for the first time, while you have memorized a few of the best moves. That gives a clear advantage even as a beginner.

## Applying it to startups and leverage

- He ties this to his definition of leverage: it produces a superior strategy for products and companies even if many things about your team, product and company are "worse" than the competition.
- A startup is a "beginner" in the market. Its "opponents" have varying preparation and skill: age, capabilities, customers, brand, funding.
- A startup needs to fight "on its own terms, not attack where the opponent is already strong."
- "Playing on your own terms changes the game."
- On a personal level, he applies the same idea: use your leverage to do and be something special, at the intersection of what is special, enjoyable, and what the world needs. He quotes Oscar Wilde: "Be yourself. Everyone else is taken."

## Accept that many customers won't want your game

- Many potential customers won't want to play your game. You'll feel bad losing them to a competitor, and they may say mean things about you, your product and your decisions, especially publicly on review sites and social media.
- Jason's take: "don't worry, you weren't going to win them anyway." They're just not into you.
- The customers who do want your game will see you're the best at it, even if you're two people facing a dozen competitors, from well-funded, media-covered startups to old-but-reliable incumbents.
- Pick a different game and you can be the best at it and become extremely successful.
- Example from his own company: WP Engine powers as many substantial websites as Google Cloud or Digital Ocean, yet has only 2.3% of that market. So 98% of potential customers are not customers, and WP Engine is still a unicorn.
- That is also why he says to seek large markets.

## Be extreme in your difference

Jason says that being extremely different beats merely being different. Examples he gives:

- Not just a minimal UI, but one so minimal it works on the command line.
- Not just great design, but design so remarkable people buy the product only for that, and it gets written up on designers' blogs.
- Not just a new algorithm for an old problem, but one that uncovers things no one else does, even at the expense of missing things others catch.

Tactics and reasoning:

- You can't be extreme on every aspect of the product and business. Even one extreme dimension is already powerful.
- Extremity is how you maximize the power of the few things that make you special.
- Competitors cannot play your game as well as you. They can't make every choice you made, so they are definitionally not the best at your game.

## Don't be deterred when others say your strategy is refuted

- Jason's closing advice: "Play your King's Gambit. Don't worry if others claim your strategy is refuted. It's not refuted for you."
- His final point: the fact that competitors think your approach is refuted is why you will win.
