## Change is usually the right call, but it's costly and needs kindness

Jason argues that nearly everyone wants things to improve, yet almost no one wants to change. Customers demand a better UX but complain when it changes. Team members want consistency but resist policies. Developers want efficiency but don't want to change how they work. Strategy fails if it's constantly in flux, but a strategy that ignores new information is wrong.

- "The right choice is almost always 'change.'" Change is a reaction to new facts, getting smarter, or a shift in the environment.
- He says death awaits any organization that picks the comfort of the familiar over the discomfort of change.
- His summary of top workplace complaints: (1) the way things are; (2) change.

## Change is exhausting, so leaders must watch for burnout

Even people who love change, who brag that "it's hard but every day is different," hit a breaking point after years of adaptation. Jason notes that constant learning is simply tiring.

- Even the most stoic, change-loving people sometimes need a break from change. His analogy: "it's a marathon" but sometimes you need to walk a mile to catch your breath.
- Leaders should look for signs of burnout or decision fatigue and address them proactively.
- This matters for everyone in a startup, not only managers, because constant change can feel like management has no plan and no strategy.
- It takes careful consideration to tell a rudderless company from a culture of self-reflection and improvement.

## Not all change is an improvement, and it's harder to verify than code

Sometimes an attempt to fix a problem makes it worse, such as when making code faster breaks it.

- Code has safety nets: slow code is objectively visible in a profiler, changes can be wrapped in unit tests and code reviews, you can iterate before shipping, and in the worst case revert to the previous version.
- Those safety nets don't exist when the change is happening to a whole team, a major product release, or a cross-departmental strategic initiative.
- Sometimes it's objectively impossible to know ahead of time whether a change is right, and you have no choice but to place a bet.

## Deciding what to change is hard, and success creates two opposite traps

Jason describes two ways successful companies stall out:

- **Forgetting why they won.** They lose sight of the fundamental reasons for their success: the key insights and UX of the product, or the key culture and values that attracted their first hundred or thousand employees.
- **Dogmatism.** They cling to their strategy or "non-consensus but correct" ideas, so when the world changes around them, or scale breaks their previously correct notions, they fail to adapt.

His conclusion: it is not generally true that "what got us here will get us there," which means deep change is required.

## The mindset for handling it: be kind

Jason's prescription is a single word: "Be kind."

- Don't judge too harshly when your organization tries to improve one thing and breaks another, or takes too long to implement a change.
- Don't judge too harshly when a teammate needs to work on something easy for a few sprints or take a vacation.
- His example: Edison tried thousands of materials before finding the one that made lightbulbs practical. Would you have judged him for "thrashing?" He says invention is often frustrating.

## When to judge harshly instead

Kindness has a limit. Jason says you should judge harshly if nobody is thinking about change at all.

- If nobody cares whether there's change or not, if there's no rhyme or reason to the company strategy, or if everyone is expected to act and feel happy and productive all the time, then you should definitely judge.
- "An organization that isn't striving to improve, will rot and disintegrate."
- His closing view: there are no straight paths in life or startups. All you can do is keep being introspective and keep attempting the right sort of change.
