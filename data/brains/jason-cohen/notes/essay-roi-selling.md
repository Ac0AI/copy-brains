## Why "ROI" fails as a selling argument

Jason sold Smart Bear's Code Reviewer, a tool that helps developers review each other's code, for seven years. He tried to sell it with a "saves you money" argument, and some customers demanded an ROI spreadsheet. He says the argument "never worked, not once," even though the reasoning was sound and customers asked for it.

## The ROI math he used, and why it was correct

- Premise: Code Reviewer cuts code review time roughly in half by eliminating busywork. It led the one-page leave-behind, because in 2004 sales calls were in person.
- Calculation:
  - Two developers each spend an average of 30 minutes on a review, once a day, which is 20 hours per month.
  - At a fully loaded developer cost of $150/hr, that is $3,000/mo.
  - Halving it saves $1,500 every month.
- Price: Code Reviewer cost $499 per developer, one time. That was the pre-SaaS "lifetime" licensing era, with maintenance and upgrades sold in later years at 20% of the original price. Payback came within the first month.
- Despite that, the deal did not close on the spreadsheet.

## Why savings are invisible to buyers

- Better, higher-volume code is valuable, but it is "essentially impossible to measure, and is in fact not measured." The savings are real but invisible to the customer.
- Budgets are siloed. The "Salaries" budget is separate from the "Tools" budget.
- In seven years of selling software, at deals from 10 seats to 2,500 seats, Jason never saw a company turn a saving in the Salaries budget into an increase in the Tools budget.
- Customers bought because they genuinely believed the tool made their team more productive, even though they could not quantify it. His conclusion: "Spreadsheets are worthless. They either believe, or they don't."

## What to do instead: create value so large it needs no calculation

- Make the value so obvious that nobody has to compute it. If payback is under a month, it is evident.
- Change a workflow so drastically for the better that people can't live without it, whether or not it saves money.
- Improve a customer's marketing campaigns so obviously and drastically that they don't need a spreadsheet to see the value.
- He treats "value" as broader than cost savings. His list: more leads, more sales, faster code, efficiency, time-savings, cost-savings, delight, corporate brand, personal image, societal impact.

## Price to ability and willingness to pay, not to "value"

- Set price according to the customer's ability and willingness to pay and the structure of your business model, rather than as a direct function of so-called value.
- A software development department has a tools budget, and different companies have different ways of arguing internally to expand it. Your price has to match those constraints.
- Example: a marketing department might be willing to pay for services but not for tools.
- Bottom line: a simple "cost savings" equation is rarely how you win sales or set prices.
