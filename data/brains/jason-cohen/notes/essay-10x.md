## The 10x rule of thumb

Jason argues that startup outcomes follow 10x patterns often enough that "10x" should be the default assumption until proven false. He admits people love stories and see patterns in noise, but claims this one holds up as "an excellent rule of thumb."

- Things are not always exactly 10x.
- Some 10x properties last for years but can evolve until there is no other 10x left.
- The practical advice is to assume a 10x truth exists and spend your time finding it, rather than fiddling with one hundred variables, almost none of which matter.

## 10x in products, people and decisions

- Winning products have at least one attribute that is 10x better than all competitors. It is not necessarily design, though it can be.
- Great people are more than 10x better than average.
- For multi-product companies, one product often earns more than all the others combined. At Smart Bear and 37signals, the largest product was 10x larger than all the others combined.
- One or two decisions or principles are 10x more important than all the others combined.
- Companies do not naturally 10x their size or quality. That takes intentional focus and investment.
- Scale is 10x harder than operating small, because complexity is non-linear.

## 10x in markets and venture outcomes

- SaaS returns in a market follow a power law, and the winner can take 10x the profits of the next. His examples: eBay ≥ 10x Etsy, Google ≥ 10x Bing, Facebook ≥ 10x Twitter, Salesforce ≥ 10x the rest of its market.
- VCs must invest in 10x winners, so they push every portfolio company toward that outcome, even at the cost of more risk and sub-optimal cash use.
- Business models change with pricing in roughly 10x increments.
- Warren Buffett earned $77.4 billion of his $77.7 billion net worth after his 50th birthday.

## 10x in marketing

- Your best marketing channel is often 10x as productive as the next best. This is also true within content marketing.
- One article or tweet often outperforms all the rest by 10x.
- The implication is to find and concentrate on the one channel or piece that works, rather than spreading effort evenly.

## 10x in customers

- About 1/10th of customers are likely gross-margin unprofitable. Jason says this does not mean you should "fire" them, but the calculation is worth understanding and measuring.
- Changing a policy or product to shrink that unprofitable set can be a quick, strategic way to generate profit.
- About 1/10th of customers generate 10x more support calls than the rest. Some of that is fine, but it can signal a problem with the product, with those customers, or with who you are selling to.
