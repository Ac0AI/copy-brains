## Strategy is required even when the prediction is wrong

Jason argues that experts fail badly at predicting the future, so "skate where the puck is going" is less reliable than it sounds. His evidence:
- Goldman Sachs analysts missed T-Bill rates, oil receipts and solar installations for about 25 years, often not even directionally right. Solar installation forecasts were about 3x too low for two decades.
- McKinsey studied 1,700+ new-drug sales forecasts across 260 drugs: almost two-thirds missed peak revenue by 40% or more. Follow-on drugs were no better.
- The base-rate method (start from what similar companies did) is also shaky. In 2015 the base rate said no company of Tesla's size had exceeded 40% CAGR over 10 years, and the average was under 3%. Tesla's actual CAGR since then was 55%.
- Chess and World Cup favorites lose to long shots (Giri at 10% or less won Tata Steel; Argentina won the 2022 World Cup).

His conclusion: you still need a strategy, because "a strategy gives you a direction. A direction creates something interesting." Even a wrong strategy attracts early fans and surfaces the real answer. Slack came from Game Neverending, then Glitch. Flickr (sold to Yahoo for $25M in 2005) was the byproduct of chat and image sharing.

He also says that "making a decision and moving forward is often more effective than extended deliberation," provided the company is willing to face the truth when the strategy proves wrong. He tells founders to "dispense with the idea that there is One True Solution."

## Follow customer behavior to upgrade the strategy

Jason sees no contradiction between holding a strategy and following customers. The initial strategy is known to be wrong, so customers are how it gets corrected.
- A feature request is not the new strategy; the intent behind it might be. "Push notifications for my status updates" in WhatsApp really meant "circumvent SMS." The winning strategy became "SMS, but free."
- The strongest signal is when customers abuse the product to do something else, because they want it enough to use the wrong tool.
- His own example: Smart Bear's first product, Code Historian, visualized a codebase's history and few paid for it. Users abused it for peer review, asking to "package up what's on my screen and send it to someone else" and "write directly on a line of code and send it back." That became Code Collaborator, which "within a few years represented 97% of the revenue of the company."
- Stewart Butterfield wrongly believed "yet another chat" wouldn't sell. Slack became one of the fastest companies ever from $1M to $100M ARR anyway.

He notes some strategies are right from the start (Google, LinkedIn, Amazon, Apple, WP Engine). For WP Engine he used a customer-development system to discover what mattered before building.

## Build optionality: have more than one way to win

Jason says a plan where many things must all go right ("and") is a bad bet, since it assumes you can predict many components. He lists the "ands" of a startup: an insight, a compelling product, reachable customers, cost-effective acquisition, a price customers accept, being better than competitors for a segment, keeping a key team member, and no global economic failure. A resilient plan is built from "or":
- Reach customers through social media or paid ads or influencers or channel sales. A product sellable several ways beats one sellable a single way.
- A very low-cost product has many pricing options, so it is more likely to find one that works.
- A product in a large, growing market has many niches, personas and channels to try.
- Some indie developers build several small products and pour effort into whichever takes off.

He cites Taleb: with optionality "you don't have much need for what is commonly called intelligence, knowledge, insight, skills... you don't have to be right that often."

## Leverage your assets and build a moat

- **Leverage:** Jason cites Sun Tzu ("strike where you are strong and the enemy is weak"). Founders often chase lucrative ideas outside their competence. At minimum, use your capabilities, knowledge, network, professional friends and accumulated assets. That may mean a very different company, product or market, which is the point: pick the battle you are best suited to win.
- **Moat:** A long-term, durable, structural advantage others cannot disrupt is resilient to market volatility. He points to a companion article on moats.

## Stay reactive, and hedge with "insurance"

**Intentionally reactive.** If you can't predict, don't spend time planning. React to what you see, using judgment and a long-term strategy. He is blunt that people say "iterate fast" but then take four months for one feature or 18 months to "launch." He wants constant delivery, feedback and learning loops, quick updating of prior decisions, no ego about who was right, and treating all doors as two-way doors, encoded into the organization's DNA. Frameworks he names: building in public, the OODA loop, iterative hypothesis testing, Continuous Discovery and Continuous Delivery.

**Hedged bets** work like life insurance: you come out ahead either way but pay a tax and cap your upside. Examples he gives:
- Multiple vendors for the same service, shifting workloads on price and performance (it also strengthens price negotiations). Wrap third-party APIs in a proxy layer, use multiple clouds and banks.
- Multiple brands, created or acquired, each finding a different niche.
- Multiple simultaneous solutions: Apple had the iPod team grow its OS while the Mac team shrank theirs to fit mobile, which caused in-fighting but picked the right path for the iPhone.
- Redundant systems ("Two is one, one is none").
- Disrupt thyself: Bezos told the new Kindle leader, who ran the book-selling business, "Effective tomorrow, your job is to kill your old business with a Kindle."

## Reduce exposure to the unpredictable: novelty, coalitions, simplicity, constants

- **Extreme novelty:** Zappos, Airbnb, Uber, SpaceX, Tesla, Bitcoin and OpenAI created categories others thought impossible. They avoided competitive unpredictability, since "it doesn't matter what's happening at Amazon if you can return shoes for free even after 364 days." The upside must be immense for the risk to be rational.
- **Coalitions:** Massive objects resist being moved. Examples: open source projects, industry standards (HTTP, HTML, SSL) that let members build for decades without hedging, and peace treaties. He adds that price collusion also works but is unethical and illegal.
- **Stay simple:** More tasks, dependency arrows and intricate analysis make estimates less believable. A simple product with a simple value proposition in a simple market has fewer dependencies to predict.
- **Bet on what will not change:** Bezos cites low prices and fast delivery. For WhatsApp, people want to chat and not pay. For WP Engine (founded 16 years ago), people want sites that are fast, stay fast under traffic, and are secure.

## Map futures, but don't stall

Jason recommends mapping possible futures (a "futures cone," extended with "unknown unknowns") rather than predicting one. This does not let you predict, but it helps you mitigate plausible problems and notice sooner when an "improbable" thing is happening. He recommends the pre-mortem: "It's 12 months from now, and the project is a disaster. What went wrong?" The goal is to pick a few things to mitigate intentionally and leave the door open for the rest.

His closing lessons:
- Have a strategy and decide quickly.
- Follow customer behavior, treating product "abuse" as a strong signal.
- Include a moat or two and create optionality, so single failures aren't fatal.
- Leverage existing strengths.
- Either keep it simple, form coalitions, or go completely novel.
- Bet on what won't change.
- Do your homework, but avoid analysis paralysis.

He ends with the 2024 chess World Championship: Gukesh was the overwhelming favorite, Ding won game one, pundits flipped to 50-50, and a pundit said "there's a 1% chance Ding doesn't draw" a dead-drawn final game. Five minutes later Ding blundered and Gukesh won.
