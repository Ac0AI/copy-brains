## Why every SaaS hits a growth ceiling: cancellation always beats paid marketing

- Jason argues that cancellation beats marketing because the two scale differently. Paid marketing (AdWords, Facebook ads) adds a fixed number of customers per month regardless of how many customers you have. Cancellation is a percentage of the base, so it grows as you grow.
- Worked example: 1,000 customers, marketing adds 100 a month, 5% monthly cancellation. At 1,000 customers, 50 leave. At 3,000 customers, 150 leave, so the company shrinks even though nothing else changed.
- Many founders think 5% monthly cancellation is good. Jason says "You're wrong." At that rate, growth stalls once cancellations equal new customers.
- He uses real Kit (formerly ConvertKit) data: new customer adds were holding up, but at a 5% cancel rate the math shows top-line growth plateauing around $3 million MRR at roughly $150K of new MRR per month.
- Buffer shows the same plateau. Its cancellation rate was about 5% and essentially never changed, and its growth flattened. Kit kept growing because it continually pushed its cancellation rate down.

## The "elephant curve" and the growth ceiling formula

- Jason calls the typical shape of a marketing channel the "elephant curve". It rises quickly once you figure the channel out, then flattens and often sags.
- Reasons channels sag:
  - The people who will see the ad have already seen it.
  - Competitors bid up ad costs until it is no longer cost-effective.
  - The audience moves to other platforms.
- Stacking several channels does not escape the curve, because each channel follows the same shape and channels run out. He cites a content affiliate campaign at WP Engine from 10 years earlier and his own content marketing as real examples.
- He says to search "elephant curve" for about 20 real-world examples and a longer analysis.
- Growth ceiling (maximum MRR) formula: new MRR per month divided by monthly cancellation rate. That is the most you will reach if you change nothing.
- Examples: $150K of new MRR a month at 5% cancellation gives $3 million.
- He recommends computing this number and tracking it every month. "We'll never get more than 2,500 customers unless we do something differently" is more motivating than a vague goal like moving from 5% to 4.5% cancellation. It "lights a fire under your ass".
- Cancellation is also personal to him. A customer has gone through a gauntlet to sign up: roughly 1% get through the website and about another 1% pay, so around 1 in 10,000. If they still cancel, "I failed them."

## How to find out why customers cancel

- Cancellation surveys with a "Why did you cancel?" form fill in randomly. Jason says he has tested this, because leavers are not spending time on you. "Too expensive" is a common answer and is picked at random.
- "Too expensive" is never the real reason, because the customer saw the price and bought anyway. The real reason is something specific: a feature that did not do what they thought, a missing integration.
- Do not lower prices because "too expensive" shows up in 30% of responses. Lowering the price would not have saved that customer. Fix the thing they actually wanted.
- Groove's email example, A/B tested until it worked:
  - Informal, not capitalized, short, from a named person (Adam), no images and no template.
  - Usable response rate of 10%, meaning feedback they could use to improve the product.
  - Changing "Why did you cancel?" to "What made you cancel?" doubled the usable response rate.
  - Jason's theory: "Why did you cancel?" puts the problem on the customer, while "What made you cancel?" admits you failed them, which makes it easier to answer. He says to test it yourself.
- Catch people before they leave, because once they cancel they do not want to talk to you.
  - Compare behavior a week, a month and 3 months before cancellation against customers who stayed.
  - What matters is behavior that differs between cancelers and stayers. Many customers contact support whether or not they are in trouble, so support contact alone may not be an early signal.
  - Reach out to at-risk customers while they are stuck. You may save some, and even if not, you learn why.

## Fix onboarding first

- If you do not know where to focus on cancellation, Jason says onboarding almost always has the most impact.
- He shows a retention curve from his own video (the "profit whale curve") where about half the viewers leave in the first 30 seconds. Cohorts in a SaaS company often look the same, with many customers leaving in the first 3 months, week or day.
- Improving the early drop-off can double the number who stay to the end. Customers who survive the start and stay long create a long lifetime value, so a few more long-term customers has a large financial effect.
- Trying to save customers late in the curve makes little difference.

## Ways to keep up with cancellations: proportional growth and upgrades

- Jason separates two kinds of marketing:
  - Paid channels: fixed output regardless of customer count, so cancellation overruns them.
  - Marketing proportional to your size: reviews, referrals, referral programs. With 10 times the customers you can get about 10 times the reviews or referrals, so this can keep pace with cancellations.
- Early on, with few customers, proportional channels do nothing because no one is there to promote you. Start with the paid or direct channels, then add proportional channels once you have customers and product-market fit.
- Upgrades (expansion revenue) can also offset cancellations, but with caveats:
  - They work when the cohort is still large and the cancel rate is low.
  - If the cancel rate is high, too few customers remain to upgrade enough.
  - "Flogging the people that remain" is not the solution when people are leaving in droves. Fix why they leave first.

## Pricing: you guessed low, so raise prices

- Jason borrows the phrase from Pat Walls that you guessed too low. His line: "There's no way your prices are optimal. Full stop," and they are probably too low.
- Standard economics (the law of demand) says higher prices mean fewer sign-ups. Too cheap means lots of customers but little money, with costs per customer (marketing, support) still there. Too expensive means barely any customers. You want the middle where revenue (the rectangle) is largest. Founders fear they are in the middle and will fall off to the right, but they are probably on the left.
- In practice, sign-ups often increase when bootstrapped companies raise prices. His explanation, using Tibo Maker's account of Tweet Hunter (launched at $9 a month, bootstrapped, sold for $8 million):
  - Tweet Hunter raised prices repeatedly and expected people to leave, but "revenue exploded each time."
  - $9 a month attracts casual users and hobbyists who quit and optimize for cost. $50 a month attracts business owners who need results and do not quibble over $20 to $50.
  - Businesses may also assume a $9 product "can't be good" and will not consider it.
- Jason's view: raising prices does not just move you along one demand curve. It changes the market you are in, to a healthier one. Hobbyists really will leave, but they are poor customers with no money and little commitment.
- He cautions that you do not want to price into the enterprise zone by accident. Pricing selects the market.

## Strategy and the ideal customer profile: Craigslist and Buffer

- Craigslist is, in his words, "hideously minimal" and has barely changed in 25 years. Niche competitors (he shows several, each worth billions) beat it in categories. Yet in 2024 Craigslist made about half a billion dollars in revenue with 50 people, roughly five times more profitable per person than Google or Facebook. He says only OnlyFans is more profitable per employee that he knows of.
- His explanation: its weaknesses enable strengths that competitors will not copy.
  - Looks like newspaper classifieds, so non-technical users can use it.
  - Never changing is a plus for people who hate tech and change.
  - Huge selection, and very low cost: simple CRUD, cheap servers, few engineers.
  - Works on old phones and slow connections.
- He calls these strategic choices: a choice that closes a door but enables something others cannot compete on, because competitors will copy your strengths but not your weaknesses. For the right person, your weaknesses are not even weaknesses.
- Definition of the ideal customer profile (ICP): "the person that loves you for you, loves all of you." They see your weaknesses as irrelevant or as strengths. Build everything for them: product features, copy, positioning, advertising, community, events. Most customers will be in the wider target market who accept the trade-offs, and you win them by being opinionated and special for the ICP.
- Strategy, as he puts it: self-reinforcing choices that include weaknesses, producing something special (not necessarily unique) that an ICP loves.
- Buffer example, which contradicts his own pricing advice:
  - CEO Joel Gascoigne wrote an open strategy letter saying they had forgotten who their best customer was and tried to be everything to everybody.
  - The best customer is the prosumer, the roughly 100 million people not very good at social media who want to get on board.
  - Buffer refocused on them and lowered prices, with lower introductory prices and a free trial, because that is what that customer needs.
  - Jason's takeaway: knowing and staying true to your ICP wins over the generic "raise prices" advice. Raising prices is more likely right if you know nothing else about the business, but ICP knowledge overrides it.
- Niching down is one way to focus on the ICP, but not the only way and not the primary idea. Craigslist did not niche down and Buffer did not either.

## What "grow or die" means

- Jason agrees "if you're not growing, you're dying" is true in a broader sense. People at shrinking companies report poor morale, trouble keeping talent and unhappy founders.
- He suggests defining growth as "constructive evolution" rather than only number go up. Example: Sarah Savage plans never to exceed 1,000 customers to keep a special relationship with each, but is constantly innovating on infrastructure and automation to stay lean.
- Plain growth can mean profit, not only revenue. If you the human are not growing or evolving, feeling stuck or burnt out, marketing tactics will not fix that.
- He mentions his book "Hidden Multipliers" (pre-order at hiddenmultipliers.com), about ways to jump-start growth with an existing budget and team.
