## Blundering decides most amateur contests, including chess

- Jason's premise: planning is usually taught as vision, goals, actions, measurement. He argues success is equally about what not to do, and that roughly 80% of winning comes from not blundering.
- He tested chess coach Ben Finegold's claim that amateur games are decided by blunders, not skill gaps, on his own games. He is a mediocre player and checked his last twenty decisive games against opponents of similar strength.
- Results:
  - He won exactly 50%, confirming the opponents were evenly matched.
  - The higher-Elo player won only 55% of the time (average gap about 74 points out of roughly 1100), barely better than a coin flip.
  - The player with more blunders lost 86% of the time.
  - In 40% of games both players had equal blunders, so he built an error score: mistakes + 2 x blunders. The player with the higher error score lost 81% of the time.
- His conclusion: "To win at chess, blunder less."
- The same pattern holds in amateur tennis, where about 80% of points are lost by errors rather than won by winners. The strategy that follows is to be conservative and let the other side blunder.
- Caveat: at professional level it reverses. Pros rarely blunder, so the other skills decide results. Jason applies the "avoid blundering" rule to beginners and amateurs, and treats most startups as amateur play.

## Look for root causes, not proximate ones

- Jason says "ran out of money" or "founder stopped" are like "the heart stopped" as a cause of death. They are the last thing that happened, not the underlying disease.
- He also warns against digging too deep with "5 Whys" until the cause is irrelevant. A useful blunder is neither merely proximate nor so distant it no longer matters.
- Proximate causes he dismisses as non-reasons:
  - Ran out of cash. It is a consequence unless you overspent with no plausible way to recover through revenue.
  - A pivot gone bad. You pivoted because it was already failing.
  - Founder stopped. "As opposed to what?"
  - Didn't find product-market fit. It restates "it didn't work."
- Bad luck he separates out, because there may be nothing to learn: a sudden economic shock (COVID closing a movie theater), a surprise co-founder breakup, and a "known brittle point" (a single point of failure every new business has, which sometimes gets triggered).

## Commonly blamed causes that he says are not blunders

- **A bad idea.** Great ideas often start out looking like bad ideas. The failure is in finding the market and executing, iterating the idea into a working business.
- **Can't build the product.** Founders usually can build. Building while ignoring everything else is the typical problem. AI is the exception, since most AI products don't work well enough to succeed.
- **Number of founders.** Successes and failures exist at every count. He personally treats 3 founders as a warning flag and 4 or more as a bright red flag, since some usually bleed off. More important is that the founder count fits the founders' personalities and style.
- **Location.** It matters for startups aiming to scale to unicorn size, which must hire many experienced people quickly and raise money. It matters less every year and is irrelevant if the startup doesn't have that goal.

## Blunders about the customer and market

- **Didn't talk to customers, or didn't listen.** Not talking to them before and after starting is a blunder. Talking to them and being dishonest about what they said is faulty analysis. He points to the Iterative Hypothesis Method or another reputable customer-development framework.
- **No market need or a bad market.** The checklist for a good market is well known, so there is no excuse for finding out only after six months of coding. You could have checked whether the market is small or shrinking, whether enough people agree they have the problem and that it is worth money, whether they have budget and accept the price, and whether they are happy with what they have.
- **No target audience or clear value proposition.** "Speaking to everyone means speaking to no one." It is laziness or fear of deciding who the product is for. The choice sets the language on your home page, ads, sales calls and product. You must sell to your ideal customer persona ("Carol") and know exactly who she is.
- **No differentiation.** It is obvious from day one if you can't say why you or the product are special, why someone should buy from you, or why you will win where others failed. He lists many kinds of leverage you could have used and says failing founders chose none.
- **Refusing to seek or see the truth.** Typical forms:
  - Customers love the competitor you call "dumb."
  - The thing you call "broken" isn't broken.
  - The pain you insist customers have, they don't have.
  - He asks: "Did you even ask?" and "were you listening?"
  - If you haven't made major changes to strategy, product and positioning, you're blundering.

## Blunders of timing, scaling and focus

- **Too many things had to go right.** Every startup has gaps. If skills, objections, market health, distribution channels and niches are all uncertain at once, the chance that everything breaks your way is near zero, and you knew that at the start. Your path should consist of "or's," not "and's," so you can satisfy the Startup Drake Equation.
- **Launching too early or too late.**
  - Launching a crappy MVP instead of a simple but lovable v1 is a blunder.
  - So is building for six months or two years with no customers, because builders love only building.
  - He adds: don't "launch" at all. No one cares except friends, and you must win customers reliably for the next three years anyway, so work on that.
- **Premature scaling.** Before product-market fit the job is discovering what works:
  - what customers will pay for and keep paying for a year later;
  - who the target customer is and what they want;
  - how to reach and close them;
  - what to charge.
  Scaling is doubling down on what works. Doing it earlier wastes time and money until you run out of both. He calls it a blunder "possibly of ignorance, but typically of putting ego before the truth."
- **Going enterprise before about $20M ARR.** This doesn't apply if you were built for enterprise from day one (sales, onboarding, legal, security). The common mistake is seeing "Salesforce bought some seats!" and deciding to go upmarket. Individual teams inside big companies buying your tool is good, but it is different from the multi-million-dollar top-down sales that targeting "the enterprise" requires. Take the team orders and stick with the leverage you have.
- **Expanding the target market before winning it.** Example: a founder at $2,700 MRR announcing a new segment. He says winning another customer in your existing market is ten times easier than expanding. Expansion dilutes attention, marketing dollars, positioning, messaging and product sharpness. "There is a time for expansion: Later."

## Blunders of business model, effort and people

- **Unworkable business model.** Unit economics can be measured early: you roughly know production costs and that marketing and sales cost money and time. Mismatching price to business model by an order of magnitude is preventable. "We'll figure that out later" admits you're blundering and hoping your future self stops.
- **Writing code instead of winning customers.**
  - A builder says "I need more features" or "too many bugs."
  - Both are wrong. There are customers who need only the current features, but you haven't won their attention with marketing, their interest with the website, or their business with sales.
  - Potential customers don't know about a bug yet.
  - Coding is comfortable and everything else is hard, unclear and costs money.
  - His fix: put down the compiler and talk to customers.
- **Founder or investor breakups.** This can be bad luck, but you picked your co-founders. Either you knew them for a while (poor selection) or you didn't (rash decision). A common amplifier is a departing founder holding a huge amount of stock because no vesting schedule applied to all founders. That is a blunder, and the team did it to itself.
- **Lack of passion or endurance.** This only counts as forgivable self-discovery if on day one you honestly:
  - took stock of your passions and strengths;
  - had a mission you cared about beyond "make money" or "be able to say you're a founder";
  - read about the emotional whiplash startups entail;
  - weighed the trade-offs against other important things in your life.
  Otherwise it is a blunder. If it really was self-discovery, forgive yourself.

## Applying it

- Jason's point is that most of these blunders are visible in advance and avoidable, and he links a specific method for each. He points to his Roadmap for Product/Market Fit as a lower-risk way to build startups.
- He does not claim it is fail-safe: "Even in chess. The rest of the game does matter."
- His summary: when so much risk is removed by avoiding avoidable things, with frameworks that spell out what "not blundering" looks like, why wouldn't you? "80% winning is not blundering."
