## Startups consume all available time, so health and family get sacrificed

- Jason's thesis: "Maximizing your chance for success means sacrificing health and family." He says this applies to small, bootstrapped companies, and he frames it as a trade-off to accept knowingly, not as a lifestyle to recommend.
- He admits that more hours do not automatically mean more output. Studies show an under-slept, foggy brain is not offset by extra hours, and optimizing how you spend time can multiply productivity several times over, which hours alone cannot match.
- He still holds that the path to success in a tiny company runs through sacrifice.
- He cites examples of founders who paid physically and personally: Jeremiah Owyang (out of shape, stressed, bad sleep, high blood pressure), Mark Cuban (slept on a couch or floor, seven years without a vacation, "I was focused") and Penelope Trunk (always sick, "There's no time for family").
- He dismisses the objection "many workaholics succeed, but that's not the only path" by pointing out the structural problem: the work never runs out.

## The "Rule of Time in Startups": a bootstrapped company takes all of it

- Jason compares it to the "Rule of Closets": the stuff you own expands to fill all available closet space, and adding shelves just reveals you have more stuff.
- His version: "How much time does a bootstrapped company take? All of it."
- Even ten people could hardly keep up with everything in small business. His list: creating, consulting, designing, fixing, self-promotion, blogging, networking, bookkeeping, taxes, customer support and cultivation, and reading startup blogs for ideas.
- He adds the hidden time sinks, such as losing an afternoon troubleshooting an outsourced IP phone system that was supposed to let you "work from anywhere."
- With one, two or three people, "of course it takes all your time." The same holds for someone running a startup on the side of a day job, who has no time to exercise or play with kids before bed.

## Obsession, not passion, is what makes a small company go

- He says: "It takes obsession to make a little company go. Forget 'passion'... it's 'obsession.'"
- The distinction: passion means you love working; obsession means you cannot stop working.
- You put your entire self on the line: finances, career and ideas.
- The obsession persists away from the office, at lunch with a friend or reading to your kids. His wife pointed out in the early years of Smart Bear that his "mental and emotional bandwidth" was entirely consumed. "You're physically there, but you're not really there."
- He calls the pattern "self-destructive devotion" and says you don't go through "this meat grinder" because you merely "like something a lot." His line: "If you love it so much, why don't you marry it?" "Exactly."

## Accept the penalty deliberately, and time it to your life stage

- Jason concedes that the work-life-balance advice from life coaches is correct as a way to live your whole life. His point is that you must decide whether it is OK to incur the penalty right now.
- His own timing: he did it in his 20s with no kids, enough savings to risk everything for a while, and a wife who ran her own business and so understood the workload and why he spaced out at dinner.
- Conditions that made it workable for him: no dependents, financial runway, and a spouse who understood.
- His bottom line: "Every successful bootstrapper I know puts work before self." He adds the qualifier "until financial freedom is achieved," meaning the self-sacrifice phase ends once financial freedom is reached.
- He did the same himself.

## Context from the editor's note (written 15+ years later)

- The essay dates from 2009, before the "Hustle Culture" label and before Lean Startup, and was somewhat controversial even then.
- Jason says he stands by the original claim: startups take all the time and take sacrifice.
- He rejects the way people interpret those ideas today, and does not endorse the modern "toxic hustle culture" reading.
