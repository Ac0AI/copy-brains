## "Extreme" brainstorming questions as a way out of tiny thinking

- Jason argues that brainstorming fails because people are blinded by daily work ("no forest, all trees"). They latch onto ideas that have been knocking around for years, and customer interviews, support tickets and sales calls produce tactical ideas that "cement our tiny thinking."
- His fix is a set of prompts that stretch one dimension of reality to an extreme, so extreme it is nearly nonsense. An idea that is only a 60% solution in the extreme hypothetical can be a 2x or 10x idea in reality.
- Unique business models emerge when at least one dimension is so extreme that critics and competitors can't conceive of it. A right-extreme idea can be a company's whole strategy and a long-term moat.
- He lists examples once considered impossible that won anyway:
  - Zappos: free shoe returns 364 days after purchase.
  - Robinhood: $0 trading fees.
  - Amazon: free delivery with Prime.
  - Netflix: mail-order DVD rental.
  - Open-source companies charging for something 100% free with 0% secret IP.
  - AirBnB, Uber and Lyft: strangers trusting strangers in homes and cars.

## Pricing and business-model extremes: 10x prices, flipped model, cost is no object

**10x prices**
- Question: if forced to raise prices 10x, what would you have to do to justify it? Think brand look and feel, positioning, website and product design.
- Think about which subset of the market you would target. Would the high price be a positive, because it signals success or because "buying from the best" lowers perceived risk? What badge could they display?
- Ask which expensive services you would supply (human support, infrastructure) and whether they could cost "only" 2x or 3x more, making the net effect hugely profitable.
- Takeaway: early startups often charge too little, and established companies struggle to raise prices on existing customers. Working out what could justify a massive hike may let you do some of it and justify at least 2x.

**Flipped business model**
- Question: what if you had to charge in a completely different way? Usage-based becomes a flat monthly rate; monthly tiers become daily metering with a formula.
- Ask how positioning, customer mix, brand, messaging, sales and marketing would change, and how costs and architecture would have to change so most customers stay profitable.
- Ask which features you would need (e.g. so customers can control their costs) or could now supply because customers see the utility and will pay more.
- Takeaway: business models force frames for how you monetize value. The goal is to deliver far more value than you charge for. Shaking up the model shakes up the value/cost equation, and sometimes a different model is better for everyone.

**Cost is no object**
- Question: what if it didn't matter how unprofitable you were? Spend 10x or 100x more, or make the product free. Think of it as philanthropy.
- Consider the personal touch: what would a dedicated concierge do? If software could do 30% of that job at 1/100th of the cost, that could be a good feature.
- Takeaway: converting money into real customer value is surprisingly hard even when money is free. If you find a way, it may become a new pricing tier with a sustainable model added back.

## Differentiation extremes: no customers, complete rip-off, no website

**No customers**
- Question: if all customers vanished and you had to earn growth and brand from scratch, what would you do? Consider the home page, pricing model, brand, and positioning as a fresh competitor.
- Would you drop features that take lots of work but few people use? Build something new? Change infrastructure, architecture or UX given what you now know?
- Takeaway: companies skip important changes out of marketing complacency, lack of a compelling positioning statement, or fear of upsetting customers. "We don't want to make even 5% of our customers mad, even if it would be better for the other 95%." There will be 10x more future customers than current ones, but only if you build for them today.

**Complete rip-off**
- Question: if your biggest competitor copied every feature, how would you still win?
- Possible answers inside the product: ease of use, ease of sharing, pleasure of great design, or shipping the next unique feature so fast no copycat catches up.
- Possible answers outside the product: a higher purpose or brand promise, "rooting for David against Goliath," a special culture, a distinctive brand ("it's the best" or "it's the biggest"), trust from customer service or how you handle failures, or giving back through open source, community or philanthropy.
- Takeaway: "All your good ideas will be copied; it's just a question of when." Competing only on features means bullet-point battles, the weakest way to win sales. Bigger, more emotional distinction wins, and it breaks the habit of believing incremental updates will dramatically increase differentiation or growth.

**No website**
- Question: if you couldn't have a website, how would you grow? Options: word of mouth (does the product help people do it proactively?), channel partners or ambassadors, social media good enough that people sign up without more info, or making the product itself the marketing.
- A "website" inside the product could let prospects learn and try things so they become users before they realize it.
- Takeaway: advocates already use your product, yet "getting more customers" is left to marketing. Viral products know this (you can't use a chat service without inviting others), but non-viral products can build growth machines in-product too. "What if 'in-product marketing' is more impactful on growth than any useful feature?"

## Self-service and customer learning: no tech support, no customer contact

**No tech support**
- Question: if you could never provide support in any form, what would have to change?
- Onboarding must improve to the point customers self-serve happily. Jason calls this possibly the hardest step, because the customer knows the product least and is least motivated to push through barriers. Setup is often done once, so you rarely learn how to improve it from existing customers.
- The product would need more user control, better visibility into information (they can't ask support to look it up), and more intuitive actions (they can't ask how to use functionality they know "is in here somewhere").
- Takeaway: self-service lowers cost and scales, and also makes customers happier and more loyal (he cites *The Effortless Experience*). Improving it is often profitable for both top and bottom line.

**No customer contact**
- Question: if you could never talk to customers again, how would you decide what to build?
- Measure behavior well enough to tell quickly whether any change is positive or negative, and find clear signs of value or happiness to use like conversion in A/B testing.
- Test ideas faster: ship a feature as a button that says "coming soon," asks "why did you want this" or "what did you expect to happen," or offers "get emailed when we make this feature," to measure likely use before building.
- Analyze what customers, competitors and observers say online, remembering the loudest voices aren't a random sample but may still be useful.
- Takeaway: interviews sometimes yield wrong feature ideas. People say they'd use something, mean it, and then don't use it after five months of building. Empirical signal avoids that and may surface breakthroughs.

## Speed and focus extremes: no time, only one thing this year

**No time**
- Question: what if you had to ship a complete MVP (or actually an SLC, simple/lovable/complete) feature in two weeks that would delight some fraction of customers? He quotes Leonard Bernstein: "To achieve great things, two things are needed: a plan, and not quite enough time."
- Strip all complexity and reduce scope to a minimum. That reduces who can use it on day one but increases what you learn on day one.
- Tactics: stub out an API and implement it manually for now; hook up a 3rd-party system and build something more efficient later; skip documentation, internal support training and perfect error handling just long enough to see how people like it.
- Scaling down: a 1000-page book becomes 40 pages of Cliff's Notes, a 1-page blog post, then one sentence with a main theme and one unique perspective. "It can always be smaller" if you trade off detail, precision or completeness.
- Takeaway: hackathons show teams can build cool things fast with trade-offs. If you've lapsed into waterfall with two-week report-outs, this might be the needed shake-up.

**Only one thing this year**
- Question: if you could ship only one thing this year, what single initiative makes the most difference? There's time for release, fixes and enhancements, but only one topic.
- Ask what generates the most revenue, and what is so differentiated and desirable that customers buy and stay even with no other product changes, and even with missing features or ongoing bugs.
- Takeaway: a business's trajectory often comes down to 1-2 big decisions per year (a critical launch, entering or exiting a market, a key hire, a competitive insight). Thinking this way forces only the most impactful ideas, which are probably the ones to focus on regardless.
- He closes with Francis Crick: "The dangerous man is the one who has only one idea, because then he'll fight and die for it. The way real science goes is that you come up with lots of ideas, and most of them will be wrong."

## Maximize fun

- Question: what would be the most fun thing to build? It must make the product better, not be a random technology or unrelated market.
- It could be a feature you think would be cool for customers, a technology that's fun and happens to deliver a feature customers want, or a refactor using interesting modern technology that also improves cost-efficiency, scalability, testability or maintainability.
- He suggests holding a one-week hackathon and actually building one of those things.
- Takeaway: people work better and harder on fun things and are happy doing it, so biasing toward fun biases toward productivity and long-term fulfillment. It is "practical and logical, not merely indulgent." Work must still advance the strategy and improve something substantial for the customer or team, but "typical prioritization under-appreciates the power of fun."

## Organizational extremes: no meetings, sociopathic CEO, mortal wound, philanthropist

**No meetings**
- Question: what if there were no synchronous meetings ever? Consider onboarding, operating and deploying the software, and finding where things are located. What systems and materials would need to exist, in a format that is easy to update?
- Consider making decisions without meetings. Brainstorms may even improve, since people aren't influenced by others before forming their own thoughts. Decisions may strengthen, since people have time to think, research and try things.
- Also ask how you would build social ties, inside jokes and 1:1 and group relationships if never in the same place at the same time.
- Takeaway: meetings remain best for social bonding, deep discussion where ideas ricochet, and decisions where you need to "look in everyone's eyes." But "too many meetings" is a top complaint. A bigger question than meeting hygiene is whether systems and processes can prevent them and add utility, e.g. a new hire reading "the why" behind the last 20 major decisions gets up to speed faster.

**Sociopathic CEO**
- Question: what if you could change anything regardless of what anyone thinks or feels? Examples: terminating a product line, or pivoting the entire company even if it breaks the organization, erodes trust, causes departures and angers investors.
- Explore whether the pivot could be done by reorganization rather than layoffs, and whether anything is worth such a move.
- Takeaway: you never want to contemplate catastrophic actions, for good reason, but that aversion prevents certain thoughts. You should at least explore them. "The very best ideas should not be avoided only because they are hard."

**Mortal wound**
- Question: what externality could kill the company? Examples: a competitor releasing a product at a price that cuts new sales to a trickle and loses half your customers in a year; a security breach big enough to drive most customers away; an economic shift where nobody buys your software.
- Then ask what you could do to mitigate it, or whether a pivot removes the threat.
- Takeaway: Jason ran this at WP Engine and the team came up with 35 existential threats. It isn't wise to negate all of them, but sometimes you find an idea worth doing anyway that also shores up defenses, or one threat is likely enough to act on ahead of time.

**Philanthropist**
- Question: what if the only goal were to create the most good in the world, personally for your customers? Ignore how to make money; assume it works itself out. Don't argue about which world-wide causes matter most.
- Ask how to make their lives better because the product is doing something important, not because it has more utility, and how to help them reach their ultimate goals rather than "use features." Examples: help small businesses grow, writers improve their craft, performers get more attention, enormous companies use scale and wealth for good, customers from all walks of life gain access and equity.
- Takeaway: a mission bigger than yourself, more than metrics, creates more and more loyal customers and fulfilled employees. He cites Gen Z surveys saying they care about a company doing good as much as compensation. So there are "self-interested capitalist" reasons to create more good, "not just more MRR."
