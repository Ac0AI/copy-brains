## Money accelerates execution, not exploration

Jason separates what money can fix from what it can't, and argues the second list holds the most valuable things. "What can't be solved with money, are the most valuable things." Concepts, behaviors, knowledge and process can't be fabricated with money, and probably can't even be accelerated. Once a founder knows what to do, or knows how to learn quickly, money becomes an accelerant.

His shortest version, using explore vs execute: execution can be solved with money; exploration cannot.

Why he cares even if you aren't raising: if you're good at the things money can't buy, you stay competitive against a well-funded competitor. And if you have no money to waste, you need those skills even more.

## Team: money helps only after you find the person

- Finding good people is "almost impossible." Jason has a blog, a Twitter following, local attention and social favors to call in, and it's still nearly impossible.
- Great people always have options: an existing job, other excellent offers, the freedom to not work for a while.
- If someone handed him a million dollars it wouldn't help him find an awesome person; they're scarce.
- Once he finds someone, money helps a lot: large salary requirements, relocation, a signing bonus, a leased car, whatever makes money "not the issue."
- Raising rule: an investor knows $200,000 won't assemble a team you don't have. If the team exists but works only in spare time because day jobs pay the mortgages, that's a good reason to raise. Don't raise money if you haven't assembled the core team.

## Marketing: money buys volume, not authority

Most marketing can be solved with money:
- Spray ads everywhere.
- Try 20 campaigns even if most utterly fail.
- Try 50 titles, descriptions and landing pages through AdWords until one converts decently.
- Fund a freemium offering.

The big exception is anything that needs authority, like blogging and social media. You can't buy the kind of attention where people listen because they want to, interact because they respect your opinion and enjoy your style. If your business depends on "tell-your-friends" social-media punditry, you have to do that without money.

If marketing is just pouring more dollars in the top to crank out more revenue at the bottom, Jason calls that a machine investors like to invest in.

## Paired examples: what money buys vs. what it doesn't

- Writing more code, faster: yes. Knowing what to write: no.
- Acquiring more leads: yes. Knowing how to convert them: no.
- A prettier website: yes. What text to put on it: no.
- Leeway to make mistakes: yes. A philosophy of learning from mistakes to improve: no.
- A QA team to improve quality: yes. Knowing when a bug isn't important enough to block shipping: no.
- Time to outlive a recession: yes. Making something people want to buy even during a recession: no.

## How to use this when raising money

1. Emphasize how spending money will improve specific things that are broken or missing today only because money is lacking.
2. Show proof you've mastered what money can't buy: your ability to learn, change and improve.
3. For things money accelerates and where you've spent a teeny amount, show how well that went. It's easy to imagine more money bringing more results.
4. For things money can't solve and you haven't mastered, raise them before investors do, with a clear, plausible plan that shows self-awareness. If you don't have that plan, don't raise yet.
5. Don't raise if the core team isn't assembled.

## Demonstrate adaptability, don't claim it

Jason is unimpressed by claims like "I'm in love with A/B testing," "I'm not afraid to admit when I'm wrong," "I listen to my customers," or "I'm a big fan of Eric Ries." Everyone is now a Lean Startup expert, so founders must show evidence. He wants to hear:

- How and why your pitch changed after vetting the idea with potential customers.
- A walkthrough of an app screenshot, with the customer feedback behind each part.
- Your company dashboard of the numbers that matter: why each is there, what you expect it to do, and what you do when it doesn't.
- Your first idea that turned out wrong but led to the second, and how much easier the second was to sell.
- What happened at different price points, and why you settled on the current one.
- Which parts of your landing pages improved with A/B testing and which needed old-fashioned creative thought.
- Why customers are willing to pay, in their own words.

What this really shows is that you know how to think, overcome roadblocks and figure out what to do. Those are the things money can't buy, and combined with money they make companies most likely to succeed.
