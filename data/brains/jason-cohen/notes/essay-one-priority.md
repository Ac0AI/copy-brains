## Focus on one metric and ignore the rest

- Jason relays Noah Kagan's rule: "A startup can focus on only one metric. So you have to decide what that is and ignore everything else."
- He says this is not the usual "startups need focus" platitude. The point is to pick the single number that matters most and let everything else go.
- It took him 15 years, and a side comment at a group meeting, to understand it.
- At WP Engine the metric he chose was growth in sign-ups. He still thinks cancellation rate matters, but he narrowed in on the one thing whose movement would change the business.

## Only do things that can move the business "that much"

- Jason's starting priority at WP Engine was cancellation rate. Dharmesh Shah had told him it was the key number.
- His case for it: it shows whether the service is genuinely valuable, and it drives customer LTV. Example: $50/mo for 30 months is $1,500 in lifetime revenue.
- Kagan asked what would change if cancellation fell from 3% to 2.5%. Jason said LTV would rise about 15%, so they could spend more on ads, and he admitted things wouldn't change that much.
- Kagan's rule: "You should only be doing things that can be 'that much.'"
- Jason's takeaway: "At a little company there's no time for small changes achieving small goals."
- Example: lifting conversion from 0.9% to 1.1% is meaningless if only 100 people visit the site a day. Getting 1,000 visitors a day is what matters.

## Why a worse cancellation rate can still be a win

- Jason's objection to doubling sign-ups: he could do it by spending 5x on AdWords, but lead quality would drop and cancellations would spike.
- Kagan's counter: don't assume they'll cancel at a ruinous rate. If sign-ups triple and cancellation rate doubles from 3% to 6%, "you'd still be growing almost three times faster."
- Jason ran the experiment. Cancellation rate went up only a little, "and it doesn't matter."
- General lesson: a big gain on the primary metric can swamp a modest loss on a secondary one. Don't let a secondary metric veto a large move on the main one.

## Leave incremental optimization for later

- Jason: "You can always optimize that little stuff later." Squeezing out 0.1% or 1% more conversion is mechanical, doable and in your control, and there are plenty of people you could hire to do it.
- Early on, spend effort on big needle-moving levers. Examples he gives: doubling revenue, solidifying the funding story, pushing the viral coefficient above 1, and building the asset of an active user base.
- Small incremental work can wait until there is spare time.

## What counts as big stuff, and how strict to be

- Part of "only do big stuff" is knowing what big stuff is, and that requires choosing one metric.
- With one metric chosen, you can pursue actions that could move that needle regardless of the other numbers.
- He says this kind of focus tends to break you out of incremental changes and into a major change in the business's trajectory (a link to his "local minimum" post).
- Kagan is strict: no A/B testing, no AdWords copy tweaks, no landing page optimization.
- Jason admits he is less disciplined and takes a softer line. 15% lifts from small tweaks are real, and "you can still do the 15% thing."
- The trade-off he frames: hours iterating to a 15% lift versus pouring time, emails, social media, creativity, new features, marketing, ads and measurement into a 2x or 3x change in the Most Important Number, which is an order of magnitude better.
