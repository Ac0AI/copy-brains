## What "real company" milestones Jason uses (and why the sign mattered most)

Jason (founder of Smart Bear, started 2002) kept asking: "When do you know you're a real company?" He answers with a checklist of milestones, and he treats them as real signals of maturity rather than vanity.

His list of "you're a real company when...":
- You have a domain name that isn't weird.
- Your business cards don't say "VistaPrint" on the back.
- You take purchase orders in addition to credit cards, and you accept that you must ship the product before they pay you.
- You switch from Quicken to QuickBooks.
- You hire the first real, full-time employee.
- You pay someone else to print pay stubs and handle monthly employment taxes because you're tired of doing it yourself.
- You have a sign on the building.
- A real artist does your website and handouts.

## The sign on the building as the milestone that stuck

Jason bolded one item: "You have a sign on the building." It is the one that "always stuck with me."

- Smart Bear got its sign roughly a year before the 2007 post, and he calls it "a proud moment."
- Years later (the 2023 addendum), he stood in a public park with WP Engine CEO Heather Brunner and a few dozen of the hundreds of WP Engine employees, watching the company sign go up on the building in Austin, Texas.
- He treats a physical, public sign as the moment a company becomes undeniably real, more than any internal operational change.

## Operational maturity signals: payments, payroll, accounting

Several of his markers are about stopping the scrappy, do-it-yourself way of running the business:
- **Payments:** moving from credit-card-only to taking POs, and being fine with shipping before getting paid (invoicing terms rather than prepayment).
- **Accounting:** upgrading from Quicken to QuickBooks.
- **Payroll:** outsourcing pay stubs and employment tax filings once doing it yourself is just a chore.
- **Hiring:** the first real, full-time employee.
- **Brand polish:** a domain name that isn't weird, business cards that don't say "VistaPrint" on the back, and a professional artist designing the website and handouts.

## Usage numbers as a "real company" feeling

Jason also tied the feeling to customer scale:
- In 2007, the 8000th user of Smart Bear's software products came online, which "makes me feel like a real company."
- Smart Bear had a book with a glossy cover (his peer code review book) with over 7,000 copies in circulation.
- 2009 update: 45,000 books and 30,000 paying users.
- He joked that the only thing missing was a 256-page hard-backed glossy book chronicling the company's ascent, like one he saw in Adobe's lobby.

## Lesson from the Adobe lobby: the receptionist's pitch

Visiting Adobe's Ottawa office, Jason saw a 256-page color hardcover book about the company in the lobby.
- He asked everyone he met about it; no one but the receptionist had ever seen it.
- The receptionist knew exactly what their branch did and could explain why people bought their products in about 15 seconds.
- Her elevator pitch was better than the 10-minute diatribe he got from a senior manager later that day.
- Takeaway implied: a clear, short explanation of what the company does and why customers buy matters more than polished corporate artifacts.

## The 2023 story: the sign on the childhood bagel-run building

Sixteen years later, Jason retired the dated items (paying employees is no longer hard; VistaPrint and Quicken feel like relics) and told a new story.
- As a boy in Austin, "Downtown" felt like a frightening place the family never went (government buildings, law firms, perceived crime).
- The family drove to the edge of Downtown for "real" bagels, because his parents, who grew up in and around New York City, insisted there was only one place for them.
- He remembers a long, sloping ramp leading to a parking garage and waiting in the car, staring at the scary office building across the street.
- Forty years later, that same building is where WP Engine's sign went up. The company's logo was checked against a phone photo to make sure each piece was oriented correctly ("We couldn't remember ourselves!").
- The punchline is the building itself: "You're a real company when..." the sign goes up on a building that has been imprinted on your memory since childhood.
