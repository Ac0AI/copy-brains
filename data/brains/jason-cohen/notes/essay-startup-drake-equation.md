## The core model: a startup is a chain of "ands" that fails by default

Jason argues that "why do startups fail" is the wrong question. Startups fail by default, so the useful question is why any survive. He borrows the Drake Equation (Frank Drake's 1961 estimate of how many alien civilizations we should see) as a model. In marketing terms: "The galaxy produces many warm leads, but every step of the conversion funnel is brutally leaky."

- A startup faces a chain of risks, a chain of "ands", and the failure of any one is terminal.
- He estimates perhaps 999 out of 1000 side projects drop off the chain before becoming a real business.
- Factors in his list:
  - A product people (really!) want to pay for
  - Getting their attention amid the noisy internet
  - Pricing they accept that is substantially above costs
  - Being competitive and distinctive enough to be chosen
  - Being able to build what the home page promises
  - Sustained value delivery so customers stay and keep paying
  - Funding, through early profits or fundraising
  - Working well with co-founders
  - A repeatable, profitable acquisition process
  - Attracting and retaining talent
  - Psychologically handling years of effort, stress and pain
  - Luck (capture good luck, dodge bad luck)
- He gives example failures for each factor, such as the non-technical founder who outsources to a consultant, or the technical founder who builds forever without validating.
- Two takeaways:
  1. Spend more time identifying and addressing the biggest risks.
  2. Reason in terms of "reducing risk" or "increasing chances" rather than "best ideas", "unique strategy" or "changing the world".

## Completely crush a few areas so they overcome your weaknesses

No startup is low-risk everywhere, but a few areas can be pushed to 100% or beyond, which then compensates for deficiencies elsewhere.

- A top-1% engineer largely removes the "can we build it" risk.
- A founder of a successful open source project with a community gets a competitive and marketing advantage that covers missing unique features or advertising skill.
- Teaming up with an influencer who already has distribution can be worth 50% of the equity, because it far more than doubles the likelihood of success.
- Being a renowned expert in a market lowers market risk, as a marketing advantage and through insight others lack. Entering a market you know nothing about, your education may prove fatal. He also warns that your experience can blind you.
- A singular winning attribute gives both focus and a higher chance of success, "even when you're terrible at everything else".
- Be extreme in the difference, not merely different: "Not just a minimal UI, but so minimal it works on the command-line." Great design so remarkable people buy it only for that. An algorithm that uncovers things no one else does, even at the expense of missing things others catch.
- You can't be extreme at everything; even one extreme attribute is already powerful.

## Pick the easiest product, market and customer for you

Jason suggests swapping the success-oriented question ("Who would be delighted by this product?") for a risk-oriented one: "What would be the *easiest* customer segment for us to target?"

- "Easy" includes profitable, not just doable or fun.
- Negative example: indie hackers like selling to other indie hackers, but they have no money and usually go out of business in under a year.
- Better example: stable small businesses like dentist offices have large budgets, rarely change software and last for decades. He notes the catch: dentists are hard to get on the phone and aren't usually shopping for software. "Nothing is easy!"
- Large, growing markets are easier still:
  - Many niches to start in
  - Many adjacencies to expand into
  - Incumbents fight over new customers rather than worrying about small new entrants
- The "for us" part matters. A one-person company can't deliver the integrations, governance protocols and professional services even one Enterprise customer demands, so Enterprise is a risky segment for them.
- He likes the word "easy" because it keeps things personal. If you think it will be easy, you're wrong (harder than you think but possible). If you already think it will be difficult, it's worse than you fear: impossible.

## Use the founding team to zero out risks

- A top-1% engineer co-founder takes execution risk close to zero (except in AI, where he says normal rules differ).
- A top-1% growth marketer co-founder, if the market exists, takes getting-attention risk to zero.
- This is why investors like two founders: "one to build it, one to get rid of it" (a line from his ITWatchDogs days). The value is not just double the work or a partner to commiserate with, but dramatically improving two different variables in the equation.
- Two technical co-founders is "far less interesting".

## Design out weaknesses instead of fixing them

Rather than becoming good at seven different things, make strategic choices that make your weaknesses irrelevant.

- **Limited time (day job, toddler):** serve an audience that doesn't want tech support or accepts a 48-hour response time. That shapes product complexity, intuitiveness, expectations and price. It is a superior strategy, not just a constraint: higher margin (no support cost), sellable worldwide (language and timezone are no barrier), and a product so delightful that communication is asynchronous praise on Reddit. This works at scale too (Gmail, Facebook, Twitter, most hardware).
- **Bad at design:** don't build for designers or marketing agencies. He says he is a terrible designer and sells to infrastructure engineers or backend Enterprise systems managers.
- **Bad at marketing:** build a collaborative product where people must invite others to use it. It is hard to start, but even poor marketing can then grow a healthy company.
- **Can't code but good at selling:** run a productized service, using internal workflows, spreadsheets and no-code tools that only need to be good enough for your own staff, for low cost and high profit.
- Acknowledge weaknesses as fervently as you're proud of strengths, then embrace the knock-on implications. He points to his Pivot Points article for detail.

## Play asymmetric games

The probability of success is typically unknown, so some failures are expected. What matters is whether the magnitude of the few successes exceeds the failures.

- He notes that in most startups "most things are mostly a disaster most of the time", and new hires often say, "I can't believe you're doing ______ and yet you're still in business!" That shows a few powerfully positive things can overwhelm deficiencies.
- Jeff Bezos quote he cites: "I've made billions of dollars of failures at Amazon.com. Literally. But a few big successes compensate for dozens and dozens of things that didn't work."
- Asymmetric marketing examples:
  - Fixed-cost, fixed-upside efforts like paid ads (you pay whether or not someone signs up)
  - Fixed-cost efforts that can bring customers for years, like reputation (social media, community forums) or media (successful SEO)
- Asymmetric markets have many ways to win: many niches, routes to customers and expansion paths. Avoid stagnant or shrinking markets where every sale is a scrap against desperate, monied incumbents.
- Rule: "Pick games where the downside is 1x but the upside potential is 10x or 100x." If the realized benefit is "only" 3x, the game is still a success.

## Attack the highest-risk areas first

Applying the Risk-First Heuristic: tackle high-risk items first.

- Starting with low-risk tasks guarantees early success but leaves the big risks unresolved. Nine months later you may find the venture unworkable, or have to rework everything.
- After designing your market, product and strategy to avoid risks, circle what remains and address it first.
- Examples:
  - **AI in the product:** make sure it works in real life first, because usually it doesn't.
  - **Unsure what customers will pay for:** find out before writing code, since people often have a problem but won't pay to solve it.
  - **Never marketed a new product:** build ads and landing pages leading to a waiting list, to confirm you can get attention before building something no one will see.
  - **No reputation for talking to customers:** do things that create those conversations.
- Even with this model, most startups will still fail. But articulating the risks, reducing some through the team, choosing the market and product right for you, crushing one or two areas, designing around weaknesses and attacking the remaining risks first dramatically improves the odds.
