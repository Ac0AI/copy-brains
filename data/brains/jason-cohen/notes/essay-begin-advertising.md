## Jason's rule: you can't predict which advertising channel will work, so pick for fast, cheap, measurable learning

Jason ran about a hundred campaigns at Smart Bear: paid search, trade shows, postcard mailers, newsletter sponsorships, and print magazine ads. His conclusion: "I learned I have zero ability at predicting which campaigns would produce."
- The only way to find out is to put accurate measurement in place and try it.
- He negotiated a lower "first time" rate for the trial.
- He eventually built a system that measured campaign efficacy with pinpoint accuracy, even for print.

## How Jason chooses where to start advertising

Of a dozen options, he picks the one that maximizes these, in this order of precedence:
1. **Measurable.** You must know objectively whether the value to your company exceeds the money spent. Online is generally easier to measure than offline, but not always (e.g. contests, special offers, secret landing pages can make offline measurable).
2. **Quickest** to learn whether it worked. Fast learning gets you to a working advertising model sooner.
3. **Cheapest.** At this stage the goal is learning what advertising works, not maximizing total revenue. Once you know how to spend money to make money, pour money on it as fast as possible.
4. **Comfortable.** Pick something you like, such as a news site you already read, because your gut isn't useful for prediction anyway. Knowing the site's culture lets you build an ad suited to its audience.

His examples: he was particularly successful on StackOverflow (he was an early beta user) and on Coding Horror (he was a long-time reader).

His closing advice: don't fret over picking the "right" campaign, because you can't. Make sure these key things are in place and "just go."

## Campaign quality changes over time, so keep re-measuring

Jason's Dr. Dobbs magazine ads show how results decay.
- He started with a 1/4-page ad in the back (what he called "in the zoo", over the ad salesmen's objections to that characterization of their remnants).
- It worked great, and a full-page ad worked better.
- Over the years it became less and less effective, "like clockwork", and he kept demanding a lower price.
- Three years before the magazine died, it had been one of his top lead sources.

Lesson: "Things change. Can't predict." Past performance of a channel doesn't guarantee its future performance.

## Be skeptical of ad salesmen's arguments

Jason describes the standard pitches he heard when pushing for lower rates:
- "Our readership is increasing," backed by BPA stats. He calls BPA a third-party "auditor" who always certifies strong growth and a monied readership.
- "You'll lose credibility if you stop advertising. People will wonder whether you've disappeared."

He didn't let these override his own measured results. The magazine went under a few months after that conversation.

## Be wary of lead-gen lists

Jason is dismissive of typical "lead-gen" offers: CSV dumps of contact information for so-called "pre-qualified" people who were tricked into downloading a whitepaper. He asks whether any such channel is actually good at generating sales, and his answer is that you can only tell by measuring.
