## Competitive strategy as limiting the competitor's options

Jason Cohen takes a lesson from computer Othello. In the 1990s the winning AI strategy did not maximize its own tile count or board position. It scored each position by how many valid moves the opponent had, where fewer was better. Jason's summary: "Limiting choice is more important than what the choices are." He argues the same principle applies to business competition.

## The Othello lesson: minimize the opponent's moves

- Typical board-game AI looks many moves ahead and rates each resulting position. The hard part is the "goodness" metric.
- Conventional Othello metrics counted your own tiles and valued edge tiles above middle ones.
- The winning metric was different: how many valid moves does my opponent have?
- The insight is that the contest is less about your own pieces and more about shrinking what the other side can do.

## Defensive sports as the same pattern

- A defense can never cover every contingency, so it forces the offense into higher-risk, lower-percentage plays.
- Basketball: you can't cover the long shot and the charge at once, so you elect to give up the low-percentage three-point shot.
- Football: you can't cover both in-routes and out-routes, so you force a throw into as much traffic as possible.

## The business question to ask

Competitors always have options. Jason's question is: "How can you limit their choices to things that are low-percentage or expensive?" The goal is to make them spend more money, get more lucky, or be more creative to compete.

## Tactics that limit competitors' choices

- **Honor the competitor's coupons.** This removes the coupon as a way for the competitor to "beat" you, so it is no longer a choice for building a competitive edge.
- **Overpay for the best advertising slots.** You are paying too much if you measure direct ROI alone. The extra value is forcing competitors into a low-converting ad position, which means many fewer customers at any price.
- **Pay for exclusive deals with the largest affiliates.** Again you overpay on direct ROI, but competitors are forced onto affiliates who can generate only about 1/10th the leads.
- **Exclusive deals with brand-name suppliers.** Suppliers are often invisible to customers, but sometimes their identity is known or integration with a well-known brand is part of your value proposition. If customers value that integration and you make it exclusive, competitors must find other, less-desirable ways to deliver it, or cede the capability to you.
- **Patents, with a caveat.** Patents limit how a competitor can compete, since others must think of another way. Jason says this is useless in software, where it is always easy to do it another way. Software companies don't protect themselves with patents; other moats are available instead (he links his essay on moats).

## How Smart Bear applied it

- Smart Bear positioned itself as "the experts in code review." Evidence Jason lists: they literally wrote the book, ran the largest-ever study on code review, have years of history, and have the most popular tool.
- This makes it expensive and slow for a competitor to claim parity, let alone surpass them. It is possible but very hard to compete on that basis.
- Why it matters: in Jason's opinion, being "the expert" is the best qualifier for their market, the Enterprise. Customers include Microsoft, Qualcomm, Intuit, IBM and Adobe.
- Those customers care less about "lowest cost" or "simplest installation" (though Jason notes their installation is simple).
- If his opinion is right, he has removed the single best product-positioning choice from competitors, who must take a less desirable, lower-percentage path.
