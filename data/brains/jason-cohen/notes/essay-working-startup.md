## Choosing a side business that fits the constraints of a day job

Jason says a side venture has constraints a "normal" business doesn't:
- He can't answer the phone or email during business hours.
- He can't afford to hire three developers.
- He has to work in fits and spurts.

His advice is not to fight these constraints but to "turn them into competitive advantages."

- **Don't promise what you can't deliver.** If the website claims "stupendous customer service" but there's no phone number and a 10:00am email isn't answered until that night, that is a "missed expectation." He calls it "pissing in the wind"; it comes right back at you.
- **Use the day-job income to underprice.** If someone complains there's no phone number, point out that at $5/mo you can't afford a phone staff.
- **Pick a product where simplicity is an advantage.** "We do only one thing and we do it better than anyone" is a good marketing slogan. He notes you don't have time to build a Microsoft Excel knock-off, so don't try.

## Embrace slow growth for a bootstrapped side project

Jason argues that any bootstrapped company should aim for slow, consistent growth rather than explosive growth, and that this matters most for a business run in spare time.

- Hoping a blog post gets 267 votes on Hacker News, or that a v1.1 release sets the blogosphere ablaze, is not a plan.
- Slow growth maximizes the chance of success because success doesn't depend on some unlikely, massive event outside your control.
- Slow growth lets you change drastically in the early days without sinking the company.
- Because you have a job, you don't need explosive growth to be successful.

## The real goal: enough to quit the day job

Jason says the immediate goal isn't to make millions but to "build a business just solid enough to quit your day job."

- It means enough profit that you can already live (frugally) off the company.
- Or it means enough customers to have "proved" a viable business model, so you can raise money on sensible terms.
- He is explicit that quitting and hoping revenue will show up is not the plan.

## Tell your employer about the side project, in writing

Jason's core legal advice: the best way to avoid a lawsuit is to prove your employer knows about the side project. He is not a lawyer and says none of this is legal advice.

- He knows founders want to operate in secret because they fear being fired, distrusted, or looked at funny at lunch.
- His reply: what's worse, one of those things or a lawsuit? If you don't think they'd sue, you shouldn't fear telling them. They'll find out anyway, so tell them on your own terms.
- He says he has always told his employer and "it always worked out for the best."
- He has been on both sides: he has done a startup while employed, has employed people capable of running their own side startup, and has known people who were sued over it, "and not all of them won."

**His letter template** is a short note "To Whom it may Concern" that says:
- He has a hobby that does not in any way conflict with work, described in a sentence.
- He works on it only at home on his own time, it is not in conflict with his employee agreement, he owns it, and the company has no ownership or rights to it.
- Like any hobby, it could generate a small amount of income.
- If it ever conflicted with his job, it's his responsibility to notify them immediately.

**Get it signed by someone with authority.** That means someone who can legally represent the company, typically a C-level officer or the company's legal counsel. A supportive boss signing is not good enough because "he can't speak for the company."

## Check your employment agreement before starting

Jason's template letter says the side business doesn't conflict with the employment agreement, and he says that had better be true.

- Employment agreements are typically biased toward the employer. Some have outrageous clauses claiming anything you do, even at home, on your own time, and unrelated to work, is automatically company property.
- Some also make you responsible for identifying and reporting those things, with "no limit" to the damage you could have caused if you don't.
- Courts have thrown out some egregious contracts, but that's unusual, you can't depend on it, and you don't want to go to court at all. You also signed it.
- Action: read the agreement and confirm the side business is legal under it. If it isn't, write a letter like the template that specifically states this is a valid exception to the employment agreement.

## Never use company property or company internet

Jason says the typical clause that anything done physically at work, or on company-owned software and equipment (laptop, customer lists, Photoshop), belongs to the company is fair. If the project is really on the side and really yours alone, it can't be assisted by a company laptop, software, or connection.

- Be paranoid: assume every laptop records keystrokes, clicks, screenshots, sites, and emails, and that internet activity is logged.
- Don't try to hide it with tools like an anonymizer plus Gmail over SSL. Covering your tracks looks like a sign you're doing something wrong.
- If the company sues, "discovery" lets them search your email records. If they find 100 emails you sent during work hours, "You lose."
- His summary: "Just don't do it."

## Time management techniques for a side startup

Jason says a startup generates infinite work and already takes the 40 hours a week you don't have, so you can't afford bad habits or working on the wrong tasks. He admits he is naturally bad at personal time management (procrastinates, disorganized). He says there's no silver bullet and workflow is personal, but lists these techniques:

- Check email infrequently.
- Inbox Zero: process email until nothing remains, for psychological and practical benefits.
- GTD (Getting Things Done): he says it "literally changed my life"; few people implement 100% but everyone can take away a trick or two.
- Work in sprints (Pomodoro-style): short, focused bursts eliminate the waste from context-switching and interrupt-driven behavior.
- Sleep enough: trading an hour of sleep for an hour of coding "is never efficient," because productivity plummets.
- Use RescueTime (free) to see empirically where time goes. Its founder, Tony Wright, started that company while employed and wrote about it.
- Optimize the slowest tasks first: fixing the slowest tasks raises overall productivity more than you'd expect.
