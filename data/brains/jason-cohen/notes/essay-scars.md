## Scars can be a defining strength and a blind spot at once

Jason argues that the emotional baggage and past experience that make a founder unique also act as blinders. He cites examples of people whose strong opinions are really scars showing:

- The guy a little too steadfast that "VCs are evil."
- The founder who says she doesn't need to talk to customers before a $200,000 development project because "customers don't know what they want until you show it to them."
- The developer who is sure that "Java sucks."

He says dwelling on our peculiar trauma is a comforting way to develop uniqueness, and most advice says to embrace that uniqueness and own it. His caveat: some of that baggage is a hindrance whether or not it is also "defining me." Even if you know your scars, embrace them, and can rationalize every decision as correct for you, a decision can still be wrong.

## Bootstrapper scars at a funded company: the $1000/month hosting example

Jason says he still sometimes runs WP Engine like the bootstrapped startup it was for its first 18 months, instead of the funded growth machine it evolved into.

- Last week he spent about 10 hours saving about $1000/mo in hosting costs.
- The financial case looks good: 10 hours to earn $12,000 over the next year means his time was worth over $1000/hour.
- He says that is "not no matter what." The same 10 hours could have gone into making the website speed report (speed.wpengine.com) easier to share.
- His estimate for that alternative: 1000 people try it over the next year, 10 move their WordPress site to WP Engine, and a few consultants put 20 of their clients on it. At $50/mo, that is 30 × $50 = $1500/mo in direct new revenue, plus side benefits in marketing and branding.

## When cash now beats growth later, and when it doesn't

Jason's rule for choosing between saving money now and investing in growth depends on whether you have money in the bank:

- **Bootstrapping:** getting the $1000/mo right now is the better choice. Money is scarce, time is precious, and "$1000 today is better than $5000 next year (which you might not survive to see)."
- **Money in the bank:** it is "just the opposite." The point of an investment is to turn money today into long-term value, meaning a growing, profitable, predictable revenue machine.

The lesson is that the right call depends on your company's stage and funding, not on what your instincts (your scars) are comfortable with.

## Step one: define your core beliefs

Jason says to decide your inalienable tenets, the things you want to live by no matter what, even if holding them means:

- making less money
- increasing risk
- hurting your brand or your feelings
- limiting your fame
- closing doors
- losing relationships

These beliefs are strong enough that if a relationship contradicts them, the relationship is what's wrong and it's good that it dissolves. If a way of making money contradicts them, you don't want to make money that way. These are your core values and also form the basis of your company's cultural values.

## Step two: build a company where the right decisions come naturally

Jason says to make sure your startup journey conforms to your values and to your natural strengths and weaknesses. Decide to build a company in which the correct, consistent decisions are the ones you'll naturally take:

- If you love optimizing the last dollar out of the process (as he apparently does), build a bootstrapped company.
- If you don't like working with people, be a Micropreneur like Rob Walling and Patrick McKenzie.
- If you want to leave a mark on the world with big ideas, lots of people and lots of money, seek advice from people who have walked that path. Find advisors who help you become a better version of yourself, rather than pushing you to be something "rational" but unnatural.

## Step three: surround yourself with trusted advisors to find your blind spots

Jason says that once you have defined yourself and made decisions consistent with it, you still have to find where you are wrong, ignorant, or doing the wrong thing by your own definition of "right." You can't do that alone, "almost by definition," because otherwise you would already have fixed it.

- Surround yourself with trusted advisors.
- Be completely and continuously honest with them.
- Then actually listen and learn.

He applies this to himself: he knows he is naturally a bootstrapper, a "get to the first $10M in ARR, but only after seven years, and then what?" type, so he needs constant course corrections. He stresses that you "cannot know you're wrong," even if, like him, you are proactively introspective, have escaped Impostor Syndrome, and have a few successful startups behind you.

## Summary principle: you are your scars, but more than that

Jason's closing guidance is to embrace your scars and let them lead you to who you are and what you believe. Construct your life, relationships and organizations around them. Then, on that solid foundation, seek compatible guidance on how to execute despite the weaknesses, blind spots and flaws those same scars create. His last lines: "You are your scars. But you're also more than that."
