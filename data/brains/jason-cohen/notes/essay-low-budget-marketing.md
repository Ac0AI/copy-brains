## Maximum inventory decides whether to keep optimizing a channel

Jason treats "should I optimize this channel?" as a question about inventory, not "it depends." In advertising, inventory is all the available ad space; in AdWords it is the maximum clicks available to you across all relevant keywords. Being #1 for a keyword means you already have the max inventory for it.

- If you sit in lower spots on most keywords you care about, or aren't bidding on other relevant keywords, there is likely a lot more inventory to take.
- Optimizing an existing campaign is easier and cheaper than starting a new one, and can even lower cost per signup.
- His rule: "If you think there's 2x or more inventory you could go get, I say go get it."
- Example at WP Engine: #3 on AdWords and #3 organic for "WordPress Hosting", and #1 for "Managed WordPress Hosting". Traffic on related keywords drops off fast, so they estimated less than 2x inventory left. Even with heavy spend and losing money on every sale, it wouldn't be a significant growth area.
- Once you approach inventory limits, look for other campaigns that can double the business.

## Cut a campaign that fails out of the gate; don't give it the benefit of the doubt

When a new channel utterly fails, the temptation is to keep spend low and iterate (better phrasing, better design, lifting click-through from 0.1% to 1.0%). Jason says this line of thought is usually wrong.

- Basis: his experience across 100+ campaigns over 11 years at Smart Bear and WP Engine, with no hard data. Campaigns that were bad out of the gate tended to stay bad.
- Possible reasons he gives: by the third campaign your messaging is decent, so early failure points to a bad channel rather than a bad message; iteration gets you incrementally better, not drastically better; failure may mean you don't understand the channel at all, which iteration won't fix.
- Example scenario he describes: $600/mo of banner ads yielding two signups in a month, and an affiliate program with $50 per signup where 100 affiliates joined but most sold once or never, and two produced about five signups a month.
- Advice: "cut out early and go find another channel."

## Optimize the website before individual campaigns

Website optimizations beat per-campaign optimizations because every campaign leads to the site.

- A 10% improvement in bounce rate off the pricing page means 10% more revenue across all campaigns: paid, organic and word-of-mouth.
- Landing-page learnings can be shared across campaigns. The exact text can't, but layouts that raise click-through, a form that captures an email instead of signing up directly, or a message for a customer segment that flows through multiple campaigns can be reused.
- Early-stage sites often have large-effect problems: missing or weak testimonials or demo page, testimonials not on all pages, a home page that would triple click-through if it were long-scrolling and well designed, no strong call to action, a confusing pricing page, or a main hook and product description that turns people away.

## Only chase large effects when your data is small

When you're small, almost no data is statistically significant, so most variation is random noise, even if you use tools that claim to handle the statistics.

- Seek large effects, not incremental improvements.
- Example: at one signup a day, a "10% improvement" is still one signup a day. You need two a day, and "you'll know if you get a change that drastic."
- Changes should be drastic but also based on a theory, not "throwing shit at the wall." Otherwise A/B tests can be wrong even with a positive result.

## Low-budget and high-budget marketing are opposite playbooks

Jason's advice is explicitly for cash-strapped companies. Large-budget marketing works differently.

- High budget: try as many channels as possible, as fast as possible. Hire experts to accelerate or de-risk. Find what works quickly, then ramp to full inventory immediately. For channels like affiliate programs where a big check doesn't make them work, build a whole team to extract value.
- Burning cash on things that don't work is "exactly the trade-off you want": accelerated cash burning for accelerated learning.
- VC-funded companies should do this to grow faster. Even bootstrapped companies might, if they adopt annual pricing.
- Low-budget summary: use inventory limits to decide whether to push a channel, exit bad channels quickly, optimize the website before channels, and don't get excited by tiny, immeasurable results.
