## Why Jason distrusts most startup advice

- Advice is usually the giver talking to themselves. Jason says people "really are doing is giving themselves advice" and rarely ask about the asker's goals first.
- Good advice starts with questions: what are you trying to get out of this, what does good look like, do you want employees at all, do you want to be famous, or rich, or just independent.
- His own original goal at SmartBear was to "have my own job and no one tells me what to do" and make as much money as possible. After about 18 months he switched to raising money and going big. He says the two paths have different constraints and goals, and neither is better.
- He was CEO of WP Engine for four years and has not been CEO for the last five. At SmartBear that would have "sounded like hell".
- Survivor bias: a blogger with one or two wins may just have gotten lucky, since so many people try. Ask whether others did the same things and failed. If the behavior correlates just as much with failure, it proves nothing.
- Example: Jim Collins's *Good to Great* picked 11 outperforming companies and extracted a formula. Five years later 40% were bankrupt, and public-company peers did the same things too.
- Venture capitalists do nothing but try to pick winners and can't. Only about 25% of VC firms ever make money. His conclusion: "there's not a formula".
- You can't split test experience, so you can't tell whether a founder's choice caused their success.

## How to evaluate advice you receive

- Check the giver's goals, motivations and context against yours. If they don't match closely enough, it may still be good advice, but you can't know. If the person is similar to you, started the same way and seems smart, weigh it more.
- It is fine to ask whether the advice resonates with how you feel.
- If competing advice (never use Twitter versus build a following on Twitter) can all work, pick the one you're most excited about. You will put in extra energy and wake up thinking about it, so you will likely execute it best.
- He is explicit that this is not "follow your passion", which he calls crap. It is a tiebreaker when you can't tell the options apart.

## Build something people love, not "beat the competition"

- Jason scores high on competitiveness (StrengthsFinder) but thinks "how will I beat them" is probably the wrong mode. The better question is how to build a product you're proud of, and find customers who are excited about it.
- People buy for many reasons: best features, best product, culture, or purpose.
- Examples:
  - 37signals: early products weren't great, and the abandoned ones were bad, yet people loved the company, its ideas and its contributions.
  - Jason bought FogBugz from Joel Spolsky because he loved Joel's blog, not because it was the best bug tracker.
  - Patagonia: people buy for the culture and mission (company policy is to bail out employees arrested protesting for the environment).
  - Google won simply by being the best.
- You still must track competitors and keep features operational, but "you can't build a company by well, here's how we're not this other person". Decide who you are and execute.

## Tearing down a pitch, then filling it back up

- On a podcast he called "Doctor Laura for startups" he critiqued ideas directly. He also does pitch practice with companies at Capital Factory in Austin.
- His method: shred the pitch honestly, then point out the real value the founder isn't telling you. Weak pitches focus on a feature (resizing an image) instead of what it does for people.
- Example: a tool that helps people earn money online can change lives. It gives an extra $1,000 a month to a single parent, a stay-at-home parent, an artist, or someone who wants to quit a bad job or lives where it's hard otherwise.
- Good criticism names specific problems (he might see three where everyone else said the idea was good) so the founder can work on them, and ends with what is compelling.

## How WP Engine's customers and motivation changed

- At first he was excited that someone would pay for it, and that the engineering problems (optimizing, securing, caching) were fun for him. Signups were easy, a sign of product-market fit.
- Customers were often freelancers building sites, or business owners with a triggering event, ready to move from a $5 host to a $50 plan: an SEO person said the site was slow, a traffic spike took it down, or the site got hacked.
- Over roughly 18 months or more his motivation widened beyond money to what the company did for people. Example: a customer who makes guitars got press and a traffic spike, and her site survived her biggest sales day ever. WP Engine keeps one of her guitars in the support area.
- Employee impact also mattered to him. A third of employees have no college degree, which is unusual for a high-tech company, and the executive team has many women. His view: you have to measure diversity or you can't claim to care, and the door has to be opened but people still have to earn it.
- The cheapest WP Engine plan is now $35, down from $50 at launch.

## Bootstrapping honestly: time, money and the options

- He pushes back on "work 10 hours a week and screw off the rest". Even 3 hours a day on a project means thinking about it all the time. A full-time job plus about 20 hours a week on a startup plus family is 100% of your time.
- His advice to a founder with about $4,300 MRR and 220 customers after 3 months: with nobody on it full-time, you can't act on big strategy, so you just need to get customers.
- Real options, all costly:
  - Salary job: least stressful, since the paycheck settles that anxiety, but time is very tight.
  - Consulting: spiky, "the hourly always wins" over product work, and when it drops you're stressed about money.
  - Raise money: includes newer bootstrapper-friendly sources (he names Earnest Capital and Tiny Seed). You give up structure, meaning equity or control.
  - Grind with your own time: burnout is a main reason these fail.
- Everything gives up something: equity or control, or your time. Co-founders already give up some control.
- On 37signals/DHH's side-project story: it was effectively four people part-time, about 40 to 60 hours a week total, funded by an agency, and took about two years. Be honest about that.
- He rejects "struggle porn": struggling means the business isn't working yet, which is a problem, not a badge. With a team of ten, 100-hour weeks signal a leadership failure. But it is also insulting to say you've failed for not having a $20K/month business after four months.
- Rule of thumb he agrees with: $20K a year in revenue after three years is probably not a good use of your time.

## Making hard decisions: filters, then one or two dimensions

- The options aren't ranked good/better/best; they differ on multiple dimensions with incomparable units (more hours versus giving up equity), so you can't stack rank or use a rubric.
- Step 1: set filters, limits you won't cross. Examples: "I have four kids and a wife I'd like to keep, so nothing that jeopardizes that", a time you'll be home, or "I will never have an investor". Filters shrink the option set and let you stop second-guessing: you know why something is a no.
- Step 2: pick one or two dimensions to maximize (most money, or what excites you, or learning something) and decide on those, knowing you're trading off the rest.
- Wants change over time. He started wanting interesting work and customer payments, and later cared about diversity and culture. He notes Balsamiq's founder wanted a one-person company, and it grew.

## The like / good at / company needs framework

- Check three things: what you like, what you're good at, and what the company needs. Each failure mode:
  - Like it and company needs it, but you're not good: fine when small, a failure at scale, since important things need real expertise.
  - Good and company needs it, but you don't like it: burnout.
  - Like it and good at it, but the company doesn't need it (e.g. he'd love to code): common for founders because nobody tells a founder to stop.
- He used it to decide he was not the right CEO for WP Engine at around 60 to 70 people. The company needed someone to lead large groups, hire an executive team and open global offices. That wasn't his strength and "sounds like hell", and it is the successor's favorite job.
- Everyone deserves roles where they like the work, are good at it and it's needed. He says he didn't invent it and similar frameworks exist.

## What is hard at scale

- At around 600 employees, finding ideas that can make tens of millions a year is very hard; ideas worth a couple million a year aren't worth doing.
- Human events (births, deaths, marriages) happen constantly, and the company is small enough that everyone feels them. He says that is hard with no tactical fix.
