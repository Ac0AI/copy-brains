## Pitch must survive noise, not just full attention

Jason argues that a company's pitch is judged under distraction, not in a quiet room. Prospects are like teenagers on a car stereo: they have tiny speakers, background chaos and competing interests. He frames the real competitor as "competition for *attention*," not features or benefits. His standard: "It's not enough to be compelling when you have 60 uninterrupted, full-attention seconds. Your pitch has to work in the noise."

## The Snoop Dogg cassette-player test

Jason uses Snoop Dogg's track vetting as the model for stress-testing a message.
- On MTV Cribs, Snoop did not use a sound-proof room or $50,000 speakers. He played sample tracks on a cassette player on the floor, turned up so it crackled and distorted.
- The reason: songs must sound good on a cheap car stereo with distractions, tiny speakers and a messy back seat.
- Music producers recommend mixing on crappy speakers. Jason's takeaway: "If it sounds good on crap, it will sound good anywhere."
- He applies the same logic to a pitch: test it in degraded conditions, not ideal ones.

## Every medium has its own distractions

Jason lists how attention gets competed away in each channel:
- **Web page:** "You've got mail" alerts, instant messages about funny YouTube videos, and the "back" button as the ultimate escape.
- **Magazine ad:** a ringing phone, and a more interesting picture on the next page.
- **10-second pitch at parties and tradeshows:** cocktails, room noise, and a more interesting story in the adjoining conversation.

The point holds regardless of medium: assume partial, interrupted attention.

## Test a spoken pitch in a noisy environment

- Record the pitch in a noisy place, such as a bar or a playground.
- Play it back and ask whether it still makes sense when you can only make out about 60 percent of the syllables.
- Ask friends to point out what is engaging and what is not.

## Test printed material by covering parts of it

- Lay the printed piece on a table and cover different parts with a magazine.
- The whole message does not need to shine through.
- The check is whether the visible fragments are eye-catching enough that someone notices them and becomes interested.

## Repeat the message to push some of it through

- Distractions and other priorities will sit between you and your target most of the time.
- Jason recommends marketing techniques that repeat a message: newsletters, a blog, Twitter, Facebook.
- Repeating often enough can push *some* of your communications through the chaos. He frames this as partial success ("some"), not guaranteed delivery.
