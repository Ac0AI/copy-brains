## The right number of items depends on the list's purpose, and it is almost never 10

- Jason's opening argument: if people had evolved with nine fingers, there would be "Top 9" lists, so "a 'Top 10' list probably doesn't have the correct number of things."
- His test for any list: ask "What is the purpose of this list?", then ask whether that purpose calls for more or fewer items, and whether there is "a _good reason_ why this the right number of things."
- His default: "Usually, fewer is better, and the right answer is almost never '10.'"

## One item is the best choice when speed and focus matter

- Jason calls it "reductive to say only one thing is important," but a single item "ensures that it gets done, as quickly as possible."
- He links this to his maximized-decision idea: one priority gets full attention and the fastest completion.
- Applied per team: with 17 teams, 17 items (one each) is arguably a repeat of the "one thing" rule at the team level.
- Caveat: 17 is still arguably too many if projects span multiple teams.

## Three items work because people like threes and can remember them

- Humans across the globe have always liked things in threes.
- Three items are small enough that you can remember all of them.

## About seven items is the memory limit, and the real number may be nearer five

- Jason cites one of the most-cited psychology papers, "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two": seven items is what the average person can hold in their head at once.
- His rule of thumb: "If you can't keep it in your head, even when you're trying very hard, it's too many items."
- He jokes that post-COVID brains are now 5 ± 2, and says plainly he has no data for that claim.

## A long list is justified when the goal is to finish every item

- Example: a security audit with 132 flags can have the goal of addressing all of them.
- Why 100% completion is powerful:
  - It is a clear statement to make: every item in a specific set is done.
  - It works like "1: Focus" with sub-items, where a checklist shows progress as the team works through it.
  - It explains why there are 132 items: that is the audit's count.
  - It gives a clear state change at the end. His example: "We pass this audit, and then [large customer] renews for three years."

## Declare bankruptcy on huge backlogs

- Example: an inbox with 402,302 unread emails is effectively the same as an empty inbox, except that the number looms over you.
- His advice: declare "bankruptcy" on the email inbox or the Jira backlog and archive it all. "The important things will come back."
- He says archive rather than delete, because disk space is close to free and you still have the items if you really need one.
