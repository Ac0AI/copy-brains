## The wisdom of crowds works only when there is an objectively correct answer

Jason opens with jelly-bean and game-show evidence, then explains where it breaks.
- Michael Mauboussin (2007): 73 Columbia Business School students guessed the beans in a jar. Guesses ranged from 250 to 4,100; the actual count was 1,116.
- The average individual error was 700, a 62% miss, so the students were "awful estimators". The group average was 1,151, just 3% off, and only 2 of the 73 students guessed better.
- Jack Treynor's 1987 version: 850 beans, 56 students, group average 2.5% off, only one student better. It has been repeated many times with similar results.
- His rule: "There is 'wisdom in the crowd' when there is an objectively-correct answer, and when the errors cancel out."

## Why the crowd beat the "smartest friend" on Who Wants to be a Millionaire

Jason uses the show's lifelines as a natural experiment.
- The studio audience picked the correct answer 91% of the time, across all domains and difficulty levels. Their only qualification was being in a TV studio that afternoon.
- "Phone a friend" (the smartest person the contestant knows, with Google and Wikipedia) was right only 65% of the time.
- Takeaway: for factual questions, a crowd of average people beats a single expert.

## The mechanism: ignorant votes cancel out and a few informed votes decide

Jason works the arithmetic to show why crowds are right.
- Take 100 audience members where only 16 know the answer is A and the other 84 vote randomly. The expected result is roughly 37, 21, 21, 21.
- A mere 16% of voters made A the clear winner, nearly double the next closest answer, because "the ignorant people vote randomly and their votes cancel out".
- More complex scenarios give the same distribution. For example, 11 know it's A, 15 each know B, C or D is wrong and vote randomly among the rest, and 44 have no clue.
- The crowd is only wise when ignorance is random noise rather than systematic bias.

## Votes are vetoes, so the crowd kills innovation

Jason explains why the same process fails for creative work.
- The holiday-meal analogy: some guests can't take spice, Uncle Bill is allergic to garlic, Aunt Sarah skips red meat, Timmy won't eat anything green. The only dish that pleases everyone is bland chicken and rice.
- Chicken and rice doesn't please anyone; it is just what everyone "hated the least".
- His key line: "Votes don't converge on something wonderful. Rather, votes are *vetoes*."
- "In creative work, votes eliminate the interesting edges, because votes result in subtracting rather than adding, leaving only the boring residue that no one hated enough to vote off the island."
- "That's not how great products are made."

## Where consensus is the right goal and where it isn't

- A wedding caterer serving 300 guests should cook chicken and rice. Nobody goes to a wedding for the food, so the goal is to annoy as few guests as possible.
- Startups are different. Jason says design-by-committee is "the antithesis of innovation", and average products for the average consumer are the opposite of innovation and probably a bad product strategy.
- Small companies need a niche, "a market space they can completely, unquestionably own, not some gray middle-ground where your attempt to offend no one also means exciting no one."
- He is skeptical of messaging, product features and ad layouts honed by focus groups, because these are creative decisions that require innovation.

## Rule of thumb for using crowd input

- Use crowds (surveys, polls, averaging many estimates) for questions with a factual, objectively right answer, such as estimates and forecasts, where individual errors are independent.
- Don't use votes, focus groups or committee consensus to decide positioning, features or design, where you need a distinctive point of view.
- Jason's further reading on when crowds are wise: Surowiecki's *The Wisdom of Crowds* (five elements of a wise crowd) and Scott Page's *The Difference* (diversity makes a group smarter), which inspired his Millionaire example.
