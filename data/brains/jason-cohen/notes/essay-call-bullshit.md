## Calling out spam and disagreeing in public: why Jason stopped being polite

Jason gets a steady stream of "special" offers, guest-post requests, pleas for attention and mild bribery because of what he calls his "moderate, only-in-bubble fame". Most are sincere, and even the blatant marketing pitches (for example, "review this fantastic new book that's so relevant to your readers") are professional, direct and arrive with a gift, often just a free book.

- His default reply was always well-mannered, even to obvious spam. Example: thanking someone for the chance to "e-interview" a celebrity they represent, then declining because the book was not directly relevant to his audience.
- He now thinks the real reason for that politeness is "terrible": he was afraid of annoying someone famous, or someone who might become famous, who might then refuse to work with him or say something bad about him in public. He calls this "Pathetic."

## What finally pushed him to stop being nice: a guest-blogger scam

The trigger was what he calls the worst solicitation to date, titled "Guest Blogger Proposal--An Interesting Twist." It ran 729 words, nearly the length of his own article.

- It used the standard lines ("We are only looking for a handful of sites... and don't want you to miss out") and heavy exclamation marks ("saving you time and effort!!").
- It asked for "our own restricted login (preferred)" so the senders could publish articles directly to his blog without review.
- It guaranteed the posts would carry sub-headlines and bullets on "how to...", "7 tips for...", "5 reasons..." topics.
- It left out that the senders were affiliate marketers, and that the auto-published articles would push products for them to monetize.
- His view: letting strangers publish anonymous, self-serving, product-pushing posts on his blog would pollute it and betray his readers.

## The reply he sent, and why it mattered to him

He replied bluntly instead of declining politely. In summary, he called it a "fascinating scam," said he would tell everyone he knew about the offer to "pollute my blog with garbage," and asked them to send more contact details so he could post them online for people to send "a token of their appreciation."

- Jason says the recipient surely couldn't care less. What mattered was that he "finally grew the stones to say what I feel even if it pisses someone off."
- He admits he wrote it after two "poor-man's martini's" (three ice cubes dropped in, swirled, no shaker). He cites the ancient Macedonian practice of making important decisions only when drunk: with the societal filters and self-doubt lowered, you think and act as your true self, so supposedly more truthfully and "correctly."

## Social media "honesty" is mostly performance, and Jason includes himself

Jason argues that although social media is supposed to be about "honesty," "being human" and "genuineness," most of it isn't.

- Bloggers "fluff their feathers," coming across as more confident, smarter and more sure than they are. He includes himself, though he says he at least tries to reveal the "emotional underbelly" (he links his posts on impostor syndrome and how emotionally draining startups are).
- Twitter users retweet, follow and pander to minor celebrities, hoping the lesser deity will retweet them someday, or accept their email as non-spam because they have already "joined the conversation." He includes himself here too, and says he "still" does it.

## The deeper question: when do you earn the right to say what you think?

Jason asks whether he dares to publicly disagree with the well-known figures he respects, and what he fears would happen if he did.

- Disagree with Seth Godin, and he might not be considered for Godin's Domino project.
- Disagree with Jason Fried, and Fried might lay into him the way he did with Mint.
- Make fun of Joel Spolsky, and he might not be invited back to Business of Software (he had written a satirical "letters to Joel Spolsky" piece).
- He asks: "When do I earn the right to say what I think?" and "Why do I think I need to 'earn' that right?" He also asks how many RSS subscribers or Twitter followers it would take before it feels fine to rail against asinine offers or gently disagree with luminaries he does respect.
- His answer is deliberately flat: "This many." The implication is that there is no threshold. The permission is one he has to give himself.
- He closes with lines from Amanda Palmer about not wanting to be the person you want to be, and possibly dying before you see that you already are that person.
