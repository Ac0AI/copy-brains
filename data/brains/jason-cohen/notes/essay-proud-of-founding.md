## Founder as seed, company as oak tree

- When visitors to WP Engine's building meet the founder in his small office, they say "You must be so proud of what you created." Jason answers "What *we* created," and says it isn't false humility.
- He didn't create what exists today. Over four hundred people were building it as he spoke, and he hadn't been CEO for more than a decade.
- His metaphor: "WP Engine is an oak tree. I was the seed." The seed mattered and holds a permanent place in the history of the tree, but nothing you see today is the seed.
- He says a founder's job is to start the thing and then work with amazing people who collectively create the result.
- He frames this as the answer to "How does it feel to have created two unicorns?" He says he only knows what it feels like to start them, not to build them alone.

## The unglamorous work is what keeps the company alive

- Jason splits a company into what outsiders can see and what they can't.
- Visible: sales calls, trade-show booths, product launches, an incredible tech support experience.
- Invisible: the 3am security upgrade, the start of a long-term new product category and brand strategy, collecting accounts receivable, quarterly financial reporting.
- He gives a quality example. A widespread but unreproducible bug was caught before it was ever deployed, because of a mature quality practice.
- His conclusion: "It is all vital to the life of the tree." Branches, cells, leaves and roots all matter, not just the externally obvious parts.

## Employees are acorns: people will leave, and that is healthy

- Jason says each person at WP Engine will eventually leave. He hopes not for years, since "we have so much more to do," but he calls it inevitable.
- He compares departing employees to acorns. Some birth their own trees. More often they join another tree to strengthen it and grow in symbiosis.
- Some alumni started companies, not just in tech but in salsas, comic books and consulting.
- Some joined niche small companies, others the largest resume-enhancing big-tech names.
- He treats people leaving as a normal outcome of a company that develops its people, not as a failure of retention.

## What the company does for people's lives

- Jason lists outcomes he sees as part of a company's real impact:
  - Some employees used their success to be the first in their family to buy a house.
  - Some were the first in their family to travel internationally.
  - Some were the first in their family to attend college.
  - Some arrived with one career and left with another, with more responsibility and making 10x more money than they thought possible.
- He treats this career and life growth as a core part of what a company is for, not a side effect.

## The test of whether a company was worth it

- Jason says it is up to all of us to make sure each person looks back on their time at WP Engine as well spent.
- The questions he wants people to answer yes to:
  - Were they proud of the work they did?
  - Did they grow as a person, as well as professionally?
  - Did they have an impact on others' lives, helping the next generation thrive as they were helped?
  - Were they gratified by the important role they played in customers' lives?
  - Did any of it matter?
- He says if the answers are yes, "it was all worth it."

## Companies are mortal; the purpose is the journey

- Jason says the oak tree drops acorns for decades if not centuries, but is eventually returned wholly to the soil.
- "All mighty trees inevitably dissolve into their base elements, to be recombined by and for the next generation."
- He applies this to both companies and lives: their purpose "resides in the journey, not in the end."
- Implication for founders: success is measured by the people helped, the customers served and the work worth being proud of, not by the company lasting forever or by the valuation.
- He says he rarely talks about this future with strangers, preferring to talk about what exists now.
