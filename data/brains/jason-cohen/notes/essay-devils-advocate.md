## Stress-testing your pitch with a "Rude Q&A" instead of waiting for the market to do it

Jason's core idea: before you pitch, launch, or raise money, write down the nastiest questions a smart, hostile critic would ask, then answer them in writing. He borrows the concept of Rude Q&A from Scott Berkun and treats it as the startup equivalent of swinging a heavy bat before stepping up to the plate.

- "Nothing clarifies things quite like a hyperactive, all-knowing, all-seeing, real asshole of a devil's advocate beating the living crap out of you."
- The hardest part is generating the questions, because the best ones target exactly the assumptions you least want to examine.
- Questions should include unfair ones and ones built on wrong information, since reporters, clients and the public ask those too.
- Answers to unfair questions take longer to write because they must be more polite and mature than the question, and must refute its assumptions without being dismissive.
- Jason's advice if you're unhappy with some answers: that's a good sign. It means you're being honest, so keep looking for better answers and discuss the hard ones with other people.

## What the exercise forces you to sharpen

Jason argues the value isn't only testing whether the idea survives. Being grilled removes the room to be vague about the things that matter most:

- Marketing messages: no space for generic claims.
- Target customer profile: you must know exactly who it's for.
- Feature set: you must defend what's in and what's missing.
- How you differ from competitors and where you are strongest.
- Exactly what customer pain you solve and why customers should love you.
- Company values, and which compromises you are or aren't willing to make.

His point is that a hostile question leaves no leniency for "we're better, faster, more customer-focused" answers.

## A starter list of hostile startup questions

Jason offers these as generic seeds, to be made more specific to your own business:

- Your biggest competitor just dropped their price to $0. How do you justify your price?
- If your idea is good, copycats will come from incumbents and startups, funded and bootstrapped, large and small. How will you persevere?
- If the economy stays bad for two more years, how do you survive?
- "The last thing anyone needs is *another damn tool*." What's the overwhelming reason to look at you?
- Competitor X is doing better than you. What are you going to do about it?
- If you raised prices 50%, would any customers stay? How do you get 10x more of those customers?
- What are the top three features your competitor has that you lack, and what's your plan for the next six months?
- How can you call yourself an expert after only a year?
- There are only two of you. What if someone leaves?
- What's the single most important thing to accomplish in the next 12 months, and how will you do it? (Do you even know what it is?)
- Your growth will top out because of high cancellation. How do you fix so many people disliking you even after buying?
- What are three tangible, undeniable ways your product saves more money than it costs and more time than it consumes?
- Truly great products and companies are rare. What makes you think you have what it takes?
- Thousands of consultants claim high quality, on time, on budget, happy customers. What makes you different?

## Make the questions specific: the "why read this blog" example

Jason says generic questions are only a start; you need specific attacks. For his own blog he sharpened "why should I read it?" into: there are already too many startup blogs, those are far more popular, their authors more famous with excellent advice, and Smart Bear's success is nothing like Steve Blank's. Why should anyone listen to you?

His answer, in two parts:

1. **Every piece of advice has a counter-argument, so the goal is to find advice that fits your context.** Someone must point out that he made millions with poor graphic design and too many features, against Jason Fried's "simple design is better than complex features." Likewise, against Seth Godin's "1,000 true fans" idea, someone should say it isn't true in practice. Readers need to match advice to their own goals, personality, constraints and strengths, so a blog sourced from his specific experience helps people become a better version of who they already are.
2. **Impact doesn't depend on inventing the idea.** Think of your best teacher or mentor: they didn't invent the subject. What changes lives is the exposition, the turn of phrase, and the accidental time and place where someone hears an idea for the seventh time and is finally ready for it. He says he has received thousands of messages saying his writing changed someone or was "what I really needed to hear today," and suggests that may be the definition of life's purpose.

He notes the answer was written in 2010, just before founding WP Engine (which became a hyper-growth unicorn), and says it's more instructive to see how he answered before earning that credibility.
