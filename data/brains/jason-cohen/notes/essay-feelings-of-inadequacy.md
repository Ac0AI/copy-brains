## Why Jason says driven people feel inadequate (and why the comparison is invalid)

- Jason uses Erica Douglass as the example. She bootstrapped a hosting company (Simpli) and sold it for $1M, which is success by any definition.
- After watching WP Engine's marketing push for a few months, she concluded she "must have really sucked at growing my business" because WP Engine was growing faster than her company had.
- Jason says "all driven people do this to themselves, and it has to stop." He frames it as a problem for himself and for the reader, not just for her.
- Arguing that her definition of success is wrong (growth rate and gross margin versus happiness or fulfillment) wouldn't help. Type-A obsessive people don't respond emotionally to that argument, even when they accept it intellectually.

## Self-flagellation is rationalization, not cause

- Jason points out the criteria are gerrymandered. Erica values "growth rate" but not "monthly cash loss," and values "gross profit margin" but not "percent ownership."
- He argues you could credibly say she is intentionally selecting only the metrics that make her feel inadequate and ignoring the rest.
- His conclusion: her stated explanation "is a rationalization rather than a cause." The real source is Impostor Syndrome, which he says is well documented in studies.
- He notes that by definition, high achievers using self-invented, impossible yardsticks "always come up short."

## Even on a pure money-and-power definition, the comparison is wrong

- Jason concedes there's nothing wrong with defining success by money, power and speed of accumulation. His point is that the comparison fails on those terms too.
- His thought experiment: start WP Engine in 2002, alongside Erica's Simpli, with WordPress around v2.5 (circa 2008) to give his company a boost.
- Why it would have gone badly:
  - WordPress was far less prevalent, so customers couldn't be found "in droves."
  - No global network of WordCamps to reach potential customers.
  - He's not a hardware geek, and cloud hosting didn't exist, so he'd have to build, configure and maintain hardware, with false starts and legacy configs.
  - He'd be in Austin, not San Jose, with roughly 100x fewer customers at meetups.
  - No Twitter or Facebook, and little blogging culture, to let zealous fans spread the word.
  - No prior startup successes or moneyed contacts from blogging and speaking, so no way to raise money the way WP Engine did.
  - A post-bubble recession, with companies not paying a premium for WordPress-specific hosting.
- Likely outcome: failure, or painfully slow growth at about 1/10th the income, while holding a day job. He'd then claim to be profitable and to have Product/Market Fit, eventually realize that's a cope, and write a blog post about feeling inadequate next to someone like Erica.

## Businesses have no symmetries

- Jason borrows the physics idea of symmetry: an experiment gives identical results if you move it elsewhere, provided all influences move with it. He notes that constant linear motion symmetry, combined with the constant speed of light, gives rise to special relativity, which he describes as "half of modern physics."
- Physics symmetries aren't always obvious. Rotation is symmetric at a fixed angle but not under continuous rotation. A mirror is almost symmetric, but not quite.
- For businesses, "where two businesses are symmetrical: Nowhere!" They differ in time, location, market, features, founding team, talents and gaps, investors, money raised, social following and everything else.
- If there were even near-symmetries, you could use them to predict success and failure. He says even professional investors manage that so rarely it's hard to say they "predicted" anything.

## Humans invent symmetry, and how to use it healthily

- People expect, seek and invent symmetry, craving patterns and connections. Pitches like "We're Netflix for art" imply a symmetry that rarely holds: same site structure, pricing and delivery in a new market.
- In detail, each company has its own execution, route to market, margins, opportunity, competition, technology and strengths to leverage, so each is a new, unpredictable challenge.
- Healthy use: apparent symmetry can spark an idea ("That's a creative marketing campaign, maybe we could try something similar").
- Unhealthy use: seeing symmetry that doesn't exist, then building a straw man to fuel self-loathing.

## The practical takeaway

- Jason says "stop comparing yourself to others and just be as good as you can" is wise but useless for high achievers, who can't follow it.
- His substitute advice: "Stop comparing yourself to something that is objectively incomparable." In his words, doing otherwise "is just irrational."
- He credits Erica for being willing to admit this publicly to help others suffering from the same delusion.
