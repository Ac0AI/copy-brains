## Choosing between two good options by asking which is more fun to build

Jason treats a choice like "raise money or keep bootstrapping" as a case where neither answer is wrong. His advice is that once both options are valid and rational, you should stop analyzing and pick on a different basis. He also says to never let anyone make you feel guilty for not choosing the other path.

- He says "there's no wrong answer" doesn't answer the question, so a tiebreaker is needed.
- He applies this to a founder with thirty paying customers, bootstrapped so far, who sees a big opportunity to raise money but could also continue as is.
- That founder already knew the pros and cons. Jason does not re-list them.

## Why outside advice can't settle a major business decision

Jason argues the answer cannot come from other people.

- While bootstrapping WP Engine, he constantly heard that he was hamstrung by not taking investment.
- After raising a Series A, a different set of people were disappointed that he had "sold out."
- His verdict on who was right: "Both. Neither." That is why you cannot look externally for the answer.
- Other people can still help by asking you more and better questions. But "only you can decide the answer."

## Indecision has a daily cost

Jason's case for deciding fast:

- Every day of delay costs you. Dithering moves you toward neither goal.
- It drains time and energy.
- It blocks other decisions: spending money, hiring, lifestyle, stories for the press, features, "everything."
- Since you need a fast decision and others can't give you the answer, that "leaves you, alone."

## The tiebreaker: which company sounds like the most fun to build?

Jason's yardstick is fun, enjoyment and fulfillment. He suggests asking yourself:

- Do you like working alone or with a very small team? Does hiring ten people in the next 12 months sound exhilarating or depressing?
- Do you like having a hand in everything, or would you rather be evangelizing and leading?
- Do you enjoy slowly growing a backyard garden, or would you have more fun "strapping on an afterburner" to see how much impact you can have?
- Do you like being good at what you're good at now, or do you want to learn a completely different kind of business?

## Test your feelings about bootstrapping versus funding

Jason offers a gut-check on identity:

- Are you proud of bootstrapping? Do you enjoy telling a funded founder you're in control, don't need help, and have a product so good people actually pay for it?
- Or does bootstrapping make you feel small, knowing you're not growing, reaching or earning as much as you could?
- Whichever reaction is stronger points to your answer.

## Fulfillment is undervalued in company decisions

Jason's broader point is that most founders let joy and fulfillment barely enter the calculus, and that is a mistake.

- His question: why build a company, or anything else, if it's not fulfilling?
- A choice between two valid options lets you pick on something "we all really ought to be valuing" but often lack the means or courage to value.
- He tells the reader: "But you can."

## Hindsight won't tell you which was right

Jason also offers a way to take the pressure off:

- Five years later you still won't know which option would have been "right."
- Even if your chosen path "failed" in some objective sense, you can't know the other would have been better, whether in money, fulfillment or happiness.
- So optimize for the choice you can enjoy making and living with now.
