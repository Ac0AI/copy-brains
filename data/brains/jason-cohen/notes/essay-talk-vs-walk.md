## The Talk vs Walk chart: what it is and how to run it

Jason co-invented this strategic exercise at WP Engine to engage both Marketing and Product. Items are plotted on a chart with a vertical axis "Talk: How much/loudly do we claim this" and a horizontal axis "Walk: How well do we pay this off in practice." The question underneath is "You talk the talk, but do you walk the walk?"

- It works on the early-stage company exaggerating on its home page ("Fake it 'till you make it").
- It also works on the venerable company that under-claims world-class qualities because they feel second-nature.
- It analyzes current state and also surfaces actions for both Marketing and Product to sell better and build better.

## Write-storm the cards, don't brainstorm

Jason has everyone independently write sticky notes and place them on the chart, on a whiteboard such as Miro, LucidChart or Google Slides. He may allow several days so people can think when their brains work best.

- **Do not brain-storm one-by-one.** Live brainstorming follows the fastest and loudest people, filters out ideas that take longer than a few minutes, and rules out shower "ah-ha" moments.
- He cites Leigh Thompson in _Creative Conspiracy_: "There is not a single published study in which a face-to-face brainstorming group outperforms a [write-storming] group."
- Write-storming also restores creativity over Zoom because it is independent.
- Put direct quotes in double quotes to flag "we really do say this!"

**Sources for cards:**
- Things you say: public website, ads, presentations and webinars, employee quotes in the media, sales materials, knowledge-base articles and copy/paste shortcuts.
- Things you do but don't talk about: systems you maintain, recent sprint work, long-term planning documents, and prompts like "What are we really good at?" and "What technology assets or human capabilities have we built up over time?"
- Things you neither say nor do (hardest): feature-request backlogs from customers, support, sales, marketing, product and engineers; differentiating things competitors say or do; your own big initiatives not yet started.

## Debating placement and the three kinds of disagreement

Debating where each card goes is about one-third of the value. Merge duplicates into one card. If duplicates were scattered across the chart, that signals a discussion is worth having. Skip discussion when agreement is immediate.

1. **Lack of knowledge.** People disagree about what is actually said where ("we say this in sales but not on the website"; "we used to, before the website revamp"). If it varies, place the card in the middle and add a comment recording the detail for later.
2. **Disagreement on terminology.** If you can't agree internally on what a phrase means, customers and new employees are confused too.
3. **Disagreement on target persona.** Some customers would say you walk the walk, others not.

**The "Enterprise-Grade Security" example at WP Engine:**
- Everyone agreed it was high on talk. Walk placements ranged from the middle to far right.
- Evidence for far right: customers include some of the largest security companies and financial institutions, multiple accreditations, security products at every layer of the stack, third-party pen-tests for every new product and periodically for all, annual company-wide social-engineering training, and enforced written security policies.
- One person placed it mid-chart because of a few backlog items that would harden one corner of the platform, though there had never been an incident from it.
- The group concluded that great security isn't defined by an empty backlog. Nearly all software updates contain security patches. Fixing things that aren't broken, thinking steps ahead and preventing incidents is what makes you secure, so the ticket belongs far right.
- Result: engineers got a better mental model of "what security means," and marketers got more stories to tell.

**Handling persona splits:**
- Place the card at a weighted location. If 80% of customers by revenue say fully walking the walk and 20% say low, place it at 80%, with a comment explaining.
- Or split the card, e.g. "Fast UI (for customers <100 properties)" and "Fast UI (for customers with ≥100 properties)."
- If many cards split, make a separate chart per persona.
- His example: a UI is fast for most, but 2% of customers with huge data see it crawl, and they may be reference customers or top payers.

## Actions by quadrant

Most of the value is in acting on the chart. For each card, ask whether to shift it. Each quadrant has a different status-quo interpretation.

**Quadrant A: Exaggerations (high talk, low walk)**
- Do nothing, but ensure it isn't unethical, e.g. a nonexistent medical benefit or charging for a feature that doesn't work.
- Shift → (live up to it): the message resonates and is critical, so build and ship specific things in the next six months.
- Shift ↓ (don't write checks you can't cash): you attract wrong-fit customers who waste support time, cancel anyway, and give no useful product feedback because they aren't your target persona. Stop saying it.

**Quadrant B: The trusted brand (high talk, high walk)**
- Trust is earned by repeatedly keeping promises; this is your brand and a strength.
- Do nothing: incremental improvement won't win more customers (Kano "must-be" or "indifferent"). Track it as a KPI, promise a minimum threshold, and invest elsewhere.
- Shift ↑: more confident positioning, advertising, content marketing, sales emphasis, product UI highlights. Possibly emphasize [one strength above all others](https://longform.asmartbear.com/one-benefit/).
- Shift → (double down): often wise early in a product's life, when it's hard to have even one special thing. Only works if customers value more of it (Kano "attractive" or "performance").
- Shift ↓: only if the company is pivoting its target or positioning.

**Quadrant C: Speak no evil (low talk, low walk)**
- List known weaknesses and things you say "no" to, especially common office topics or things a competitor successfully talks and walks.
- Do nothing is correct for nearly everything: turning a weakness into a strength is expensive and high-risk, so execute on current promises and double down on strengths.
- Shift → only if it is truly strategic and critical to succeed in the market.
- Don't shift ↑ (talk) before → (walk), because:
  - It tips your hand to competitors so they can react first.
  - It sets wrong expectations and trains customers to disbelieve you when you can finally pay it off.
  - Talking first can "freeze the market," but only if your leadership is so strong that a press release changes buyer behavior. He calls that unlikely.

**Quadrant D: The strong, silent type (low talk, high walk)**
- Do nothing may be right: trade secrets (though you can brag that a trade secret is part of the magic), or boring internal operations (though customers may be comforted by operational sophistication, and a tech blog helps recruiting).
- Shift ↑: even as a secondary or tertiary message, arm sales with material, test it in advertising, or build a landing page for the subset of customers who would be impressed. His rule: "If you are excellent, why not get credit for it?"

## Adding competitors to the analysis

Build a Talk/Walk chart for a competitor. Copy your cards onto their board (many will land in Quadrant C), then add cards for them, and add those cards to your own chart too.

**Prompts for generating competitor cards:**
- What they emphasize in big or bold type on the home, pricing and about pages.
- Main features on their features page.
- Where you lose to them in sales: legitimately great versus just good at selling.
- Where you beat them: deserved versus just good at claiming.
- What their customers say (Twitter, testimonials) and what your customers say when complaining or requesting features.
- What an investor pitch on their behalf would say, and how much is true versus aspirational.
- Cultural or moral principles their founder or CEO espouses.

## Reading competitor strengths against your board

Place the competitor's strengths on your board in a unique color and evaluate whether you also talk about and do each one.

- **A: You're exaggerating against their strength.** You both claim it, only they deliver. Prospects say "Yeah, everyone claims to have X, but only P really does X." Before racing to match, ask whether you have the long-term will-power, time and skill to catch up to where they'll be in a year, and whether it pays off. Usually the answer is no: move the card down, stop claiming it, and invest in your own Quadrant B differentiators.
- **B: Table-stakes.** Maintain them but don't over-invest at the expense of differentiators or of converting key weaknesses. Your comparative advantages are the Quadrant B cards the competitor doesn't share. If you have few, inventing some may be the most strategic thing you can do.
- **C: Their differentiation against you, and that's OK.** The only problem is lacking your own [unique strengths](https://longform.asmartbear.com/worse-but-unique/). Don't try to become strong where they are strong, except for at most one or two truly must-win items. Often you'll only reach mediocre, and it must be [treated as a Rock](https://longform.asmartbear.com/rocks-pebbles-sand/).
- **D: Don't give them a free pass.** You're as good but they take all the credit. You'll probably just match rather than differentiate, but since you've done the work, don't concede an uncontested win in sales calls.
