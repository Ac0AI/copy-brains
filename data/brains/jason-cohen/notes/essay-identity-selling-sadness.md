## Selling a startup feels like losing your identity, not just a company

- Jason says almost all founders feel deep, prolonged sadness after selling, even after an outrageous success. He felt it himself while faxing page 34 of 72 of the Smart Bear sale paperwork, then "sadness. A profound sadness," like "the punch-in-the-stomach of discovering your dog was hit by a car."
- His explanation: a barista who sells art on Etsy says "I'm an artist," because that is who she really is, and the barista job is just a means. **A startup founder lacks this distinction between personal identity and work identity.**
- A startup is an obsession, not a "necessary evil" en route to what you really want to do. It is the thing you think about before sleep, the thing that won't let you sleep, and the first thing in your head in the morning. He started a second and eventually a fourth startup "because it's in my DNA and I have to do it."
- Selling is therefore "less like selling your baby and more like selling your own identity. Now you have to find a new one," and there is no guarantee a new one exists.

## Evidence that the post-exit blues are the norm

- Famous cases he cites: Vinay Hiremath (sold Loom for nearly $1B, nearly died in the Himalayas, lost his girlfriend) and Markus "Notch" Persson (sold Minecraft to Microsoft for over $2B, then melted down on Twitter, directionless and socially distant).
- A Columbia Business School study of 22 entrepreneurs who sold: every one of them felt this effect. Some refocused on a new venture, but most took years to find something that replaced the excitement and the identity. Many never found it, many wished they had their companies back, and a few bought them back.
- The same pattern shows up in Olympic athletes ("post-Olympic blues"), and anyone who devotes their identity to a cause that suddenly ends.
- A brain-imaging study (Halko et al., 2017) found entrepreneur-parents' brain activity looking at their own company's brand resembled activity looking at their own children. Jason takes this to mean the attachment, intensity and worry are real.
- He doesn't call it identical to baby blues, but the shared element is a "feeling of loss and mourning": "A piece of yourself has been eviscerated, irrevocably."

## Money and an exit won't answer "what do you really want to do?"

- Selling forces a hard question: if you could do anything, what would you do? What do you really want to be when you really grow up?
- The trap: the startup already was that thing. It's a grinding, risky, irrational adventure nobody picks as the easy path, so you wouldn't have done it unless it was what you wanted.
- Jason warns founders to resist believing millions of dollars will make them fulfilled or happy: "Money doesn't make you happy" is cliché because it's true. You need to understand how to be fulfilled regardless.
- Guilt compounds it: you can't complain about not knowing what to do with time and money most people only dream of.

## Selling is still often right, even when it hurts

- Jason does not conclude that selling is the wrong choice. Like ending a bad relationship with someone you love, "just because it's painful doesn't mean you don't need to do it."
- It's easier to decide if you're saying "I can't wait to start the next thing." It's riskier if your thought is "I don't know what I'll do with myself."
- His honest answer to "what should I do?" is often "it depends," or "every choice is a path of pain."

## The job changes as the company grows, whether or not you sell

- Building a company in year one is completely different from year seven (when he left Smart Bear) or year fourteen (when he left WP Engine). The CEO's job description changes, and so does the company.
- Questions he asks founders to test whether they want to stay:
  - Are you OK with innovation taking a back seat to scalable, mature processes?
  - Are you OK releasing day-to-day control to managers, then releasing the managers to an executive team?
  - Are you OK getting out of Visual Studio, AdWords, the website, live chat and sales calls, and not becoming the meddling micro-manager you hate?
  - Are you OK carrying the livelihoods of dozens or thousands of families instead of "pulling 90 hour weeks" to push code out with a co-founder?
- Successful startups grow into businesses: sustainability, risk-avoidance, HR law, strategic planning, executive meetings. The founder-CEO still steers, but it's "a different sort of ship."

## How Jason's own choice differed at Smart Bear and WP Engine

- At Smart Bear he didn't want to lead a huge company, give up Eclipse and the ability to check in code, manage managers, or work out what hiring, strategy, products, marketing and sales it would take to reach $100M/year.
- So he sold at a good point: before he needed a C_O, but when the company was big enough to produce enough money to cross his "Freedom Line."
- At WP Engine (written early 2013) his ambitions were different. He was the CEO managing managers, setting vision rather than running day-to-day operations, worrying about culture, without SSH access to all the servers, and driving toward a company he believed could generate $100M/year.
- Editor's notes: Heather Brunner joined as CEO later in 2013 and was still leading 10 years on, which he calls a "late-joining founder." WP Engine did reach that revenue at hyper-growth velocity, and by 2023 revenue was many times the $100M figure.
- His takeaway: he will always love writing code and taking a company from $0 to $1M/year, but at that point in his life, running the larger company was who he was, "for reasons unknown even to me." He later developed a personal framework for avoiding burnout and staying with a company long-term.
