## Early traction at wildly successful startups looks like failure, and Jason says you can't tell the difference from inside

- Jason argues that even companies now famous for explosive growth (Facebook, Slack, OpenAI, HubSpot) spent their first years in "torturous chaos" before the growth curve appeared.
- The usual growth charts start at $1M ARR ("year 1"). He asks what those companies looked like before that point, and the answer is: not obviously good.
- His description of the early period: "barely-controlled chaos, awake at 2:43am worrying how to make payroll or whether the numbers will look good enough by next spring to raise another round." Founders are unsure which features to double down on or kill, and stay bright and cheery for press, customers, investors and employees while worried about collapse.
- His summary: "It's always like that." Pessimism is realism, yet optimism is required.

## HubSpot's first years: 3 customers, then 48, then slow linear growth for about three years

- HubSpot is Jason's worked example because its data is public. At the start of 2024 it was at roughly $2B ARR and still growing quickly.
- The curve looks similar at every zoom level, only with smaller absolute numbers, until you reach the very beginning.
- HubSpot ended 2006 with just 3 customers and ended 2007 with only 48. That is less than one new customer per week, for a year.
- It took about two years to reach the first 500 customers. Co-founder Dharmesh Shah has shown a chart of slow linear growth for three years, followed by fast growth for five.
- Jason's point: that early data "sounds downright bad", like a company you might shut down because it is "obviously not working", and it prompts an existential struggle over whether to continue. It was not an obviously bright future validated by a torrent of customers with open wallets.

## Struggling companies and eventual winners feel identical, and no objective measure separates them

- Jason says the same struggle, uncertainty and mess happens at companies that never reach Product/Market Fit and never earn a dime. The company that really should have shut down feels the same as the one that becomes huge.
- Asked how to tell the difference: "I'm not sure you can." He notes there are questions you can ask yourself, but they are not conclusive.
- His examples of near-identical products with very different outcomes:
  - Basecamp v1.0 versus its knock-offs: all were "the tool we wish we had, so we built it ourselves," all simple, web-based, usable by non-technical people, and missing half the features customers wanted.
  - StackOverflow versus twenty copycat Q&A sites and thousands of forums, and why Quora could still stand out.
- He lists what does not explain the difference: feature comparison charts, SWOT analysis, financial metrics, social media, whether they raised money, whether the founders went to Stanford, and whether the market or competition already existed.
- Even his own Product/Market Fit roadmap has exceptions. He says founder-fit is needed, yet the AirBnB founders had no hotel or hospitality experience and the Uber founders had no car-service experience. He says there are exceptions to every "rule."

## Real founders who looked like they were failing for 1-2+ years

- Peldi (Balsamiq Mockups): blogged a bit but had no head-start online presence like StackOverflow, English is his second language, he lives in Italy away from American startup culture, and he was an Adobe lifer with no bootstrapping experience. His year-1 (2008) revenue chart started slowly. Jason asks how he felt in the first six months, noting the revenue was one-time rather than recurring, so "it could stop at any time."
- Nathan Barry (ConvertKit): in January 2015, more than two years in, there was almost nothing to show in MRR, before growth took off.
- Tim Bennetto (Pallyy): two years of making less than $3k/mo in MRR, and then it changed.
- Jason's framing: each of these founders was in the same emotional spot as someone who is actually failing, with no way to know it from the inside.

## The same pattern in books: rejection counts before success

- Robert Pirsig's _Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance_ was rejected by 121 publishers, then published by William Morrow & Company in 1974. It stayed on best-seller lists for decades, with total sales of over 5 million copies. Jason asks how you keep going after "just" 100 rejections, or even 20.
- Dr. Seuss was rejected by 27 publishers before _Green Eggs and Ham_ (which has sold 8 million copies).
- Stephen King's _Carrie_ was rejected 30 times before Doubleday took a chance.
- J.K. Rowling was rejected 12 times. She said "I was the biggest failure I knew," then went on to sell 450 million books and be worth over $1B.
- He quotes Michael Jordan: "I've missed more than 9000 shots in my career. I've lost almost 300 games. 26 times, I've been trusted to take the game-winning shot and missed. I've failed over and over and over again in my life. And that is why I succeed."

## How Jason frames the pain: it proves nothing either way

- Jason is explicit that persistence can be in vain. "The vast majority of authors never succeed. Nor startups. Nor new product lines inside established companies. Most of the time, it doesn't work; that's the brutal truth."
- But he says the following feelings do not mean you are failing: being in over your head, barely able to will yourself to continue, being in the dark, working yourself toward an early grave, sliding two steps back for every one forward, nothing ever being good enough, friends and family not understanding why you are turning yourself inside out with no apparent progress, not enjoying the journey, and doubting you are even capable.
- His conclusion: "These things mean you haven't yet succeeded, but neither do they prove that you're failing. It's always like this, until it isn't."
- Practical takeaway: do not treat a slow, painful first one to three years, or a flat early growth chart, as evidence that the company should stop. Those conditions look the same at successes and at failures, so they carry no information on their own.
