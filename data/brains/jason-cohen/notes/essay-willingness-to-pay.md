## Utility vs. Love vs. Coercion: three kinds of willingness-to-pay

Jason argues that "willingness-to-pay" (WTP) is the maximum price a customer would have paid, and that economists treat any transaction as proof that WTP > Price. The gap between WTP and price is "Consumer Surplus." The gap between price and cost is "Profit." His ideal business "delivers $4 of value, charges $2, and costs them $1 to do it."

He dislikes the word "willingness" because it hides intent and ability to pay. Examples he gives:
- Martin Shkreli raised Daraprim from $13 to $750 per pill (treatment cost from about $1,000 to $63,000). Patients had no choice, which isn't really "willing."
- Gas prices rise at once when oil rises and fall slowly when oil falls.
- Cable TV bundles exist because consumers are not "willing" to pay for every channel.
- "Willing" is not "able": many people would be willing to pay $1,000 for a top phone, but few are able.

Genuine willingness does exist, and he says it "creates the best, most durable, most profitable businesses." Customers are happy to pay more, become evangelists, and ignore competitors who compete only on features and price.

## The Love category: what builds allyship

Jason's first category of WTP is Love. The result is "allyship": customers are glad to do business with you, root for your profit, advocate publicly, don't consider competitors, and would accept a small price rise.

Drivers he lists:
- **Mission:** supporting a change bigger than the customer.
- **Reciprocity:** giving before taking, exceptional service, being deeply human.
- **Exceptional design and quality:** joy in use, and relief from reliability.
- **Personal identification:** the brand becomes part of the customer's own brand.
- **Culture:** treating employees and vendors well (counter-examples: Walmart and Amazon).
- **Transparency:** Buffer is the extreme case, sharing salaries, strategy and monthly revenue.
- **Social or environmental impact:** fair, sustainable practices.
- **Community:** members teach, support each other, and form a tribe.
- **Ecosystem:** members earn more money or prestige than they would outside the group.

Examples he uses:
- He pays more for Anker chargers and power strips because of their quality, with no lock-in and no recurring revenue.
- TOMS (One for One shoes) and Patagonia win on authentic missions. Patagonia has a formal policy to bail out employees arrested while protesting peacefully.
- Skechers copied TOMS exactly, including the style, a name ("BOBS") and the altruism. Consumers were outraged and Skechers cancelled the line after 24 hours. His point is that the strategy was individual to TOMS, so being publicly visible and copyable didn't matter. TOMS has weaknesses (poor service, shoes developing holes) but wins on its individual story.
- Hollow Knight (released 2017, made mostly by three people) is sold at $15 with four free expansions. Fans beg them to charge more and they don't. Jason says rabid fans generate millions of purchases, and that impact is far larger than closing the WTP/price gap.

## The Utility category: fair exchange of value

Utility WTP gives a "fair exchange of value": the product is useful and not excessively painful, and it is "easier to stay than to leave," with no particular desire to leave. Drivers he lists:
- Cheap, even if functionality is low.
- Integrations, system-of-record status, and training investment (each makes switching costly).
- Market-share leadership as social proof.
- Location (airport coffee) and convenience (grocery delivery).
- Simplicity, quality, and risk reduction.
- Unique functionality.
- Onboarding experience: he cites ProfitWell data that ease and reciprocity in onboarding raise WTP.
- Familiarity with a workflow.

Utility grows existing customers and is neutral-to-positive on attracting new ones. Organizations buy more seats and more infrastructure as they grow, and at scale that internal growth outweighs new-customer growth. But a customer buying ten more JIRA seats isn't advocating for it on forums, and may still try a new competitor. His example is Linear, which built a rabid fan base on UX: JIRA users could imagine switching to Linear, but "it's laughable" to imagine a Linear user pushing to switch to JIRA.

## The Coercion category: why it leaves you exposed

Coercion keeps customers paying against their will, and the result is "adversarial." Jason's list:
- Contract lock-in and data lock-in.
- Effective monopoly (including purchased ones, like Uber subsidizing rides for tens of billions to drive out rivals, then raising prices) and effective price-fixing.
- Middle-man positions, such as ticket resale at 10x.
- Bundle-stuffing: packing in things the customer doesn't want to charge more.
- "Scale Anti-Pricing": raising prices once an installation is at scale, because switching cost is so high.
- Predatory pricing: pricing below cost, funded by another business unit or VCs, until competitors are gone.
- Patents used to block normal competition.
- Corporate policy (the only approved vendor) and government fiat or regulation.

Consequences he describes:
- Coerced customers are "allied with competitors." They say "just give me an excuse," and will build a case that switching pays for itself ten-fold.
- Renewal time is when "you should be very afraid."
- When someone asks on Twitter what to use, your customers answer "we use X, but don't make the same mistake."
- You are exposed to disruption "even by mediocre competition."

## How Love drives growth

Love produces "inexpensive, non-linear growth" because customers are allies:
- Repeat purchases with no extra marketing or sales cost.
- Word-of-mouth: fans answer "what should I buy?" questions, and the product ranks first on review sites. The effect compounds as the customer base grows.
- Retention against better or cheaper competitors. There is a limit (the product eventually has to be good enough), but it carries you through ordinary one-upmanship.
- He points to retention as growth: look at the growth curve of any startup that went from 7%/month cancellation to 2%/month.

## Create more WTP, then split it with the customer

Jason quotes Michael Mauboussin: when you raise WTP, "you're adding value for the consumer, and then figuring out how to split that with the consumer." His order of operations is to create value first, and only then decide how to share it, either by keeping Consumer Surplus for advocacy, retention and growth, or by raising prices.

- Value created via Love or Utility is both sustainable and profitable. Value created via Coercion is "temporary at best."
- The strongest organizations use all three. Apple creates Love (design, personal brand, privacy), Utility (familiarity, convenience, being the center of photos, files and notes) and Coercion (no export from Notes, older devices slowing after mandatory OS upgrades, changing cable connectors every 5-10 years). Apple's net profit margin ranged from 21-26% from 2010 to 2022, which he reads as increased WTP split with customers.

## Why the cold-blooded investor should still pick Love

Jason's thought experiment: two companies identical in product, market, customers, price, costs, revenue, profit and WTP. Company 1's WTP comes only from Love. Company 2's comes only from Coercion. Which grows faster and is the better VC investment over five years?

- He picks Company 1. Its customers help it grow efficiently while competitors can't convert them even with unique features and lower prices.
- Company 2's customers are trying to leave, hoping for a competitor to rescue them, and publicly warning prospects away.
- Company 1 can add Utility or even Coercion later. It is much harder for Company 2 to generate Love from where it is.
- He is clear that Coercion isn't banned: like the Agile Manifesto's "working software over comprehensive documentation," it means the other two are better places to spend your energy.
- His conclusion: "Love beats Coercion, even as cold-blooded, money-grubbing capitalist investor," and it also makes the world better. "So choose Love."

## Related frameworks: moats, engagement and buyer utility

- **Moats:** raising any type of WTP strengthens your ability to capture and keep customers. Network effects appear in all three types: Love (community and ecosystem), Utility (eBay as a functioning marketplace people used because it was useful), and Coercion (insurance "preferred vendor" networks that force you to switch doctors when you switch insurers).
- **Start with Why:** a strong, permeating mission is "impossible for a competitor to destroy." Buffer has a relatively undifferentiated product and pays lower salaries than many can get elsewhere, yet its culture and transparency draw people. TruthSocial has downtime and slow innovation but a rabid fanbase because of mission and community.
- **Kano model:** Love maps to "Delight," Utility to "Performance," Coercion to "Inverse."
- **Blue Ocean buyer utility:** productivity, simplicity, convenience and risk map to Utility; fun & image and environmental friendliness map to Love. He says "customer productivity" is too broad because it's "an order of magnitude more important to increase value" than to save cost.
- **Engagement metrics:** in a multi-year Facebook experiment, sending fewer, higher-quality notifications first lowered app usage but raised satisfaction. After a year, usage rose above its prior level and stayed higher. His takeaway is that real satisfaction produces more long-run engagement, and traditional growth hacking did not find that solution.
