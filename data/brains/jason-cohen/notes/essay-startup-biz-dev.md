## Why startup biz dev proposals fail: the "1% for one month" math

Jason (founder of WP Engine, which serves millions of websites for hundreds of thousands of paying customers) gets weekly emails from startups proposing a "win-win" deal. In his words, "approximately zero have resulted in a successful deal."

- The standard pitch: the startup has fresh product, the big company has distribution. Trade innovation for distribution.
- The big company emails its customer base, generates "easy sales at no cost," and the revenue is split.
- Jason says this is never accepted. The startup's equation leaves out a cost to the big company: its reputation.

## Why distribution-for-innovation pitches look attractive but aren't

- Jason defines distribution as "getting in front of potential customers," and calls it the hardest thing for a young startup.
- Big companies have solved distribution (his proof: 100,000 paying customers). They struggle with innovation because scale makes it hard to keep building things people love while delivering quickly with extra requirements.
- Each side's weakness maps onto the other's strength, which "feels like the kernel of a deal."
- The startup assumes big companies are hunting for excuses to up-sell their base and lack up-sell material. That assumption leads to the doomed proposal.

## The WP Engine math: a plugin sale is worth roughly 8 hours of revenue

- Typical inbound pitch: a WordPress plugin or theme maker with a $20, $50 or $200 product wants to market it to WP Engine customers, framed as a service to those customers.
- Jason runs deliberately optimistic numbers: 1% of the customer base converts, and WP Engine gets $50 per sale (higher than the typical offer).
- WP Engine's 2014 plans ran from $29/mo to $99/mo or more, so he assumes an average of $50/mo (the true average is higher; this keeps the math simple).
- Result: 1% of customers each give one extra month of revenue. That is a 1% revenue boost for one month only. The next month there is no boost.
- "By definition a company earns 1% of their monthly revenue in about 8 hours." A one-time 8-hour revenue bump isn't interesting to a company of any size. It's "not enough to even discuss or negotiate."
- The price of that bump is spamming the customer base.

## The test: grow the big company the way it already makes money

Jason's core question for any biz dev proposal:

- "How can the smaller company make money for the larger company *in the manner that the larger company already makes money?*"
- Restated: how can you grow the larger company in a way that matches its existing business model?
- Applied to WP Engine: how can you create new hosting customers for WP Engine?

## Worked example: send your customers to the big company

- Instead of selling to WP Engine's customers, the plugin or theme maker asks their own customers whether they'd like hosting too, maybe with a limited-time discount.
- A couple of dozen new hosting customers per month can "move the needle." The reason is not the size of overall growth. It's that the revenue repeats month after month.
- Even a small customer may refer others, and Jason says WP Engine can see that value even if it doesn't track it.
- That arrangement makes it easy to justify announcing to WP Engine customers that there is a new partner "optimized for our hosting platform, both in code and in business," worth serious consideration.
- His summary: bring customers to a big company, even a few, rather than "milking their existing customers for a sliver of revenue."

## Criteria for a deal that can actually work

- Pursue a deal that brings the big company new customers, or one that produces material revenue in a way that fits how it already earns.
- Jason's threshold for "material": 2% or more of the larger company's top-line revenue, and repeating, not one-time.
- He admits this is hard to find, "that's why most biz dev deals don't work."
- Meeting these criteria is what makes a real win-win possible, and might "mint your company."
