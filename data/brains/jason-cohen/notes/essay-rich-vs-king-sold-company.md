## Why Jason sold Smart Bear: the "Rich and King" hybrid

- Jason sold Smart Bear in December 2007, after a buyer offered him enough money that he "never [has] to work again."
- He used Noam Wasserman's "Rich or King" framework: "Rich" founders are in it for the money, "King" founders for lifestyle and identity.
- Wasserman says successful founders pick one up front and stay consistent. Jason says he mixed the two and it worked.
- His goal was always "Rich," specifically acquisition. He achieved it by running the company as if the goal were "King."
- He doesn't see a contradiction: a long-term, sustainable business is exactly the kind that gets acquired.
- He tells people the hard part is that "King" companies like 37signals and FogCreek would never sell, which is rational for them. His own choice was rational too.

## Profit as the rule behind every decision

- Everyone Jason hired was told up front: "we're here to make money, and if someone offers to buy the company someday, I'm going to sell it, and all of us will make money."
- "Profit was the rule behind every choice we made." The end goal was acquisition, but he argues the best way to get acquired is to be profitable.
- Why profit matters to a buyer:
  - It proves the business is operating well.
  - It validates the market.
  - It makes minimum valuation easy.
  - The buyer converts balance-sheet cash into bottom-line P&L, which every large company wants.
- The biggest benefit: profit means you don't need to sell. He argues you have little negotiating power in any deal unless you can happily walk away.
- He notes (editor's note) this applies to small self-funded companies, which are most attractive as cash machines. Other companies sell on growth or daily active users.

## Building a genuine company while aiming to sell

- Jason says he would only be happy building a great company, which meant:
  - A product that solves a real pain.
  - White-glove customer service, with "tech support" as the only sales force.
  - Leaving the world a little better than he found it.
  - Employees who are smart, get things done, and are trusted with any decision.
- He openly admits he also wanted the ego rewards of running a company:
  - Saying "I run my own company" at parties.
  - A free book that got popular enough that people asked him to sign it (the joke was that signing doubled its value).
  - Walking a tradeshow floor like "Norm on Cheers," knowing everyone.
  - Speaking at venues like the Business of Software Conference.
  - Using Smart Bear's success to give his blog credibility.

## The Freedom Line: why crossing it matters more than how far

- Jason describes a non-linear relationship between cash in personal savings and financial freedom.
- There is a "Freedom Line" where savings alone fund a reasonably lavish lifestyle, freeing you from restrictions on life, family and career.
- Two observations:
  1. Crossing from left to right of the line changes your life fundamentally, giving freedom to do what makes you happy, forever.
  2. Once you cross it, it matters little how far past it you go. $100m versus $10m is a different lifestyle, but nowhere near as critical as crossing the line.
- Implication: it almost didn't matter what the offer was, so long as it was big enough to cross the line.

## The Box Game: why not wait for a bigger exit

- The common argument against selling: the company was growing 100% year over year and profitable, so waiting a year would double revenue and make it "six times more valuable" (assuming a 3x revenue valuation, his 2009 ballpark for a growing software company).
- Jason's counter is the Box Game:
  - Box A holds $10. Box B has a 50% chance of $20 and a 50% chance of nothing. Most people pick B, because the stakes are trivial and it's more exciting.
  - Change it: Box A holds $10,000,000. Box B has a 50% chance of $20,000,000 and 50% of nothing. You pick A.
- The two boxes are statistically equal. The choice measures your attitude toward risk, not math or intelligence.
- Rule: a chance of moving further is not worth giving up certainty of a life-altering event, namely crossing the Freedom Line.

## Evidence the risk was real: the 2008 crash

- Jason says the economic crash in 2008 made the correctness of his choice "painfully clear." Holding out for "another year and far more money" would have meant finding an empty box.
- He cites a case he knows firsthand (company unnamed), offered a deal at the same time as him:
  - The founder wanted to roll the dice and delayed the buyer.
  - Two quarters passed and revenue failed to grow, so the buyer cancelled the deal.
  - Months later, with a recession in sight, the founder went back willing to take a low offer. The buyer refused.
- He points to other founders who passed on a deal in 2007 and sold for a fraction of it in 2012.
- He adds that running a company can get very hard in the later years, and that most founders are in his position rather than a "King" one.

## What the sale bought him

- Since December 2007 he has the freedom to work on any project he wants for the rest of his life.
- He no longer worries about bills, debt, housing, or paying for any college his daughter chooses.
- He can stay home with his wife and new baby girl as long as he wants.
- His summary on money: they say it can't buy time and memories, but "in the sense of securing that freedom, it can."
- He notes that 100% of the negative reactions to the sale ("sell-out," "how could you sell your baby") came from people who had never started a company.
