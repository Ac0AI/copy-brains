## Why hiring a consultant at $30/hour barely pays

Jason argues that the "nominal" cost of an employee is far below the real cost per billable hour. He walks through a $60,000/year hire (2,000 working hours, so $30/hour nominal) and adjusts step by step:

- Subtract 10 US federal holidays (240 working days instead of 250): about $31/hour.
- Add roughly 15% employment tax (Medicare, Social Security), taking the annual cost from $60,000 to $69,000: about $36/hour.
- Employees bill about 36 hours a week, not 40, which loses roughly two days a month (18 working days/month): about $40/hour.
- Dentist visits, waiting for the A/C repair person and a sick day cost about two more days (16 working days/month): about $45/hour.
- Gaps between projects: at least 10% of time, often 2-3 weeks of downtime between large gigs (14 working days/month): about $51/hour.
- Career development (conferences, open source, learning new tech) is another 10% (12 working days/month): about $60/hour.

His conclusion: "The true cost of an employee is double the nominal cost." Billing a "$30/hour" employee at $60 only breaks even. You need roughly $100/hour to make any profit.

## Why you can't just re-bill at a higher rate

- Clients don't do the math above. They judge "fair" by the person's nominal rate plus a bit of profit for you, which caps what you can re-bill before they feel ripped off.
- Even at $100/hour you earn about $40/hour, or $80,000/year, on that employee.
- Jason's counter: if your employee can bill at $100/hour, you could bill at $150/hour yourself. Working 20 hours a week on average at that rate clears $150,000 a year with no hiring or managing.

## Scaling a consulting company is worse than it looks

Five employees looks like $300,000/year in profit, but scaling adds time and cost:

- Keeping 3-6 projects running year-round is a full-time pipeline job that falls on the owner.
- Turnover means you are permanently hiring, and it is hard to time new hires with departures.
- Six people need office space, or a real travel budget for quarterly get-togethers.
- You can no longer also manage projects, client relationships and internal product work, so you need a project manager, salesperson or office manager, paid out of profit.
- None of the new work is fun or creative. "It's drudgery, and it's all on you."

The so-called $300k often shrinks to about $150k, and you are now doing things you hate.

## Charge more, and watch discounts

- Any extra amount you charge goes straight to profit, so even a small rate increase matters at year end.
- Charging much more usually pushes out your existing client base. The new clients tend to be bigger, more dysfunctional companies with 47-page Master Service Agreements and shifting requirements, which is a different life from fun projects for entrepreneurs.
- Discounts cut the other way. A 10% pre-pay discount comes straight off the bottom line.
- Better pre-pay tactic: raise prices by P%, then offer a P% discount for pre-payment. Cash-rich customers pay the same as before, and others pay more in exchange for delayed payment.

## Bill more hours

- Billing even 5 more hours per week significantly increases profitability.
- Employees see extra hours as unpaid overtime on a fixed salary, so give them a reason. Jason's suggestion is to split overtime billings with them: it is pure profit to you and a meaningful bonus to them.
- Review hours weekly so nobody consistently falls behind 40 billable hours.

## Why a consulting firm's side product usually fails

Jason notes that 37signals, Fog Creek and Pivotal Labs grew products out of consulting, but hundreds if not thousands of firms never ship theirs. Reasons:

- Turning an internal tool into a user-friendly product means drudgery (onboarding, docs, UI, installers, password reset, fixing 200 workaround bugs), and it is no one's job.
- No one markets or sells it, and a roomful of consultants may lack the skills. Hoping "social media word of mouth" delivers users does not work.
- Attention is spread across five kinda-cool projects, so none gets finished. "Lack of focus kills."
- Billable hours always beat product work, so the product stays second-class.
- Assuming a market exists because the tool is "great for us." Usually there isn't one.

His fix is to treat the product as a client:

- List it as a client alongside the others.
- Give it a budget, even a separate bank account (maybe a separate corporation), with the project paying consulting fees (perhaps at cost rather than full rate).
- Schedule and prioritize its hours like any other client.
- If potential income can't justify the cost, stop rather than limp along as a money-losing project.

He still says "ultimately, it's just hard."

## Subcontractors instead of employees

- Paying only for hours you re-bill avoids employment tax, 36-hour weeks, vacations and project gaps. It is simple time arbitrage.
- But subcontractors charge a lot more per hour, for exactly the reasons above. The same person as a consultant would charge $60/hour instead of $30, so profit is unchanged; you have moved the costs onto their P&L.
- Still worthwhile for less headache: simpler taxes, no vacation bargaining, no prodding for 45-hour weeks. Psychologically it is easier to give up $60 when you charge $100 than to pay a "day's work" when there is no work.

## Is consulting still worth it?

- Jason calls consulting a great way to earn a living and a smart way to self-finance a startup, if you avoid these traps.
- Most consulting companies don't make much profit, and only about one in a thousand has the discipline to launch a successful product in off-hours.
- You need to be "serious, disciplined, and relentless," and set up incentives such as employee profit sharing.
- The payoff: a self-financed startup, a nice living, less risk than a product-only startup, and a great team.
