## Why Jason says not to aim at a bootstrapped billion-dollar company

- Jason argues you "shouldn't" go in trying to bootstrap a billion-dollar business. Reaching that scale on a reasonably short timeline requires fast growth, and fast growth almost always requires raising money.
- Growing slowly (about 20% per year for 20 years) doesn't work, because a 30-year-old technology is probably no longer relevant by then. Even at venture-style growth rates, he says it takes 7 to 10 years just to reach about $100M.
- Spending only money you already have almost never produces that growth speed.
- He started WP Engine bootstrapped, expecting it to be a company making roughly $30K-$50K in profit per month. After about two years (4 or 5 people, strong unit economics) he raised a first round of $1M.
- Why he changed his mind and raised:
  - The market was far bigger than he expected. WordPress was already about 10% of the internet (W3Techs numbers) and winning in corporate homepages and media, not only blogs. It is now about 43%.
  - Demand was higher than he expected.
  - He wanted a different kind of challenge: a bigger team, with the load not all on him.
- Jason says optionality is a strength. If you only have one definition of success or one way to go to market, the company is brittle. Being in a large, growing market is good even for a small company: the market proves demand exists, and it has many niches too small for the big players.
- He raised every round at a fair valuation: not so low that it over-dilutes operators, and not so high that you can't grow into it. Even the hundreds-of-millions rounds came when ARR was also nine figures.

## Strategy as a set of mutually reinforcing decisions (Southwest Airlines)

- Jason says conflicting advice such as "riches are in the niches" versus the opposite is useless as stated. The better question is "when is this advice applicable?"
- A niche suits a solo founder who wants high profit, has no hires and no real competition, because they can be "the best" for a narrowly defined customer. That lets them charge more and avoid fighting on marketing spend and features.
- A general product could also work for a solo founder, but only with other decisions that fit together. His made-up example: no tech support, a very visual product with almost no text so it supports 50 languages, and a price of $1 to $5 per month.
- Strategy is "overall how you're going to win." It is a set of decisions that is unique in aggregate, not any single decision. He cites Thomas Sowell: "there are no solutions, only trade-offs." He adds that decisions also come with downstream consequences (second-order effects).
- A competitor can copy one decision but not the whole system. His example is Southwest Airlines (a Michael Porter diagram, published in HBR in 1995):
  - Southwest is the only US airline to never go bankrupt and to be profitable every year.
  - Its edge is the lowest fares, strong on-time performance and friendly staff.
  - Those follow from other decisions: one type of plane (mechanics learn one thing, one set of tools, deep expertise, cheaper training) and short-haul routes only.
  - The costs to customers are no overseas routes, no long routes, no transfers to other airlines and no food.
  - The question is whether enough customers want the benefits and tolerate the negatives. For Southwest the answer is yes.
  - Decades later nobody has copied it, partly because the strategy shapes culture and people, so copying may be effectively impossible.
- Play to your strengths and avoid your weaknesses. Jason says if you can't write code and don't want a technical partner, don't build a technical product. A non-technical solo founder can't hire a good developer, so the product should be easy to build.

## Designing a bootstrapped business: recurring revenue and annual prepay

- **Recurring revenue.** Jason calls one-time revenue a "terrible idea" (support is one reason). Knowing next month's revenue can be the difference between quitting your job or not, so it matters beyond the math.
- **Value must also recur.** Many founders bill monthly for something customers use once a year (event software is his example). A telltale sign is someone saying "2K MRR but churn is 20% per month." Half the customers are gone in about 4 months, so it is not recurring revenue.
- Recurring examples he gives: accounting tasks that happen monthly, and daily workflows like marketing analytics. He asks founders to explain how a customer gets value in month 6 and month 30.
- **Annual prepay.** Example with a $100/month plan:
  - Offer a year up front for two months free, so $1,000 now.
  - You get the cash immediately, while AdWords is billed to your credit card and paid one to two months later.
  - If you spend the $1,000 on ads and even one person signs up within 30 to 60 days, the ad spend is paid for.
  - As long as the channel is profitable, your marketing budget goes from limited by monthly income to effectively unlimited.
- Accountability without control: he thinks "building in public" on Twitter is weak. A group of about five like-minded peers who know each other's businesses works better, and nobody in it has control.

## Governance, control and operating like a public company

- Jason says even selling 5% with preferred stock gives up some control, such as deciding to sell the company alone. Typical board-level items are governance, big investments or acquisitions, big expenditures, and leases longer than 1 year.
- He says super-voting-rights situations like Zuckerberg's are rare and overrepresented in the press.
- As a company grows (WP Engine has about 1,200 employees), it needs more controls and the available exit paths shrink to a short list: stay independent, private equity, IPO, or being acquired.
- WP Engine has operated like a public company for at least 5 years, maybe 7: public-company-standard audits and books. The reasoning:
  - If it goes public, nothing changes.
  - If it is acquired, probably by a public company, due diligence is easiest after years of clean audited books.
  - He says it is "the right move regardless of which thing happens."
- If you don't want to give up freedom, "then don't make a big company" and don't claim to want a billion-dollar company at the same time.

## Customer development before launch

Jason did this at WP Engine in 2009, before the Lean Startup book came out in 2011. He kept using the same approach for 14 years and has written it up on smartbear.com under "customer development."

1. Decide what you want to learn. This is not a sales pitch, which teaches you nothing. His list of questions to settle:
   - What is the customer's life like, and what are their problems in their own words?
   - What tools do they use now, or do they do it manually?
   - Is there a budget already allocated, and what is it named?
   - Are there different kinds of customers?
   - Where do they go to buy tools, and what do they read?
2. Find the trigger event (idea borrowed from Bob Moesta): "Who wakes up in the morning and says, 'today's the day I'm going to buy new WordPress hosting?'" What happened that day? For WP Engine one answer was getting hacked, after which paying $50/month instead of $5 feels worth it.
3. In a spreadsheet, write your hypothesis for each question.
4. Write non-leading questions. Bad: "The internet is scary, hackers are everywhere, what do you do to keep your site safe, and how much do you spend?" Good: "What do you think about internet security? How much do you spend on it? What do you buy?"
5. Run the calls and adjust your hypotheses. Some will be right, some wrong, and a third category will be things you hadn't thought of. Look for customer types (for example "two kinds of people").
6. Stop when you are never surprised anymore. That means the method has run out of value, not that you know everything.
- Numbers: he tried 50, talked to about 40, and 30 became paying customers before launch. He launched officially with 30 paying customers at $50/month, then later lowered the price.
- He criticizes taking too long to ship. If it takes longer than about 4 months to get a first paying customer, "you're doing a project, not a company," because the difference is customers. He says a long build can be justified only if it is de-risked some other way, such as an existing big audience.
- He built the first version of WP Engine in 36 hours, and notes he is technical.

## Knowing your strengths, avoiding burnout, and changing roles

- Jason says it took decades to know his strengths and weaknesses, and they change over time.
- A 360 review by a third party, interviewing people around you, surfaces strengths you didn't know you had. Often your personal bar is so high you underrate yourself on exactly the things you are best at.
- He also suggests probing questions (on his Smart Bear site under "fulfillment"): what you do when you've been off work for 3 weeks, what you did as a kid. A fun exercise is to have a spouse, friend or colleague answer them about you and compare.
- Link to strategy: knowing your strengths makes for a better strategy and prevents burnout. Many founders end up building a company they hate. He did this at Smart Bear (2002 to 2007, sold for single-digit millions), burned out and sold. He says nearly every study he knows of finds founders are unhappy after selling.
- At WP Engine, about 4 years in (50 to 70 people), he realized he wouldn't enjoy being CEO as it scaled (a global sales team, global offices, a big support operation) and that he'd be learning on the job in every area at once. It took about 4 months of struggle to reach that conclusion, and he stepped aside.
- His roles at WP Engine over 14 years: CTO running all product and engineering, then product, then special projects, then corporate strategy, and now the corporate data team (a personal strength). He talked it through with the CEO and adjusted his role every 6 to 12 months.
- He is proud that the culture allows others to try management, change their minds and go back, and he sees that as a good culture.

## Managing executives, and hiring above yourself

- Jason rejects both "hire and get out of their way" ("what people who don't know how to manage say") and micromanaging. Your job is to hire people better than you, even in areas you are good at, and hold them accountable for results.
- Accountability includes numbers, culture fit of their team, strategic contributions, and whether they make other people better. Executives should also look across the company, not just their own department.
- If you are getting into the details of their work, either they aren't capable (your hiring mistake) or you're interfering unnecessarily (also your fault): "it's always your fault."
- Founders carry "an invisible hammer." A casual "how's that project going?" was taken by a support person as an order to resume work his manager had paused.
- When the CEO and CFO came in, the three of them met about 3 times a week for 3 hours at first, for knowledge transfer. Now the exec team has a weekly 2.5-hour meeting plus another meeting of about an hour.

## Strategy vs. planning, and AI startups

- **Strategy** is the broad, market-level decisions: target customer, channel, price, product shape, and why. It contains no deadlines or dates. It can be a page or half a page.
- **Planning** (his simple method):
  1. Pick the 1 to 3 strategic objectives that most advance the strategy, assuming no problems.
  2. List the 1 to 3 biggest obstacles.
  3. Decide the 1 to 3 things to do next (about the next month or two) that attack or sidestep those obstacles. If money is the obstacle, tackle it by raising money or switching to annual billing, or sidestep it by getting creative on a low budget.
  4. Tell the whole company. Everything after that is just work.
- He ran this once a quarter on a team of about 50 people, changing something each quarter, and says it got better over roughly two years.
- A strategy that never changes is wrong, but a constantly changing one can't be followed. The bigger the company, the simpler and slower-changing the strategy should be, with more fanfare when it does change.
- Frameworks like OKRs fit some cultures and not others. He suggests trying a framework with a team and deciding together whether to keep or tune it, instead of announcing it top-down.
- Before product-market fit, experiment constantly. After it, you test how to talk to the customer, not who the customer is.
- **AI startups:**
  - The usual startup edge versus incumbents is that incumbents avoid risky, uncertain new markets. With AI that doesn't hold, because public-company incumbents must have an AI story or their stock drops.
  - Using the same models (e.g. GPT-4) gives no differentiation, and your own prompts are easily copied by Adobe and others. Training your own model costs too much for a bootstrapper.
  - Niches (for example, the existing model but local, such as for Norway and in Norwegian) can still work.
  - He likes a "human API" approach: expose the interface, have a human do the work behind it at first (e.g. a Jira ticket), then automate what scales. AI fits well by shortening human steps.
  - Today, he says, AI often gets things about 70% right, which is impressive but not dependable enough to fully replace humans.
