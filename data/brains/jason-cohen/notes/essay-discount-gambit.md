## Why Jason refuses to discount, and what he does instead

Jason argues that the typical software sales move, "buy now and I'll give you a discount," is worse than a price lock. He compares two approaches:

1. "If you buy now, I'll give you a discount."
2. "The price is going up, but if you buy now I will lock in your rate."

He says "the evidence is overwhelmingly in favor of #2," even though #1 gives the customer a lower price. Most software companies use #1, often to force a close before month-end or quarter-end. He asks why 90% of them still pick it.

## Why discounting breeds mistrust

From the customer's point of view, a discount for acting now means the original price was never the real price.

- The "price" must have included a slice of pure profit the seller is willing to give up.
- The customer concludes they were being gouged, and only learned it because it was the end of the quarter.
- Jason says "#1 breeds mistrust," the opposite of what a seller wants to build.
- A price lock works the other way: "In #2 you're looking out for my interests." The seller warns of a possible rate increase and protects the customer from it.
- The customer knows an increase may not come soon, or at all. It still reads as protection, not a gouge that was graciously revealed.
- He ties this to the "Love" type of willingness-to-pay.

## The Parasoft `.TEST` story: discounting turns a customer into a hostage

About four years before writing (circa 2004), Jason trialed Parasoft's `.TEST`.

- The software was buggy. Even after hours of remote-desktop sessions with tech support, it would not stay up long enough to scan his code.
- The salesman quoted $20,000. Jason had expected $2,000 and said free tools (FxCop, NUnit) could do the same thing.
- Jason said it was "an order of magnitude problem." The next day the salesman called back with $1,500.
- Jason did not buy. A reference customer told him he had paid $20,000 and had not even installed it yet, because "the demo looked great."

Jason's lessons:

- If the price can drop from $20,000 to $1,500, it might drop to $1,000. The customer now "doesn't know where the floor is" and has every incentive to haggle.
- The process drags out, "ending at gunpoint." In his words: "Meanwhile your 'customer relationship' is now more of a 'hostage situation.'"
- The seller gains maybe 10% on each order but creates an adversarial relationship.
- That is a bad trade in enterprise sales, where a pilot might be 30 seats and the roll-out 2,000. Souring the customer over 10% on the 30-seat part risks the 2,000-seat order.
- Word also gets around. If the reference customer learned the product was available for $1,500, inconsistent pricing would look unfair and dishonest. "Goodbye 2,000-seat order."

## Discounting damages your brand image

Jason separates this from the honesty argument.

- Companies known for discounting include Wal-Mart, Target and Walgreens.
- Quotes for 1,000 desktops from Microsoft, Oracle or IBM are "negotiable, everything's discountable." That conjures haggling and struggle, or at worst low quality and a desperate need to "meet numbers" at the expense of everything else.
- Companies that never discount include Apple, Google (AdWords prices are not haggled) and Constant Contact. There are no discounts on iPhones.
- Their image is "Desirable. The best. Worth paying for."
- His rule: "The leader doesn't have to compromise. The leader isn't desperate for orders."

## Discounts attract the wrong customers

Jason asks what kind of customer responds to discounts, coupons and "act now" language.

- Not the customers who find the most value in the product.
- Not the ones whose opinions you want when planning the next round of features.
- Not the ones who will love the brand and spend more with you over time.
- His conclusion: "Strategy #1 attracts the least-valuable customers and repels the best ones, while establishing a weak brand."

## Why a price lock signals growth and community

Jason says a price-increase message does two things that a discount cannot.

**It implies growth.**
- It plants the idea of higher prices in the customer's head.
- Software "supply" is unlimited, so a rising price must mean rising demand.
- Customers will not do this reasoning consciously, but the product feels more valuable.
- His framing: "Discounts feel like unloading unwanted product; price increases feel like success."

**It makes the customer part of a club.**
- The customer got in early, on the ground floor, before the product takes off and prices rise.
- They are rewarded for loyalty and for taking a risk with you, with price protection.
- He calls it a "thank-you" and says this is "actually a good reason to give some a discount," and you are giving it proactively.

## Summary: discount versus price lock

Jason's side-by-side comparison:

- **Discount (#1):** less money now, an adversarial relationship, a never-ending struggle over money, and a message that the product may need a discount to be desirable.
- **Price lock (#2):** more money now, a consistent and fair pricing policy, an inclusive and special customer relationship, and a message of market leadership and growth.
