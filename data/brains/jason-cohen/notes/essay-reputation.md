## Reputation gave Jason far less launch traffic than people assumed

- Jason announced WP Engine, his WordPress hosting company, to a blog audience of about 18,000 RSS subscribers, plus similar page-hit volume from Twitter and HackerNews.
- Readers called it an unfair advantage: "You're starting with 18,000 prospects. How convenient."
- The announcement produced two new signups. He had expected 10-20 at minimum and hoped for 50, so he was wrong by an order of magnitude.
- Eric Sink, a well-known writer on software business, got a similar result with Winnable Solitaire. He had sold six copies after one month, and the product eventually sold for a small sum on $216 of revenue.
- Jason cites other well-known founders and creators who reported the same pattern: a large following does not turn into launch sales.

## Where the first 50 customers came from

- Before the announcement post went live, WP Engine already had 50 paying customers, not prospects.
- Most paid $49/mo. A few paid over $1000/mo (large blogs with serious traffic).
- His conclusion: whatever the company did without the blog's help mattered far more for the first customers. He promised to explain exactly how in later posts.
- The blog was still instrumental, but not by pushing hosting onto 18,000 subscribers, a.k.a. 1000 "true fans." Its real contribution was building the team.

## Why a famous-person team is worth more than a mailing list

- Jason says the team is a critical factor in a startup, yet there is little advice on how to assemble a stellar one.
- The usual answers ("we went to school together", "we worked together", "we met at StartupWeekend") boil down to putting yourself where capable people are and hoping for a social fit.
- WordPress hosting was already a commodity, with offerings at every price point: $0, $5, $15, $40, and even $500/mo plus $200/hr consulting.
- His rule: "In a mature market you need severe points of differentiation" or moats, and he felt one of them had to be the team itself, a team that "would itself be an unfair advantage."

## The Aaron Brazell hire as the real payoff

- Aaron Brazell is a WordPress core contributor and author of WordPress Bible (Wiley), recognized enough that strangers at WordPress conventions ask him to sign their copies.
- Jason expected value from Aaron in several forms:
  - a network of WordPress-community followers as a source of free leads
  - instant credibility transferred to the company
  - press connections (WordPress.com, CopyBlogger's media group, ProBlogger, Mashable, TechCrunch) for traffic spikes and legitimacy
  - deep knowledge of WordPress internals and roadmap, for a technically better service
- Aaron was charging a high hourly rate for consulting in Washington DC, but wanted the startup life. He accepted less money now for possibly more later, and for building something lasting instead of fixing hacked WordPress installs.
- Jason says "we got Aaron, and it's because of this blog." Aaron could have joined or started many startups, but wanted to start one with Jason because the blog showed his attitude, perspective and credentials.
- Aaron moved to Austin and was already instrumental to the company's success.

## Takeaways on fame, expectations and startup difficulty

- Fame helps in important ways, enough to deserve the label "unfair advantage," but it showed up in recruiting, not in direct sales.
- Startups remain hard and unlikely to succeed regardless of who leads them. Eric Sink's failed experiment is the case in point.
- Jason's own revenue was orders of magnitude above Eric's, but his expenses were too, so having advantages does not make it easy.
- He frames the difficulty as part of the point: "if it were easy it wouldn't be worthwhile."
- In 2023 he adds that WP Engine went on to become a unicorn and is still growing and profitable. He also notes that his launch forecast was an example of why you cannot predict the future.
