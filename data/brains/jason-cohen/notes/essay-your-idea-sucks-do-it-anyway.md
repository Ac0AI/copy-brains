## Jason treats the first idea as disposable

- Jason argues that a founder's first idea probably sucks, "and it doesn't matter" because the business will probably turn out to be something completely different.
- He pushes back on a friend who waited to start a company until the idea was "completely fleshed out". He says the only way to find the right idea is to try the wrong one and see what happens.
- He says you won't find it "by fiddling around with PowerPoint slides and Photoshop mock-ups". His advice is to get out there and make some mistakes.

## PayPal began as a Palm Pilot money-beaming idea

- In 1998 a company received $4.8 million in funding to "beam money between Palm Pilots". Jason code-names the product MoneyBeamer.
- The flow was clumsy:
  - Alice and Bob both had MoneyBeamer installed on their Palm Pilots.
  - The app only remembered that Alice wanted to pay Bob.
  - The money moved later, when Alice synced her Palm Pilot to her computer and dialed up to the internet.
- Jason asks whether you'd have invested in an idea like that. His point is that you'd have been wrong, because it was PayPal.
- The company's encryption work, combined with an idea for a consumer-targeted online banking system, made it the easiest way to send money by email.
- PayPal sold to eBay for $1.3 billion. At the time of writing it processed $2,000 of payments every second.

## Flickr grew out of a game that never launched

- Game Neverending was an in-browser multiplayer online game "with no way to win, nor any definition of success". It never saw the light of day.
- Alpha testers found one feature most interesting: dragging game objects into chat windows to share them. The engineers read that as a useful enhancement to chat in general.
- As the game fizzled, they built a Flash app for real-time chat plus file sharing, with an emphasis on images.
- The app was only real-time, so pictures vanished when you closed it. Jason says this was fatal, because people cared more about the sharing than the real-time part.
- The team rewrote it as a regular website, and that became Flickr, the largest photo-sharing site in the world. It had 3 billion photos, with 5,000 more uploaded every minute.

## Smart Bear's own pivots

- Jason's first product at Smart Bear was Code Historian. It dug through a file's history to show what changed. He calls the name accurate but the product "almost useless".
- He walks through the company's website over the years, from hideous to presentable. He says the early versions sold several tools, and some looked like a "Suite".
- At one point the company sold six different tools. The only one that mattered in the end was Code Reviewer. His take on the later site is that you "admit 'code review' is all that matters".
- The lesson is that the product you launch with is rarely the one that ends up mattering. The company he ended up with was built around code review, not the original idea.

## Start small, because you'll figure out the rest

- Jason quotes Neil Davidson, co-founder of Red Gate Software: "You don't need stratospheric growth and a billion-dollar addressable market to bootstrap a software company. A $50,000 market opportunity is enough to get you off the ground--once you get started you'll figure out the rest."
- Jason cites Red Gate as the example. It started as yet another online bug-tracking system that no one cared about.
- It became a popular seller of SQL database tools with 95,000 customers.
- His takeaway is that you need a small foothold to get started, not a huge market.
