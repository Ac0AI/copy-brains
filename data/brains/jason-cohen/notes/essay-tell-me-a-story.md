## Why Gladwell's flawed theories still win: the story is what sells

- Jason notes Malcolm Gladwell is about as successful as a non-fiction writer can be. *The Tipping Point* was a surprise #1 best-seller for months in 2000, and several of its stories became part of American vernacular.
- Jason says "it all appears to be wrong. And no one seems to care."
- He still likes Gladwell, whose books "are entertaining and they make you think."
- His explanation: Gladwell is "a master storyteller." Readers who have been "beguiled with interesting, provocative stories" happily accept his "unproven, non-sequitur conclusions."

## Broken Windows: the claim that fell apart

- Gladwell defines the Tipping Point as the critical point where a system can go either way. A trend can take off or fizzle, and a few small changes can snowball into big effects.
- His example was Broken Windows, credited (in Jason's telling) with fixing New York City's crime problem in the early 1990s. The city fixed small, easy things such as squeegee guys, Times Square, and pictures of real windows over broken ones in burnt-out crack houses, and crime fell fast.
- Jason's counterpoint is that correlation does not imply causation. Doing X and then seeing Y does not mean X caused Y.
- Steven Levitt's chapter in *Freakonomics* points out that crime fell everywhere in America, at the same time, whatever each city or state was trying (more police, curfews, after-school programs, Broken Windows). That implies none of those was the primary factor.
- Levitt's common factor is Roe v. Wade in 1973, which meant millions fewer unwanted births and fewer people at highest risk for crime turning 18 in 1991. Jason adds that states that legalized abortion earlier saw crime drop 18 years after their own legalization.

## Critics, and Gladwell's non-defense

- Duncan Watts built a career in network theory partly by debunking the "Tipping Point of trends" idea. Jason quotes him: "It sort of sounds cool... But it's wonderfully persuasive only for as long as you don't think about it."
- Watts takes Gladwell's studies one by one and shows how each was misunderstood or misapplied.
- Gladwell does not defend himself. He says books and articles can only "uncover a little piece of the bigger picture."
- Jason calls this a cop-out. The book reads as if it is convincing you of a theory, and he thinks Gladwell was too embarrassed to say, "Yeah, it's wrong, but at least it had a lot of great stories."
- Jason points out the irony that Gladwell blurbed *Freakonomics* ("Prepare to be dazzled") even though its main argument disproves the central theory of *The Tipping Point*.

## Marketing lesson: tell a good story

- Jason read Gladwell's *Blink* cover to cover. He shouted at its "illogical conclusions and meandering, self-inconsistent theories" yet loved every page and did not want it to end.
- He ties this to Seth Godin's argument that marketing should be defined as telling a good story.
- His closing line: "Sometimes everything else is a second-order effect."
- The takeaway is that a compelling story can carry a product or an idea further than its underlying rigor, and audiences may not care that the logic is weak.
