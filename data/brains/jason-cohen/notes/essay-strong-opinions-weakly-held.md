## Reconciling conviction with openness: weight your opinions by how they were formed

- Jason's operating attitude, "strong opinions, weakly held" (a phrase he credits to Paul Saffo), is that conviction is necessary for action but must be tempered by a constant search for disproof.
- He says being wishy-washy gets in the way of inspiring people, working toward a goal and getting things done. Ignoring contrary evidence is "irresponsible and even dishonest."
- His summary of the tension: "A strategy that changes all the time cannot be executed, but a strategy that never changes is definitely incorrect."

## "Weakly" is a dial set by where the opinion came from

- Jason argues the two forces reconcile once you see that how weakly you hold an opinion should depend on how it was originally formed.
- Low-consideration opinions: made without real thought, so easy to change. His example is Smart Bear's website having a "Resources" page separate from an "External Articles" page for no reason. If someone offers a different idea with a rationale, make the change.
- High-consideration opinions: forged with a great deal of work, so they need proportionally strong evidence to overturn.
- His rule: "If it deserved this much consideration before, it deserves the same consideration now. Perhaps more."
- His one-line summary: "Be ready to change with reason, but don't change for no reason."

## What a hard-won marketing message looks like

- Jason describes Smart Bear's marketing message as the product of many years of work. Everyone in the office, and people beyond it, brainstormed ideas, vetoed concepts, defended theories and crafted phrases.
- It went through iterations, with success measured objectively (e.g. AdWords conversion rates) and subjectively (e.g. reactions when giving talks at conferences).
- The message had worked on the web, in print, at trade shows and in demos.
- Because of that history, he treats it as something that shouldn't be changed flippantly.

## Handling an outsider who wants to change your messaging

- The case: Smart Bear hired a dedicated in-house AdWords pro, a full-time job because the algorithms, monitoring and tools change regularly.
- Part of the job is perfecting landing pages. The page should be tuned to match the ad text, both to entice customers and because AdWords placement depends on the relevance of the page's content.
- The AdWords pro began asking them to change the proven marketing message on those landing pages.
- Jason has no problem with criticism, noting that the purpose of Smart Bear's software is to generate criticism. The question was whether to let an "outsider" with no context change proven text.
- The case for listening: a stranger unencumbered by history and biases may supply the insight needed to disprove assumptions. New perspectives add depth, and old ideas shouldn't be kept just because they're venerable.
- The case against: Jason cites Ted Matthews's argument that branding doesn't work unless you stick to your guns, and that new hires quickly suggest change without understanding the battle behind a decision. He compares this to wanting to rewrite an application because of a little ugly code.
- His resolution: he disagrees with Ted that "branding" can be a shield against good ideas, however entrenched the old ones are. But where existing ideas are proven and great work has molded them, new ideas should be held to proportionally high standards.

## Practical test for whether to change something

- Ask how much consideration and testing produced the current choice. Little or none: change it readily when given a reason.
- A lot, backed by measured results: demand evidence at the same level before overturning it.
- Neither "never change" nor "always change" is acceptable. Both are failures to weigh the evidence.
