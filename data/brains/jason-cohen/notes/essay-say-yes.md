## Qualify "yes" instead of saying "no"

- Jason argues that "focus" says to refuse most things, while experimentation says to try new ideas. His way out is to never say "no" and to carefully qualify "yes." He wants new ideas explored without losing the plan.
- He frames it as: "Never say 'no,' but rarely say 'yes.'" Every "yes" is conditioned so it only happens when it is clearly worth the distraction.
- He expects most qualified yeses to be rejected, and he counts that as a fine outcome.

## The two-sided test for a qualified "yes"

Set the conditions so that:

1. **If they say "yes," you're happy.** The terms or money are so good they more than compensate for the distraction, and may fund the thing you really want to do.
2. **If they say "no," you're happy.** It wasn't a worthwhile return on your time and effort anyway.

If a deal fails either side of this test, the terms are wrong.

## Price the "yes" to fund something you want

- Jason calls "funding the thing you really want to do" the single best way to decide how to qualify a "yes."
- Example conditions he lists:
  - "Yes" if it pays for an entire additional person.
  - "Yes" if it extends the startup's runway by at least three months.
  - "Yes" if it completely funds development you'd like to do anyway.
  - "Yes" if it means a co-founder can quit her day job.
  - "Yes" if it pays for three new marketing efforts.
- He treats the money like another form of funding: "Funding is always a distraction from actually running your business, so the amount of money you get must be transformative to the business."
- If a deal wouldn't move the needle, he is fine losing it.

## Early example: $100/hour Mac Java work

- In the mid '90s, at 17, Jason did Macintosh contracting at about $25/hour.
- A company with a new Java product that was broken on Mac called him. Their investor wanted a Mac demo. He didn't want the job: Java was new and buggy, and he was a C/C++ person.
- On the advice of an older, wiser friend, he showed up and quoted $100/hour instead of declining. He expected to be laughed out.
- They agreed. Everything was working in about an hour and a half, for roughly $150. He says the difference was "all in how I phrased 'yes.'"

## WP Engine: big bloggers and the "pays for a developer" rule

- Early in WP Engine's life (about a year in), large bloggers wanted to move over. They had tens of millions of monthly page views, traffic spikes, custom code, perfect uptime and 24/7 support needs.
- The stated target was the "middle market": people who outgrew free blogs and don't want to run servers, but aren't huge.
- Saying "no" might close the door on a pivot, since serving 100 large blogs could beat serving 1000 medium ones. Saying "yes" could hurt a blogger, bring bad press, or create an unprofitable "albatross."
- Solution: quote high enough to be sure of good money, enough to partially fund something else, such as a big ad campaign or another WordPress expert.
- For one especially large customer, the deal had to cover operating costs with a reasonable profit and also pay a full developer salary (a low, bootstrapped one). The customer would eat much of that person's time, and the remaining time came "for free."
- Many of those qualified yeses were rejected. Later, with more large customers, WP Engine could say a plain "yes" to the same bloggers, for example one with 30 million monthly page views.
- Editor's note in the essay: by 2023 WP Engine was 13 years old with 200,000 customers, and hosted more large WordPress sites than anyone.

## Smart Bear: $2,500/day consulting trips

- Customers flew Jason out to help implement peer code review. Often "management" wanted him to convince the team and invent a painless process.
- It was a poor use of his time: they already owned the software, so it sold no new seats. Counting a travel day on each side, the time could have landed a customer or gone into the code.
- He first said "no," then reframed to "yes" at $2,500/day including travel days, which he calls unheard of since travel time is usually unpaid and only expenses are reimbursed.
- That cut out most trips. The remaining ones brought in about $10,000 for a week of easy work, often combined with a long weekend with his wife. He adds that those clients "really wanted me there," which made the work more enjoyable.
