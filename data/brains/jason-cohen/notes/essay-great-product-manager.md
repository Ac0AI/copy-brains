## Why the "Great" Product Manager is an impossible job, and what Jason does about it

- Shreyas Doshi's tweet-storm lists about 16 traits of a "Great PM". They include improving company trajectory singularly, being a world expert in the domain, blending quantitative and qualitative inputs, learning "a lot more about their craft in their personal time", and working hard while being "rarely overwhelmed".
- Jason finds the list funny and impossible. These lists always include an "out" (Doshi's 31st tweet says Great PMs treat the ideas "as signposts, not as commandments").
- His answer is to make the whole product team collectively the mythical "Great PM", built from plausible, real humans. He used this framework at WP Engine.
- His claim is that no individual can be all four roles at excellence, and the manager's job is to design the organization so every role is covered.

## What a Product Manager actually does: decide what to build

- Jason says the role varies between companies but centers on "deciding what to build".
- Looking backward are the inputs to that decision: long-term vision and strategy, plus an intimate, evolving understanding of the customer. Without these, decisions are not good.
- Looking forward is execution: engineering delivery, plus coordination with marketing, sales, customer service and external partners to deliver the whole experience.
- He admits the breakdown is somewhat arbitrary, but finds it especially actionable for hiring, careers and designing the PM organization. It maps onto other models, e.g. Lenny Rachitsky's "Shape, Ship, Synchronize" or his five skill areas (execution, collaboration, leadership, vision and strategy, user research).

## The four roles: Strategist and Customer Whisperer

**Strategist**
- Answers: how do we apply our durable advantages to market opportunities to win, what does success look like years out, what are the biggest challenges, and what must we do to overcome them.
- Covers competitive, market and customer analysis, and choosing the few key personas to focus on.
- Positions the product to maximize value and perceived value, and therefore price and willingness-to-pay.
- Selects high-level metrics, while acknowledging not all important things are numbers. Revenue and retention "are outputs of great strategy plus great execution, not themselves a strategy or even an operational goal".
- Requires the fortitude to say "no" to 90% of ideas so the team says "yes" to exactly the right ones.

**Customer Whisperer**
- Not just listening but "hearing" customers: understanding underlying needs, motivations and emotions.
- Not order-taking the features customers can name. It means finding what they worry about so much they would pay to reduce it, or the result they need to earn a promotion or reach their ultimate goal.
- Requires wanting to figure it out, and the "taste" to sense useful features without clear quantitative data.
- Includes the nuance separating a serviceable UX from a delightful one that makes an NPS-10 customer actually "promote" in real life.
- Translates insights into features and use-cases that make the customer a hero.

## The four roles: Scrum Product Owner and Orchestrator

**Scrum Product Owner**
- Owns the work backlog and executes it with engineering: breaking epics into stories, and prioritizing and scheduling against engineering demands, requests from other teams and company needs.
- Works with engineering to solve "most value in the least time (or risk)" puzzles, finding the 80% solution that takes 20% of the time so the team delivers and learns quickly. He does not mean shaking engineers "upside down until estimates fall out of their pockets".
- Uses story points, velocity and retros for team-wide introspection.
- Stretches to maximize delivery but stays practical and celebratory so striving does not become death-march burn-out. Is "in command" of the product and team.

**Orchestrator**
- Delivers Geoffrey Moore's "Whole Product", not just working code: enabling marketing (attention and curiosity), sales (converting leads into sales), customer service, marcomm teams for PR and events, and external integrators and consultants.
- Tracks and communicates status, celebrates wins and states challenges crisply, so stakeholders know the team is in command even in dire situations.
- Runs great meetings: clear goal tied to strategy, tight agendas, participants prepared, decisions made and recorded.
- Engages the whole company occasionally with inspiring all-hands presentations so product teams feel proud and seen.

## Rule of thumb: one excellent, one good

- Jason's rule: "A Great PM is excellent in one area, good in at least one other, and doesn't have time for more than two."
- He finds the four buckets match natural divisions of ability and temperament in real people. He can name people excellent at nearly all of one area, mixed at a second, and wrong in temperament or desire for the others.
- He doesn't believe in "magical unicorns" excellent at all four, and notes you would find many candidates who imagine themselves to be one.
- Even a unicorn lacks time:
  - Strategy is ongoing, as markets, competitors and customers change.
  - Customer discovery is slow: scheduling and running interviews, compiling results, mapping what was heard, prioritizing, and testing assumptions with data.
  - Being a Product Owner takes at least twenty hours per week to write great stories and run Scrum ceremonies.
  - Program-managing multiple teams across departments (scheduling, cajoling, status updates, data gathering, meeting prep) takes a lot of time.
- He quotes Peter Drucker: "We are not going to breed a new race of super-humans; we will have to run our organizations with the humans we have."

## Designing the product organization: assemble excellence across all four roles

- One person can't do everything, but across the entire product team you need excellence in all four areas. So the head of Product is an "organization designer", achieving this through intentional hiring, not just a "manager".
- The roles often sit under other titles:
  - Communication and collaboration: Program Manager. He argues that if you think the role isn't useful, you may not have worked with a great one.
  - Marketing, sales and support enablement: Product Marketing, often under the CMO rather than the CTO.
  - Highly technical back-end teams with little customer exposure: a sociable engineer or architect can be the perfect Product Owner.
  - Mature organizations: UX Research teams that build data-based personas, craft interviews, compile results, and pool insights across product teams sharing customers.
- Even incremental improvements matter because organizations often have excellence "nowhere". Symptoms he cites:
  - 95% of employees (per an HBR article) say they don't understand the strategy or their role in it.
  - Founders in PM roles run on instinct more than systematic customer analysis.
  - Engineering teams complain of underspecified or under-explained stories.
  - Engineers are surprised by the gulf between what they know about the product and what Sales claims on the phone. Customer service is likewise surprised by the gulf between what they know and what engineering thinks is "a job well done".
- His advice: skip unicorn-hunting; in your career or team design, aim for one excellent area, a second good area, and fill the remaining roles with other people.
