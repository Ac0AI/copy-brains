## Comparing yourself to other startups is the wrong yardstick

Jason argues that measuring your company against other startups produces envy and bad decisions, because the comparison set is always distorted. His example is the run of headline exits and valuations around 2014:

- Instagram sold for a billion dollars with 13 employees, about $77,000,000 per employee, after roughly two years and with $0 in revenue.
- WhatsApp sold for 19 billion dollars with 55 employees, about $345,000,000 per employee, after four years.
- Slack was valued at over a billion dollars less than a year after launch, adding more than a million dollars of annual recurring revenue every month.

He notes that each new headline made founders feel worse, and that even Instagram's founder was told by pundits he had "totally missed this wave" once Instagram was said to be worth "$15B today minimum."

## Why outliers and extremes are useless as a benchmark

- Jason says you "mustn't judge yourself with the yardstick of other companies, particularly not against the rarefied few who dwell in the extremities of reality."
- Comparing your worst fears (exaggerated negative) against the public façade of other people (exaggerated positive) and against media darlings guarantees you look bad.
- This leads to depression about your own results and, in his words, is "not a path to finding good answers."

## Why averages and medians don't fix it either

- Measuring against the average or median is no better. Knowing the "average" company is at `P` while you're at `Q` tells you little.
- For any metric where you look worse, Jason says he can show another company in an even worse position on that metric that is extremely successful for other reasons.
- Founders then envy each other's strengths while ignoring their own, and get increasingly discouraged. He sums it up: "Sometimes, benchmarks aren't healthy."

## The better question to ask

Instead of "how do I compare," Jason says to ask: "What is the best thing I can do for my company, with the specific challenges and benefits that I have in front of me?"

This applies to every metric he lists:

- valuations
- growth rates
- retention rates
- NPS scores
- early-stage uncertainty
- ratio of revenue to employee
- CAC
- cash burn
- LTV
- gross margin
- selling your company

## A decision test for any metric

Rather than asking what the average or extreme is at some other company, Jason reframes it as: "Am I happy with this metric, and if I change it at the expense of other metrics, or of innovation, or of my lifestyle, or of my happiness, is that the right trade for me, for the company, for my customers, and for my employees?"

- Change a metric only if the trade is right for you, the company, customers, and employees.
- If you can answer yes across all of those, "you have your answer."

## Learn to reason about your own situation

- Jason says that if you aren't sure how to reason this way, "that is your next big challenge."
- Without confidence in reasoning about yourself, you have no choice but to fall back on comparing your fears to others' public image, which he ties to impostor syndrome rather than good decision-making.
- His closing frame: you're the captain of your ship, navigating your own waters. "Don't forget it."
