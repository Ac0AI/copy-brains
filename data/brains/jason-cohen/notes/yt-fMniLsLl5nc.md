## Choosing funding: bootstrapping vs. raising money

- Jason never planned to raise money. He bootstrapped Smart Bear and sold it while still bootstrapped; the buyer was Insight Venture Partners, who then put in more money and bought other companies.
- WP Engine, his fourth company, also started bootstrapped. About two years in, the market looked far bigger than he had thought, large enough that raising money was plausible and logical.
- He also had a different personal view by then: "maybe I want a different journey this time." He was a father and had already run the bootstrap path several times.
- The questions he says change the path:
  - Are you optimizing for profit or growth?
  - Is the constraint money or people?
  - How big a strategy can you make?
  - What kind of organization and impact do you want?
- Even with experience, he says every company is new: new customers, new market, new product. "You really have to have beginner's mind with each company."
- He credits "incredible product market fit" for making funding a viable option.

## No tribalism about investors or acquirers

- Jason doesn't tie his identity to a funding approach. He identifies with the companies and ideas he builds, not with processes or decisions like whether to raise.
- Having done both, he says each has upsides and downsides, and there are bad actors in both places.
- Some investors helped hugely, including with culture and people. Others pushed the wrong pressures onto a company.
- As an investor in dozens of companies, he has seen founders who wanted to sell. Some investors tried to talk them out of it. He and others said "good for you, let us help you do that." The founders were burned out, so that outcome was good for everyone.
- He calls buyers like Tiny/SureSwift-style small-company acquirers (he is an LP in one) good for the world:
  - Founders get liquidity or relief from burnout.
  - Employees get leadership roles there was no room for before.
  - Customers get continuity instead of a company that dies when its founder burns out.
- He supports calling out bad investor behavior, but not the blanket claim that "investors are bad."
- Extreme positions are easy to follow, like a simple diet rule. They help you focus and go deep. The cost is a skewed picture of the world and missed ideas.

## Study competitors' incentives instead of demonizing them

- Competitors may have raised money, or may simply be large. Either way, you need to know their incentives, culture and goals to build your own strategy.
- Example: a venture-funded competitor must prioritize growth. If you go heavy on personal customer relationships (reaching out to each new signup), they won't follow, "even if they could," because it isn't their culture or goal. That makes your strategy unique.
- Funded competitors almost need to spend money, because the point of raising is to spend it on growth or de-risking. Don't try to outspend them. Find a different way.
- "Part of what makes strategy good" is when you can say the other side won't follow you.
- Calling a competitor dumb or evil blocks objective analysis of their real strengths. You need to know where they are strong so you can go head to head or avoid them.
- A different set of constraints and goals than yours is "the Genesis of a good strategy."
- He distinguishes needing to do it your own way (healthy, gives you energy) from believing everyone else is wrong. Many founders, himself included, just need to do it their way without claiming they are right.

## Hiring people better than you

- Founders who say "only I can do this" and won't hire often burn out. Jason's view is that the founder can't be replaced in everything, but hiring a developer or marketer doesn't replace all of you.
- Claiming no developer is as good as you is, in his words, "a personal failing," not something to be proud of.
- A leader's job is to make the organization better over time. So you hire people who are better than you at each function: "If you don't then the company will never get better than you personally are at every function, which is a complete failure of leadership."
- How to evaluate someone who knows more than you:
  - Ask how they would approach a problem you actually have (e.g. "we've exhausted our one channel, what do you do?"). If their answer sounds rational and compatible with your thinking, yet several steps ahead, they are likely better than you.
  - In reference checks, ask what environment the person thrives in and where they would fail. Don't reveal your own culture or expectations, so the answer stays honest.
  - Ask people in your network who know the function to help interview. Consider paying them a fee ("a grand, five grand, ten grand"), which may beat a recruiter who "just shuffles resumes and charges you a lot."
- He adds that it is fun and effective when others have ideas better than yours.

## Staying solo without burning out: keep decisions consistent

- Wanting to stay solo is fine, but he says burnout is the most common outcome, and he burned out at Smart Bear too.
- His fix is a consistent strategy. Every other decision (customers, market, product, support, marketing, sales) must fit the choice not to have employees.
- Examples:
  - If it's just you, don't run 24/7 support or sell a product customers depend on around the clock. Four hours of downtime should be survivable for them.
  - Being the only person with a pager forever burns you out.
  - Alternatively, contract out support or the pager. Support is risky to outsource because those people talk directly to customers and become your brand. Technical on-call is less visible.
  - Or design the product with runbooks and rollbacks so you can outsource on-call once you are bigger.
- Inconsistent decisions "will kill you." You must decide what you really want, such as staying solo versus building a bigger company. You can also limit growth or close signups.
- Making hard tradeoffs produces a company aligned with what you chose and better-fit customers: "that's what strategy should look like when it's done right."

## Building in public

- Benefits:
  - It forces you to evaluate yourself constantly, since you must decide what you think about each thing you post.
  - It brings feedback (which you still have to filter) and builds a network for questions like Stripe Atlas or design A vs. B.
- Costs:
  - It takes a lot of time and fragments attention. Context-switching hurts far more than the raw hours suggest, and he is skeptical of "five or ten minutes a day."
  - Mitigation: limit it to set times, like lunch or mealtimes.
- Strategic upside: if you build in public, secrecy can't be part of your strategy. For example, you can't pretend to be a big company ("we" on the About page) when you are one person.
  - That pretense promises 24/7 support or integrations you won't deliver, so it is a bad, inconsistent strategy.
  - Better to position as what you actually are. Customers who want to support the little guy will pick you, and those who need more should not buy.
- He also sees it as good for the world to show that many people try, fail and try again.

## Jason's own path and honesty as a personal brand

- He doesn't plan another company. He has made enough money that a project doesn't need to earn any, so he'd pick something unique: writing, open source, or philanthropy. A full-time angel investor role wouldn't suit him because he needs to produce and create.
- He writes long-form strategy essays, posts about once a week, and says he earns nothing from it. Newsletter subscribers, RSS and Twitter followers are the "revenue" he wants. Sharing is how people can contribute back.
- On why founders restart after an exit: he is skeptical of tidy rationalizations and suspects people often simply need to build.
- Staying a person rather than a "persona" on social media:
  - He has written openly for almost two decades, so "this is who I am, take it or leave it."
  - Being upfront defuses situations. His Twitter bio ("keyword buzzword half truth adjective, hey look at me, founder of two unicorns") admits the brag before doing it, which resets expectations.
  - He says admitting a flaw ("I'm too hard on people about X, I'm working on it") takes the sting out of it in meetings too.
