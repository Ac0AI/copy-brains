## Startup pain never fully goes away, and the answer is to get through it

Jason argues that most small startups fail because the founders stop working on them, and the usual reason is that the work is emotionally draining. He says "I don't care who you are or how strong your ego is, you *will* have these moments," and he describes nearly closing Smart Bear many times.

- The pain is not only at the start. Paul Graham and Trevor Blackwell's rollercoaster has despair in the middle and calm at maturity, but Jason says it persists years later despite revenue, profit, daily customers and a great team.
- He was four years in, with several decently paid employees (hard for a bootstrapper), a product people bought and roughly a million dollars a year in revenue, when the worst episode hit.
- His advice is to normalize it. Other founders feel it too and often only admit it in private rooms, so commiseration helps.

## The $200,000 enterprise deal that hit him hardest

Four years into Smart Bear, Jason was negotiating his first true enterprise sale, about $200,000 of one-time revenue and the company's biggest order to date, for 400 seats.

- He does not believe in price negotiation, even for enterprise. Pricing was on the website and bulk discounts were already built into the invoice.
- The counterpart was a procurement manager, not the end user or anyone in the buyer's chain of command. Jason calls such roles "terrorists" because "they use fear tactics to get their way, yet they have no power other than fear."
- The manager demanded 30%, said he had worked with 47 other vendors who all gave at least 20%, and was told "There's always a first!"
- The manager escalated to "You have no business selling anything to anyone," then said he would recommend never working with the company in any capacity, even if given a discount.

## Why the traditional enterprise discount dance exists

Jason explains why vendors and buyers keep playing the same game.

- The ritual: the vendor asks too much, the client feigns astonishment and calls it a deal-breaker, the vendor drops 30% in exchange for a three-year maintenance contract the client wanted anyway, and the deal is struck.
- The incentives are perverse. The salesperson sometimes gets away with a high price, which pads her commission. The procurement manager shows his bosses how much he "saved."
- He grants that big companies need a defender against vendors who overcharge ("They can afford it!") or sneak things into invoices, but this procurement manager felt like an attack, not a parry.
- Jason was the one refusing the "mating ceremony" and trying to skip straight to the wedding night, so he expected pushback. The personal attack was what he did not expect.

## Feelings don't yield to logic

Jason expected his stoic, confident-entrepreneur self to shrug it off. He felt like throwing up instead.

- He felt like a "baby geek playing in the adult's house," with naive ideas of how the world should work, now exposed to people he had worked hard to earn trust from.
- He fretted for days and questioned whether the promise of money (he was bootstrapping with no regular salary) justified feeling inadequate. He asked himself why he didn't just write code and let natural salesmen handle sales.
- After days the despair turned to anger at the manager. He was angrier at himself for letting the manager make him feel bad.
- He says the moment where reasoning defeats irrational emotion "never came." He still carries the experience, because "that's how emotions work."

## The threat was empty

The procurement manager turned out to have no power to stop the deal.

- When Jason's actual buying contact joined a call and asked what was holding up approval, the manager said "Nothing, just some paperwork, we'll have it done by Friday."
- Days of angst were for nothing.
- Years later, meeting the man in person, Jason "still felt small." The lesson is that a bully's bluster can be empty while your reaction to it is still real.

## Living through it, not beating it

Jason's conclusion is that you don't need to be confident, stoic, smart, right or wise to get through a startup.

- You don't need to match the emotional stability of big bloggers, which he says is "mostly a façade" anyway.
- "It's not even about 'overcoming.' Maybe you don't overcome, you just get through." He has never "beaten" that procurement manager emotionally, if he is being honest.
- He frames it as "living through it, not beating it" and calls it your crucible.
- He is clear that tenacity isn't all it takes, but without it you stop. Stopping is easy, there are many reasons to stop, and it is how most little startups actually fail.
- He later (a 2022 follow-up) says he avoided burn-out and tamed these thoughts with a framework he detailed in a separate article on fulfillment.
