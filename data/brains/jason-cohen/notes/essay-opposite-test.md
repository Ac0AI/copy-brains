## The Opposite Test for positioning and feature bullets

Jason is tired of generic feature/benefit bullet points ("Easy to use," "Robust features," "Innovative systems," "Customer-first"). He argues they're easy to mock and they don't differentiate, because every competitor claims the same things.

He runs the test on each bullet:

1. Construct the negative of the statement.
2. Ask whether the negative is ridiculous.
3. Ask whether anyone could build a rational strategy around the negative, or whether a competitor already has.
4. If the negative is ridiculous, the bullet is "trivial, obvious, mandatory, or at least undifferentiating from the competition."

He calls such a bullet "weak, it's boring, and most importantly, it's meaningless." It takes up space on the page, and in your head, that should go to the differentiating things your product strategy is built on.

He notes the idea is well-worn and independently arrived at. Roger L. Martin asks "Is the opposite stupid on its face?" Al Ries and Jack Trout, in *Positioning*, ask whether it would make sense if a competitor did the exact opposite. It's also called the Reversibility Test.

## Worked examples: what passes and what fails

**Fails (throw it out):**
- "Easy to use" → "Difficult to use." Funny; no one would claim it.
- "Enables communication" → "Blocks communication." No one would admit that.

**Passes:**
- "Stores files as big as 100 terabytes" → "Cannot handle huge files." Not ridiculous, and sadly true of many systems.
- "Fully open source" → "Closed source." Most companies do exactly that, so it's a real choice.
- "Fully backward-compatible, even after twenty years." Most software eventually ships breaking changes to enable new architecture or features. Compatibility matters when millions of users run on-premise software with old components (e.g. WordPress plugins). The cost is that it hamstrings UX innovation, workflow and feature innovation, and architecture and performance work. Sometimes a breaking change is needed to stay modern.
- From his own product (Smart Bear's Code Collaborator): "Integrates with seven version control tools." The negatives ("not integrated with version control" or "integrated with [one tool]") are true of competitors. Going deep on a single API instead of wide across many is a valid, practiced strategy, so the statement differentiates.

## Second check: does the claim carry an acknowledged downside?

Jason adds another way to tell whether you pass: **whether there are negative consequences from your statement, which you acknowledge.**

- Supporting multiple version control systems buys compatibility.
- Supporting only one buys a deeper, more sophisticated workflow integration.
- A single team probably uses only one system, so it might prefer the "one" product. Choosing "multi" therefore has a direct cost, which makes it a real decision.

A claim with no cost and no tradeoff is usually a generic one.

## Replacing generic bullets is hard, and that's the point

If you're using generic bullets now, replacing them isn't easy. You have to work out:
- What is strongest about your product.
- How specifically it beats the alternatives.
- How to say that pithily.

He calls this a useful exercise in itself.

## The one exception: a generic you truly own

A generic claim is fine if it's your single biggest differentiator, where you're truly 10x better than the competition on that dimension and so "own" the concept as your identity.

His example is "Fastest." The negative is funny (slow operation means "lots of time staring into frozen progress bars"), but it works if it's your top priority. His tactics:
- Make it the bi-line: "The fastest ____."
- Prove it with benchmarks.
- Explain that speed isn't only about saving operator time. It enables entirely new features. If operation X is normally too slow for anyone to use, and your system completes it in two milliseconds, it becomes a feature. Even if a competitor technically has it, you make it practical.

## How it fits his broader positioning principles

Jason says the Opposite Test is another way of saying:
- Be specific.
- Avoid buzzwords.
- Commit fully to your ideal customer and what they value.
- Tell the truth.

He calls it a critical component of having a great strategy and great positioning.
