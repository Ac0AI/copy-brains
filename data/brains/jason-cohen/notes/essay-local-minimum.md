## The local-minimum trap in A/B testing

- Jason compares incremental A/B testing to water in a backyard. Water picks whatever direction is lowest in its immediate vicinity. It never surveys the yard, so it pools in a "cesspool" (a local minimum, or watershed) instead of hopping the fence to reach the sewer, the true global low.
- His point: "Maybe your A/B tests are missing the forest for the trees too."
- A typical incremental test starts from a baseline, changes one thing, and picks the winner. Example: the title "Souring on Sour Cream?" vs "Don't know when Sour Cream Goes Bad?", then another variation like "Is this Sour Cream Good or Bad?".
- Inching along this way, you believe the small gains will "add up to real money". Often they don't.

## Recognizing when you've hit a local minimum

- The sign is that small changes stop doing anything.
- This is hard to see by eye, so Jason says to (horrors!) use math to decide empirically whether anything is actually happening, with proper statistics rather than gut feel.
- He warns that you might be tempted to give up at this point, "but that's wrong too". The page is stuck at a local optimum, not at the best possible result.

## Jump the fence: try radically different variants

- Escaping the cesspool means completely changing your perspective, which can reveal "an entirely new, discontinuous, non-incremental change".
- Things worth radically changing:
  - Message
  - Layout
  - Value proposition
  - Colors
  - Target audience
- His line: "The real fun is in the sewer; you need to jump over the backyard fence."

## Run radical and incremental tests together from the start

- Looking in completely new places has far more potential than incremental improvement, so "you need to be looking for discontinuous results from the start". Don't wait until incremental tests stall.
- Recommended technique: do both.
  - Instead of running only A vs incremental-change A2, also run a B version that's radically different from A.
  - You reap the straightforward benefits of incremental improvements while also searching for something that could radically improve revenue.

## A radical win should change more than the test

- If a radically different message gets massively better results, perhaps all your messaging should change accordingly.
- It may also shift your idea of what customers want, and potentially the whole business: "Maybe your entire business can change for the better."
- His closing advice: stop making minor variations when you could be "toying with new ideas and new identities". In his words: "Play!"
