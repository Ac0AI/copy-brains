## Why "never compete on price" is only sometimes true

Jason's core claim: "never compete on price" holds when low price is a tactic, and fails when low price is an outcome of a complete strategy. Companies like Costco, Southwest, Vanguard, IKEA and Amazon win on low prices and stay profitable.

## When competing on price is a trap

- Two products solving the same problem for the same segment with largely the same features leave price as the only differentiator. He compares them to "two gas stations across the street from one another; the main difference is the number on the big sign."
- One cuts price 20% and wins share, the other matches, and the cycle repeats through special offers, permanent cuts, "branding" and influencer kick-backs until no profit is left.
- Both companies then can't invest in product development or customer service, so products stagnate and deteriorate. "Low price comes at a price, to consumer and company alike."
- Enterprise and SMB products usually differentiate on capabilities and user experience instead. An enterprise needs SSO, security, compliance and scale, which make software harder for small businesses to use.

## The math of price cuts

- A 20% price cut needs a 25% increase in customers to keep revenue flat. Charging 80% at 120% volume yields only 96% of the revenue.
- Profit is worse. At a $100 price with $20 profit, a 10% cut to $90 leaves $10 profit, a 50% drop.
- This is why established companies can't match low prices on their main product, which is an Achilles' heel startups can exploit.
- It is also why he and other pundits tell bootstrapped founders to "raise your prices."

## Why new founders underprice

- They don't know how to set prices and know the initial product isn't very good. Instead of making it simple and lovable and winning a fair price from ideal customers, they "sheepishly charge a low price."
- Low prices attract the worst customers: those who can't afford a proper solution but consume the most tech support.
- They also raise the number of customers needed before the founder can quit their day job, with no extra money for marketing, sales, design or development. He calls this "the wrong decision, made in fear and ignorance."

## Low price as an outcome of interlocking decisions

- Successful low-price strategies treat price as one component of a comprehensive strategy. The decisions are unique, interlocking and self-reinforcing, and "lowest price" results from them.
- The decisions carry negative consequences that many customers hate. The winners built strengths by leveraging specific weaknesses.
- Competitors don't copy them because they "weren't willing to copy their weaknesses, which enabled those strengths."
- The product is ideal for a subset of customers, and for roughly 100x more it is a better set of trade-offs than the alternative. Targeting a subset lets you win more than that subset.
- Bezos's line: "Your margin is my opportunity."

## Four worked examples

**Costco**
- Higher quality plus larger packages gives a lower unit price.
- A warehouse feel means a cheaper interior, and limited inventory gives flexibility.
- Drawbacks: unappealing interior, narrow selection, and an annual membership fee, which is pure profit and increases return visits.
- Margin is capped at 14% on all products to protect the low-cost brand, even though customers would pay more.
- Employees average $24/hr, roughly double typical grocery and retail pay in the same cities, which cuts turnover and raises productivity.

**Southwest Airlines**
- Many daily flights on a few short routes, with no long hauls, international flights, airline interconnection or bag transfers.
- Secondary airports are cheaper, so fares can be lower.
- A single airplane type gives pricing power over the supplier from large orders, plus one set of tools, spare parts and training.
- No first class, meals or travel-agency support.
- It stayed profitable through September 2001 and 2008 while other major US airlines went bankrupt. Copycats JetBlue, Spirit and Frontier were less successful, and two of three went bankrupt.

**Vanguard**
- In the late 1970s, mutual funds charged around 2% a year for managers and analysts. A 5% gain became 3% for the consumer.
- Vanguard offered index tracking with no human judgment and only de minimis trading fees.
- It appeals only to those who believe managers don't outperform the market after fees compound.

**IKEA**
- Customers assemble their own furniture, "often poorly, frustrated, and complaining."
- That enables flat-pack shipping and storage savings, efficient store layouts and less transport damage.
- The showroom-to-warehouse flow cuts staffing costs, and a globally standardized line avoids custom products per market.
- Trade-offs: not heirloom quality, a notorious assembly experience and limited selection per category.

## Low-price strategies don't need big funding

Jason argues the SaaS playbook that low price requires hundreds of millions in venture capital isn't the only one.

- Costco: first warehouse in 1983 for $5M, public in 1985 with a $30M offering. Membership fees covered most fixed costs, and expansion came from operating cash flow.
- Southwest: founded in 1967 with $560,000, started in 1971 with three aircraft serving three cities, and funded expansion mostly from earnings.
- Vanguard: created in 1975 with about $2M and a client-owned structure that didn't need to satisfy external investors. Index funds needed minimal overhead.
- IKEA: Ingvar Kamprad started a mail-order business in 1943 with a small loan from his father. The first showroom (1953) was funded by mail-order profits, and global growth came from reinvested profits.
- His point: these are physical companies with inventory and buildings, so software companies "should be able to do the same."

## Seven common traits of winning low-price companies

1. Trade-offs many customers find distasteful or a deal-breaker, accepted in order to dominate a subset of the market.
2. Weaknesses that were mandatory for the strengths, so copycats never replicated them.
3. Profitable after one store or a few products, not only at scale. In SaaS terms: profitable unit economics, gross margin above 70%, CAC payback under a year.
4. Profits reinvested in expansion rather than extracted as dividends, an area where bootstrapped companies often diverge from funded ones.
5. Innovation in operations, not just customer-facing features.
6. Narrow offerings and geographic footprints, expanded gradually.
7. A long-term vision with inviolable values and no short-term shortcuts.

His bottom line: being cheap is a lack of vision, while being affordable as a result of strategy is a winning one. "That's being cheap, not affordable."
