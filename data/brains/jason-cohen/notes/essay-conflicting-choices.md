## Choosing among conflicting options: balance, choice, limit, or "both"

Jason argues "it's a balance" is only one of four ways to resolve a conflict between two options, and that it is often used to dodge a decision. He says the first step is to diagnose which type of conflict you actually face.

- **"It's a balance":** right when both extremes are bad.
- **"It's a choice":** right when both extremes are rational but contradictory.
- **"It's a choice... to a limit":** right when one side is the goal and the other is a constraint.
- **"Why not both?":** right when you can invent a solution that defeats the tradeoff.

His warning: "Often people claim 'it's a balance' to justify their refusal to make a decision." Before reaching for The Middle Way, check it is the correct approach.

## When "it's a balance" is correct

- Use it when both poles are bad, so you want distance from each and look for a point in between that reduces the negatives of both.
- Examples: courage versus cowardice and foolhardiness; athletes who overtrain versus athletes who never train.
- Jason dislikes the word "balance" because it implies "equal parts of each," which is rarely optimal. The answer is the right amount of each, combined into an integrated system.
- An Olympic athlete does not split training and rest equally. The plan is built to peak on the day of the gold-medal contest, and the right system varies by person, goals and circumstance.
- Zero-sum resources such as time and money often fall in this category. A solo founder splitting time between making stuff and selling stuff needs "something like" a balance, but not an equal split.
- How to set the split: ask which problem is biggest right now. Either customers are rejecting the product, or not enough potential customers are trying it. Which one it is decides where the time goes.

## When it's a choice: no middle ground

- Applies when both extremes are rational but contradictory. Then it is not a balance but a choice, "and choose you must." Trying to balance means "failing to make a decision," which is "at least suboptimal, and probably downright bad."
- Example: low end versus high end of the market at inception. There are about a thousand times more small companies than large, but their budgets are about a thousand times smaller.
- The tempting but wrong answer for a company like WP Engine is "we'll serve everyone." A startup should find its ICP and focus positioning, marketing, brand and especially product on that one segment.
- Do not pick "the mid-market" because it sits between small and large. Pick it only if analysis says it is the best segment for your company.
- WP Engine, which Jason founded in 2010 and is now a unicorn with 1200 employees, profitable and growing, started with SMBs served by small agencies. It expanded into larger customers only after:
  - reaching $10M ARR
  - landing anchor enterprise customers
  - having enough funding to invest
  - investing simultaneously in marketing, sales, support and infrastructure for larger websites
- Other strategic choices of this kind:
  - Profitable at any size (required for self-funded businesses) versus fastest growth regardless of profit (required for winning a market, big investor returns, or impact on many people). Mixing them reduces the chance of either outcome. Internet advice often does not say which goal it is for.
  - Niche versus broad. Niche speaks only to the niche, charges more, and ships in one language. Broad must appeal to many people, be inexpensive, and may need forty languages.

## Why startup advice contradicts itself

- For any "successful companies do X" there is someone saying "success comes from avoiding X," and examples of both successes and failures exist for each.
- Jason compares this to proverbs coming in pairs: "They who hesitate are lost" versus "Good things come to those who wait"; "Many hands make light work" versus "Too many cooks spoil the broth."
- Resolution: pick the advice that matches the context you are in and the goals you have.

## "It's a choice... to a limit": maximize A, satisfice B

- Use when one choice is the answer but the other cannot be ignored. It is not a middle way or compromise; the second item is a constraint (a governor), not a goal.
- Example: a VC-backed company maximizes growth over profit. In 2024, private and public markets reward companies only if they have some profit, except AI startups. Valuations can be several times higher for otherwise-equivalent companies at +5% versus -5% profit margin.
- The current rule is to maximize growth, but only if profit does not dip below a threshold.
- Operating mode: "Maximize A, while satisficing B." Attention stays on A, and jumps to B only when it breaches the pre-agreed threshold.
- Execute it by setting an objective measure (a KPI) for the constraint. Then everyone knows whether it is satisfied (spend no energy on it) or not (fix it fast). He builds this into his quarterly planning method.
- Application to founder health: starting a company takes an inordinate amount of effort and involves sacrifice, but some people ended up seriously ill, divorced, broken down, or worse. His answer is not "work less" but "work as hard as possible without falling off the cliff."
  - The edge can be hard to see. The answer is to "toe the line, and back off slightly when you feel your feet slipping."
  - He notes that people preaching work/life balance have either built a different type of company or are far past that point.

## "Why not both?": inventing a unique strategy

- Right for novel, unique products and strategies. Tesla combined all-electric with high performance and maximal safety, which others thought could not be done together. Before it, the market offered low-performance EVs, fast gas-guzzlers (choices), and hybrids (balance).
- It requires three things:
  1. **Invention:** for Tesla, new batteries and car design, plus direct sales instead of dealers.
  2. **Risk:** a high likelihood of failure. Many failed "why not both" startups have post-mortems blaming "lack of focus."
  3. **A set of additional decisions:** batteries in the floor for handling and safety, new factories, hundreds of millions spent on recharging stations for range anxiety, targeting buyers who valued novelty and performance over reliability and could afford an extra luxury car, lobbying for subsidies, and replacing dealers with founder-cult marketing and PR (so no test drives).
- Why it becomes a moat: invention means others are not doing it; risk means they are slow to follow (it took over a decade for other manufacturers to try, and they did not copy the batteries even after Tesla open-sourced its patents); the interlocking decisions stay unique even if a subset is copied.
- The press focuses on the invention, but the real key is "what else would have to be true?" (a phrase from Roger L. Martin).
- Add constraints and decisions, even ones undesirable for customers or the company. Some customers value the upside more than they despise the downside, so you become the best product for them.

## "Why not both" examples: Zappos and Southwest

- **Zappos:** free shipping and free returns alongside low prices. Mechanism: scale from fiercely loyal customers, driven by remarkable customer service, driven by a culture giving customer-facing people unprecedented autonomy. A side effect was low margins, so the sale to Amazon at $1B was only about 1x revenue.
- **Southwest Airlines:** frequent departures on not-full planes with the industry's lowest fares, thanks to a very low cost structure. Mechanism:
  - one plane type
  - a few short routes
  - extreme employee loyalty from a nurturing culture
  - accepted negatives: few routes, no international, no food
- Southwest has stayed true to this network of decisions for decades, has the highest profits of any airline in American history, and is the only one never to go bankrupt (per Jason's essay; Michael Porter's activity-systems map illustrates it).
- *Blue Ocean Strategy* has the same idea: a new category built from combinations that are "contradictory" under existing categories, while also solving for profitability.
- Jason's takeaway: "Turn an apparent conflict into a unique strategy that others cannot copy."
