## Acquisitions are not inherently failures; companies change either way

Jason Cohen's 2015 essay rebuts Jake Lodwick's claim (PandoDaily, "An acquisition is always a failure") that founders either failed to achieve their goal or failed to dream big enough. He calls that "the false wisdom of those who haven't experienced the alternative."

- Superlative framing ("always a failure") spreads because the media runs on what people click and share, not because it is accurate.
- He argues the better questions are "When is acquisition wise?" and "What are the conditions under which acquisitions are likely to be good for all parties?"
- He objects to the framing because it insults everyone who chooses to join a larger company while providing liquidity for employees who also took a risk.

## "Youthful energy" doesn't last just because you stay independent

Lodwick lamented that the "youthful energy that created so much value was siphoned off" by the sale. Jason calls this classic founder naïveté.

- Companies are constantly changing. Cool companies stop being cool, new products stop being new, and a slow-growing startup with a great idea gets eclipsed by a faster-growing one with weaker ideas.
- A fast-growing startup, by definition, changes so quickly that year over year it is unrecognizable to the people inside it.
- Example from WP Engine: he and his colleagues laugh about how drastic the changes are. The company still has "youthful energy" for new innovations, but also all the challenges of a fast-growing company with hundreds of employees, which he says "is part of success."
- A company won't stay "a band of six people doing whatever they want forever, whether you sell or stay independent." He says expecting otherwise is foolish.

## A founder's unhappiness after a sale is not proof the sale was wrong

Jason says the author's evidence is weak: he judged the sale a failure because he didn't like being an employee at a larger company.

- More likely this is the "typical postpartum depression that naturally follows even the most successful of exits" (he links to his own essay on identity and sadness after selling).
- A founder leaving and returning to founder-style work does not prove the acquisition was the wrong choice at the time, for the founder, the employees or the products.

## Judge an acquisition by what happens to the employees, not only the founders

Jason argues the "failure" view ignores everyone except the founders.

- Questions to ask: did employees get new opportunities they lacked before, better compensation and benefits, greater job stability and peace of mind?
- Most people don't have a founder's temperament for independence and risk. Some of them wanted those jobs.
- Investment by an acquirer can create more jobs, faster.
- He knows of quite a few acquisitions where nearly everyone stayed for years, where it was the right call in retrospect, where being part of a larger company opened growth and enhancement opportunities, and where employees made good money both at the acquisition and in compensation afterward.

## Bad outcomes happen at independent companies too

Jason concedes some acquisitions go badly: the product is ruined, the culture is annihilated, jobs are destroyed, or it is "an unmitigated disaster."

- But all of those things also happen at independent companies.
- People quote statistics that most acquisitions fail, but forget that most independent startups also fail, or at least cease, over the same span of time used to measure acquisitions.
- The fair comparison is acquisition versus the real alternatives, not acquisition versus an idealized independent company that stays beloved by employees, customers and shareholders.

## Examples where acquisition was good

Jason asks whether these were "really bad for everyone there":

- Google's acquisition of DoubleClick.
- Google's acquisition of YouTube.
- eBay's acquisition of PayPal.
- The hundreds of small companies that weren't "killing it" as much as their hype and Twitter feeds suggested, but whose people now sit on solid teams with nice stock options in successful tech companies.
