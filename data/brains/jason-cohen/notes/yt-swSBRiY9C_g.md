## Choosing between bootstrapping and venture funding at WP Engine

- Jason bootstrapped SmartBear, sold it in 2007, and by his account it only became a unicorn about 10 years later. He says the two companies were built very differently, so "there is not a blueprint."
- WP Engine was his fourth company. About 2 years in he saw it "could actually be really big": a big and growing market, easy to sell, lots of people wanted it, and a great niche. He calls this "venture sized" and says most products, and most of his own companies, are not.
- Most companies are not venture backed. He notes VCs fund a few thousand companies a year, a few hundred of which survive, against a million small businesses in America alone. "Very few successful companies are funded by VCs, so let's not get carried away."
- Personal reasons mattered too. It was his fourth bootstrapped run, he was a father and older, and he wanted a different journey.
- Having already had a nice exit at SmartBear meant he wasn't forced into any particular timeline.

## How he raised money at WP Engine

- Rounds started small. The first was $1.2M from angels and the second was $2M. The $250M from Silver Lake came later.
- He never raised more money than the company's ARR. That gave enough to act appropriately for its size, "but not so much that you sometimes drown in that."
- He never raised at a crazy valuation. A low valuation dilutes you for nothing. A valuation too high means you have to grow into it, and the next round might be hard.
- Fast growth helped, but he says that if they had raised at too high a valuation it might still have been hard to raise again.
- He sees healthy companies with decent growth struggling to raise now because "you don't have AI." Keeping your metrics together is what prevents that.

## Why SaaS is not dying, and why timelines matter

- He has "yet to find someone" paying for a substantial SaaS product who then vibe coded a replacement and stopped using SaaS.
- His HubSpot example: over $2B in revenue, growing 30% year over year, profitable, and the stock still fell. He reads that as temporary or previously overvalued, not as HubSpot going away.
- Predictions without a timeline are meaningless. His examples: people predicting a stock market crash in 2010 (it came in 2020-21, which is "terrible investment advice"), and Elon's self-driving cars "in about 2 years" for 15 years. A prediction that doesn't help you make decisions now isn't useful.
- Customers have the same problems they always had. AI offers new and possibly better solutions to the same problems.
- Legacy non-AI products face a shrinking market. The only way to get a new customer is to steal one from another legacy product, and a switching customer is unlikely to pick another legacy one.
- Building a big company takes many years, even in AI. "Almost all of them don't" get big in a month, so "settle down."

## Applying AI to the same customer problems at WP Engine

- Customers want to build WordPress sites, add content, get found on SEO, track visitors, be found globally, and convert visitors. Media sites want clicks for ad revenue and e-commerce sites want purchases.
- Example: shoppers who use site search on an e-commerce site are 3x more likely to buy, because they show intent. WP Engine has tens of thousands of e-commerce stores with significant revenue, so it has the data.
- They built an AI-based e-commerce search bar that is semantic and understands the product catalog. A shopper can type "I pronate my foot and I like a wide toe box" and get a relevant shoe. It makes sense for WP Engine to build it because it has access to the customer's product database and is physically there running the website.
- The best question for any company: how do you make your customers successful in the way they measure it? For e-commerce that is revenue. For a finance tool it might be "zero discoveries in the next audit."
- Even if you can't measure that outcome, state it as a vision statement. It aligns everything you control (features, sales pitch, homepage promises, weekly reports). You can also simply ask customers whether the product is helping.
- Time-in-product is a weak metric. Long time can mean the product is confusing, and short time can mean it just works.

## Capital allocation and three kinds of AI products

- At scale (WP Engine has 1,000+ people) the CEO becomes a capital allocator. Some budget goes to big new bets, but they still need to be adjacent: "It doesn't make sense for us to try self-driving cars." The usual bet is a new product for existing customers, because distribution is harder than building.
- In AI that flips: distribution is easier than building because "AI products almost never work." Everyone tries a new AI product, then "they all cancel in 4 months."
- He splits AI products into three categories:
  - **Incumbents adding AI** (Notion, Google Sheets, SharePoint): it doesn't work very well yet, but they should keep doing it.
  - **AI for experts** (developers, designers, marketing writers): often works, because the expert knows what to do when the AI is wrong and can fix or discard its output. A startup in this zone can tolerate AI not working every time.
  - **AI for "muggles"** (non-coders building apps, non-designers making sites, non-writers writing books): not good enough. It is fun for demos, but people can't get the last bit to make it good or maintainable. "I've never heard of someone getting to that point who's not a coder. Not once ever."
- If you do bet on the muggle category, put a timeline on it. The odds of success are lower than people give it credit for.
- He doesn't write about AI because it changes every 6 to 12 months. He aims to write things that are true 50 years from now, and some of his articles are almost 20 years old.

## Founder psychology, customer discovery, and the evergreen topics

- He considers these topics unchanged by AI:
  - Founder psychology: believing your ideas are better than everyone's while also having imposter syndrome, and deciding whether to persevere or give up after 6 months.
  - What kind of company to build, how to make a good strategy, and which markets are good.
  - Poking at your own ideas with intellectual honesty.
  - Interviewing customers before you have a product.
- "With AI you can build something no one wants, I don't care how efficient it is that you built it."
- The discovery question is what people would actually want to buy, "where the word want and buy are both doing a lot of work."
- Before WP Engine he did 40 customer interviews (he asked 50). He found many people wanted a fast, scalable, secure website and that he knew how to reach them. He wrote out his full interviewing system online and says it still works and he used it throughout WP Engine.
- He started WP Engine partly because of his love for optimization and fast, scalable engineering.

## Finding your CEO role and handing it off

- He says he was a great CEO up to roughly 50 people. He was WP Engine's CEO for 4 years, then hired Heather Brunner, who has been CEO for 11 or 12 years. He calls it "such a good decision" and moved to CTO.
- His test for any role is the intersection of three things: what you like, what you're actually good at, and what the company needs done. He compares it to ikigai and Daniel Pink's work. He kept shifting roles over the years as that intersection changed.
- You can't know ahead of time what running a company is like (he compares it to having kids). Pro/con lists don't help because you don't know what to put on them or how to weigh it. So don't second-guess daily, but don't cling to your original plan. Check in once a year or when something happens: "Is this right? Am I up for this for another 3 months, 12 months? Does something need to change?"
- Burnout and "two options" thinking: "If you're picking between only two options in that sort of huge life-changing situation, then you haven't explored the options enough." Burned-out founders often think they must leave or sell cheaply. Other options he lists:
  - Promote someone internal to CEO and keep some of the profits.
  - Get a partner.
  - Change your own role.
  - Step out of the CEO role but stay, as he did for 12 more years.
  - Change from growth to profit.
  - Choose a smaller, lower-risk company or a larger, higher-risk one.
- Basecamp, in his example, chooses profit over headcount because the founders would hate having lots of people.
- Get someone who has seen these options to act as a mirror and expand the menu, not someone who tells you what to do.
- He burned out at SmartBear, didn't realize it, didn't consider options, and sold. He says that was the wrong decision even though WP Engine worked out after: "If you make the wrong decision and get lucky, that's still wrong. If you make the right decision and get unlucky, you still made the right decision" (the Annie Duke poker idea).
- How he stepped out of the CEO role without confusion: he was openly vulnerable about SmartBear burnout so people understood why. Then, whenever someone came to him, he asked "What did Heather say when you asked her?" and sent them back to her, every single time. Boundaries and redirects, "a 301 redirect, not a 302." CTO matters still came to him.

## After the exit: identity, optionality, and the book

- He cites a Credit Suisse paper on about two dozen entrepreneurs who sold and no longer needed to work. All but one had symptoms like postpartum depression. He also describes MRI studies in which an entrepreneur's company logo lit up the same brain areas as a picture of their kids.
- There is no blueprint for what to do next, so you have to redefine who you are. He notes you have money and experience but less energy, so he wouldn't start a company and wrestle with AI now.
- His strategy rule: understand your strengths, weaknesses, capabilities and assets, then do a project where the strengths are used and the weaknesses either don't matter or are even strengths to your target market. His example: long-form writing is a weakness if people don't read, but a strength for readers who want depth.
- His book is *Hidden Multipliers*, on pre-order at hiddenmultipliers.com, about jump-starting growth with your current budget and team. Each of the 10 or 11 chapters covers a systematic, fairly repeatable mechanism he has experienced himself or seen others use. He doesn't claim every chapter will double revenue, but expects some to apply.
- His goals for the book: make the best book he can, and maximize the number of readers who actually get benefit. He would rather sell a couple thousand books to people who read and use it than a million where 1% read it. "One of those emails is worth like selling 10,000 books."
- His articles are free online (no ads, courses or paywall) at his site "a smart bear" .com, with an RSS feed containing every article in simplified markdown so people can load them into an AI.
- Optionality is both a blessing and a curse: it's better to have it, but you may need to impose your own limits, goals and deadlines for motivation. He frames a game as self-imposed rules and restrictions, and says with money you get to pick your games. He also suggests asking what purpose bigger than yourself you could pursue, such as philanthropy.
