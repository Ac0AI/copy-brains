## Reposition from "less of a negative" to "more of a positive" to charge up to 10x

- Jason argues almost every product sold to a pain point can be repositioned so it sells more value or transformation (something positive the customer already values) instead of removing a negative like time or cost.
- Most tools pitch "saves time, saves money, saves cost". He says that is easy to fall into, and repositioning often lets you "charge 10 times as much".
- Worked example (from his book Hidden Multipliers): a tool cuts AdWords cost per lead in half.
  - Pitched as savings: a marketer spending $40K a month saves $20K, so they might pay around $5K a month and keep most of the savings.
  - Pitched as growth: at the same cost per lead, the same $40K now buys double the leads, or they spend $80K for double the leads and double the revenue. The CEO hears "doubled leads", not "saved $15K".
  - The same product could then be priced around $40K a month instead of $5K.
- Principle: customers already spend heavily on what they value (more leads, more revenue), and they will spend even more if they get more of it. Pitch the product as creating more of that.
- The book gives about 15 other quick examples in different shapes and sizes, including consulting companies.

## Handling AI uncertainty: anchor on what stays true and the customer's core value

- Jason says the future is especially uncertain, and past experience, pro/con lists, rubrics, and statistics and probability are not useful tools for it. You need different tools for making decisions under uncertainty.
- Two failure modes: doing nothing, or spreading thin across many things when you can't tell which one matters most.
- Tactic: use Bezos's question of "what will still be true in 10 years". People will always want to save money and time, get more leads, close more revenue, be entertained, get things faster, get good service, and (for a billion people) get the cheapest option.
- Treat AI as part of the solution space (how you execute the product), not the problem space (what the customer is trying to do).
- Ask "how do I use AI to get more leads?" not "how do I put AI on the page?". Putting "powered by AI" on the homepage is just a box to check so you get seen.
- If you double a marketer's hot leads and revenue rises, they will pay a lot "no matter how you achieve that. Could be gremlins in a box."
- If you can't articulate the core transformation you deliver, adding AI won't fix it. Don't be surprised when nobody cares, because you're either not delivering value or you're identical to everyone else.

## Where AI replaces work and where it doesn't

- Jason's rule of thumb: "Any job where there is such a thing as a best version of that job will get automated." Where there's no such thing as a best, AI changes how the work is done but doesn't replace it.
- Example of a "best": a Honda plant machine that bolts wheels on and records the exact torque on each bolt. The best is perfect, measured tension, and a robot beats a human on speed, accuracy and repeatability. Once you reach the best, only cost is left.
- No "best" exists for design, software, writing, ideas, entertainment, strategy, or deciding to make Linear instead of Jira. He says he doesn't know if this is a law, but it's a guide for what to invest in and how to avoid having your legs cut out from under you.
- Chess analogy: computers have been better than humans for 25 years, yet chess is more popular than ever. Nobody watches computers play, and people watch human creators. Computers did change how humans play (for example, how pawns and pieces are valued).
- He expects human personalities to keep winning for ads, videos and podcasts. In his view, companies that don't fire all their marketers will have better marketing and content, and the best company videos and ads he sees are always people.
- Systems of record and precision tools (finance and tax systems, customer service desks, sales, GitHub, CI/CD, deploy) are not being replaced by LLMs, which are "amazing and not precise". He says AI needs GitHub and CI/CD more than ever.
- AI supercharges senior developers but not juniors, who produce demo-ware that can't become a real product. Vibe-coded output is impressive when there's nothing there yet, but AI gets worse as a codebase grows. He sees "not hiring because of AI" as largely an excuse.
- He says model leadership is unpredictable (for example, OpenAI is no longer the leader, which nobody predicted), so he declines to forecast 18 months out.

## Adopting a framework in a team: why it usually fails and how to make it stick

- Jason says most frameworks fail when one excited person tries to bring them into a company, because the culture and mechanisms reject them "like the body rejects the organ". He doesn't know how to predict fit. His own blog frameworks work for him but probably not for most people, and there may be 20 good frameworks for something like feature prioritization, of which a couple fit you.
- If other people aren't already interested in the specific thing, it almost never works: "It's a self-fulfilling prophecy if you didn't want it to work."
- Tactics:
  - Share the article or video with the team and ask whether it might work before starting.
  - Try a subset of it.
  - Frame it as "better than what we do now, but probably only 70% right". Agree to review every two weeks what's still wrong and change it.
- His experience: he tried OKRs about four times at WP Engine and they never worked. The version that eventually stuck doesn't resemble OKRs but keeps the spirit and ownership.
- Use the Crucial Conversations idea of finding "common truth" first. If you don't agree on the facts, goals and assumptions, nothing else works, and getting there is often 70% of the conversation. For example, a team resisting OKRs may really not want accountability at all, so the framework isn't the issue.
- Getting a metrics-averse team comfortable with metrics (from his KPI framework):
  - Admit that many important things aren't numbers, but some are, so why be blind to those?
  - Have each person pick one metric they actually believe in and would want to know for themselves.
  - Keep it private (for example, a Google spreadsheet visible only to them) so nobody can be held accountable to it. Do it for one quarter.
  - Later, show a few metrics alongside an equally prominent column of important non-metric items on the same slide.
- On metrics generally: everyone wants to know how they're doing, but metrics can be gamed and can mislead. Example: a support rep closing 27 tickets a day with great ratings versus one closing 2 a day with mixed ratings who gets the hardest problems and may be the best on the team. Metrics are an ingredient and don't replace good management.

## Strategy: aim for "special" fit, not "unique"

- Jason thinks "unique advantage" and USPs are overplayed. Gmail isn't unique, and Yahoo Mail has plenty of customers. Many successful companies have no USP, and someone can often copy a unique thing anyway.
- He prefers being "special": your strengths, capabilities and assets built up over time make a strategy, product or market natural and easier for you, so you do it better than others or they'd have to spend 10x as much.
- Pair that with a subset of the market whose wants, dislikes and indifferences match what you offer. That is not most of the market, but "that puzzle piece, that's where you win."
- You'll still do things outside your wheelhouse and get non-ideal customers, but be clear on the fit. He says this is very hard and that the book and the skills show how.
