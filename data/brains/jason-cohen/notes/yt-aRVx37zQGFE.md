## Marketing and finding customers: de-risk the weakest link first

Jason's core claim is that a company only works if many independent things go right at once: a real problem, reachable customers, winning against alternatives, and the founder's own stamina. Probabilities multiply, so even ten "reasonable" 70% odds give a low overall chance, and many are closer to 10%.

- Identify the weakest link and decide whether strengths elsewhere can overwhelm it or whether it is a deal breaker. The test question: "what else is true that makes this okay?" "We'll have good features" is not an answer, because everyone says it.
- Tackle the weakest link first. An engineer who is unsure they can get customers should not build the product, because that does nothing to answer whether customers can be found. He says go find customers instead, interview them, and learn what to build.
- Narrowing the niche improves several odds at once. You can be the best product as defined by a specific customer, even if the market gets smaller.
- A small market can be okay if offset by something overwhelming, for example a co-founder who already knows 70 of the 100 potential buyers and customers who pay very high prices.

## Niche down without refusing other customers

Jason argues you should always focus on the narrowest ideal customer profile you can, but he says people misread this as "I can only sell to them."

- Being specific lets your homepage message, advertising, pricing and feature choices be compelling to the ideal customer, who sees your strengths as strengths and your supposed weaknesses as irrelevant or even good (a high price can signal quality).
- A much larger group outside the niche (he says possibly 100 times larger) still buys, because the clear trade-offs make it easy to judge. "When the trade-offs are clear people are more willing to accept the weaknesses."
- Evidence he cites: products whose negative reviews are specific about what is wrong have higher sales and fewer returns than products with the same star rating (for example 4.1 stars). Buyers already accepted the flaw.
- Expand later. WP Engine began with small and mid-size businesses, often via freelancers. Around four or five years in it saw appetite from larger companies for campaign use cases, then spent tens of millions of dollars over a couple of years on sales and marketing with a separate message for that segment.

## Why WP Engine won in a commodity market

WordPress is open source and there were around 20 easy competitors. Jason is careful to say he can't be certain of the causes, but his best reasons:

- **Pay more, get more:** managed hosting cost about 10 times as much as shared hosting and was multiples faster. The first swag shirt read "My blog is four times faster than your shirt." Security and service were also much better.
- **A "moat" of things competitors won't do:** great human support hurts gross margin (VCs don't want it, since under 70% margin means "not a software company"). Funded competitors skipped it for cultural and incentive reasons, not because they couldn't copy it. Companies rarely change their culture, and when they try they often fail.
- **Execution in a field where few execute well:** it was his fourth bootstrap-style company, he hired strong people, and the first raise was only $1 million (two years in), followed by a $2 million round. Execution is a poor moat in AI, where everyone is strong, but is a real edge in sleepy fields like hosting or grocery.
- **Pricing reset in 2012:** after customer conversations and stories, WP Engine adjusted its prices (not dramatically). He says that is when the growth curve "bends and starts shooting up and never stops," while noting he cannot isolate variables. Attending WordCamps and talking to people probably helped too.

## Brand follows execution

Jason's view is that brand usually comes from being obsessed with a topic or product and delivering well, not from designing a brand up front.

- Every company has a brand, but not every company has a moat. Brand matters competitively when customers choose you because of it, as a top-three reason, even while you lose on features or price. His example is "no one ever got fired buying IBM"; Apple is another.
- WP Engine's brand grew out of execution, customer love and word of mouth. He thinks a different logo would not have changed its trajectory.
- What you need is consistency, so there is a memorable handle. He compares it to code brace style: studies suggest the specific style doesn't matter, but everyone using the same one does.
- Linear is his example of a product winning on design decisions and sharp focus on the user (not the buyer), not on its name.

## Thinking about AI: it's the solution space, not the problem

If starting a company today, Jason separates AI used internally (coding, marketing) from AI in the product, and says the second needs care.

- Corporate budgets are heavily biased toward AI, so a product probably needs some AI component to avoid a budget and attention fight. But "I need an AI product" is the wrong frame.
- People have the same problems as always: more leads, better conversion, good customer service, fewer bugs. Nobody says "I need AI in sales"; they say "I want 10x outbound volume at the same conversion rate." AI is part of the solution space.
- His example of a good framing: a restaurant phone line handled on the first ring in 40 languages (menu, ordering, hours), where voice AI is just the mechanism.
- Pitch decks that list AI as the problem space are a red flag to him.

## Which AI products to build: experts over beginners

Jason notes that AI "doesn't really work" reliably yet. When researching his book, he found its deep research simply wrong about half the time when he checked primary sources. His three categories:

1. **Incumbents adding AI to existing products** (talking to Notion or Sheets): not very useful so far, but they have to do it.
2. **AI for experts** (developers, writers, designers): his preference. You sell to a narrower audience and face incumbents, but errors are fine because the expert fixes them.
3. **AI for beginners ("muggles"):** the market is 100 to 1,000 times larger, but users get to 70-80% and then get stuck and can't fix it. A vibe-coded app is not a SaaS company. He doesn't judge it, but it is not what he would build.

He personally takes a B2B problem-solution approach: solve a real existing problem, with budget, using AI to do something previously impossible, aimed at experts.

## Look for 3x to 10x, not 20%, and ignore competition

- Marginal gains don't motivate buyers. "30% faster" hosting was weak, which is why WP Engine said four times faster and had customer data to back it. At that multiple, he says, a commodity stops being a commodity.
- Apply the same to AI: going from one article a week to two good ones a day is a product; a 20% boost isn't worth the hassle. Prefer 3x more value for the customer, not just cost savings, which is a weaker pitch.
- Be specific about context. Studies in real engineering departments don't show 10x from AI coding, and individual claims often come from people selling AI. But in some contexts, like using an unfamiliar library, it saves days in minutes, while in a 100-developer code base it can be slower. Find the narrow domain where it is truly a big multiplier.
- He assumes every reasonably sized market will be flooded with AI products using the same models and prompts, so there is no tech moat. His answer is a vision for one narrow ideal customer, built so well that others also want it, then following his nose once real customers are using it.

## Hidden multipliers: his framework for small, systematic improvements

His book *Hidden Multipliers* (pre-order at hiddenmultipliers.com) collects ten ideas, one per chapter, with new material rather than blog rehashes. He defines a hidden multiplier by three tests:

- **Small change, outsized effect.**
- **Systematic:** it relies on how companies, buyers, teams or math (such as A/B testing) reliably work, so it is not luck.
- **Doable with your current team and budget,** without a big investment. Usually the fix to slow growth is not raising money.

"Hidden" means you didn't know it, or it sat on a to-do list without being seen as important, or you knew it but didn't know what to do or feared acting. He notes that when people say "I already knew that," their actions often show they didn't. He also says strategies often fail not from missing information but from not realizing how important one thing was.
