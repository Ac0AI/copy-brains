## Validating by trying to invalidate, and testing price early

- Jason's rule for validation: go in trying to invalidate the idea, not confirm it. Most ideas are "pretty okay", and a few friendly conversations will always feel like validation.
- His example: before WP Engine he tested a different product. Everyone said "that's perfect", but then their suggestions diverged. One said price it at $5,000/month and sell through agencies, another said make it ad-tech only, another said make it freemium for millions. Divergence is the signal that the idea is not good, even though the first reaction was positive.
- Always ask about price, because "price does determine the product". Someone with 120 interested emails had never asked whether they would pay, or whether it would be $1 or $1,000 a month.
- For both WP Engine and the earlier idea he floated $50/month as a starting number. No flinch is a good sign; "I thought it would be free" tells you something else.
- He validated WP Engine by getting about 30-40 people to commit to $99/month.
- Take money up front if you can. Cash the checks ("you can always give it back later") or use Square for card numbers.
- Crowdfunding is weaker validation than it looks. Kickstarter backers are very early adopters and not a repeatable distribution system, so they may not represent a market. Still worth doing: "it's getting the engine turned over".
- If you can't describe the product in a short phrase, you haven't found the root of what it is. You need that phrase anyway for headlines, buttons, AdWords and a t-shirt.
- The phrase's job is only to make the right person say "tell me more", not to state price, market and plan. Google's "organize the world's information" is a mission (know everything about us, which powers ads, maps and self-driving cars), not a business-model description.

## Deciding whether to quit a slow-growing business

- Jason says there is no formula; he calls Seth Godin's book *The Dip* useless for this question.
- His best test is whether you are fulfilled and glad to do it, separate from the stress and slow revenue. "Revenue not growing fast enough and I love it, so who cares" versus "not growing fast enough and I hate it".
- Entrepreneurship is almost always a bad financial choice. Everything will be slower and harder than you want; that's life, like remodeling a kitchen.
- He cites Steve Jobs' morning question (if this were my last day, would I want to do what I'm about to do?). The point is not loving every moment but being proud and fulfilled.
- Most of what a founder needs to do is uncomfortable. Engineers want to open the IDE and build features, but the right work is usually distribution, the website, new customers, support or hiring.
- Bootstrapped founders have more freedom to shut something down, since they have no investors to see it through for.

## What product-market fit felt like across his four companies

- Consulting version: you are telling people no instead of scrambling for work, and can raise rates and be choosy.
- Product version: growth speeds up and you can't explain why. No new ads, social campaign or release. At WP Engine this happened in January 2012 as a literal hockey stick, and it never dropped back after the holidays.
- He says "that's actually signal". The same unexplained shift happened at Smart Bear and IT Watchdogs.
- His path at every company was a "random walk" seeking something:
  - Smart Bear started as a .NET tool integrating with version control. It sold poorly until he noticed people abusing it for code review, then rebuilt around that and rewrote everything in Java (a full rewrite he says is usually wise to avoid, but it worked there). Code review was the real market, and it did not exist as a category yet.
  - Smart Bear's customers moved to mostly large enterprise (Lockheed Martin, Adobe, Microsoft, Qualcomm, Cisco) with zero salespeople. It started around three years in.
  - IT Watchdogs made server-room climate monitors. Sales rose a lot when the product went from a USB-style dongle to a rack-mounted unit.
- Once at scale you need predictability to hire, but early on you can't predict it.
- Experience is not a durable asset. Smart Bear got going because AdWords was new, he was the only ad on the page, and clicks were five cents. That world no longer exists, so keep a "beginner's mind".

## Pricing around how the customer buys

- At IT Watchdogs a buyer said he'd take a base unit and two sensors for about $490-ish because "if I spend less than $500 on my credit card I don't have to ask permission." They rejiggered the bundles so typical purchases totaled roughly $450-$490, and sold a lot as long as totals stayed under about $600.
- Lesson: ask what the buyer can approve without permission. Austin Independent School District's limit was $20, which Jason uses as a reason not to sell to schools or government.
- Hardware channel margins stack. A web-enabled power strip with a $15 board in it sold for $3,500 because every reseller/distributor step took roughly 30%. Software and the internet remove those middlemen.

## Raising prices and moving upmarket

- Jason says to always push prices up, and going upmarket (e.g. $99 minimum) is smart if you want a couple hundred customers paying more instead of many paying little. It must fit your company style (one or two founders can't service IBM).
- Example 1: a company at Capital Factory sold at $300/year. He told them to change it to $300 per month ("just to see what happens"). Sales were exactly the same, a 12x price increase. Their customers included the USGA.
- Example 2: a Facebook-ads optimization tool at $15/month. He had them test $30 for a week; signups did not change. His advice was to double again until signups drop.
- Fear of "what if my customers say this" is solved by "do the right thing": you can keep old customers on the old price.
- Raising prices on existing customers when small: he helped craft an email that was honest and personal. It said they are two founders with a few hundred customers at $20/month who can't feed their families, hope the customer likes the product, and are raising prices (to roughly $79-89 at the low end). It acknowledged "you did not sign up for this", said they'd understand if people leave, and explained the money would hire an engineer and keep phone support. Signed with names and photos. Only about two of hundreds of customers quit.
- His principle: "honesty wins most of the time", and customers want small companies to succeed. Bring them along on the journey; they already know a two-person product has rough edges.
- Enterprise buying (POs): quote high so you can give procurement a 20% discount and land where you wanted. Get into their vendor system and get the PO issued early, even if payment is triggered on delivery, so you don't wait an extra six months after shipping.

## Resellers, old-school channels and enterprise selling

- Reseller and distributor models (Graybar, VARs) fit physical goods in warehouses with setup and shipping; each layer takes about 30%, and the vendor carries inventory and buy-back risk. IT Watchdogs used some resellers abroad (e.g. the UK).
- For software he thinks the model mostly fails because the internet disintermediates it: no inventory, and you can sell directly. He sold to Cisco and made seven-figure-scale sales to Adobe himself, and says enterprise buying is complicated but "not that hard", just under-written-about.
- When everyone ignores something there may be an opportunity, so a Graybar-style channel could still be an overlooked edge for someone.
- Going against the grain worked for Smart Bear: he sent out over 100,000 copies of a physical book (while people said ebooks and PDFs). A physical book arrives in the office, nobody throws away a book, "basically a brochure that never gets thrown away". He made it bright red so it stands out.

## Finding channels, ignored buyers and unseen personas (live Q&A coaching)

- For a purchase-order software company (30 customers, about 5 new a month, all from paid comparison sites like Capterra/GetApp) wanting 20 a month, he advised:
  - Don't just broaden, go specific but different. Instead of CFOs (hard and expensive to reach at 50-100 employees), target the controller, VP of Finance, junior accountants entering receipts, or executive assistants.
  - "I love it when there's people who are underserved and ignored." They are harder to find (not at conferences) but love a tool that gives them a win; a junior accountant can replace part of their job and move up.
  - Pitch the real value: PO control is control over spend and approvals, auditability and Sarbanes-Oxley; the controller's job is to control.
  - Find out where the buyers already go. If comparison sites aren't the only way people find tools, work out where competitors get traffic (AdWords, etc.) and what buyers use instead (even an Excel template as a foot in the door).
- For a founder with a time-management app (25 companies pitched, 7 using the method in Excel, $49/user/month, 10-25 users per company) and 2-3 months of cash: get cash sooner. Start PO and vendor-onboarding paperwork now. Don't worry if they stay on Excel as long as they pay $50/month. Pitch 25-75 more companies at the same rate, since about a quarter said yes.
- For a two-sided hiring marketplace: charge from day one, because free or cheap implies low quality and sets precedent. Small scale is still compelling (placing one person with one company is real value and that company will want a second). The hard part of hiring is scaling, not starting. Matching on fit matters; he cites letting go of strong engineers who were a culture fit for Facebook but not WP Engine.

## Why keep going, and stress

- Jason says starting companies is irrational and "on chromosome 13 or something", which is why he's doing a fourth for the same reason as the first. The higher reasons: giving jobs to people without college degrees (fewer than half of WP Engine's roughly 300 staff have degrees), hiring on attitude, aptitude, empathy, curiosity and accountability; benefits including same-sex marriage coverage even when small; and helping non-technical customers who would be victims of technology.
- WP Engine put some of its stock into a nonprofit and gives back to Austin and WordPress.
- He says entrepreneurs have "almost an obligation" to create important things; making money is fine, but ask what you do with it.
- Stress doesn't go away with experience. Personnel decisions (letting people go) stay emotionally hard. Letting someone leave a bad fit is doing them a favor, since they often won't leave on their own.
- What helps him: meditation, distancing himself from angry internet comments ("it's probably about them"), accepting that all companies end, and focusing on the journey over the exit.
- "No one in this room is their own boss": customers, the market, family and employees (whom you pay first) are all bosses.
