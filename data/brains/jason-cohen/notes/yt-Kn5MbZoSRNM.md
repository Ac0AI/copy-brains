## Founder burnout is predictable, and the cause is avoiding emotionally hard choices

- Jason cites a Columbia Business School study of 22 entrepreneurs who exited and made $10M+ personally. 21 of the 22 were depressed afterward. He says this is what "always happens," not a rare case. His example is Markus Persson, who sold Minecraft to Microsoft for hundreds of millions and spoke publicly about being near suicide.
- He compares it to frog boiling: nobody intends it, it unfolds slowly, and the "balloon payment of emotional toil" arrives at the end.
- His thesis is that you must make emotionally difficult choices early to build a healthier company and avoid that payoff.
- He has built companies both ways. He sold earlier startups for millions. At WP Engine (CTO and co-founder, about eight years in at the time of the talk) the company passed $100M ARR, and he is still there and not burned out.
- He read an anonymous Hacker News post from a founder who was about to be acquired and cried in the bathroom each morning. Jason says the stated reasons ("the product is too complex for anyone else," "it's boring so I can't retain developers") are good-sounding excuses, not the real reasons. The "only I can understand it" framing is itself the self-importance problem.

## Why founders fire too late, and how to act

- Jason asks a room who has fired someone too late (most hands) versus too soon (about 1 in 100). He calls this a predictable, universal failure of judgment.
- The excuses founders give:
  - "The team can't cope until I have a replacement."
  - "I'm a bad manager and haven't set expectations, so it's my fault."
  - He counters that if you are a bad manager, the other seven people on the team are thriving anyway, and the company has to operate now. Someone not fitting here does not make them a bad person.
- The real reason is simple: it is scary. He lists real risks (they cry, you cry, other employees react, someone turns violent, a former employee becomes depressed afterward). He says some of these have happened to him, including needing a restraining order and a death after a firing. He still says to do it.
- How to do it: act quickly, because delay hurts the team, the person and you. His CEO Heather Bruner's phrase: "General Patton on the decision, Mother Teresa on the exit." Be decisive, then be kind.

## A 2x2 for spotting avoided work: the 2am test

- Two axes: things you want vs. don't want to do, and things the business needs vs. doesn't need done. Founders drift into doing what they want that the business doesn't need, because no boss stops them.
- The task you should be doing but are avoiding is in the "needs doing, don't want to do" quadrant (for example sales work or dealing with a difficult person).
- His diagnostic: if you can't stop thinking about it when you wake at 2am, you are probably delaying something that needs to happen.
- His logic: when one choice is easy and one is hard, you only get stuck if the hard one is the better one. If the easy one were clearly right, there would be no dilemma.

## Build an organization that doesn't depend on you

- If "quitting would kill the company," Jason says the founder has built a brittle organization, either by not hiring people who can run functions or by not letting them.
- He rejects the "hire A players" slogan as incomplete. A founder who is an A player in one area (say, engineering) hires A players there. For everything else (AdWords, sales, marketing), founders try to learn it themselves over a few months so they can hire and manage well. Jason says that makes you a C player at best, and you end up with an organization where most functions are weak.
- His alternative is the editor model. He points to Steve Jobs as someone who hired the best designers and operations leader (Tim Cook) and held them to a very high standard, acting as editor rather than maker.
- The goal is that everyone in the organization is better than you in their area, so the company feels like it is boosting you rather than leaning entirely on you.

## How to hire and manage people who know more than you

- In interviews, look for someone who enlightens you: you learn a lot just by talking to them and think "even if we don't hire this person, we should do half of what they said." He also repeats Zuckerberg's idea: hire someone you'd work for if circumstances were a little different.
- Hire for results orientation, not action orientation. His event planner example:
  - Action-oriented answer: curtains, up-lights, a deal on the shrimp, "people seemed to really like it."
  - Results-oriented answer: the goal was 150 attendees; 200 registered and 168 came; leads came out of it, two have already closed; they plan three more events in other countries.
- Why it matters: by definition you can't tell them how to do their job, so you manage the results and hold them to the bar. People who like being measured by results are comfortable with that.
- He says this is the right way to manage everyone, including people in your own specialty. If the architect does all the inventing and everyone else does scut work, you have taken away the only fun part of the job.

## Selling, staying and stepping aside as CEO

- Jason argues "never sell" is not a rule. He founded SmartBear and sold it to Insight Partners in 2007. It was later sold again for $450 million (the talk says "Frisco Partners," likely a transcription error). He jokes that his regret about selling is real, but says the company was not on that trajectory under him and he was burnt out.
- When Insight made an offer, his wife told him he had to sell because he was unhappy, which he hadn't recognized. He then spent a year as a stay-at-home dad (2009), "wouldn't give that back for anything," and founded WP Engine in 2010.
- Counterexample for staying: other bootstrapped, profitable companies in his space did worse over a 15-year span, so neither "always sell" nor "never sell" holds in hindsight.
- WP Engine now has over $100M annual revenue, a $250M investment from Silver Lake, and by his account is worth more than SmartBear.
- Startups always change. The CEO's job is different at 100 and at 500 people (WP Engine was around 500 at the time). You may be unhappy, give up the CEO seat, or get bored after ten years, so you have to build change in.
- Heather Bruner joined WP Engine four years after founding and became CEO. Jason says much of today's success is due to her building the executive team and strategy, and that would not have happened with him as CEO. They won EY Entrepreneur of the Year together. Both she and he got credit; the executive team is majority female and half of engineering management is women.
- The emotional unlock, from a board member in an Austin bar: "You want the credit... you'll always get the credit." Jason says founders don't have to hold the CEO title for ego reasons.
- He knew at about 80 employees that he was not a good CEO, moved to CTO (product and engineering), stopped managing once engineering hit about 100 people, then ran product with zero direct reports.

## A career-fit framework: like, good at, and what the company needs

- Start with the Venn diagram of what you like and what you're good at. The overlap is where you are happy and effective. Liking but not good at it is learning; good at it but not liking it is toil.
- Then add a third circle: what the company needs. Jason says each mismatch is a failure mode.
  - Flow trap: you like it and are good at it, but the company doesn't need it (for example, an engineer shipping a feature three customers want when the company needs to double sales).
  - Wrong focus: you like it, the company needs it, but you aren't good at it. Startups are constrained by time more than money, so it has to be done well.
  - Burnout: you're good at it and the company needs it, but you don't like it. Jason criticizes "servant leadership" as a way founders take on scut work and burn out. Find someone who is good at it and likes it.
- The target is the center of all three circles. He admits it's an idealization and you can't live there always, but the more you do, the better.
- He encourages everyone at WP Engine to use this framework in career talks, and says if someone's overlap no longer exists at the company, that is an acceptable conversation to have. He frames fulfilled employees as more important than ARR.
- His closing quote is from the Tao Te Ching (the best leaders are barely known; "we did it ourselves"). His summary advice: hire the right people and let them do it themselves, be a shepherd not an emperor, be an editor not a tyrant, do the hard things, and set your ego aside.
