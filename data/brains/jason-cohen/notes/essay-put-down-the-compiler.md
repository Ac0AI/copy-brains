## Why people don't buy: talk to lost sales, not existing customers

Jason says an early-stage company's core problem is "we don't have enough sales", usually shown as many downloads or signups with few purchases. This is the stage where v1.0 is shaky but should be more viable than it is, and the website copy is good enough to get people to sign up or download. He says the problem has one solution: get feedback from lost sales, which means empirical data rather than the founder's own theories about why people aren't buying.

- The people to talk to are those who found the site, read the copy, downloaded the product, and then "gave up without even an email".
- They are the low-hanging fruit, "in your grasp", and should be buying today.
- They have self-identified as the ideal customer, yet the product didn't deliver on the promises made.
- He cites Steve Johnson: "All the answers are outside the building." He also cites Eric Ries: "Not listening is the cardinal sin."
- He admits he used to hold the "I already know why" attitude himself, and says technical founders fight this advice hardest.

## Existing customers can't explain why others don't buy

- Jason says to follow customer requests, especially early, but that existing customers only help increase the product's value.
- Customers bought in spite of the barriers to sale, so they can't identify those barriers.
- He calls using them to diagnose sales problems "classic survivor bias".
- The 98% of trial users who didn't buy hold the keys to more sales.

## "We need New Feature X" is almost never the answer

- He says "this is almost never true" and challenges readers to find a successful v1.0 product that lacked obvious features.
- The world is full of successful v1.0 products without obvious features.
- He points to a post by Ben Yoskovitz on the "one more feature" fallacy.
- He quotes a Nintendo line: "the most important feature is the one no one asks for."
- If a founder has no evidence a new release will lift revenue, he asks why release it. The only draw is that it's different, and "more stuff" may not address why people download and then abandon.
- Until the founder can answer that with empirical data, there is no reason to expect the new features to be more compelling than the last ones.

## Don't wait for polish, and don't trust your own taste as a user

- On cleaning up before seeking feedback: the first version of Smart Bear's code review product was so hard to decipher that he can't understand how it got customers. People used it "in spite" of the problems, not because of them.
- If you solve a genuine pain, people will try the software, complain, ask for features and engage. If that isn't happening, you're solving the wrong problem or not making it obvious, and that is critical to revenue.
- After a redesign you get used to it and are embarrassed by the old version. He concludes polish isn't important if you don't have enough revenue.
- Being a user yourself usually means you have 100 feature ideas, but "more features" is almost certainly not the problem. The vision is also almost certainly not how the company will unfold.
- Real impediments are often mundane. Examples he gives: new users see a blank screen and don't know what to do, so they abandon the trial; the installer fails under Vista, so people give up.
- Being a user is the worst position for judging the new-user experience, because you can't be objective or think like a user of below-average intelligence. He says "Most of your users are dumb; almost all are dumber than you are. You are not your typical user."

## Founders don't "just know" and can't hide in the code

- On "Apple just knows what's cool": he calls it completely untrue and notes Steve Jobs specifically talks about getting feedback from customers.
- On "revenue is a marketing/sales function, I need to be heads-down in the code": he says in a startup it is everyone's job to get revenue.
- Day-to-day tasks can be divided between founders, since not everyone needs to write to bloggers or watch Twitter live-search.
- But if you don't know why people aren't buying, that is the number one bug and number one feature to work on.
- He says there are many ways to change the product or website in under a day to start fixing the problem.
- He says "it's marketing's job" really means "I'm not going to help get revenue", and calls that unacceptable.
