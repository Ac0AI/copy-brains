## Booth positioning: a mascot as a traffic funnel, not a gimmick

Jason Cohen ran Smart Bear Software, which sold peer code review tools and an Eclipse/Subversion integration. His company mascot was a bear, played by developer Roy, and he treated the bear as a real tradeshow tactic at SD West. He explains why it worked, with specific mechanics.

- Tradeshow booths "need something to help them stand out", and a person in a bear suit is one way to do it.
- Everyone notices the bear. Most people drift toward it and are usually smiling. A few go out of their way to avoid getting close or making "eye contact".
- Roy, who writes Eclipse plug-ins, really wanted to be the bear and was good at it. He spent a lot of time in the bear head at the office to learn where the ears, head, eyes and chin were. Jason once saw him fixing an obscure bug in the Subversion/Eclipse integration while wearing the full outfit.

## Physical placement: why the bear in the aisle funnels people to the booth

Jason calls the bear's position in the aisle in front of the booth "unintentionally brilliant". His chain of reasoning:

1. You have to walk around the bear, because the suit plus personal space creates a significant no-fly zone.
2. There was always a crowd around the bear checking it out and seeing if it could talk, which enlarges that zone.
3. To get past, you must swing all the way to one side of the aisle (where the booth was) or the other.
4. So you end up getting the pitch and seeing the product even if you avoid the bear.
5. The leftover space between bear and booth was so small it created a logjam. Stuck in front of the booth, you might as well watch the movie and take a free book.

The takeaway is that booth layout can force exposure. The attention-getter and the pitch material (a movie, a free book) should be arranged so avoiding one means meeting the other.

## Using the mascot to start real conversations

- People assume a poor high school student was hired to stand around in the suit. So when the bear engages someone in a discussion of peer code review, they are surprised enough to actually talk about it.
- Cupping the vestigial ears and bending them forward while saying "come again" makes people crane their necks toward the fluffy blobs. Jason notes no one considers that this doesn't actually help a human hear better.
- At least 10 other vendors at the show commented "That guy must have lost a bet", so the bear drew attention from peers as well as prospects.
- The bear also drew unsolicited jokes (Stephen Colbert bear references) and even a proposition: a woman said it would be great to have a bear mow the lawn. Roy said the bear was not for sale. Jason thought he should have heard her out, since "everything can be had for a price, even a mowing bear".

## Awards shows: standing out by staying calm

- The bear also attended the Jolt Award ceremony and stood out there. The presenter (Craig) mentioned the bear three times during the ceremony.
- Eric's explanation, which Jason relays: it worked because Roy was not jumping around or calling attention to himself. He sat calmly, as if it's normal that a bear would attend the Jolt Award.
- Lesson: a memorable gimmick lands better when played deadpan than when it tries hard for attention.

## Limits of the stunt

- Jason's P.S.: the bear was not a juggling bear. Roy couldn't see well enough out of the neck area to juggle. He got three balls going by throwing them off his furry belly to reduce error, but it looked weird, so the idea was dropped.
- Jason also notes Roy does juggle flaming clubs outside the suit, which is why a juggling bear seemed plausible.
