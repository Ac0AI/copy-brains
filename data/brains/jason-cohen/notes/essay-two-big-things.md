## You have a fixed time budget, so only two Big Things fit

- Jason's rule: "You can have two 'Big Things' in your life, but not three."
- The reason is time. The 1950s-style split of a 24-hour day is 8 hours for work, 8 for home and commute, and 8 for sleep and ablutions. Work and home are the two things that can each take 40+ hours a week.
- That 40+ hours a week is what it takes to tackle something huge: a career, a parent, a startup.
- Weekends, vacations and sick days don't add up to enough concentrated time to carry off something like a startup without making work or home suffer.
- "Work" and "home" are placeholders. If you're unattached, "home" doesn't occupy significant time, so something else can take that slot.

## What counts as a Big Thing

- Job
- Kids
- Spouse
- Social life
- Major hobby (his examples: building a boat in the garage, becoming a chess master, a video game addiction)
- Startup

He lists kids and spouse separately. Young kids strain marriages because there isn't enough time to invest in the kids and also be there for each other.

## What happens when you try for three

- Founders who run a startup on the side while holding a day job: "your family will never see you. You might even lose your family. It happens."
- This is partly why Jason says it's easier to start a company before you have a family or even a spouse.
- A job plus a social life works only if your spouse is fully integrated into, and agreeable to, that social life. Otherwise there's strife ("Going out with the guys *again?*").
- People of both genders try to "have it all", but "it's never true." At most two function well and the rest do not.
- More often, one thing gets most of the energy and the rest suffers.

## Why sleep is not a lever

- Sleep takes the same kind of time commitment, but it isn't on the list of options.
- Cutting sleep doesn't work, because then you can't function at a high level at anything.

## Don't assume you're the exception

- Jason's line: "No, you are not an exception. That's egotistical self-deception." He applies this both to sleep and to the number of Big Things.
- His test: ask the people around you whether they think you're failing at one of your Big Things.
- His conclusion: "Time to decide which two."

## Decision rule for founders

- Before starting a company, count your current Big Things. A startup is one, so it leaves room for only one other.
- If you already have a job, a spouse and kids, a startup makes three and something will break, usually family or the startup.
- If you're single and unattached, a startup plus a job (or a startup plus a social life) is workable because "home" takes little time.
