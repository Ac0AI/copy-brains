## Pascal's Wager as a decision rule: assume humility, because it cannot fail

- Jason takes Pascal's Wager (act as if God exists, because the downside of being wrong is tiny and the upside is huge) and reuses only its structure: choose the behavior that does well in every possible world.
- He is clear it is not a valid argument for God's existence. Substitute any God humans have worshipped and it "proves" belief in that one, so it is "both invalid and irrational". He keeps it as a tool for choosing between assumptions, not as proof of anything.
- Its link to maximizing expected value: some assumptions pay off in all outcomes, others only sometimes. Pick the first kind.
- He notes this is not how to size large bets. It is for choosing a default posture.
- The core claim: "Being humble cannot fail; being arrogant can."
- Founders decide from scant knowledge. In a startup essentially every decision is an educated guess, but "not terribly educated". That is why the humble default beats the confident one.

## Contacting support: open with curiosity or helplessness, not accusation

- Example from WP Engine tech support: customers often open from a posture of accusation ("I didn't *do* anything!"), implying the company screwed up. Jason says that is occasionally true, but usually it is ignorance, someone else on their team, or the customer themselves, hoping to be rescued. (He says support will rescue them.)
- Support staff are human, not "mechanized automatons" immune to rude language or assumed incompetence. They chose a field where they "help people" for a living, which he calls a noble profession, and they get little thanks call after call.
- If you open from helplessness or curiosity, people are inclined to help even when it is your fault.
- If it turns out not to be your fault, the person is even more sympathetic: you are "the nicest person on Earth, blaming themselves from the get-go", and yet it was their fault. They will personally fight to fix it.
- The usual behavior is the opposite. Customers are angry and take it out on the one person who is their only link to a fix. Support has metrics that punish it if they don't do their job, but anger is still not the way to get the most out of people.
- His test question: "is that the way to get most out of other people?"

## The assumption to adopt: you are ignorant and missing information

- "You can't fail if you assume you're ignorant, that you're missing information, that you're ready to learn, that you need help to understand."
- Humility is a default assumption about your own state of knowledge. It is not politeness or a tactic layered on top.
- If you are right anyway, the humble approach costs you nothing. If you are wrong, it saves you from acting on a false premise.

## Where it applies: any channel, especially email

- It is not specific to support. It applies anywhere, any time: tech support, vetting a new idea (he links to customer development), arguing with a spouse, or exchanging emails or tweets with a stranger.
- It matters most in email and other text where emotion is hard to convey and text gets misread.
- Misreading is common: people read things that do not exist "in-between lines that aren't the lines you thought they were".
- For vetting ideas, the same posture means going in assuming you are missing information, not defending the idea.

## A one-week experiment

- Jason's challenge: act humble in every situation for a week and observe.
- Questions to watch for:
  - Do you discover you are wrong more often, and about more things, than you thought?
  - Do you find you misread, or read something that was not there?
  - In the cases where you are correct, do people respond more positively, learn too, and go out of their way to make things better?
- His framing: "Could the simple act of being humble be life-altering?"
- His closing argument: "You have nothing to lose and everything to gain by trying. Like Pascal."
