## Meetings are for leveraging the team while the individual keeps ownership

Jason argues meetings are most productive when they create something none of the attendees could have made alone ("all of us were smarter than any of us"). The failure mode is a person who asks for help and gets overridden: someone wants ideas but is told which idea is best, or wants help choosing between two vetted options and receives seven new ones. His fix is to tap the wisdom of the crowd while the decision and responsibility stay with one individual.

## Four meeting formats, each defined by what the requester needs

Jason defines each activity by what the one person wants from the room, and by what they do not want.

- **Ranting:** the person wants to get something off their chest in a trusted place, including vulnerable things ("I don't know where to start", "I'm scared to face this") or things that sound alarming ("I'm doubting my strategy and I'm worried how my team will react"). They want understanding and support, not a fix. Example: with "people problems" in the org, he doesn't need everyone to jump in, only for the room to "be kind" to the teams and allow extra time. Payoff: lifts emotional weight and raises cohesion, trust, vulnerability and empathy.
- **Ideation:** the person is stuck or tired of their own ideas and wants new causes, solutions or options. An email goes out ahead so the group has time to think. They don't want the puzzle solved or an option picked. Example: a strategic challenge that must solve two apparently contradictory things. Payoff: ideas flow without anyone telling you what to do, and that can't be done alone.
- **Decision-Support:** the person knows the options and has analyzed them (sent ahead), but the choice is complex. They want input on evaluating the options or on what basis to decide, and may ask the room to vote, not to dictate the outcome but as one more input. The best option may not be on the list yet, so synthesizing a new one is fine, but "I don't want seven new ideas; I want to make a decision." He notes that prioritization articles, ROI rubrics and multi-dimensional charts don't make complex decisions easy; seeing your choices reflected in others' views helps.
- **Sparring Practice:** the person wants a Devil's Advocate to attack a strategy, feature concept, keynote, reorg proposal or UX design in a safe space, to surface real flaws or deflect attacks before they start. Attacks from a mistaken perspective are still useful, because future readers may share the misunderstanding. Payoff: "a brief, tight, bullet-proof document" takes a lot of work, and no one can foresee every (mis)interpretation or counter-argument alone.

## Declare the conversation up front

Jason says it is counterproductive when someone wants a Ranting but receives Sparring Practice. The requester should state which kind of conversation they want so everyone participates the right way. A facilitator can then reroute the discussion when it strays from those parameters.

## Frame meetings around "I", not "we", to keep ownership clear

Jason points out that every format above is "selfish": it is about what one person needs. This contrasts with how teams usually talk, e.g. "We need to decide on our Q2 priorities." His preferred framing: "Person X will decide the Q2 priorities by Friday, so let's discuss to ensure everyone is heard, and the best ideas are incorporated."

- It is healthy for X to seek consensus and be genuinely influenced by trusted teammates, especially when opinions are unanimous.
- But "a decision made by 'us' is owned by 'no one'," when in fact it is owned by X. Acknowledging that is useful even while spending about 98% of the conversation discussing as equals.

## Protecting autonomy when the boss is in the room

Jason says this framing also pushes back on top-down control where a decision is meant to sit with a self-managed, empowered team. His example: person P owns a project and asks for help in a room with peers and Q, P's boss. The room should help using the formats above, so P leverages the room's brainpower but stays in command, keeping responsibility and autonomy even with Q present. He calls it "a wonderful experience for a leader to assist without usurping."
