## Virality needs a message that gets repeated, not just read (the "mule" test)

Jason argues "survival of the fittest" is an incomplete reading of Darwin. His example is the mule: it survives, eats and avoids predators, but it is sterile, so it is a genetic dead end ("Mule == Evolution Fail"). The better definition of fitness is "children who themselves make the fittest children."

- Applied to sharing, a message is not viral because your direct followers (the children) find it interesting.
- It is viral when those followers want to repeat it to their own audience (the grandchildren), so a chain reaction continues.
- He frames the contest as competing for attention instead of food. A link "goes viral" when it is being retransmitted (retweeted) more often than it dies (not retweeted).
- He dislikes the phrase "going viral" itself, comparing it to "a one-night stand without protection" and a self-fulfilling prophecy.

## The chain letter shows the mechanism: the message itself demands retransmission

Jason points to the chain letter as the original viral message. It spread not because you sent it to ten people, but because it required each recipient to do the same.

- His takeaway: "The message itself needs to suggest retransmission."
- The message must contain the instruction or invitation to pass it on. Hoping people share it is not enough.

## Ask for the retweet explicitly, and write the retweet for them

The simplest tactic is to ask outright. He contrasts a plain tweet of a link with the same tweet that says "please RT". Because most people retweet with the retweet button, the plea is preserved and carried to the next generation.

- Example tweet text he offers: "[Cool Tool, plz R/T!] Find out who's talking about your blog! http://LinksFor.Us"
- The ask has to be explicit, because without it the message does not suggest its own retransmission.

## Remove every barrier to sharing: do the thinking for your friends

Jason uses a product launch example (v1.0 of a free blogger service, LinksFor.Us) to show how not to ask for help.

- Bad ask: "tell everyone you know and help me kick-start this. I'll buy you a beer." He says this "gives Francis work to do": what to post, how to say it in 140 characters, how much time to spend, and whether Francis is even sober.
- Better ask: "Tear down every barrier they have for retransmission. Do the thinking for them."
- Steps in the improved email:
  1. Frame the request as small: "just 60 seconds of your time."
  2. Supply a ready-made tweet.
  3. Supply ready-made text for an email or LinkedIn message, written so the recipient can paste it as-is (for example, "Hi! My friend Jason just released a new, free service for bloggers that shows who is talking about or linking to your blog posts.").
  4. Thank them in advance and offer a small thank-you (a beer).

## The real driver: content so good people want to spread it

Jason admits that when his own posts went viral, it was not because of these techniques. It was because many people found the content "genuinely useful or entertaining or inspiring, or hopefully all of the above."

- His number one rule: "Have overwhelmingly awesome content." It should be so good that people intrinsically want to spread it. Then it is worth being smart about helping them do so.
- Evidence: the most popular YouTube videos (the most viral of all) are not company campaigns pushing a product or service, with music videos the exception. People shared them because they plainly liked them, not because of a crafted campaign.
- He has no formula for it: "What's the mechanistic formula for generating incredible, remarkable content? Ha ha, let me know when you find one."

## Being noticed is not being remarkable

Jason endorses Seth Godin's distinction: "Being noticed is not the same as being remarkable. Running down the street naked will get you noticed, but it won't accomplish much. ... If you put it on a T-shirt, would people wear it?"

- Stunts that win attention do not necessarily earn the repeat sharing that drives a chain reaction.
- A useful test for virality is whether people would voluntarily attach their name to your message and pass it on.
