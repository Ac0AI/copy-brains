## Worry is self-fulfilling: it injects the failure it fears

- Jason argues that worrying causes the thing you're worried about to happen. "Worry" breaks things which aren't broken. It doesn't lead constructively to solutions, it only creates problems.
- Backstage before a performance, mounting anxiety injects second-guessing and fear into the performance itself. His fix is to recognize that the practice is already done, and that mistakes don't matter much because "No one is counting except you."
- At a pool table, a tennis serve or a golf swing, thinking through everything you're supposed to do while executing inevitably ruins the shot.
- His conclusion: worry is "worse than unhelpful--it's destructive." It might even be better to be blissfully ignorant than to worry.

## The opposite of worry is preparation, not apathy

- Jason says the opposite of "Worry" isn't "Apathy". The opposite is "Preparation".
- The recipe: tackle the root of the worry constructively, prepare as best you can, then "just proceed."
- Preparation replaces anxious rumination with something that can actually change the outcome.

## Long-term preparation: purposeful repetition and season planning

- He points to what great athletes do: they pummel themselves with purposeful repetition of specific skills, to the point of boredom.
- They plan energy output and practice schedules across an entire season so they peak at the most important meets.
- This is the slow version of preparation: build the skill and the schedule ahead of time, so the big moment is not the place where you try to figure things out.

## Short-term preparation: visualize, then clear your mind and execute

- Right before execution, Jason recommends visualization. He notes it is common to all sport and its efficacy has been verified by a robust set of experiments.
- Examples he gives: Olympic downhill skiers, gymnasts and ice skaters with eyes closed, mentally running their routines and routes, swaying with muted motions.
- Billiard players visualize the motion of the cue ball before they strike, but clear their minds as they actually strike.
- The sequence for a high-stakes moment: visualize ahead of time, clear your mind, then execute using muscle memory. Do not think while doing.

## Applying it to startups: what founders worry about

- Jason says the lesson is generic and applies to all of life, including startups.
- Business worries he lists:
  - How to make payroll.
  - How the competition will hurt you.
  - What someone said about you on Twitter.
  - Whether the visual design isn't compelling.
  - Whether you'll close the customer on this call.
  - Whether you're optimizing for the right metric.
  - Whether you don't know the right way to do things.
- Each of these gets the same treatment: find the root, prepare, proceed.

## Worry turned inward on yourself

- He calls out a worse form, "turning the gun on yourself": worrying that it's just too hard, that you're not following good advice, that everything you do is unsatisfactory.
- Also worrying that others are so much better than you, and therefore that you have no business doing any of this in the first place.
- He describes startups as a crucible and these as among the symptoms, pointing to his related essays on startups being emotionally draining, feelings of inadequacy, impostor syndrome and being yourself.
