## Why small, focused startups survive recessions ("too small to fail")

Jason argues that a startup aiming at a small, focused niche can keep growing through a recession, because small removes the risks that kill big, funded companies.

Evidence he cites (2009):
- Bingo Card Creator (Patrick McKenzie, one-man company, launched 2006) just had its biggest month ever. February, March and April 2009 were its three biggest months since inception.
- Balsamiq Studios (UI mock-up product) netted $162k in its first year, 2008. It took in $35k in the first week of April 2009 alone.
- His label for both: "too small to fail."

## The condition: small niche, strong need, happy customers

- Jason's formula: if the niche is small enough, the customer need strong enough, the marketing targeted enough, the product good enough and the customers happy enough, they will buy, recession or not.
- He admits it is a lot of "if"s, but argues it can work.

## Match the goal to the size: $200k to $1M a year, not $100M

- Aiming to sell $100,000,000 of software a year will almost certainly fail. It takes years, many people you haven't hired, a massive market, beatable competitors, and probably debt or investors. A down economy could be the undoing.
- Aiming to run a smaller successful business, happy at $1,000,000/year or even $200,000/year, makes many problems fall away.
- He says "when you just need to be Ramen-profitable," you can do it even in a recession.
- "Prevailing wisdom is that 'small is risky.' It's just the opposite."

## Marketing is cheap in a small market

- You don't need a big budget to get noticed. You know where customers gather: forums, blogs, community sites, influencers, local groups.
- You know the AdWords keywords and they're easy to optimize (he points to Patrick's AdWords optimization write-up).
- It takes time, not a lot of money, to participate and get noticed.

## Become the world expert of the niche

In a small market you can build:
- The most popular blog and most frequented forum.
- The best collection of how-tos and the most comprehensive eBook.
- The simplest yet most complete software.
- Jason's own example: the most popular paperback book on a subject, 50,000 copies and counting.

## Competitors are manageable

- Competitors are easy to find, track and analyze. They may be weak, or there may be room for several winners.
- You may never meet an 800-lb gorilla, because the addressable market is too small for it to be profitable. His example: "Microsoft isn't going to make a Bingo-card creator."
- A competitor starting now will be far behind; if you keep moving they won't catch up.

## Delight customers one by one

- You can build fans and cheerleaders who will buy everything you make and spread the word (the "1,000 true fans" idea).
- You can quickly collect testimonials that sell better than any brochure.
- His advice: "just be yourself."

## Why recessions hit small markets less

- Customers have a strong need, not a passing interest.
- Existing customers have a personal relationship with you and will go out of their way to help you succeed.
- Because you're small, 99.99% of your customers are still in your future, so there are always new customers to find.
- Small also opens options that incumbents can't compete with.
- His closing wager: in a year, who survives, Balsamiq or a company with $30m paid-in capital that "wasn't profitable even before the recession" but has "amazing growth potential in a hot market"? And "whose founders are more likely to put $1m cash in their pocket over the next few years?" His summary question: "what's wrong with small?"
