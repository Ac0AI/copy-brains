## Building in public as a forcing function for quality code

- Jason's premise: if you had to put all your proprietary, "secret-sauce" code in a public GitHub repository, you would be judged by peers on file and class organization, documentation, tests, removing reliance on "security by obscurity," tracking to-dos, and the artful quality of "you know it when you see it."
- The force at work is "Ego--your desire to impress others."
- His conclusion: "building in public would force you to create quality, artful code."

## Building in public forces non-technical competitive advantage

- The objection is that competitors can copy you if they see your code and growth rate. Jason's answer is that this pushes you to build a non-technical [competitive advantage](https://longform.asmartbear.com/moats/) into the business.
- Etsy example: if Etsy open-sourced its entire stack, a competitor still couldn't overtake it. Etsy has built a marketplace, and the presence of buyers and sellers is its primary asset, not the software behind the website or payments.
- Facebook example: it open-sourced its entire data center infrastructure, and that doesn't make it easy to overtake Facebook as a social network. The value in these companies "cannot be replicated with money," which is why they are valuable.
- Technical advantage can be permanent in select cases (he points to a case study of Amazon AWS), but this is rare.
- Typically a technical advantage is fleeting because most technical things can be replicated, often faster and more cheaply than the original inventors did it. "First to market" often doesn't equate to "winning the market."
- His conclusion: "building in public forces you to create permanent competitive advantages."

## Public salaries force a culture-driven talent strategy

- Building in public also applies to organizations and people, not just software.
- If you publish the salary formula for everyone at the company, you can no longer compete for talent on compensation alone. You can't add an extra $10k/year to win a critical employee with especially germane experience.
- He acknowledges it can be weird for everyone to know what everyone else makes, but says that for many people "it's not weird, it's fantastic."
- The constraint forces you to build a company where great talent wants to work, with people there for culture and mission rather than as mercenaries.

## Three ingredients of a culture that attracts and retains talent

Jason names three elements common to frameworks for building such an environment:

- **Autonomy:** freedom to explore, plus control and empowerment to invent solutions to problems and prove their quality through implementation, in exchange for accountability for the result.
- **Growth:** working on interesting puzzles, personal or professional, along a fulfilling journey.
- **Purpose:** why the company should exist, why it's worth busting your ass to see it thrive, the culture and values, and the joy of working with people you admire.

His conclusion: "building in public forces you to create a company with exceptional culture and an organization of empowerment and purpose."

## Why this produces durable companies

- Writing code you can be proud of, building permanent competitive advantages, and creating a culture that attracts talent "for reasons beyond legal bribery" are critical ingredients of durable companies.
- Durable companies keep their growth and brand unhindered by the "onslaught of well-funded, hard-working, intelligent upstarts" that inevitably join any market big enough to be interesting.
- Building everything in public forces you to do "what's most valuable" and "what isn't easily copyable," whether that is the quality of workmanship, the fortress of the business model, or a talent magnet.
- He allows that operating in secret is still possible and occasionally advisable, but says "sunlight isn't just the best disinfectant, it's a great way to forge durability."
