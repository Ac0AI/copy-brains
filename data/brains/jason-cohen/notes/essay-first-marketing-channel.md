## Pick the channel at the intersection of two constraints

Jason argues that a brand-new company has far fewer viable marketing channels than the "dozens" on offer. Constraints of time, money, brand, reach and specific knowledge about customers rule most of them out. He turns those constraints into the selection method: your first channel must sit where two things overlap.

- Where your customers already are.
- What comes naturally to you.

He frames this as a Venn diagram. If a channel is outside either circle, it is not your first channel.

## Constraint one: fish where the fish are

Jason treats customer location as something you do not control, so it is a hard constraint. You go where customers already engage rather than trying to pull them elsewhere.

His examples:

- Accounting software: customers are mostly on LinkedIn, some on Twitter, occasionally Threads, maybe Facebook for an older audience. Not Pinterest, Snapchat or TikTok.
- Miniature-alpaca yarn for weavers: Facebook, Pinterest and possibly Threads, plus weaving clubs (a better target than selling to consumers one by one), magazines (even print) and popular influencers.

Questions he says to ask beyond social networks:

- Which keywords do they search when they hit the problem you solve?
- Which podcasts do they listen to?
- Which YouTube channels do they subscribe to?
- Which consultants do they already pay for problems like yours? (Some answers will be "none".)

## Constraint two: what comes naturally to you

Jason pushes back on the usual advice to leave your comfort zone and become an "expert" in a hundred hours of coursework. His view: "Doing something unnatural, that you despise, will fail."

- You can spot impostors on any network you like: people who don't understand the medium and exploit it for selfish marketing. It's awkward, gets no engagement, and the algorithm silently buries it.
- You will also hate using a platform you disdain, and that disdain leaks onto the page and makes the work less effective.
- His own example: he doesn't understand Pinterest (boards, sharing, even what they're called), so he'd be bad at it and hate it. "Ineffective and unhappy--is this why you started a company?"

The practical step is to take stock of your Pivot Points and consider only marketing options you are naturally good at or genuinely wish to become great at. Pick your first channel from that subset.

## A smaller channel you're good at beats a bigger one you aren't

Jason says to operate where customers are and where the work comes naturally and joyfully, and that practice then moves you from good to great. That is unlikely if you pick a poor fit.

This holds even when the channel is low-volume. His example: customers are mostly on LinkedIn, but you don't get LinkedIn (it looks like AI, seems fake), while a small, vocal community exists on Threads.

- "It is better to be one of the top three creators on this topic on Threads than to be the 200th biggest creator on LinkedIn."
- Algorithms don't promote the 200th-biggest thing; they do promote the 3rd-biggest.
- You can be 10x or 100x more effective in your messaging, so a smaller audience can still produce success.

## Expand only after you're powerful on one channel

Once you are powerful on one medium, you can expand to adjacent ones. With a foothold on Threads and revenue that can pay for consulting, tools or a part-time marketer, you repurpose your Threads content in the style of LinkedIn. You enter the second channel "from a position of strength."

Jason's standing rule: pick one marketing channel and go deep. Don't spread across several channels, none of which work well.

## When the intersection is empty

Sometimes your customers are only on Facebook and you despise Facebook. Jason offers two outcomes:

- **This may be the wrong business for you.** If you're so different from your customers that you can't relate to them, you may not be the right person to run it. The business might be great for someone else.
- **Partner with someone for whom the channel is a superpower.** He describes a Facebook wizard who does in a month what takes others a year. You then focus on the parts of the business that are your superpowers while they bring in new customers daily. He says this could be the team that maximizes the chance the company works.

What he rejects is forcing yourself to figure out the channel you hate. You could, since it's your life and company, but you're adding risk to a venture that is already very risky, and that is "not the right way to solve the equation."
