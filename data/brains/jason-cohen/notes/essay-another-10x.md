## Peaking is not failing: growth multiples fade for every company
- Jason argues that the "how do we 10x" question is right for a young startup but cannot be the right question forever. "No company 10x's forever. Not even Google."
- He points to Google as proof: it became a Berkshire Hathaway-style conglomerate of separate companies, each of which could potentially 10x on its own.
- His opening questions are what-ifs: Dropbox is a great file-sharing and backup service but not a universal key/value storage API. OpenTable is a perfect reservation system and nothing more. Yelp has ratings but can't solve food-delivery logistics. Twitter never 10x's its user base but stays a critical communication and news system for a decade.
- Facebook is his example of a plateau: it can make $20b/yr in advertising, but with teens and the social media elite gone it may never make $200b/yr.
- A 2024 editor's note checks the predictions. Dropbox, OpenTable, Yelp and Twitter all proved true. Meta projected $160B for 2024, about $100B of it from Facebook, so the Facebook call was half-right: 5x over 8 years, not 10x.
- The note adds that Facebook's attempts at a virtual currency and chat monetization failed, that the Instagram and WhatsApp acquisitions were critical for growth, and that Metaverse and AI efforts burned tens of billions with no substantial results. Jason says this shows "how difficult it is to '10x forever.'"

## Why growth-multiple valuation distorts how tech companies are judged
- Jason notes that in tech, "growth" is treated as the prime determinant of a business's value. Valuations are usually quoted as a multiple of revenue, and higher growth earns a higher multiple.
- If growth dips below the level of "rivets rattling out of the plane," revenue multiples plummet. The press then writes the company off as a has-been, ripe for disruption by startups 1/1000th its size that are "growing exponentially."
- His example: Microsoft made more cash profit in 2015 than Facebook made in revenue, yet was worth only 12% more in Enterprise Value. The market was pricing in the chance that Facebook could keep adding ads, invent a virtual currency or monetize chat users.
- The point is that a profitable, durable business can be valued far below its cash generation purely because its growth rate is lower.

## You can't know whether you're done 10x'ing, but plan for the possibility
- Jason concedes that you can't know right now whether you've finished 10x'ing.
- His argument is conditional: if another 10x is not a plausible outcome, you should redirect attention instead of keeping all effort on growth.
- He treats "just ploughing back into a 10x growth objective" as an optimal strategy only up to a point. After that it is no longer the best way to make a "dent in the universe."
- The practical test he implies is to ask honestly whether a further 10x is plausible, rather than assuming growth is the only acceptable path.

## What to do instead: apply the company to betterment, not just biggerment
- Jason lists what a company could focus on once relentless growth stops being the right goal:
  - Create more jobs and better working conditions.
  - Be a catalyst for employees' personal growth.
  - Give back to relevant communities.
  - Use its logistics, technology, infrastructure and cost savings to do something important with the success it has earned.
- He says it is exactly when a company is applied to "betterment instead of (only) biggerment" that its true dent in the universe is determined.
- He frames this as a legacy question and closes with "What will be your legacy?"
