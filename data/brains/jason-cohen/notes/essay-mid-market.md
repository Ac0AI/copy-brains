## Why the mid-market is the worst of both worlds

Jason argues that selling to "mid-sized" companies (his example is around 400 employees) looks like the best of both worlds and is actually the worst.

- The pitch he hears: small businesses have no money, large companies already use Entrenched Competitors X and Y, so "the opportunity is in the middle."
- Startup graveyards are "littered with companies who tried to target this seemingly alluring market segment": customers supposedly nimble and open to new technology, yet big enough to spend real money.
- He calls it "a tar-pit" and his reaction to anyone "selling to middle" is "Yuck."
- There isn't necessarily an opportunity there. Incumbents may have already figured out that the middle isn't cost-effective to sell to.
- If you do it anyway, he hopes you have "extenuating circumstances that truly makes you the exception to the rule."

## Mid-sized companies are not nimble, but they have the same bureaucracy as big ones

- At about 400 employees they have already built much of the "lumbering process and bureaucracy" of companies ten times their size.
- Budgets and internal politics shackle them. Technology changes require expensive coordination and retraining, and "fear of change trumps potential rewards from improvement."
- The result is an arduous sales process like a large company's, without a large company's budget. There is no big reward for getting through the "painful, Herculean sales adventure."
- Why it happens, in his words:
  - Possibly a parsimonious small-business founder still "straightening used staples to save pennies" (he includes himself).
  - Even a few dozen people split into teams, departments and roles. Communication limits and tribal behavior produce semi-autonomous isolation, which gets paired with formal processes for command-and-control. A bureaucracy is born that is "self-generating and largely inescapable."
  - Some of the reasons are genuinely good ones.

## The numbers argument ("look how many there are") falls apart

- There are 220,000 US businesses with 50-500 employees and only 18,000 with more than 500. This is the usual argument for targeting the mid-sized.
- The average mid-sized company has 119 employees; the average large one has 3,100.
- If you sell a seat to everyone inside a company, you must sell about 30 mid-sized companies for every large one.
- Total employees: about 26M at mid-sized companies versus 60M at large ones.
- Sales effort is roughly the same whether you sell 75 seats or 750, so the mid-market means much more work for the revenue.

## "Mid-sized companies" isn't really a market segment

- Size says little. Industry, the problem being solved, and the manner of solving it matter far more.
- Example: a 100-person tech startup has a particular mix of developers, QA, tech support, sales and HR. A 100-person plastics manufacturer has a very different mix: no developers, probably not tech-savvy, and IT run by a Windows "power user" who runs Exchange, has never used Google Apps, and doesn't know how to open a firewall port that "my Cisco guy" installed.
- For something like server monitoring software, everything differs between the two: how you find them, what pain you solve, and what the user wants to see in the interface.
- Both monitoring 100 computers is only vaguely "the same." There are more differences than similarities.
- He points to defining your target market by the problem instead (linked from the essay as a better way to choose a market).

## WP Engine: the squeeze in practice

- His initial WP Engine idea was to target the "mid-market" of WordPress: blogs and sites too big for cheap hosting on speed, scale and support, but too small to afford full-time WordPress experts or Automattic's "WordPress VIP" program at $2,500/mo and up.
- The segment had the classic problem: small-blogger budget meets big-blogger problems.
- Trade-offs he weighed:
  - A $50/mo blogger has many of the same problems as a $2,000/mo blogger but can afford 40x less.
  - Many $50/mo bloggers never call and never have problems, so profit margin is great even if absolute profit is smaller.
  - A $2,000/mo blogger constantly hits new speed and scalability challenges, because even small configuration changes are magnified by 10,000,000 hits a month. Expensive expert human time vaporizes the supposed profit.
- His conclusion: pick one end or the other and optimize hiring, automation, marketing and sales around it. His 2012 note adds "(Narrator: They did.)"
- 2024 editor's note: WP Engine did intentionally expand into mid-market and even enterprise, but only once it could spend tens of millions of dollars on marketing, sales, service, account management, compliance and other things that made it work.

## Takeaways for choosing a segment

- Don't treat "the middle" as an opportunity just because small is too poor and large is taken. Ask why incumbents skipped it.
- Choose one end and build the whole company (hiring, automation, marketing, sales) around that customer.
- Segment by industry and problem, not company size.
- Count revenue per sales effort, not number of companies. Seat count per deal matters more than total business count.
- Expanding into the mid-market can work later, but only with the resources to fund the sales, service and compliance machinery it demands.
