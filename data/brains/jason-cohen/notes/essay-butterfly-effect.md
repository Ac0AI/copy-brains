## Big, non-subliminal effects beat subtle nudges

- Jason is skeptical of "butterfly effect" marketing: the idea that tiny changes (a button color, a logo palette, a background shade) reliably cause big outcomes. He grants it is a seductive story and that psychology studies show subtle environmental effects on behavior.
- His core objection is that real life, and the internet especially, is a "hailstorm of influences." If a person is "constantly influenced in all directions, the forces cancel each other out, and none of them matter."
- The internet is noisier still. Competing with the real world and with "tabbed browsing, Twitter alerts, back buttons, bouncing tray icons, and instant messaging" drowns out most subtle effects.
- His conclusion: "Take care of the big stuff."
- A 2024 editor's note adds that most of the cited psychology studies (priming, marble-moving, old-age words) have since been debunked because they can't be reproduced. That is further reason to doubt applying them in marketing, even for the subset that may be true.

## What counts as "big stuff" in marketing

Jason contrasts subtle tweaks with "big-ass, in-yo-face, non-subliminal effects." His examples:

- **A popular blogger mentioning you** is more influential than your logo color. His example: "200 words from Seth Godin is good for 10s of 1000s of unique visitors."
- **A compelling story people intrinsically want to spread** beats building a snappy animation for your home page.
- **Thrilling one person in a product demo** is worth more money than 1,000 website visitors being "branded" as trustworthy by a steel-blue palette and stoic font. He says he'd rather have "one Tom" (a delighted customer) than ten thousand StumbleUpon hits.

## How to prioritize effort

- Spend time on the large levers (distribution, word of mouth, deep customer delight) rather than chasing optimizations that "will be wiped away by the ambient noise of life."
- Treat claims like "a small change to your download page results in 20% more trials" or "a subtle shift in background color increases time-on-site by 27 seconds" with suspicion, since they are exactly the stories that optimization consultants want you to believe.
- He is not saying design and attention to detail are worthless ("although maybe it isn't" valuable, he notes, pointing to a separate essay on design). His point is that most such efforts are overwhelmed by distraction.

## Why the subtle-effects story is appealing but misleading

- It is believable because the mathematical butterfly effect is real for certain complex systems such as weather, economics and fractals.
- It is comforting because it implies everyone has power to change the world through tiny actions.
- It is attractive to marketers as an "inside scoop about how to manipulate strangers on the sly," like subliminal messaging but more pervasive.
- Jason's counter: even if individual stimuli work in a lab, in the wild they are one of thousands competing, so betting your marketing on them is a poor use of time.
