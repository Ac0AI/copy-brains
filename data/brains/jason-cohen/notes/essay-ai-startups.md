## Why AI startups can't rely on the usual David-vs-Goliath playbook

Jason argues that the standard startup-versus-incumbent dynamics held in the internet and mobile revolutions but do not hold in AI. He lists five lost advantages: risk-aversion, innovation gap, data, talent, and the "market" itself. His conclusion: "If an AI startup has a strategy that looks like a strategy a non-AI startup had five years ago, that may very well be the wrong strategy."

- Startups normally lean on specific advantages over incumbents to overcome mature products, distribution, brand, trust, optimized organizations, and cash.
- In AI, many of those advantages are unavailable and some become disadvantages.
- He notes blockchain is not comparable: it has had almost no business effect despite billions of dollars over more than a decade.
- Typical strategies are wrong because they assume the David-against-Goliath advantages. If strategy is "how we will win," the "how" must be different in AI.
- It is easy to create an AI startup, gain attention, and generate excitement. It is much harder to compete and win. He expects most AI startups that ignore this to fail.
- He still says all new startups probably need to include AI. The point is that they need a different view of how they will win.

## Disruption Theory does not apply to AI

Jason says the Innovator's Dilemma is not a dilemma this time, because incumbents are not ignoring the new technology.

- Disruption Theory: new technology is (a) better in some ways, (b) worse in others, and (c) cheaper. (a) plus (c) can overcome (b). Over time the technology improves and disrupts incumbents who thought they were safe.
- His example is the transistor radio. Sound was far worse than furniture-sized RCA radios, but it was portable and cheaper. "A radio at the beach that's staticky and tinny is a lot better than not having a radio at all." Sound later improved and the incumbents were disrupted.
- In AI the conditions are absent. Incumbents say the technology is groundbreaking, that they will become irrelevant if they don't embrace it, and that shareholders demand they invest huge percentages of annual revenue.
- Startups that normally depend on incumbents ignoring new technology and new markets cannot count on that. They compete directly with well-funded incumbents selling to existing customer bases.

## Incumbents already have innovation, so distribution is the startup's hard problem

Jason reframes the Alex Rampell idea that the race is "whether the startup gets distribution before the incumbent gets innovation."

- In AI, incumbents already have the innovation, via closed APIs or open source.
- Startups struggle as hard as ever to find distribution, arguably harder, because every market is over-saturated with new startup competitors, some with massive funding.
- The "hard tech" is the same for everyone: LLMs rented from OpenAI, Anthropic, Cohere and others, or open source such as Llama, Bloom and Mistral. This is a level playing field, not a startup edge.
- Differentiation exists in prompt engineering, problem break-down and use of vector databases. But startups have no edge there, such as willingness to take more risk. At best it is neutral.
- Simply matching established players' capabilities is not a winning strategy. A startup needs differentiation and distribution "even more quickly and dramatically than is normally required."

## There is no AI strategy without a data strategy

Jason says data is needed for training, testing, benchmarking and features, and incumbents have it or can afford it.

- Incumbents have data because they have customers. They can use customer data to train models and tune algorithms, so long as they maintain secrecy and privacy.
- Examples he gives:
  - Intercom's AI strategy builds on hundreds of millions of customer interactions, an advantage over a newcomer building a chatbot from scratch.
  - Google has an advantage in AI video because it owns the YouTube library.
  - GitHub's Copilot benefits from its vast code repository, including changes with human-written explanations.
- Incumbents can also pay for data. He cites Axel Springer licensing news to OpenAI for tens of millions of dollars, and Reddit striking a deal with an unnamed AI provider for $60M.
- AI will keep getting cheaper, better and faster. Access to huge training data will not; the opposite, since data owners have learned how valuable it is.

## Top talent is happy at incumbents

Jason says startups traditionally attract talent with autonomy and wealth potential, but AI changes that.

- Top AI and software engineering talent can get above-market pay at incumbents, with a large budget, all the data, and a built-in large customer base that will use what they build.
- They avoid sales, marketing, support, accounting and fundraising. For pure computer and data scientists, "This is heaven for many."
- Many people still want to create or join startups, but startups have no inherent talent advantage. At best it is neutral.

## "The AI market" is not a market

Jason rejects "AI will be a multi-trillion-dollar market, so there's enough for everyone."

- A market is a set of buyers with sufficiently similar needs, constraints and goals that the same product can be sold to all of them.
- The real AI market is companies like OpenAI and Cohere. Unless you compete directly with them, you are not in it.
- A chatbot is in the chatbot market and an SEO tool is in the SEO market. It is the same market as before, now with stiffer competition.
- "AI is in the solution-space, not the problem-space." The customer problem is unchanged. For chatbots it is still talking to customers 24/7 in any language. AI enables new solutions.
- AI can reignite growth in existing markets. Customers may pay more for better chatbots that defer more contacts from humans, support more languages and answer more questions. Companies that rejected chatbots as bad customer experience may enter the market.
- The right analysis is: "Here is how AI will transform this existing market," then "Here's how we fit into that growth."
- Even so, an incumbent with a trusted brand, customers and data has the easier path. A startup is at a special disadvantage, especially when entering a new niche or a market that is organically large and growing.
