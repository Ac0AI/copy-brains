## Doing less is a prerequisite for excellence, not a trade-off against it

- Jason's core claim: stopping most activities is what gives you the time and energy to "fully, deeply execute the most important things", and those things "are the difference between thriving and perishing."
- He answers the objection that quantity sometimes beats quality, or that you should test a bunch of things, by arguing that spreading across everything leaves nothing executed deeply.
- He frames "Focus!" as the one piece of advice given to startups, to CEOs of large companies, and then passed down to their teams. His point is that the word is only useful once you define what you stop doing.
- He cites Sylvia Plath's fig tree passage as the cost of indecision: wanting every fig, choosing none, and watching them all rot and fall.
- His framing device is "create space." Every thing you stop doing opens room for something with higher return.
- He closes with: "We don't have unlimited time or energy, but we can better spend the limited time that we have." That is why: "Focus."

## Sales and customers: drop the bad fits to multiply the good ones

- Stop selling to people who aren't a good fit. This creates space for "3x more sales pitches, each with 3x the close-rate."
- Help draining customers exit the business. This frees time for easy, happy customers who want to spend more every year and give you positive energy.
- Stop trying to win over everyone, so you can win over the right ones (the ideal customer persona).
- Stop trying to please every ill-fitting customer. That creates space to convert "satisfied" customers into "fanatics" who drive word-of-mouth growth, leave positive reviews, and never cancel even if something bad happens.
- Stop trying to win every battle with every competitor. Win the ones that matter most to the people in your ideal market.

## Product, metrics and differentiation: concentrate on the few things that matter

- Stop chasing every metric and you can "attack the one metric that will transform the company."
- Stop maximizing every metric and you can maximize the few that matter most (his product-metrics idea).
- Stop trying to improve everything by 1% and you can "improve the biggest thing by 30%."
- Stop trying to shore up every weakness and you can leverage your strengths.
- Stop trying to do everything in a novel way (UX, coding, pricing, org structure) and you create space for "a few truly impactful modes of differentiation."
- Stop pursuing every opportunity and you can fully capitalize on the one with "the thickest intersection" of upside potential and your ability to execute.
- Stop trying to "find the balance" in everything and you can maximize the one choice that creates clarity and strength.

## Marketing copy: replace generic words with specifics

- Stop using lazy, generic words in marketing. That creates space on the page to communicate what you actually do, who you actually are, or why anyone should care.
- His implication is that page real estate is limited, and generic phrasing wastes it.

## Time, management and teams: schedule less, control less

- Stop trying to schedule all work and you create space for impactful work (his rocks, pebbles and sand idea: big priorities first).
- Stop checking email and social media "every 11 minutes" and you create space to get "enveloped in the flow of creativity and productivity."
- Stop trying to control every last detail. This creates space for empowered teams to flourish, and for you to work on things only you can do, or only you ought to do.
- Stop reading garbage so you can read something meaningful, useful, enjoyable, inspiring or refreshing.

## Relationships and life: fewer, deeper

- End toxic relationships, personal or professional, for you and the teams around you. This makes space for healthy ones.
- Stop creating superficial relationships with every stranger on social media, to create "deep, mutual, caring relationships."
- Stop trying to "have it all" so you can deeply experience the few things that are most important.

## Endorsed framing: one priority makes decisions easy

- Jason closes the essay with Derek Sivers' passage from *Hell Yeah or No*: "Decisions are easy when you have only one priority."
- The image: your destination is a huge mountain peak on the horizon. You say yes to that mountain and no to everything else, and "all paths go either towards that mountain or away from it."
- With your eyes on the horizon you step over obstacles undeterred, while most people look at the ground and get upset by every obstacle.
