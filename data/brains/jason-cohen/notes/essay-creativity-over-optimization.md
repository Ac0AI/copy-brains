## Creative work earns love that metrics can't measure

Jason argues that the push toward optimization, A/B tests, metrics and funnels risks killing the fun and value of creative product work. He wants room for "taste" alongside data.

- Demanding overwhelming customer outcry before any product change means you never build the cool, effortful feature people love.
- Doing the minimum is efficient but not thrilling. Being "lean" doesn't stir hearts, and being effective isn't the same as being playful.
- He supports Lean principles and agrees most companies have too little objective feedback, not too much.
- His complaint is that every article turns the creative process of business and product design into "Vulcan objectivity."
- His position: "Sometimes you should do something just because it's cool. There's such a thing as product 'taste.'"

## Love is a stronger signal than utility, and it doesn't come from checklists

Jason cites a blogger's praise of his iMac as "a beautiful magic floating screen filled with win."

- He calls this a stronger endorsement than "It saved me $725,231." It is "beyond utility" and "this is love."
- He asks whether love comes from feature bullet points, A/B tests, or implementing whatever tops a GetSatisfaction list.
- His answer is that it comes from something "less incremental, less data-driven, more gut feel, more emotional."
- Love can also drive willingness to pay, which he links to elsewhere ("Love wins").

## Case study: the Code Historian "mini-viewer" (2002)

Code Historian was Jason's first product at Smart Bear, in 2002. It was the first file difference viewer with built-in version control support, showing historical versions of a file side by side with one-click switching between versions.

- Its mini-viewer was a UI element in the bottom-right corner that summarized added, changed and removed lines, so users could see how many changes there were and where.
- It also showed the current scroll position with black brackets, and used color to show kinds of change.
- He calls it "in every measurable sense a terrible business decision."
- Competitors used what he calls "boogers": small marks smeared along the scrollbar showing where differences are. He admits he enjoyed mocking them.

## Why the mini-viewer lost on every "lean" argument

Jason lists six standard arguments that said he should have used boogers:

1. Boogers were semi-standardized, and the principle of "least surprise" says to honor familiar metaphors. No one else had a mini-viewer.
2. Boogers take minimal screen space, a strip no wider than a scrollbar, sometimes drawn on top of it. The mini-viewer had real width and claimed the right side of the screen.
3. Boogers sit next to the scrollbar, where users already look when navigating.
4. Boogers need less computation than the mini-viewer's color-variation algorithm.
5. Boogers are drawn once and change only on window resize. The mini-viewer had to redraw as the user scrolled, so speed mattered. Drawing directly on screen flickers, so it needed off-screen buffering. He says: "the mini-viewer is a lot more programming effort with a lot more chance for bugs."
6. The mini-viewer didn't convey more information than boogers.

## The result: customers loved it anyway

- "Everyone loved the mini-viewer."
- Users emailed to say they bought Code Historian just because of it.
- Developers wrote in asking how he rendered it so efficiently.
- It was a consistent high point in product reviews.
- His takeaway: it was wasteful but fun, with no measurable usability benefit, yet "filled with win." It "took extra effort but it was endearing," an attribute "not easily captured with metrics and spreadsheets."

## When to cut corners and when to add delight

Jason keeps the balance explicit rather than rejecting efficiency.

- In many areas of business and product development, you should stop obsessing and cut corners.
- Often it is right to accept 80% of the benefit for 20% of the effort.
- Customers generally prefer the right features over more features.
- But "sometimes it's your job to fill the screen with joyous win."
- Practical reading: pick a few places where costly, non-optimal delight is worth it, and don't demand data justification for every one.
