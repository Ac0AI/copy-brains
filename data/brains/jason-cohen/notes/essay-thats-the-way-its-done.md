## Founders can ignore "how it's done" if they accept the cost

Jason argues a founder should not let "that's not how it's done" settle a decision on its own. He sold to large enterprises at Smart Bear with choices "They" said would fail:

- A silly company name ("Smart Bear")
- No traditional human sales force and no Salesforce
- Selling over GoToMeeting, with a live demo, no slides, and no webinar, from a geek
- Amateur design and a small-company, human voice

He did all of it and ended up with millions of dollars of revenue, largely from enterprise customers.

## "They" are not wrong, and tried and true is fine

Jason does not claim conventional wisdom is mistaken. He says "They" describe how most successful companies sell to The Enterprise, and "Nothing wrong with 'tried and true.'" The problem is only when the standard playbook "doesn't happen to jibe with you."

## Reasons to break from convention come from who you are

He lists personal reasons a founder might not follow the standard path:

- You can't bear to hire a traditional "enterprise sales guy."
- You can't stand content-free language and choose honesty over formal "corporate" language on your website.
- You prefer excitement over ROI-style selling.
- You would rather compete with big companies than become one.

He says he wasn't trying to be "disruptive," "innovative" or a "maverick": "I just *couldn't do it*." Departing from convention can come from inability or distaste for the standard way, not from a strategy to stand out.

## The cost: a harder road with no safety net

Jason is clear that ignoring "Them" has a price:

- It leads to gut-wrenching awkwardness.
- You exit "the safe and well-travelled road," so when you hit an unusual problem you can't rely on conventional wisdom to get you out.
- You must go in with your eyes open to address the special challenges you are creating for yourself.

## Being different can be enough, if it's for a good reason

His upside case is modest: "at least you'll be different," and "Sometimes just being different is enough, if it's for a good reason." His closing stance is that "it's not done that way" should not win an argument by itself: "Maybe it *should* be done your way."
