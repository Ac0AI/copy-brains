## Unfair advantages are only the things that can't be copied or bought

Jason argues that anything that can be copied will be copied: features, marketing copy, pricing, and anything read on popular blogs. Being passionate, hard-working or "lean" is not an edge. He says investors' question about big companies copying you is really a prompt to ask what you are doing now, knowing copying will happen.

- Other scenarios to plan for: a funded, TechCrunch-featured startup copy, four free open-source competitors, or employee #2 leaving with code, roadmap and customer list to sell at one-tenth the price.
- His warning: "Almost no one I talk to has good answers, but they think they do." That belief is fatal because it stops them working on a fix until it's too late.
- His rule: "The only real competitive advantage is that which cannot be copied and cannot be bought."
- Advantages he lists: insider information, a hard One Thing, personal authority, a dream team, the right celebrity endorsement, and existing customers.
- He says almost no one has an unfair advantage at the start (he didn't at Smart Bear), but you can build one intentionally.

## Insider information as a legal unfair advantage

Jason says deep knowledge of an industry and its specific pain points is a legitimate, hard-to-replicate advantage.

- His example is Adriana, a psychiatrist of 10 years who moved into leading software product teams. She knows both the pain of existing practice-management software and the customers first-hand.
- She can exploit modern shifts, such as web apps replacing installed software and new interpretations of HIPAA that allow web apps to store medical records.
- Her position: an industry expert who can "geek out" with customers and can also lead a product team. A rival would struggle to find or assemble anyone with that integrated knowledge.
- Copiers can only copy after the fact, and by then she has moved on to version two.

## A single-minded obsession with one hard thing

A unique feature alone is not an advantage, since it is easily duplicated. Jason says most of Smart Bear's code review innovations were already duplicated by commercial and open-source competitors. The advantage is "unwavering devotion to the One Thing that is (a) hard, and (b) you refuse to lose, no matter what."

- Apple compromises everything for design. Jason notes the products are overpriced, buggy and have poor tech support, yet "their stuff looks and feels nice."
- Google's search algorithm was simply better, which won eyeballs and then monetization. It remains the company's biggest focus a decade on, backed by hundreds of millions of dollars.
- Photodex (an Austin image browser maker where he worked in the 90s) competed only on speed, not on stability, formats or features. It now makes tens of millions of dollars a year and still won't compromise on speed.
- 37signals earns millions of customers by refusing to compromise on simplicity, transparency and owning their company. Competitors could build equally trivial software, but "without the single-minded obsession it's just software with no features."
- The One Thing must be difficult to achieve to stay un-copyable. Google's search plus the infrastructure to query trillions of pages in 0.2 seconds took Microsoft and Yahoo years to match. 37signals' blog (131k followers) and best-selling book are nearly impossible to build even with a team of writers.

## Personal authority converts into sales and can't be bought

Jason cites Chris Brogan ($22,000 for a day of consulting in a field where the information is free) and Joel Spolsky (millions from bug tracking, a crowded market). He says authority cannot be purchased: VC money can't buy it in a year, big companies can't decide to be thought-leaders, and smart geeks don't get it just by coding well.

- He was not an authority when he started. He had no blog, column or lectures, and wasn't an expert in code review or even development processes. He built authority over years, and Smart Bear became the leader in revenue and ideas in peer code review.
- It costs years of effort plus luck, and that is exactly why it works as an advantage.
- Conference example: competitors pay thousands for a booth, then thousands more to lure passersby who are bombarded by other pitches. As a known authority, he gets an entire hour with about 100 self-selected people.
- After a talk, 5-20 people typically want one-on-one chats; some go to the booth for demos, and some get private demos on hallway sofas. "It's not unusual to get $10,000-$50,000 in sales over the next three months from people who saw me at that talk."
- Other effects he points to: a blog read by tens of thousands, and writing the book that serves as the modern authority on code review.
- He hopes his blog authority will help launch his next venture, though that isn't why he blogs.

## The dream team and the right celebrity endorsement

Jason says a strong founding team is hard to duplicate and reduces startup risk, though it doesn't guarantee success. He cautions that famous pairs (Gates & Allen, Page & Brin, Fried & DHH) are easy to see only in retrospect, which is "a terrible teacher."

- ITWatchDogs, which he helped bootstrap and sell, had three elements: varied skills (Gerry, an experienced startup and sales person; Jason, software; Michael, hardware), a common vision to build a product and ultimately sell the company, and insider knowledge (Gerry's prior startup in the space, Jason's embedded software experience, Michael's decades building cheap circuits).
- A team member who is already successful, with a big blog, a prior startup win or a huge Rolodex, is a proxy for advantages that can't be bought.
- He was building a new startup whose main advantage was a dream team (details to follow).
- Endorsement example: Hiten Shah's KISSMetrics entered a crowded marketing-metrics market with hundreds of competitors. Its advantage was advisors and investors who are celebrities in that exact market: Dave McClure, Sean Ellis and Eric Ries, who promote it on blogs, Twitter and in appearances.
- Those people are "taken," and there are only a limited number of respected authorities in any field. Their endorsement turns a feature gap into a pitch: these experts say the extra features are distractions.
- Jason says that kind of jump-start can't be bought for millions, because buyers trust Hiten through people they already trust. He adds that investors like a co-founder or advisor who has succeeded before, since "the VC game is more lemming-like than most care to admit."

## Existing customers and staying willing to change

Jason says everyone you've sold to, including trial users who abandoned, holds the most valuable market research, and a new competitor will not have it. He admits "I listen to my customers" is now as hollow as "we're passionate," so the advantage only holds if you keep learning and keep moving.

- Momentum cuts both ways. Smart Bear has 35,000 users, so a drastic UI or workflow change would mean too much retraining, even if the result were better.
- Even "agile" 37signals is trapped by its "do less" philosophy. For example, Highrise doesn't suit traditional sales organizations with more than a few salespeople, which need pipeline reports, geographic territories and campaign management. He calls those features necessary rather than unnecessary complications.
- An entrenched company still has more money, brand recognition, happy customers to spread the word, employees to build with, and more experience of what customers actually do and need. "Any new competitor would kill for just one of these advantages. If you're not using them, how silly is that?"
- He quotes Zoho's view that "Companies don't get killed by competition, they usually find creative ways to commit suicide." If Zoho stops innovating it dies anyway, with or without Office 2010.
- His closing advice: find something they could never beat you on, "hang your hat on that and don't look back," and be intentional about building it.
