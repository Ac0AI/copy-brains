## Frameworks fail without being wrong: "You didn't do it right" is an unfalsifiable excuse

Jason tried OKRs four separate times at WP Engine (within one team, across a department, and across the whole company) and failed every time. The team read John Doerr's *Measure What Matters*, paid for training, gave presentations and coached people one on one. The experts' answer was always "You didn't do it right."

- He accepts that OKRs are a great framework, since plenty of organizations swear by them. He also says that if a framework is that hard to do right, it may be too hard to do.
- He sees the same pattern with Scrum (Ron Jeffries calls it "Dark Scrum"), jobs-to-be-done, Aaron Ross's prospecting/closing split for SDR teams, content marketing and Holacracy.
- Content marketing example: two years, two hundred posts, flat organic traffic, and the excuses never run out (not 10x content, no topical authority, no links, the algorithm changed).
- Holacracy example: at Zappos, 18% of the workforce took a buyout, and the company later reverted. He says the other companies he knows of that tried it also reverted.
- He has heard "You didn't do it right" about Scrum from teams that then succeeded with Kanban.

## The Precision Fallacy

Jason names the fallacy behind the retort: the belief that a more precise understanding or implementation would have worked, where the precision is either undefined or so intricate that nobody can follow it.

- Its distinguishing property is that it can't be disproved. No matter how many sincere, capable teams fail, the answer is never "This isn't the right process for you" or "It's the wrong framework in these circumstances."
- That means the framework never gets boundaries for where it applies.
- He cites Karl Popper, who watched friends do this with Marx and Freud: "whatever happened always confirmed it." A theory that no evidence can disprove isn't a valid theory.
- Four sincere, well-trained failures at WP Engine are not evidence that OKRs are universally applicable. A team that went on to succeed with a different framework is not evidence that the first one was a poor fit for them.
- His conclusion: every framework works inside some context and not outside it, and he believes "not working" is the most common case.

## Try a framework on like clothes

Jason uses a clothing analogy for adopting frameworks.

- Having no framework is like sewing a shirt from scratch when you don't know how. It will be a mess, which is why you went looking for a framework.
- A framework is a shirt off the rack. It's shirt-shaped and fits some people.
- Maybe 15% of the time it fits perfectly and you're done. These are the well-known successes that framework experts cite.
- The normal case is that it fits OK but is wrong in about three places. The answer is to tailor it: ask what you liked and didn't like, and adjust.
- Sometimes the fit is so wrong it can't be iterated into shape. Even then you've gained experience that helps you pick a different framework with more fidelity.
- Frameworks, like people and products, must match the culture, the people, the other processes and the collective desires of the organization. Even the creators and proponents typically don't know the conditions under which it will succeed.

## Roll out a framework as an experiment, not an announcement

- Announcing to the whole company "We're switching to OKRs" creates instant detractors, and the rollout will probably fail.
- Instead say: "One team is going to try this for a quarter and find out what we like and what we don't."
- He says this isn't only a communication trick to get naysayers to try it. It's how you actually try it on, make alterations, or put it back and try something else.

## What worked at WP Engine: a self-chosen metrics framework

OKRs never worked for Jason's teams. What did work was a metrics framework he created and then tuned through feedback (his Product KPI framework, written up in his product-metrics article).

- It wasn't "better than OKRs." People liked it because it held them accountable in a way they wanted to be held accountable.
- It did not hold them accountable for outcomes they influenced but didn't completely control. He calls that "the unlock."
- From his metrics article: "Whenever management requires a KPI that the team doesn't believe in, doesn't respect, doesn't care about, the team always wins."

## How he rolled out the metrics framework, step by step

1. Start with one team.
2. Each person picks one metric, anywhere on the chart. He says it genuinely doesn't matter where.
3. Attach a goal to it. A very loose one is fine.
4. The only real requirement is that the person personally wants it: not because a manager asked, and not even because it's "good for the company." They should believe "we are not doing our jobs if this isn't true" or "we're failing at our craft if we don't accomplish this." It should connect to personal pride.
5. Track that one metric for a quarter.
6. Then ask what other metric belongs. Add one or two, not ten.
7. Grow outward from what the team has already accepted.

He closes without claiming it will work elsewhere: maybe it will, maybe some iteration of it, maybe something else entirely. "Just try something on."
