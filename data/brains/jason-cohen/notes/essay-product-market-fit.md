## What Product/Market Fit actually looks like, in numbers

Jason defines Product/Market Fit (PMF) as all three of the following being true at once:

1. **Easy growth (pull, not push):** the growth rate suddenly spikes up and stays at the new rate. You often don't know why.
2. **High retention:** cancellation under 3%/mo for B2B, or under 5%/mo for B2C.
3. **Critical mass:** at least $20,000/mo in revenue, or weekly active users growing by at least 200/mo.

He built this from building several companies himself and angel-investing in dozens of others, with a wide range of outcomes.

- The critical-mass rule exists because anyone can grow 1000% month over month from a base of 7 customers. Seven customers is not evidence of "fit."
- A single huge customer shows fit for one customer, not for a market. He calls that consulting, not product.
- If you're not sure whether you have PMF, you don't. He says "there is no doubt when you have it."

## The lived experience: push turns into pull

Jason treats the subjective experience as the starting point.

- Before PMF, "every day it's a struggle to get customers." After it, "every day it's a struggle to keep up with demand."
- The momentum flips from fighting for each customer one at a time to a flood of signups you can't explain. The question changes from "how do we get more customers" to "how do we handle the influx of demand?"
- It is not all good. Complaints outrun your ability to ship fixes. You feel behind in every department at once, and you can't interview and hire fast enough.
- Brand damage follows. A public review might say "I heard they were good but it took them a day to answer my support question."
- He calls it the best problem to have, but says it doesn't feel good in the moment.
- It matters because it decides whether the company is sustainable. It changes what you build, who you hire, how you plan, and whether you start tackling scale.

## The characteristic growth curve: linear, then a bend, then linear again

The recurring shape is slow linear growth, often for about two years. Then a sudden bend, then continued growth that is still linear but much faster.

- He stresses that the new growth is **not exponential**, even though people often say it is.
- Bootstrapped examples he cites: Pallyy (revenue linear for about two years, then a visible turn upward), ConvertKit (about two years of bumping along, then fast linear growth), and Tally (celebrating $6M ARR).
- ConvertKit founder Nathan Barry personally reached out to ideal customers one by one and onboarded them manually, to get both the sale and the testimonial. Jason calls it a brilliant playbook to copy, and also the definition of hard-scrabbling for customers.
- Jason himself was one of those early ConvertKit customers, and his newsletter is still on ConvertKit.

## The pattern holds across funding models, business models and product types

- **VC-funded:** Beehiiv raised a small round and shows almost the same curve. Jason's company WP Engine started self-funded and became VC-funded two years later. It eventually raised more than $300M and had a decade of hyper-growth, yet showed the same two-year slow preamble and linear segments. You can't tell from the chart when funding rounds happened.
- **Other examples:** a Y Combinator company chart shared by Paul Graham, and Balsamiq Mockups with one-time (non-SaaS) revenue, shared by Peldi Guilizzoni in 2008.
- **Second products:** HubSpot grew linearly for about twelve years, then changed to a new but still linear slope around 2021, when its second product line reached its own fit. Jason says the slope can change again when a company adds a second successful product, enters a lucrative new market, or changes its business model.
- **Viral products:** Slack and Facebook also grew linear, then bent, then linear again, not exponentially. Facebook bent in early 2009, around the embeddable "like" button. Airbnb bent in late 2010, around the realization that professional photography was key to matching customer expectations.
- **Media:** a successful YouTube channel and Lenny Rachitsky's newsletter show the same curve. When asked what he changed in July 2022, Lenny's answer was "Not sure TBH." A year later his subscriber chart showed nearly perfect linear growth for two years after the bend.
- **Hyper-growth benchmark:** he cites "2T3D" (revenue triples, triples, doubles, doubles, doubles after $1M in annual revenue, reaching close to $100M five years later). WP Engine did that and more, starting with 5x.

## What it felt like at WP Engine in 2012

Jason remembers the exact flip to PMF.

- There were only five people and the company was completely sustainable. Then everything changed.
- Support fell behind, because "the founder does Zendesk tickets" doesn't scale. They also couldn't keep up with their free white-glove site-migration service for new customers.
- Hiring made it worse in the short term. Finding and interviewing candidates takes time, and he didn't want to hire the wrong person just because they were moving fast. After hiring there was no documentation or training, so people learned by osmosis while the team fell further behind.
- Customers complained on Twitter that service was declining, and "they were right." They blamed the funding round, but the company hadn't spent a dollar of it.
- His stated error: not spending the money on hiring ahead of need, to cushion the impact of a growth increase.
- They recovered later that year and went on to win service awards and maintain 98% CSAT.

## High cancellation means there's no fit

Jason calls cancellation the one killer metric that stops growth even when the curve looks right.

- Founders often think 5-7% monthly cancellation is fine if new customers keep arriving. He says they are mistaken.
- Churn at a percentage grows in lock-step with total size. Marketing and sales do not automatically grow just because the company is bigger, so cancellations always win that race and growth tops out.
- **Buffer:** it had the linear-bend-linear curve (2012-2014, then 2014-2020), then growth levelled off in 2020 and never returned. Churn was higher during the slow period, then fell to 5%/mo, which allowed faster growth. But 5% was still too high. New MRR naturally tops out, while cancellation grows forever in absolute dollars.
- **SparkToro:** it adds about the same new MRR per month, but cancelled dollars keep growing, so at current size cancellation "wins" and growth goes flat. He praises Rand Fishkin's vision of building a great, profitable company with happy employees, customers and investors, and says this is not an indictment.
- His core point: "It's not actually about finances; it's the fact that customers are choosing to leave." The numbers are only the objective measure of that behavior.
- Hence the thresholds: cancellation above 3% for B2B or 5% for B2C indicates a lack of fit.
- Easy inbound growth with heavy churn means the promise and price were right but the product didn't deliver, or the customer's need was too brief to sustain a recurring-revenue business.
- He rejects the labels "a fit, but not sustainable" and "a fit, with limited upside." In his words, "if customers are leaving, it's not a fit." His advice is to pay attention to customer behavior, not dollars.
- He points to Twitch founder Michael Seibel's Socialcam story. It got 60M downloads in 4 months and a top-5 App Store ranking, but almost no users were retained after 10 days, so it was not PMF.

## If you haven't hit it yet

- Slow growth doesn't mean you failed. It means you haven't hit that success curve yet.
- Possible outcomes he lists: you may never find PMF but grind out a profitable company anyway; growth may be so slow that the best move is to face facts, stop, work out why it was too hard, and try something else; or your growth spurt may start today. He says it's impossible to know.
- He offers a roadmap to PMF, the system he used to build a unicorn. He also notes that his previous startup used only half of those techniques, and "no one knows; that's life."
