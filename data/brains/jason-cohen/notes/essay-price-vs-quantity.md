## Many low-price customers (A) vs few high-price customers (B): the framing

Jason frames the choice with two companies that each earned $10,000 last month in recurring revenue. Company A has 1,000 customers paying $10/mo. Company B has 10 customers paying $1,000/mo.

- He rejects "which is better?" as a bad question. Better questions: which would you rather own, what problem should each solve, under what conditions is each interesting.
- He settles on one test: which company would be easier to raise money for?
- He uses fundraising as a lens even for founders who don't want to raise. In his words, "raise money or not" is a decision everyone must make, and exploring it answers the other questions too.
- He cites the real-world origin: guest posts on his blog compared selling many cheap eBook copies against selling about 1/6th the units at a higher price, which brought in more revenue.
- He calls this "a choice that every startup founder must make."

## Market size and what 1,000 customers prove

- If the total addressable market is small, A runs out of room to grow and B wins by extracting more from a limited pool. Investors dislike small markets, though.
- In a large market, B isn't necessarily bad, but A shows far more potential.
- Companies at low price points can raise prices over time and extract more from different customer segments, so A has more revenue potential.
- Most important, A shows that a large market exists at all. If you've found 1,000 customers there are likely 10,000 and then 100,000. With only 10, there might be 10,000 out there, but you have no evidence, which is riskier.

## Market risk is the biggest startup killer

- Jason says many more companies die because they can't find enough people to pay than die because the product sucks, lacks features, or has no staff designer.
- Finding customers involves many variables: locating prospects, getting them to the site, getting them to read and click, getting them to sign up, getting them to accept the price. With that many variables it's hard to get right.
- 1,000 customers paying any amount is irrefutable progress on this front. It's harder than adding three features, because features are "a matter of time and money" while customer acquisition is largely out of your control.
- Getting 10 people to pay, even a large amount, isn't that hard. A co-founder with an industry Rolodex would likely find 10. That doesn't prove a repeatable, scalable way to find customers, or that more exist.
- One way to reduce market risk is to build a B-type company that doesn't need many sales to hit its goals. "The risk is low when the bar is low." He says this isn't an insult and that far more companies should have this attitude.

## What improves with time and what doesn't

Over years, Jason says to expect two trends:

- **Customer acquisition cost falls.** You get organized around marketing metrics, campaigns get optimized, landing pages and drip campaigns improve, and word of mouth brings sales "for free."
- **Average revenue per customer rises.** Better pricing tiers segment customers, prices go up as reputation grows, you add add-on products and services, and business development creates new revenue.

What does not automatically happen is unlocking big growth drivers.

- Many companies get stuck at a positive but slow growth rate that burns too much money on the way to cash-flow positive, and produces uninteresting profits afterward. Such a company still creates jobs and could unlock something later, but investors aren't interested in that outcome.
- A has already unlocked one growth driver. Where 1,000 customers came from a shoestring budget, other drivers likely exist. B has not shown that.
- So A's weaknesses matter less. If $10/mo seems too low, revenue per customer tends to rise. If it costs $60 to acquire a $10/mo customer, that cost tends to fall.
- Investors care about where you'll be in two years, and worry most about problems that don't self-correct with time.

## Pivotability and learning speed

- Jason says early-stage startups should actively probe the market, not just watch for pivots. That means interviewing customers and non-customers, attending industry events for real conversations (not "quipping to each other on Twitter"), and studying website, marketing and product-feature metrics.
- A common answer to "what made you successful" is "we decided to stop X and do Y." Collecting data on what customers actually pay for and where the valuable gap actually is ranks among the most valuable things a company can do.
- A has about 100x the quantity and range of customers to interview and analyze, likely correspondingly more website traffic, and can split its user base to test four ideas at once.
- Citing Lean Startup, he says learning is proportional to how fast theories can be tested. He argues this holds whether or not you take investment.

## When Company B is the better choice

Jason concedes investors generally prefer A, but lists cases where B wins:

- Support costs are high: A's support load kills profitability.
- Customer acquisition cost is 10x monthly revenue, or monthly revenue is 1/100th of what the company needs to sustain operations. "It gets better over time" fails because it's hard to justify orders-of-magnitude improvement.
- The human cost of scaling A exceeds that of B, so at scale B may be much more profitable.
- The company is staying small. B is almost always cheaper and more fun: less spent on marketing and acquisition, less time training customers, more time making customers love you (less churn), and product work for a handful of customers with homogeneous needs instead of thousands who can't agree.
- The market is small and only a few customers are reachable, so you need B's model to extract the most from the pool.

## Freemium is not Company A

- Jason often hears founders and investors use the arguments above to claim a company with 100,000 free users is worth more than one with 1,000 paying customers. He disagrees.
- Many startups had great products, marketing, growth and large user bases yet couldn't convert enough free users to paid, or couldn't get conversions to pay enough.
- If there is a conversion rate, the earlier logic applies again: conversion rates rise over time, so if the absolute number of paying customers is interesting and growth is strong, it's good. It can be better than A because there are more levers (conversion, other products, pivots).

## Which to choose

His one-sentence summary: "If you want happiness and fulfillment from a small company, strive for B; if you want to maximize growth, influence, and financial value, strive for A."
