## Why Craigslist wins despite looking terrible

- Craigslist has barely changed its site in 25 years, and a Harvard Business School case predicted new entrants would beat it with a better user experience.
- Many tried, including OfferUp, which appeared in the original case. Several became multi-billion dollar companies, but none displaced Craigslist.
- In 2024 Craigslist did about three quarters of a billion dollars of revenue with 50 employees. Jason says it is 10 to 20 times more profitable per employee than very successful tech companies.
- The CEO's stated position: "creating a superior user experience is more important than making money." Harvard read that, plus consistency over innovation, as the obstacle to growth.
- Jason's explanation is that the apparent weaknesses create special strengths:
  - A minimal, stagnant UX means anyone can use it, including non-technical people. Craig Newmark has said part of the purpose was to help people get onto the internet.
  - No images means it is fast on slow connections and slow devices, so more people can use it.
  - More users means a big selection, which matters a lot for marketplaces.
  - Cheap servers, easy engineering maintenance and easy tech support keep costs low. Supporting 14 languages is easy because the product is simple and does not change.
- His key insight: it goes beyond "everything is a trade-off." Weaknesses enable strengths that would otherwise be impossible, and that is what makes the strategy unique and durable.

## Southwest Airlines: the same pattern in a different industry

- Southwest is the low-fare airline in an expensive industry (planes, maintenance, airports, fuel, people). It is by far the most profitable US airline and has been consistently profitable.
- Jason notes it dipped the least during COVID and recovered the fastest. He also says every other US airline in history has gone bankrupt.
- Its deliberate weaknesses:
  - No meals.
  - No travel agent support. Many businesses require travel agents for pricing and expense systems, and American Airlines does multiple billions in revenue through them.
  - Limited passenger service: only short point-to-point hops, no long hauls, no international.
  - Secondary airports.
  - No connections with other airlines and no bag transfers.
- The strengths those weaknesses create:
  - Short hauls and a limited route variety allow a single aircraft type. That brings bulk discounts from Boeing and low maintenance costs, because engineers need one set of tools and spare parts and learn the aircraft deeply, so they diagnose and fix things fast.
  - Paying employees well and giving them stock looks like a business-model weakness. It produces a great culture, lean and productive ground crews, and frequent, reliable departures that customers value.
  - High aircraft utilization makes it very profitable, which funds very low ticket prices.
- The diagram comes from Michael Porter's 1996 Harvard Business Review article. Porter's point is that a good strategy is a network of interlocking decisions that reinforce each other, which creates coherence. Jason adds that the "weaknesses" are necessary to create the special strengths.

## Why these strategies don't get copied

- Craigslist and Southwest have stayed profitable for about 30 years in competitive, commoditized markets, even though their strategies are not secrets.
- Jason's theory: when you see a competitor do something great, you talk about copying their strengths ("that new feature is great," "their pricing structure is great").
- Nobody says "let's pay all our employees 30% more," "let's give everyone stock," "let's drop travel agents and give up billions in revenue," or "let's only fly short hops to secondary airports."
- Competitors copy strengths, not weaknesses. But the weaknesses created the unique strengths, so a competitor unwilling to copy them cannot win on those strengths and may not be able to deliver them at all.
- Result: the weaknesses themselves make the strategy uncopyable.

## Strengths and weaknesses depend on the customer

- Jason admits he "cheated" by labeling things strengths and weaknesses. Customers see them differently.
- Business traveler: no travel agent support and no connections are deal breakers, not mere weaknesses.
- Casual traveler (his example is a 20-something going to Vegas for the weekend): loves the low fares, does not care about travel agents, meals on a two-hour flight, which airport, or connections. Paying staff well is a positive. For this person there are no red items at all.
- Family traveling with kids in summer: likes the low fares, but multiple hops across the country is a negative, so they weigh a trade-off and sometimes pick Southwest.
- This gives a spectrum:
  - Ideal customer profile (ICP): sees no weaknesses.
  - Addressable market: from the perfect customer through people who see both sides and sometimes choose you as a trade-off.
  - Out of scope: people for whom your weaknesses are deal breakers.

## Tip 1: Focus everything on the ICP and show the trade-off clearly

- Focus means everything: marketing, sales, the website, product development and features, aimed at the ideal customer rather than the whole addressable market.
- If the perfect customer lands on your site, they should immediately see "this is perfect for me." If they don't, "you are doing a terrible job explaining your product." You need to win that whole niche.
- He argues you won't lose the wider addressable market this way. Those buyers are making a trade-off, so they need to see your strengths clearly to know what they are trading. When the choice is clear, he says you "actually sign up more of the addressable market."
- He calls this how to maximize customers.

## Tip 2: Ignore out-of-scope customers, even when they sign up

- This sounds easy but is hard, because out-of-scope people still become customers. Reasons he gives: they didn't realize it wasn't right for them, they hoped it would work, or (in business) someone else bought it and made them use it.
- They show up in tech support complaining, asking for features and integrations, reporting bugs, asking for UX changes, and leaving reviews.
- It is tempting to put all of it into the product management system and prioritize it. Jason says you must not.
- Know who your out-of-scope addressable customer is, and ignore their feature requests, bug reports and confusion. "They will drag you to a place that's not your strategy."

## The full recipe for intentional weakness

- Your strategy is a set of interlocking decisions.
- Your unique strengths come from picking weaknesses that create them.
- Competitors won't copy the weaknesses, so the whole strategy is uncopyable.
- It also defines clear target markets: who wants this set of trade-offs and who doesn't.
- The question Jason ends on: "What intentional weaknesses are you going to put in your strategy to create unique and uncopyable strengths?"
