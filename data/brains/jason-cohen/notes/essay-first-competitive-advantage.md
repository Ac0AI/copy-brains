## Why "first" is not a competitive advantage (and when it is)

Jason argues that being "first" sounds like innovation and leadership, but it is not a tangible benefit a customer can experience. He treats it as history, not an advantage. This position came from competing at WP Engine, where a rival's pitch was "We started the trend" in Managed WordPress Hosting. Jason's view: that line does not make the rival better.

- "Being 'first' isn't an end in itself": it is not an advantage, not a feature, not a benefit.
- It can be a means to one of those ends, if levered into market dominance with persistence and the right strategy.
- Otherwise it can be a manacle that lets newer competitors accelerate past you.
- He says to be proud of being first at something, but like being "disruptive," it is not necessarily desirable.

## Most market leaders were not first

Jason's evidence is that the leaders are rarely the originators:

- Google was not the first search engine.
- The iPod was not the first MP3 player.
- Dropbox was not the first cloud filesystem.
- Excel was not the first spreadsheet.
- Dell was not the first PC manufacturer.
- Facebook was not the first social network.
- Cisco did not invent the router.

He notes they are nonetheless the market leaders and their markets' greatest innovators. Early MP3 players did not get huge traction and were not creative in hardware or design, which left Apple room to innovate in design while the market was still largely up for grabs.

## "First" implies the faults of version one

Being first implies innovation, but also the flaws of a v1 product.

- His example is the first electric car: short-range, sluggish, unattractive, and constantly in the repair shop.
- In software, "v1.0" implies "buggy" and "incomplete." He asks: "Who wants their business to depend on a v1.0 product?"
- His framing for WP Engine: "We're not the first electric car; instead we're Tesla."
- A footnote concedes that Tesla was also first at some things: good-enough batteries, realizing "performance" was non-negotiable, and using EV engineering constraints to build the safest sedan ever made.

## Compete on tangible advantages, not on history

Jason counters the "first" claim with measurable differences, as WP Engine did in 2011:

- Uptime: the competitor had 40 minutes of downtime in a month, WP Engine had 0, per Pingdom, an unaffiliated third party.
- Speed: WP Engine was 30% faster at delivering HTML (also Pingdom).
- Security: WP Engine guaranteed cleanup of any hacks, while the competitor only claimed it had never been hacked. WP Engine had found malware in customers who switched from them.
- Support: more responsive and knowledgeable, because WP Engine employed more WordPress experts per 1000 customers than anyone.

His point: "Those are tangible competitive advantages; being 'first' isn't."

## How being first becomes a shackle

Jason lists the costs of being first:

- You are stuck supporting the legacy of version one.
- Features you thought were important but are not now, and maybe never were, a journey new competitors skip.
- Grandfathered customers who are no longer profitable.
- Architectural choices you wish you could change, which competitors can avoid.
- A reputation that is hard to revamp even if the company has changed.
- You made all the mistakes in public. Newcomers swoop in with your hindsight on pricing, positioning, marketing, features, and design, and get a running start without your baggage.

A footnote example: years later, founders of a competitor that did not exist at the time of writing told him how they set pricing: "We figured you'd already done the research and tested different things, so we just copied y'all."

## When being first is a massive advantage

Jason says first-mover advantage is real when two conditions hold:

- You are first and also able to expand fast and keep innovating.
- Fast expansion creates a moat that prevents late-joining competitors from catching up.

Examples he gives:

- McDonald's and Ford kept their leads because they never stopped innovating in process and product, constantly driving down costs while adding desirable features, and using the lead to fund stronger growth.
- Amazon in cloud infrastructure: "first, but relentlessly innovative." It did not just put cloud computing on the map, it kept lowering prices while expanding the depth and breadth of service. He saw no one overtaking it as long as it continued, though at least four multi-billion-dollar companies were trying.
- A 2024 note says this held true, even after Amazon stopped lowering prices, and points to strategic moats as the explanation.

He adds that this usually takes a well-funded startup, a stellar team, and a bit of good fortune. Most small companies are not in that position, and it is often not the best risk/reward anyway.

## Hindsight on the WP Engine bet

A 2024 editor's note says that when the piece was written WP Engine was one and a half years old. By 2024 WP Engine was a unicorn, while the competitor's growth leveled off many years earlier at less than 1/30th WP Engine's size, and it was then folded into a much larger company. Jason says the predictions in the article proved accurate.
