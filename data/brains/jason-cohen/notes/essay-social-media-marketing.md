## Social media marketing advice fails when applied literally in kindergarten

Jason's essay "If Kindergarten were like Social Media Marketing" (2009) satirizes common social media marketing rules by applying them to five-year-olds. Each rule is shown to break once the social context it depends on is removed. The essay ends with the question "OK, class, what have we learned?" and leaves the answer to the reader.

- **The format:** a bulleted list of kindergarten scenarios, each mapping to one piece of conventional social media advice.
- **Implied point:** these rules are repeated as universal truths, but they only work (if at all) in specific contexts, and following them mechanically produces absurd or counterproductive outcomes.

## "Be authentic" does not mean disclose everything

The essay links to Jason's separate piece "Authentic is Dead". In the satire, a teacher gives a "stern lecture on the value of being authentic." Richard Neuman then admits he pooed his pants at recess and buried the evidence under the blue slide.

- His bravery is "not met with the loving acceptance the teacher had promised."
- The joke: radical transparency has real social costs, and the audience does not owe you the acceptance that the advice promises.
- The lesson for marketers: honesty is not the same as indiscriminate disclosure, and the promise that openness will be rewarded is often false.

## Headline formulas make boring content sound like marketing

The teachers explain that "What I did on summer vacation" doesn't grab attention. They propose three headline patterns instead:

- **Actionable:** "Five ways to have fun on summer vacation"
- **Provocative:** "Why waterparks are more dangerous than you think"
- **Personally relatable:** "What Dora the Explorer won't tell you about summer vacation"

These are the standard blog-title formulas (list posts, contrarian claims, tie-ins to something the audience already knows). Applying them to a child's honest essay shows how formulaic and hollow they can be when the content has no substance behind them.

## Posting frequency as a rule: "stay top-of-mind"

In the satire, children are told to make at least one drawing or painting every day. The reasoning: "Refrigerator art goes stale quickly and you want to stay top-of-mind with your parents."

- This mocks the advice to publish constantly to remain visible.
- The implication is that frequency for its own sake has diminishing returns. A daily drawing is less valued by parents than an occasional one.

## Publishing "how to be popular" advice does not make you popular or help others

Rachel Davis, "the most popular girl in class," writes an eBook explaining how other kids can get popular too.

- It is "well received from pre-K through third grade."
- It contains "clear examples and actionable advice."
- Yet "somehow the unpopular kids still never get invited to the cool kids' slumber parties."

The point: advice from someone who already has an audience is not transferable to people who lack one. Her popularity came from something other than the tactics in the book, so the book teaches readers little about how to replicate it. This parodies authors with large followings who sell guides on building a following.

## Flattery and relationship-building tactics backfire when they are transparent

Bobby Gray learns that "making nice comments about people helps forge relationships," so he compliments the entire kickball team.

- Result: "He is denied the coveted spot and earns the nickname 'Bobby Bootlicker.'"
- The lesson: mass, indiscriminate compliments read as manipulation. Relationship-building tactics applied as a mechanical routine are recognized as such.

## Follower counts versus real relationships, and monetizing the wrong way

Two further scenarios cover audience size and revenue.

- **Friends versus followers:** Children are told that having a lot of friends matters less than having a few genuine friends who will play with you "even after you throw up all over the good blocks in front of the whole entire class." They agree in principle, but Patrick "still cries when he gets only three stickers in his Valentine's Day booklet." The satire: people say quality beats quantity, yet still feel and respond to the raw count.
- **Monetization:** Genevieve Morrow puts Google ads on her finger paintings, "but they don't generate enough cash to cover her candy necklace habit." She only makes real money by "converting her afternoon lemonade stand to resell WordPress Themes to second graders looking to enhance their personal brand." The point: ad revenue on content rarely pays, and the money in social media is often made by selling tools and services to other people trying to do social media (a business that sells to people trying to build an audience, rather than the content itself).
