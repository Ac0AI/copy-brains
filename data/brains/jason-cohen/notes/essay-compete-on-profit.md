## Why a large, profitable revenue stream is a competitor's weak point

Jason argues that a big company with a large, growing, profitable revenue stream is easier to attack than it looks. "The profitable revenue stream is a prison." It is the "Achilles heel that allows the little guy to win."

A big profitable business also tells a startup three useful things:
- The market is large (otherwise the revenue wouldn't be large and growing).
- Customers will pay far more than cost for this product (otherwise there would be no profit).
- The abundance will probably last a while, because big profitable businesses usually die a slow, sagging death rather than disappearing overnight.

## Why the market is ripe for Innovator's Dilemma-style disruption

- Jason says this combination invites disruption of the kind described in The Innovator's Dilemma.
- A startup with a new cost structure, new technology and new ideas can compete at 1/2 or even 1/10 of the incumbent's price.
- The product only has to be "just-good-enough" at that price.
- The startup's advantage is structural. It starts from a much lower cost base, so it can stay profitable at prices the incumbent can't match.

## Why the incumbent can't just cut prices

Jason's core argument is that the incumbent's goal is to protect the profit stream at all costs, even at the expense of innovation or speed.

- Valuation rests on continued growth of revenue and earnings. He notes how fast a stock drops when Wall Street thinks future earnings are in jeopardy.
- Small top-line changes cause large profit changes. A company with a 20% profit margin that cuts prices 20% doesn't see its costs fall 20%, so profit drops to 0%.
- Even a 10% price cut halves profit, for an effect (slightly lower prices) that won't materially change the price conversation with customers.
- So when a startup cuts prices 50% or 80%, the big company cannot chase.

## How the incumbent actually responds

- Jason says the incumbent asks: "If we're going to lose on price to the low end of the market, how can we charge *even more* on the top end, to make up for that lost business?"
- That strategy might work for the incumbent.
- Either way, the startup can grab 1/2 of the big company's low-end market share and still be profitable, because it began with a lower cost structure, new tech and a new business model.

## Why "growth over earnings" doesn't hold for big companies

- Jason says sacrificing earnings for growth is a young company's game, played when the goal is to dominate a market before someone else does and set up decades of profitability.
- In real, at-scale companies, valuation is about total future earnings.
- Growth matters only because it leads to more earnings, not "growth for growth's sake."
- So a large incumbent is unlikely to burn profit to fight off a startup.

## The exception: never attack a loss-leader

Jason warns that all of this applies only if you attack the product line that generates the profits. "If you're attacking a loss-leader, the situation is reversed."

- Big profitable companies often run unprofitable lines that the profitable unit funds.
- Examples he gives: Google's profitable search business funds Gmail. Amazon's retail business funded AWS, which he says was closing in on $10B in annualized revenue with 20% profit margins and now funds other projects.
- Attacking a loss-leader is a terrible strategy because the incumbent can use all its power and orders of magnitude more dollars, without needing a direct business model to justify it. He calls this "lots of resources and nothing to lose."
- Microsoft made Internet Explorer a loss-leader against Netscape and destroyed the company. This started only after Netscape was large and winning, so Jason says it wasn't Netscape's strategic error but a demonstration of what a profitable company that doesn't care about making money in a market can do.
- The reverse case: Google built a $1B business-applications line ("Docs") against Microsoft, because Office is the profitable line Microsoft can't cut prices on.

## Takeaway for picking a fight

- Jason concludes that competing against a large, profitable, growing business "might be the smartest thing you can do."
- The condition is to hit them "where they're fat, not where they're able to beat you with their size."
- In practice: target the incumbent's profit-generating product line, undercut on price with a leaner cost structure, and avoid products the incumbent treats as loss-leaders.
