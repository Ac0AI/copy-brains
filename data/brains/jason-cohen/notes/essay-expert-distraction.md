## You don't need to be an expert first

- Jason is "sick of being admonished that success is predicated on spending the next 10,000 hours of my life becoming 'an expert.'" He calls the idea bullshit.
- He argues people get so busy trying to turn themselves into outliers that they forget what actually matters.
- The "10,000 hours" and "ten years of very focused, everyday work" rules are, in his view, arbitrary definitions and unnecessary prerequisites to success.
- His conclusion: stop being distracted by them and "just get back to work."

## Expertise is not required for success (Penelope Trunk example)

- Jason's test case: Penelope Trunk wrote that she is not an expert in anything and is not making headway, and equated being an expert with success.
- Her blog had 48,767 subscribers at the time. She also had a six-figure book deal and had written for the Wall Street Journal and Time.
- He says she is clearly a success, so her problem is not a lack of expertise but the same irrational, commonplace feelings of inadequacy (impostor syndrome) that he shares.
- His logic: if someone who fails her own definition of "expert" is obviously successful, then "being an expert isn't required for being successful."
- Note the scale he points out: millions of people try to become "expert" enough at something to reach that level of success, and almost none of them will.

## Why "10,000 hours" evidence is weak, and talent matters

- He rejects the claim that today's high school swimmers beating old Olympic records proves expertise is just time spent. In his view that only shows there are more swimmers, more chances to find and train great ones, and better diet, training, technology and facilities.
- Even if it takes that much sheer effort, he says it clearly also takes talent.
- Personal evidence: he practiced piano an hour a day for more than ten years and became good. But others who practiced twice as much were worse, and others who practiced less are much better.
- He says we all know this, and asks why we let people tell us otherwise.

## Successful founders didn't start as experts

- "Not one of the successful entrepreneurs I know *started* as an expert."
- Career and expertise are developed simultaneously. Success comes when that is coupled with a few key events, "due as much to luck as effort."
- Examples he gives:
  - Sergey and Larry were not advertising experts before they started Google.
  - Joel Spolsky was not a blogging expert before starting Fog Creek.
  - Jason knew nothing about peer code review before starting Smart Bear.
- In each case being an expert up front would have been impossible: Google reinvented advertising, there were no "blogs" when Joel started posting essays, and there was no code review tool until Jason invented one.

## Innovation defies prior expertise

- His core principle: "Innovation defies prior expertise."
- If you are building something new, there is no established body of expertise to master first, so waiting to become an expert is a trap rather than a plan.
- Practical takeaway: start building the company or product and let your skill grow alongside it, instead of delaying until you feel qualified.
- Related mindset point: feeling like you are not an expert enough is often impostor syndrome, not a real gap, so don't treat it as a reason to hold back.
