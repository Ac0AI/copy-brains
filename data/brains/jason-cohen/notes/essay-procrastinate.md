## Procrastination as a prioritization tool for a swamped small company

Jason argues that procrastination is a legitimate, even necessary, tool for running a small business, and he calls himself a habitual procrastinator. His reasoning: you are overwhelmed with work, so you cannot do everything, and trying to will hurt you.

- Backlog at Smart Bear after five years and fifteen employees: a dense whiteboard of marketing ideas, dozens of index cards of feature ideas, and 7,000 open issues and minor feature requests in FogBugz that nobody has time to glance at.
- He says it was "a hundred times worse" at the start, when one person did all the work.
- The ideal founder is tireless, willing to do every job, hyper-organized, and only works on the highest-value tasks. Jason admits he is not that last part.
- He cites Mark Twain: "Don't put off 'till tomorrow what you can put off 'till the day after tomorrow."
- His conclusion: formal prioritization may be best in theory, but plain gut-feel procrastination can be the right way to go. "You can't do everything, so don't."

## Many things resolve themselves if you ignore them

Jason's main practical argument is that waiting filters out false urgency. Examples he gives:

- The big feature you got worked up about yesterday seems unimportant the next day.
- A feature a customer calls vital turns out to be an intern's suggestion and not actually blocking the sale.
- An apparent fundamental bug turns out to be a misconfiguration.
- An "important" meeting turns out to be unimportant.
- The email you didn't answer resolved itself.

Delay costs little for these items, and it saves you from doing work that never mattered.

## Even critical-seeming tasks like accounting can be deferred

Jason says many tasks matter less than you think, including accounting.

- Costs of neglect: late fees, lost receipts, penalties from the IRS.
- His claim: those financial burdens are "more than compensated" by the extra revenue from signing up one more customer with the time saved.
- Batching helps: it is faster to enter 100 receipts into QuickBooks once a month than three a day. Small tasks carry context-switch overhead, and you get faster at typing and keyboard shortcuts when you repeat the same action hundreds of times in a row.
- This applies to financial tasks in particular, which are faster done in one big sweep than as they trickle in.

## Win by getting a few things right, not everything

Jason's strategic point: companies succeed because they got just a few things really right, not because they had no problems, bugs, design flaws, or perfect books.

- Spending less time on time-sucking details frees time for the "1-2 most important things."
- Focusing on those few things is "how you win."
- Tactic: let low-value details slide on purpose so the important work gets your best hours.

## Allow yourself fun to avoid burnout

Jason says you sometimes need to do fun work at the expense of stated priorities.

- Most of what you do in a small company isn't work you enjoy, so frustration and burnout are easy to slide into.
- Recovering with "plain fun" is part of the job.
- His rule: "Whatever the cost of putting off duties, burn-out is even more costly."

## Mindset: rationalize if you must, but don't beat yourself up

Jason admits he is rationalizing ("Yeah I know I'm rationalizing") but stands by the conclusion that you cannot do everything, so you shouldn't try. He closes with: "And don't beat yourself up over it." The takeaway is to treat a long undone list as normal for a founder, not as a personal failing.
