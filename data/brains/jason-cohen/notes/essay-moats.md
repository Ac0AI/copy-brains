## Industries commoditize, and only durable advantages stop it

- Companies copy each other's best features and cost savings, so products and cost structures converge. Price is the only flexible dimension left, and in a real-life Prisoner's Dilemma it falls until profit approaches zero. That is good for consumers and bad for companies.
- Jason says economics is "useless for predicting" what the market does tomorrow, but good at predicting the long-term forces that shape whole industries. Strategists who ignore commoditization do so "at their peril."
- The antidote is differentiation others cannot copy. Best is product differentiation customers value, which should bring both higher prices and greater market share. Second-best is a cost-structure advantage, whose savings can go to profit (cash, dividends, buy-backs) or growth (sales, marketing, new product development).
- Durable differentiation is rare, especially in software, where almost anything can be copied.
- A "competitive advantage" is not enough; it must be durable. Snapchat invented "Stories," but Facebook and Instagram copied it, so the advantage never turned into market share. He argues there is no first-mover advantage unless the advantage is also hard for rivals to copy.

## Moats are the durable advantages, with a taxonomy to draw from

- Durable advantages are called "moats," after Warren Buffett's line: "a big castle and a big moat with piranhas and crocodiles." The same idea shows up in Hamilton Helmer's *7 Powers*.
- Jason points to Jerry Neuman's taxonomy of moats. Items include economies of scale, willingness to experiment, complementary assets, learning curve, brand and switching costs.
- Two uses for the taxonomy:
  - Analyzing other companies: which moats are they building?
  - Choosing which moats suit your own business to construct.
- He treats the taxonomy as only a starting point. How a given item applies to a specific company is what matters.

## Case study: why AWS avoided commoditization

- Cloud computing is a leap in customer value (pay by the hour or megabyte, scale up and down instantly). Yet each unit of CPU, disk and network transfer is identical across vendors, so if renting infrastructure were the only product, price would be the only dimension and the market would commoditize. Storage prices already show this: each storage type has near-identical, low prices across vendors.
- AWS largely escaped it. Evidence Jason cites:
  - AWS profit has been more than half of Amazon's total profit every year since 2014.
  - In 2022 it generates $21B of profit at a 30% operating margin, and is growing.
  - Google, Microsoft and Alibaba charge less for similar service, yet AWS profits are unhurt.
  - Google Cloud, with billions to spend and "all the requisite smart people," on about $20B annual revenue in 2022 was still losing nearly $4B a year.
- If undercutting on price could steal AWS's business, competitors would have done it. They did try, and it did not work, so AWS has not commoditized.

## The moats Andy Jassy named for AWS

Asked by Kara Swisher in 2019 why AWS hasn't commoditized, Jassy listed these, which Jason maps to the taxonomy:

- **Innovates the fastest** (economies of scale plus willingness to experiment). Size and line-of-sight to revenue growth justify massive innovation spending. The platform with the most innovation has the best tools for developers, which AWS believes is the key to winning.
- **Biggest ecosystem** (complementary assets). The biggest vendor attracts the most third-party support, which developers need to integrate with, especially when "everything is a SaaS."
- **Advanced operating maturity** (learning curve). Jassy: "There is no compression algorithm for experience." Even Microsoft and Google, with billions of dollars and thousands of engineers, haven't overtaken AWS.

## The moats Jassy left out: leader brand and switching costs

Jason adds two moats he thinks Jassy skipped, one to avoid talking about market share and one because it is "slightly nefarious."

- **Brand of "The Leader."** The old adage was "no one was ever fired for picking IBM." Now it is true of AWS: picking the market leader is the least risky choice for your career. The leader is often also more expensive, so this helps profitability as well as differentiation.
- **Switching costs through the biggest menu of services.** Customers who buy only commodity CPU, RAM, disk and network can leave for a cheaper cloud. So AWS built hundreds of services covering nearly every corner of infrastructure: big data, web analytics, AI, logging, alerting, change management, source repos, firewalls, and dozens of open source packages, all rented by the hour or gigabyte.
  - AWS encourages adoption by making each service free when only lightly used.
  - A developer using this array is more productive than a large team a decade earlier, but it is a "Faustian bargain": as her project grows and pricing tiers kick in, she is locked in.
- Note on market share: Jassy refused to answer basic questions on AWS's share. Jason explains that market leaders invite regulation and antitrust defense, so public companies avoid such claims. Amazon owns 43% of all eCommerce at the time of writing but positions itself as a general "retail" company so its share is "only" 6%.

## Moats must be part of an explicit multi-year strategy

- Moats don't appear quickly or by accident. They need consistent investment and prioritization over years, which is what a strategy is for and what "just be agile and react to whatever happens" will not deliver.
- A good strategy identifies one or very few moats to build, plus the primary themes of action that "excavate them and fill them with Buffett's piranhas."
- "We will create switching costs" is not a strategy. It states what you hope becomes true, not how you will make it true.
- His test: name the moat, say how it will be built, and state the second-order consequences, including undesirable ones you accept and expect. With those trade-offs decided, "it is a strategy, not a hope."
- Final instruction: be explicit about which moats are being constructed and the major multi-year activities required to build them.

## Worked example: a switching-costs strategy, written the way he recommends

- **Objective:** create permanent customers through high switching costs, caused by extensive integration with their technology stacks, development workflows and IT administration.
- **How:** maximize the number of cloud services each customer uses, which requires two things:
  1. Create the most services as fast as possible, so there is more to integrate with. Customers need to see more than the competition and a reputation for "innovating faster than anyone else," so they don't look elsewhere if their favorite service isn't there yet.
  2. Build compelling "on-ramps" so services actually get used: free tiers, great documentation, pre-integration with other APIs, and bundled offerings that include many APIs.
- **Consequences to accept in advance:**
  - Some services will be duds or only strategic as part of a larger offering. That is a "cost of doing business," not evidence a team has failed or the strategy is failing.
  - Many services will be commoditized, especially when reselling open-source projects. They must still be built because integrating many into one customer project is the objective, and customers say they want to buy them. Genuinely unique services are more defensible but take about "100x more effort."
  - Quantity plus speed makes this a costly bet: it needs billions of dollars over three years and a scaled-up hiring process.
