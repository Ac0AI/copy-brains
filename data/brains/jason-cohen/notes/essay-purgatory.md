## Product Purgatory: why customers who love a product still don't buy

Jason describes "Product Purgatory": the prospect genuinely loves the product, wishes they could buy it, and still never does. He separates it from polite social white lies, where founders mistake kindness for validation because they "yearn so desperately for validation--nominally product validation but actually personal validation". Purgatory is the case where the enthusiasm is real. He gives two distinct causes: the product fails the Magic Wand Test, or it lacks urgency. They need different fixes.

## The Magic Wand Test: would they take it for free, fully implemented?

- The test: "I have a magic wand, and when I wave it, our product will be fully implemented in your company. Everyone is trained. Other tools are integrated. Processes are changed. Security requirements are satisfied. And best of all, it is *completely free*! So! Do you want me to wave this magic wand?"
- Jason says the answer is often "No." Every product carries friction, risk, time and often ongoing cost, so value must "significantly exceed" this activation energy.
- Value only slightly above the penalty is not enough. Change is difficult and time is limited, so customers will not spend effort on minor impact.
- Founders "revel in all the value they generate, but don't see the penalties that they incur." Customers see the penalties.
- Even with a fair price, real costs remain: implementation, training, integration, and users being unproductive while they get used to a new interface and process.

## Why new companies fail the test even when free

- A new company has no brand and no track record, which adds risk: it may be out of business in two years, or may not handle real-world use cases.
- His example is an Austin startup about twenty years ago, responding to news stories about employees leaking documents by email. It routed all corporate email through its servers to scan for sensitive material and return or flag questionable messages.
- It solved a real problem and still sold nothing. Putting a brand-new startup in the middle of all company email creates a security issue (a hackable startup, outsiders potentially seeing all email) and a bug risk that would be catastrophic for email. The value did not significantly exceed the penalty.
- He applies the same logic as a buyer. He won't use "the newest code library" for anything beyond throwaway projects, because he doesn't want to rip and replace in a few years, wants thousands of bugs already found and fixed, and wants to hire people who already know it.

## Urgency: the missing ingredient when the product passes the test

- Even a product that passes the Magic Wand Test can stall. The prospect asks good questions, thinks the price is fair, hates their current tool, and still says "call back in nine months."
- Jason's diagnosis: at any time the buyer has one to three top priorities (in the quarterly plan, or an emergency that overrode it). The product is priority seven or ten. It is on the list, which separates this case from failing the Magic Wand Test, but not near the top.
- They won't displace a top-three priority, and the product isn't number four either, so it won't happen soon. The delay is their way of waiting until the top priorities are done.
- His conclusion: "You got so many things right... but there's no urgency, no reason it has to be done now. **Urgency is the missing ingredient.**"

## Example: accessibility at WP Engine

- Jason's company WP Engine made its website and user portal accessible (screen readers, resizable text, semantic navigation, high contrast, image alt text). It generated appreciative messages and real sales from customers who chose WP Engine over competitors.
- It passes the Magic Wand Test, yet few people need the accommodations and implementation requires ongoing work. He says there are probably five other website projects that would drive more sales, so for nearly everyone accessibility is "priority number eight."
- The question is therefore not whether customers see value but which customers are ready to buy now: those for whom it is a top-three priority, driven by an emergency, a strategic decision, or company culture (as at WP Engine).
- When accessibility becomes a blocker: companies pursuing government contracts that require accessible vendors, companies being sued over accessibility, companies selling to audiences with accessibility requirements, and companies integrating with platforms like Walmart or Amazon that mandate it.

## Escaping Purgatory: narrow the market to "...and needs it right now"

- The fix is to narrow the target market with the condition "...and needs it right now." Then aim all marketing, sales and new product features at that ideal customer profile.
- There is no product that is always valuable, always low-risk or always a top priority. There are segments where those things hold and segments where they don't.
- Questions to find pockets of urgency:
  - Who is the product critical to strategically, especially when tied to revenue? Examples: regulatory compliance, industry certifications, public strategy declarations, entering a new market, buying another company, a competitive shift, new fundraising or an IPO, modernizing legacy systems, customers publicly demanding new requirements.
  - What emergency would force a purchase? Examples: getting sued, executive turnover, a PR debacle, a negative analyst report, a loud competitor announcement, a significant employee leaving, a public security breach, a regulatory investigation, market-share decline, supply-chain disruption, a manufacturing defect, scaling issues.
  - What personal life changes trigger action (for consumer products)? Examples: becoming a parent, a child leaving for college, a serious diagnosis, inheriting money, losing a job, moving, retiring, starting or selling a business, divorce.
  - What competitive pressures create urgency? Examples: new entrants, competitors gaining share or announcing major features, trade publications highlighting what they lack, technology disruption, negative viral attention.
  - What financial pressures create urgency? Examples: budgets closing at a certain time of year, needing to "use up" budget, new fundraising bringing new expectations and budget, new teams, a change in profitability or financial goals.
- To locate such customers, he suggests watching: official announcements, CEOs and founders on podcasts, consistent complaints in reviews and social media, shifts in messaging, acquisitions, changes in job openings, Glassdoor comment trends, analyst reports, trade publications, venture capital allocations, regulatory change notices, new leadership, new departments or teams, and life events.

## When the segment is too small, and why a narrow focus still works

- The answer may be bad news: there may be no target segment, it may be too small or too hard to find, or it may be disqualified by no budget or vendor lock-in. In that case the product may not be workable, but asking the question is how you find out.
- Even if the universe of "perfect customers" looks small, he says "for every customer who is in the center of that bullseye, there are 100x more who will find it useful enough to buy anyway." The reasoning is in his ICP article.
- Focusing on such a small surface area is scary, but he says it works, and "you have no choice, if you want to exit Purgatory."
