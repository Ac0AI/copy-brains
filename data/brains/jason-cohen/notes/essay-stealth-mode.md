## Why "stealth mode" is a bad startup strategy

Jason's essay is sarcasm: every "strategy" in it is one he argues you should do the opposite of. This section covers the stealth-mode core of it.

- He opens with "The last thing any startup needs is people finding out about it," meaning the reverse is true: you want customers and attention early.
- He mocks the fear that a competitor launching at the same time kills you. A head start of "a full 4 months" is no moat against "two Stanford kids with $9,000,000 in funding."
- Secrecy blocks customer conversations, so you never learn what people want.
- He mocks refusing to talk to potential customers who pay "$20/mo" because they might copy you. Their feedback is worth far more than the risk.
- He mocks avoiding entrepreneurs and advisors who "have trod this path before." His point is that mentors and people vetting ideas are valuable and almost every founder story includes them.
- He links this to his customer development essay (talk to customers early).

## Ship an imperfect v1 and gather feedback

- The sarcastic advice is "Get it right the first time": hold the release until there are no bugs, "even if that means years."
- He points out the obvious counterexample: if products shipped finished, "feedback gathering" tools and bug trackers wouldn't exist.
- The "ounce of prevention" proverb is a trap here: waiting for perfection costs time and learning.
- The implied principle is to release a simple, lovable but imperfect v1 (he links his SLC essay) and let real feedback guide the next steps.

## Ask people if they'd pay, and don't assume the math sells itself

- The mocked belief: "Of course they'll pay," because the ROI math is obvious. His example: a tool that saves 45 minutes a day, at $20/hr and 20 working days a month, saves $300/mo against a $20/mo price, so it looks like "a money-printing machine for customers!"
- He argues buying decisions are not "cold, rational, and based on micro-economics 101."
- So verify willingness to pay directly. Don't skip it because the value calculation looks obvious.

## Founders do work hard, and obsession is normal

- Sarcastic claim: great founders have "30-hour work-weeks," and Jobs, Gates, Zuckerberg and Tim Ferriss (4 hours a week) are cited as examples of not working constantly.
- He mocks the idea that the 2,000 founders interviewed on Mixergy are all lying about their intensity "to keep potential competition at bay."
- He mocks "A startup is a job just like any other" with "leave work at work."
- His real view, from the linked essay on sacrificing your health for your startup, is that startups demand a level of effort and obsession most jobs don't. The essay itself doesn't give details here.

## Don't just write code: selling is the hard part

- Sarcastic advice: "Write the code first." Coding is what you know best, so you double down on it, and attention and sales are "easy."
- He lists what he mocks as easy: getting people to a website (with "1,317,681,984 other websites clamoring for attention"), converting them, getting consistent media attention, and landing reseller deals.
- The reality: "The world isn't littered with failed startups with decent products but only four customers." A great product does not sell itself.
- The fix is to "face your fears now": work on the part of the business you least understand early, namely distribution, marketing and sales. He links his essays on startup biz-dev and on code being your enemy.

## Raising money before you have customers is not the default

- Sarcastic claim: "Every successful startup raised a big pre-pre-pre-pre-seed round before they had customers or revenue."
- He mocks giving away control early ("Who cares if you already gave away control?") to fund ten engineers building something trivial like "dark mode."
- He mocks the easy-money story: a Figma mockup, a "$48B TAM justification," pitch competitions and "three cocktail events on Sandhill Road," with no questions about customers or revenue.
- The implied lesson is to get customers and revenue first, and keep control.

## Don't copy competitors, and don't sell to everyone

- Copying: he mocks copying a competitor's feature list ("or just 70% of it"), their pricing, and being "20% cheaper." Competitors' pricing isn't necessarily optimal, and they target different segments with a different brand.
- He mocks skipping customer conversations because "the competitors obviously did all that research already."
- On differentiation: "forget about differentiation. Just match their features but 20% better and 20% cheaper" is the mocked advice. Better and cheaper means little if "better" is undefined.
- Targeting: he mocks "sell to everyone" and writing "everyone" on the home page. Products make different trade-offs in features, usability, integrations, design and price, and people buy from competitors for reasons.
- The fix is to pick a specific segment (he links his ideal customer persona essay) and differentiate on purpose.
