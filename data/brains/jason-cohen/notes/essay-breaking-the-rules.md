## Learn the rules first, then break them with purpose

Jason argues that rule-breaking only works when the breaker has proven mastery of the rules. The choice to break them is what separates innovation from error.

- Picasso painted "The First Communion" at 15, a fully classical work. Jason's point is that this proves later "crazy" work like "Dora Maar au Chat" (sold for $95 million at auction in 2006) was a choice, not an inability to paint conventionally.
- "The choice is everything." Without proof of competence, Picasso could have been dismissed as a hack.
- Takeaway: "When you know the rules, you're allowed to break the rules."
- Warning: "if you never bothered to learn the rules, you're probably just mistaken."

## Rules are defaults, not straitjackets

- Jason treats rules as "a reasonable default when we don't have a better idea."
- "There's no rule that can't be broken, so long as it's broken with purpose."
- He says truly innovative ideas are "frequently the result of breaking a rule that no one else is willing to break."

## Case study: Volkswagen's "Lemon" ad breaks the no-negative-words rule

- The rule: never use negative words in headlines, because you don't want people subconsciously associating negative words with your product. It matters for advertising and branding.
- Volkswagen's 1960s ad called its own car a "Lemon", about the worst thing you can say about a car, and it got attention.
- Jason notes the casual style of the copy is "as effective and relevant today as it was in the 1960s."

## Why the VW ad worked: the copy resolves the negative

- The body copy reveals the "lemon" is a car rejected for a blemished chrome strip on the glove compartment that "you wouldn't have noticed."
- It uses concrete proof of quality control: 3,389 inspectors at the Wolfsburg factory, 3,000 VWs produced daily (more inspectors than cars), every shock absorber tested, every windshield scanned, 189 check points, and one VW in fifty rejected.
- It ties the process to customer benefit: lasts longer, needs less maintenance, and a used VW depreciates less than any other car.
- The payoff line flips the negative: "We pluck the lemons; you get the plums."
- Jason's read: the authors "played with words exactly enough to get your attention but not so subtly that you missed the point." That balance came from understanding why the rule exists.

## Applying the lesson in marketing and product decisions

- Before breaking a convention (headline rules, pricing norms, positioning norms), be able to state why the rule exists and what risk it guards against.
- Break it deliberately, and design the rest of the execution (like VW's fine print) to neutralize that risk.
- Treat the rule-break as the attention mechanism, and make sure the payoff makes the point unmistakable.
