## Simple, Lovable, Complete (SLC) beats the MVP as a first version

Jason argues that the MVP ("Minimum Viable Product") is a selfish process: it treats customers as test subjects so the team can "learn". His alternative for the first version is SLC: Simple, Lovable, Complete, pronounced "Slick". He coined it years ago and uses it at WP Engine ("We don't build MVPs at WP Engine").

- He agrees with the MVP's motivation: build something small so it is quick and cheap to test, get it to real customers fast because real learning only happens with a real product, then trash, hard-pivot or invest.
- His objection: "Customers hate MVPs." Nobody wants an unfinished product its creators are embarrassed by (he cites Reid Hoffman's "launch early enough that you're embarrassed by your v1.0"). Customers want great products they can use now.
- His summary: MVPs are "too M and rarely V". What is bad for customers is ultimately bad for the company.
- The test question: "What's the 'Slick' version of your idea?"

## The three SLC ingredients

- **Simple:** complex things can't be built quickly, and you must ship quickly to learn quickly before you run out of money and willpower. Customers forgive a simple product as long as it never claimed to do more than it does. Early Google Docs had about 3% of Word's features but did its core job (simplicity and real-time collaboration) well.
- **Lovable:** crappy products are insulting, and you didn't start the company to make crappy products. Love overpowers the fact that the product is buggy and feature-poor. Products that do less but are loved beat products with more features that are disliked. The "Darwinian success loop" of a product is a function of love, not features.
- **Complete:** products exist to accomplish a job. The customer should want to use it as-is, as "version 1.0 of something simple", not "version 0.1 of something complex" or broken. Simple is good; incomplete is not.

## Simple and complete is possible: examples

- First versions of WhatsApp, Snapchat, Stripe, Twilio, Twitter and Slack were small but complete.
- Some later grew complex (Snapchat, Stripe, Slack); some kept simplicity as a permanent value (Twitter, WhatsApp).
- Virgin Air and Southwest Airlines each started with one route. Southwest is the most profitable US airline in history.
- Snapchat v1: a screen where tapping anywhere took a photo you sent to someone, and it disappeared. No video, filters, social network, comments or storage. The "no storage" insight was critical, and the simple interface plausibly was too. Video, filters, stories and camera sunglasses came later.
- WhatsApp started with only a status message ("What's up?"). Chat was added after people were found abusing it to avoid SMS fees.
- Dropbox started with one folder that would eventually sync across devices.
- Twitter had only 140-character messages. Replies and retweets were invented by users by convention, then folded into the product.
- Starting SLC does not preclude becoming complex later.

## Many ways to generate love (it is not only UX)

- Design: elegant UX and delightful UI (the current-in-vogue way).
- Company attitude and culture: Buffer's blog with open salaries and corporate metrics; MeetEdgar's blog genuinely helping entrepreneurs; HubSpot's blog, which early on was at least as instrumental to customers' success as the product.
- Connection to the customer's psyche: Heroku filled its homepage with command-line examples instead of benefit statements, instantly connecting with its geeky target customer.
- He points to his "WTP" (willingness to pay) essay for many more forms of love. His advice: "Pick some." "Minimum" and "viable" are definitely not among the ways.

## Life after SLC: better options than an MVP

- An SLC needs no ongoing development to create value. "An MVP that never gets additional investment is just a bad product. A SLC that never gets additional investment is a good, if modest product."
- If it succeeds, you have already delivered value and have several futures, none urgent. You can build a v2 with more time to decide, and ask existing happy customers what v2 should be rather than alpha-testers who just want a broken thing fixed.
- Or you can leave it alone: "Not every product has to become complex. Not every product needs new major versions every two quarters."
- SLC can still fail, as can an MVP, because either is an experiment.

## Why Maslow's hierarchy is the wrong model for product

- Jason says Maslow's hierarchy has weak empirical support (no evidence at original publication in 1943; a 1976 review found little for the ranking or a definite hierarchy) and is wrong for product too, though teams act as if it were true.
- His product pyramid, bottom to top: Useful (solves a problem), Reliable (always works as promised), Easy to use (effortless; makes tech support obsolete), Delightful (exceptionally wonderful, eliciting strong positive emotion, likely surprising), Meaningful (identity, belonging, higher purpose, impact, legacy).
- A classic MVP follows Maslow: minimally useful, ignoring every other level until Useful is satisfied.
- Mature enterprise software maximizes the bottom layers, because the buyer isn't the user.
- SLC instead leads with Delight to win hearts even though the product is less useful and reliable than it will become, then rewards already-happy customers with features. Differentiation comes from more than feature bullet points.
- Another framing: SLC is Delightful and Useful on day one, but the scope of "Useful" is narrow enough to be Complete.
- "Products that prioritize delight win over products that don't." It is a decision to prioritize Delight over Utility, since utility is what the customer's budget is allocated to and delight needs insight and great design.

## Meaning as the top of the pyramid

- Starting with delight can reach Meaning: products people tie their identity to. His examples: Linear honoring the developer rather than the project manager; Basecamp and the culture of 37signals; Moleskine and the romantic ideal of the brooding writer; Patagonia as an environmental badge; Vim vs Emacs as a badge of geekery.
- He says almost no company tries to create meaning for customers, and the proof is that they track no metric for it: "out of sight, out of mind."
- You can build large, even great, companies without it (most do), but if you care about it, SLC is the right way to start.
- SLC is "another way of prioritizing the customer: the human being, not just their 'job to be done.'"
