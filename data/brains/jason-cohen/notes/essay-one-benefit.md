## Why one advantage beats many

Jason argues that a company should be able to win a sales conversation with a single advantage it owns completely. He frames this as a forcing exercise: imagine you are allowed only one advantage over the competition, then build the 30-second pitch, the skeptical-question handling, and the close around it.

- He says the skill pays off in every sales call, whether you are defending a few advantages against a specific competitor, arguing the virtues of small businesses over large ones, or answering a perceived lack of functionality.
- "Hanging your hat on just one advantage *that you can own completely* is stronger than diluting your message by making many assertions."
- The same focus carries beyond sales calls. Your homepage, advertisements, 30-second pitch, blog and Twitter topics, and 5-minute product demo all reinforce one singular point.
- Everyone then knows who you are and where you stand, especially your ICP (Ideal Customer Profile).
- On that one point, you are untouchable.
- His closing question: "Why try to defend ten points when you only need one or two to make your case?" He urges focusing message, behavior, UX, product, target customer, and sales pitch, because "It's already hard enough to stake out a niche in this massive world! Don't dilute your message."

## Positioning as "Most Expensive" (the Cadillac)

Jason's first worked example claims to be the most expensive and the best, and uses that one claim to answer every objection. He admits "most expensive" does not automatically imply "best," so you say that up front and argue that here you get what you pay for.

- **Against open-source ("it's free"):** "Open-source is free like puppies are free." You do not write a check, but you support it for life. Employee time, working around bugs, and relying on the internet for tech support are not free.
- **Reframe the price:** the revenue is not lining pockets, it is spent making the customer maximally effective. Examples: answering the phone on the first ring, connecting customers directly with developers instead of off-shore Level 1 support, staying on the line at 3am, and doing conference calls on best practices for the customer's use case.
- **Against a big competitor's 24/7 support:** argue quality, not quantity. Level 1 support and email are staffed by actual software developers who can read the code, and can change the code to fix a bug or add a feature. Their consultants write blog posts on best practices, live with the team every day, and train with top experts the company can afford because it is the Cadillac.
- **Against a cheaper, simpler competitor:** doing less does not mean easier to use. The company integrates with 20 other packages, which does not add complexity for a customer who ignores an integration. "There's a myth that 'more features are always more complex,' but that's just bad user interface design." High price funds top UI experts to avoid that mistake.
- **Against "I still have to justify the budget":** factor in the cost of failing. That means months of installing, integrating, training and customizing, then ripping the tool out and starting over. Better support and more features make that less likely. Add the personal angle: "besides the catastrophic expense, what's the effect on your personal career? What's best for you *and* your company is to bet on the best."

## Positioning as "Obsessed with Quality"

The second example builds the whole pitch on quality, and Jason is careful not to over-promise.

- **Opening:** software is so crappy that people expect failure, bugs and helplessness. The pitch is that this is unacceptable.
- **Handle "so you have no bugs?" honestly:** no, but the company is maniacal about finding bugs and incredibly fast at fixing them. It is "not unusual to have a fix in under 24 hours." All software can have bugs, "but no one is more committed to fixing them."
- **Against a competitor with more features:** more bullet points is not always better, and the more features a product has, the worse each feature usually is. Test it, such as uploading a huge file to the competitor's tool and watching it break. Claiming a feature that is "full of holes" is dishonest; the company would rather have fewer features it stands behind.
- **Against free open-source:** free until you hit a bug, a security breach you did not notice for two years, incorrect data, or a need with no one to ask. "You can fix it yourself" is not free, and if the project rejects your patch you must re-patch on every update.
- **The proof point:** the company never charges for bug fixes, even years later, because it is "100% committed to quality code."

## Positioning as "Small Company"

The third example turns small size into the advantage, and answers the usual fears about small vendors.

- **Opening:** if you haven't worked with a small company, expect smart people you can talk to, who care and go out of their way to make you successful.
- **"Small companies fail":** big companies are not stable either. They are hit by recessions and accounting scandals, and they cut or sell product lines, even profitable ones. You cannot know when a big company will discontinue your product.
- **"They have 1000 developers and support engineers":** "Willing" to help, but able? Developers are shielded by powerless Level 1 support that feels like a shield, not a help. Big companies ship infrequently, so you may wait a year for a fix. A small company lets you complain directly to the engineers and get fixes in weeks, sometimes days.
- **"Do you have 24/7 support?":** no. He contrasts "folks we've never met $1.25/hour to answer the phone at 3am PST so they can tell you to reboot your machine and RTFM" with paying actual software developers $75/hour to talk with the customer about exactly what's wrong. They sometimes write special code just to get the customer running again until a proper fix ships.
- **"They have more features":** the customer really wants their specific needs met, and "more features" only potentially means that. The key question is what happens in six months when the customer finds 10 more things they need. The small company commits to proactive meetings to learn what the customer needs and add those features as soon as it can.
- **A reframing question for buyers:** don't ask "Which tool will satisfy my needs today," ask "One year from now, which tool will be satisfying my needs, including the ones I can't foresee?"

## How to use the exercise

- Run the one-advantage exercise to get a clear talk-track, even if you never run with a single advantage in practice. Jason admits of each example, "Will this work? I don't know, but at least the talk-track is clear."
- Use the single advantage as a lens to answer any skeptical question: each objection (price, features, support, risk) is answered by rolling it back to the one thing you own.
- Practice the skill because every real sales call involves defending a few advantages against a competitor, a size class, or a perceived functionality gap.
- Apply the same narrowing to the homepage, ads, pitch, content topics and demo so they all say the same thing.
