## Being "in command" versus "in control"

Jason argues that being "in control" of a business is impossible and perhaps not even desirable. Being "in command" is the ideal: "honest, introspective, agile, aware, and proactive." When a founder, CEO or team leader is in command, he doesn't worry about that business, whatever its challenges.

- In command does not mean meeting every expectation. It means you *have* expectations of yourself and your team, and when you miss them it bothers you and you react.
- In command does not mean being in control of everyone and everything. It means "proactively seeking the truth, working rationally, and communicating."
- The payoff is "autonomy, coupled with accountability, with realistic expectations."

## Metrics, bugs, backlog and prioritization

- **Metrics:** Healthy or moving the right way is not the test. You have already thought about what to measure and you watch the things that really matter. When metrics go awry you are the first to spot it, and you act in proportion to their importance and how far off the mark they are.
- **Bugs:** A product can have a list of 100-1000 bugs. The sign of command is that you are working on the top three that matter most.
- **Backlog:** A long backlog, most of which you will never get to, is fine. You have identified the few "Rocks" that are your bets on how to win strategically, and you have a system for prioritizing the rest, which also means doing almost none of the rest.
- **A/B tests:** Not every test succeeds. You run them and honestly assess the results, so you improve over time.

## Strategy and changing your mind

- Great strategy means you have one, it is written down, a normal person can understand it, and people genuinely try to follow it.
- Being in command does not mean the strategy is completely correct. When flaws become apparent, you write those down too, work on one or two at a time, and update the strategy "calmly but purposefully."
- A mind that never changes is very likely wrong. You write down what you think so it is more obvious when your mind needs to change, then communicate the change, both what and why, even if just to yourself.
- He quotes Paul Samuelson, Nobel laureate in economics (1970), who was criticized for "not being able to make up his mind" as his models of wartime inflation shifted: "Well when events change, I change my mind. What do you do?"
- Surprises still happen. When they do, it is not due to negligence: something actually changed or an important new fact surfaced. Even better, you were intentionally seeking new information and thus seeking surprise.

## Customers, sales and feature requests

- **Customer knowledge:** You do not know everything about your target customer. You actively seek out what they say and do, so you can understand them and decide better.
- **Churn from missing features:** Customers may leave because of lack of features. You have identified which features fit your strategy, which lead to the ultimate benefits the customer is seeking, and you are working on one or two of them right now.
- **Wins and losses:** You do not win every sale. You look for patterns in wins and losses. Where a pattern intersects your strengths and strategy, and thus where you *should* win, you make changes so you can win the next such deal.
- **Stakeholder and executive requests:** You do not do everything they ask. You listen with curiosity and empathy, use a system to reject bad requests and prioritize good ones, and communicate the result and the rationale.

## Team, hiring and not doing others' work

- **Great team:** You are not always the smartest person in the room. You have hired great people who challenge you and bring diverse perspectives.
- **Problems with team members:** Problems may exist, but you have already talked to the person and are working with them to change, either changing and staying, or changing by leaving. You proactively and intentionally solve for fulfillment of the whole team, yourself included.
- **Editor, not writer:** You are not constantly telling people what to do. You act as an editor rather than a writer, unless writing is necessary to make progress. If you are constantly doing the writing, you change the team so that it stops, which might mean changing someone else or changing yourself.
- **Whose fault it is:** "If you're constantly having to do someone else's work, that's your fault." Either you hired incorrectly, or you hired correctly and are not allowing them to do their job.
- **Unpopular decisions:** You are not always popular. You make tough decisions, even if poorly received, because they are in the best interest of the customer, the team and the business.

## Mistakes, threats and overwhelm

- **Mistakes:** You still make them. You are proactively on the lookout for them and call them out when you find them, so you can rally many people to find solutions and make a change.
- **Existential threats:** They may loom over the business. You have articulated what they are because you sought them out and faced them head-on, and you mitigate the more likely ones with a few logical actions.
- **Overwhelm:** You still feel it. You recognize it, take steps to manage your stress, and seek support when needed.
