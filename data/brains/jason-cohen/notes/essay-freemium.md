## Freemium is a marketing expense, not a business model

Jason argues that freemium is best understood as a customer-acquisition channel and should be accounted for like AdWords. In his 2013 essay, "Freemium means high costs, low conversion, and customer feedback dominated by the non-ideal customer. Treat it as a marketing expense to ensure ROI." Founders often adopt it because it sounds easy, and it "usually crushes companies, not only because of direct costs, but because the founders didn't realize the business model itself caused them to make incorrect strategic decisions."

- The free tier is lowest-priced, free-forever, with trip-wires arranged so "real users" getting "real value" start paying.
- It can work wonderfully, but the costs are routinely underestimated.
- Retool expectations: it is "a *marketing cost.* It's more expensive than you give it credit for, but it could very well be the best marketing strategy."

## Freemium users are not customer development

A common belief is that 1,000 active free users will teach you what to build next (feature usage stats, votes on the roadmap). Jason says this misleads you.

- "Those freemium users are not like those who will actually give you money." Features paying customers want often never show up on freeloaders' radar.
- Almost no freeloader converts, even to the lowest tier. Charge $1/mo and most would quit, because their need is not pressing enough for even a pittance.
- People who pay $10/mo or $100/mo differ "in kind," not just in scale.
- Free-tier users are not your ICP. Some ICPs sit among them, and price works as a sieve to let those rise up over time. "95% will never rise up."
- Free users outnumber paying customers 20:1 or even 100:1, so in feedback forums "they drown out the voices of those who actually matter."

## Why freemium conversion makes marketing expensive

Jason shows the math on funnel conversion.

- A really good free-to-paid rate is 4%, as Dropbox achieved, and that took a ton of work from one of the most successful freemium products ever. Normal is closer to 1%, since most free users neither convert nor stay active.
- He informally surveyed a dozen small startups that do not use freemium. On average they saw about 1% of web traffic convert straight to purchase.
- Visitor-to-free-tier conversion is higher than visitor-to-paid, but only to about 3%-5% (10% at the extreme). Then only roughly 2%-5% of free users pay (outliers like Slack and Spotify are around 30%).
- Compounded, the visitor-to-money rate can be many times worse than non-freemium startups.
- Benchmarks he cites: OpenView data (458 respondents) gave 6% visitor-to-freemium and 5% freemium-to-paid, about 0.3% overall. FirstPageSage's 2025 report gave 13% and 2.6%, again about 0.3% overall.

Consequences:

- Paid advertising and most other marketing channels become uneconomic for driving growth unless you accept big losses to get started.
- You essentially have to build a viral product, which is very hard and still needs seeding, so the marketing expense problem remains.
- The clear freemium winners also raised tens or hundreds of millions in funding, partly to get over this hump.
- Annual pre-pay might help, but "it's a huge hill to climb."

## The hidden cost of free-tier tech support

Jason walks through why every support policy for free users has a price.

- "Just point them to forums" ignores that people emailing support want a response, and that other customers may not want to be your support team.
- Refusing support for free users makes them less successful, which cuts conversion and word-of-mouth. Spreading the word is one of freemium's main benefits, so this could be fatal.
- Lesser-grade support forces each rep to decide "how much to help." That is awkward for the customer and demoralizing for reps who intentionally help people less, day in and day out.
- Expect people who say "If you help me through this, then I'll pay." He asks whether you are hard enough to shut the door, knowing they probably still won't pay.
- Great support is one of the few true competitive advantages a small startup has, and good support also drives sales and reputation. Segmenting who gets treated well undermines that.

## The real advantages of freemium

Jason acknowledges four benefits, and notes that all of them are marketing, not product utility:

- **Easy to start.** Even a 30-day free trial or money-back guarantee is a much bigger barrier than "free." Getting a visitor to stop perusing and start using is a critical step.
- **Easy to upsell.** Users already use the tool, so special offers, pricing changes, or outgrowing a tier all become paths to payment.
- **Stats for selling.** "Join 10,000 happy users" is social proof, like a newsletter's subscriber counter. It also proves to bigger customers that you scale technically and in training new users.
- **Not using the competition.** One more user for you is one fewer for them. Market share has its own value, especially in zero-sum games.

## How to charge the marketing department

Jason's technique is to bill freemium to marketing exactly like AdWords or any lead-gen campaign, measuring total cost of acquiring each new paying customer.

1. Treat freemium as a theory: spending money to support freeloaders builds an efficient path to paying customers. Revenue could come from AdWords, SEO, social media or other channels, so compare ROI against them.
2. Compute the free tier's effective "price": imagine charging free users only enough to recoup costs (infrastructure plus tech support), amortized per user, with no profit. That amount is what marketing "reimburses" the rest of the company.
3. Deduct the value of the other benefits (a competitor not getting that customer, word-of-mouth from a larger market share). Ask: "What would you be willing to pay, per customer, per month, only to prevent them from being a customer of a competitor?" The answer is probably more than $0 but not infinite. If you truly have no money, freemium probably isn't your model.
4. Make sure the net cost per acquired customer is far below total lifetime revenue. Many startups fail this equation because they don't honestly count the total cost to acquire.

His bottom line: "You have no excuse to not be measuring this net cost-per-user" so you run freemium knowing the costs and compare it to other marketing.
