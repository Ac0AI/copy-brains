## Builders build; businesses are built elsewhere

- Jason argues that a technical founder's strength is also their weakness: love of creation. Builders wake up thinking about what to make, not how to sell, and open the editor before the to-do list or CRM.
- Most startups fail despite excellent coding and design. They fail because not enough people reach the home page, visitors don't try or pay, acquisition costs too much, nobody tells friends, or the product isn't solving a pain people know they have. None of those is "whether the code works or whether it looks good."
- He frames startup success as a Drake Equation of many things that all have to go right, and code is only one factor.
- Jason has "made millions of dollars on companies with hideously ugly websites and buggy code." Customers don't buy based on unit test coverage or font choice.
- He says coding and design are necessary but insufficient. He tells builders to force themselves out of their comfort zone and *mostly* do the other things that create a valuable business, while still doing some coding and design.

## Talk to fifty potential customers before building

- Jason's first test of whether you're working on the right thing: have you talked to 50 potential customers? He means "*fifty*, not a dozen."
- Small samples mislead. After the first 10 interviews on one idea he thought he was onto something, but later interviews were less clear. The early people had been selected with accidental bias, obvious only in hindsight.
- Another time, everyone said it was a great idea and it wasn't. That only became clear after dozens of interviews.
- If finding that many people is hard, he says it will still be hard after you've built the product. At that point you've likely built the wrong product and wasted months. Solve the hard problem now.
- Vet the price in the same conversations and make sure people will actually buy. Otherwise the interview doesn't count.

## Get daily traffic before polishing the product

- Jason's second test: are people coming to your website every day? Getting attention is "much harder and much more outside your control than building software."
- He poses a CTO choice: a company with 1,000 daily new, unique, qualified visitors and a buggy product, or one with a clean, stable product and 10 unique visits.
- His answer: you know you can fix the buggy product in the first case; nobody knows whether the second will ever be a real business.
- Staying nose-down in the code instead of getting attention builds the second case. He asks whether you're sure the market even exists.

## Attack the high-risk part first, like a hard module

- Jason uses a coder's analogy. On a large development project you tackle the high-risk, poorly understood modules first, because you understand the rest and need time to pivot and re-plan if the unknowns fail.
- In a business, attention, marketing, positioning, selling and defining the problem are the high-risk, poorly understood part.
- All the coding and design is low-risk and well understood by comparison, so it should not get the first and biggest share of your time.
- His one-line prescription: "Put down the compiler and talk to customers."

## Peers still make the same mistake

- Jason notes that the essay dates from 2013 and was used as a pep talk to kick off a Three-Day Startup challenge event.
- In 2024 he points to a tweet from Rob Walling and many successful bootstrapped founders agreeing that engineering founders still don't listen. He presents it as confirmation that the lesson is correct.
- He describes the pattern as a rite of passage that developers learn "in blood" by finding out for themselves. He asks whether you can learn it today, "just this once," without paying for it.
