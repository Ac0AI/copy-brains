## Be specific: the core rule of Jason's writing advice

Jason's single best piece of writing advice, for marketing copy, blog posts, sales pitches, investor pitches and humor, is: "BE SPECIFIC." He argues you make text clear, powerful, engaging and even funny by swapping generic words for specifics. His method is to demonstrate with a worked example rather than preach.

## Step 1: remove generic words

Jason defines generic words as ones that span broad concepts instead of conjuring a specific image.

- In his sample sentence, the generic words are "usage," "many," "effort" and "better."
- He calls generic words "a sure sign of lazy writing." You say "usage" because you can't be bothered to describe what people actually do on Twitter, and "many" because you won't quantify the number of industries.
- Generic words are boring, and different readers interpret them differently, so you can't be sure what message arrives.
- Worked fix: "usage" becomes "people increasingly make buying decisions based on Twitter conversations," "many industries" becomes "non-technical industries," and "better spent" becomes "a higher return on your time investment."

## Step 2: move from truth to accuracy

After the generic words are gone, some words are still technically correct but add too little meaning or persuasion. Jason's example is "expert."

- When he thinks of Twitter experts he thinks of Chris Brogan and Tony Hsieh. They aren't merely knowledgeable; they promote Twitter and teach others to become experts.
- That distinction matters because experts in other fields often aren't evangelical. An expert classical guitarist may not teach or care about getting millions of people to pick up a 12-string, and few of the thousands of physicists at CERN have YouTube channels teaching high-schoolers.
- So "evangelist" is more accurate than "expert" for the point being made (that they promote Twitter without qualification).
- Other swaps: "higher return" becomes "profitable," and "people" becomes "consumers."
- Result: "Twitter evangelists insist that consumers make buying decisions based on Twitter conversations. But Twitter hasn't made inroads in non-technical industries, so traditional marketing channels might be more profitable."

## Step 3: add concrete examples

Jason says examples are bloat only if brevity is your primary goal. Otherwise they help persuasive writing in five ways:

- They clarify abstract arguments.
- They make arguments more believable.
- They make arguments harder to counter.
- They make it easier for readers to apply your points.
- They make it easier for readers to do their own research.

Applied to the sample, he names the evangelists (Chris Brogan and Mike Volpe), names the industries (agriculture, construction, retail) and names the alternatives (direct mail and resellers). He notes that putting a name to the evangelists makes the claim tangible even for readers who have never heard of them.

## Specificity is the easiest way to be funny

Jason says almost any statement becomes funnier just by being specific, with specific nouns, verbs and adjectives. He lists several techniques, each with a rewrite of the same dull sentence:

- **Exaggerate to extreme:** "Evangelists wet their pants every time someone uses Twitter to find a coffee shop."
- **Exaggerate to banality:** evangelists proudly point to Twitter enabling "people of every creed, color, persuasion and nationality to join the global conversation on what they had for lunch."
- **Invent an example:** "Farmers looking for a deal on a new tractor aren't peeling iPhones out of their Wranglers and thumbing out tweets through work gloves."
- **Highlight the absurd thing:** Twitter as a way to gather "advice" culled from the stray comments of millions of strangers and bots.
- **Blow something out of proportion:** customers are mostly "twittering about how to twitter," most of those people are bots, and the human non-twitters are "missing out on this incredible exchange of knowledge."
- **Transfer the concept to another context:** the principle behind his satirical post on the "rules" of social media, which he says demonstrates all of these techniques.

## Quick checklist Jason's approach implies

- Hunt for vague words ("usage," "many," "better," "effort") and replace each with what actually happens or a number.
- Ask of each noun whether it is merely true or actually accurate (expert vs. evangelist).
- Add named people, industries and tools wherever a claim feels abstract.
- To add humor, pick one specific detail and push it to an extreme, a banality or an absurd image.

He points to Naomi Dunford, Joel Spolsky and Amy Hoy as writers who use these techniques well.
