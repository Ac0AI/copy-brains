## Why "build it and they will come" fails, and what actually cuts through

Jason's core claim is that most technical founders have no real marketing strategy, only a list of tactics every other startup also lists. He says "A/B tests and accumulating Twitter followers is not a marketing strategy, just as 'we'll use salt and cheese' isn't a recipe."

- He asks founders how people will find their product and usually gets silence, then a recited list: A/B-test AdWords, A/B-test landing pages, "we're better at SEO," a friend who knows Twitter, blog reviews, starting with their own network, an affiliate program, a "Retweet" button in the product.
- His objection: every new startup says the same things. These are as undifferentiated as "we'll have a website" or "we'll have an email address." Millions of others do them too, so you stay invisible.
- The question he poses instead: how do you rise above the noise of the internet? His answers follow in the sections below.

## Radical transparency as a marketing engine (Balsamiq)

Jason holds up Balsamiq Mockups (founder Peldi) as a ludicrously popular wire-framing tool. He credits its success to the founder's startling transparency rather than to feature selection or bug-free releases.

- Peldi published revenue figures even when they were still pathetic.
- He pledged loudly and eagerly to give away lots of free copies to non-profits.
- He revealed all his marketing strategies, which were remarkably effective, even though competitors would learn them too.
- Jason's distinctions: "He didn't just have an 'authentic voice,' he made public promises. That's compelling." And: "He didn't just 'tell it like it is,' he gave up his marketing secrets and opened his company books. That's newsworthy."
- It is almost too much honesty, like answering "how's it going?" with a detailed account of a weird pustule on your toe.
- Rule: everyone is "joining the conversation," so you must truly bare your soul to compete on transparency. It is not for everyone, but if you use it, "don't half-ass it."

## Make virality part of the product's DNA, not a bolt-on

Jason's example is Calendly, which lets people book meetings in your open time slots without seeing your calendar.

- The customer sends the Calendly link to other people, perhaps many times a month. Those recipients must use the product to schedule the meeting.
- That is the viral step: having used the tool, the stranger may adopt it herself, and so on.
- He says this works so well that smaller bootstrapped competitors like YouCanBookMe and SavvyCal compete with Calendly despite it being large, well-funded, profitable and growing.
- He knows of no company that went viral because of a "like on Facebook" or "Retweet" button. Buttons are fine and cheap to add, but they do not make a product intrinsically viral.
- Not every product needs to be viral. But if it is not, you still need a killer method of finding customers. If it is supposed to be viral, "it better be encoded in the DNA of the application, not bolted on as an afterthought."

## Lead with a story, not a description of what you do

Jason says the number one mistake founders make when trying to get press is talking about what the company does rather than telling a compelling story.

- His contrasts: Twitter gets press for helping Iranians fight an illegitimate government, not for a new internal IT process to increase uptime. Apple wins hearts through its obsession with design, not its development APIs. 37signals has over three million users because it motivates designers and entrepreneurs through its writing and philosophy, not just because the software is "better."
- Without a powerful narrative, he says, the chance of big press and enthusiastic users who spread the word approaches zero.
- His own progression at Smart Bear took years:
  1. "Smart Bear makes data-mining tools for version control systems." Accurate, but esoteric enough that not even a hardcore geek would know what it is or why it matters.
  2. After refocusing on code review software and eliminating the other 5 products: "You know how Word has 'track changes'... We do that for software developers." Better, but still no press, because it described what it is rather than why anyone should care.
  3. A story tuned to each audience. For journalists, the angle is that software developers traditionally review code in isolation, and Smart Bear brings the age-old benefit of peer review (which journalists know is how another person finds problems you can't see yourself) to an industry that needs it but where it was too hard to do.
- Tactic: write a different version of the story for each category of listener, and make the reason for excitement explicit instead of leaving the reader to infer it.
- It took him five years to realize he needed a story and to find what it was. His verdict: "one story beats a pile of AdWords A/B tests."

## Paid advertising still works if customer acquisition cost is below lifetime revenue

Jason pushes back on the idea that marketing is now only "relationships" and "authenticity," which cost time but not money. Advertising is not dead; you can still buy eyeballs.

- The model: average cost of acquiring a customer is C (advertising, sales, support, demos) and lifetime revenue per customer is R. If C < R, you have a business.
- Ways to drive C down: cheaper ads, better lead quality, a more efficient conversion rate, and straightforward trials with minimal tech support.
- He warns that many business plans he has seen unintentionally omit real acquisition costs, so it is less simple than it sounds.
- He is not describing arbitrage tricks like buying AdWords that link to a page of ads. Most companies on Earth do not rely on "joining the conversation" to acquire customers.
- He recommends Sean Ellis's VentureHacks interviews on finding a repeatable, profitable model where C < R, and only then optimizing and growing. He notes they lean toward huge VC-style companies.

## Use an existing network and reputation as an unfair marketing advantage

Jason says celebrity endorsement or personal reputation can be an untouchable competitive advantage and a way to burst out of the noise of internet marketing. He says he is "no Seth Godin," then shows what he could do as a co-founder of a new software development tool company:

- Relationships with CEOs and influencers at hundreds of software development companies: they would brainstorm during ideation, be guinea pigs in beta, and some would become paying customers after v3.0.
- Relationships with editors of nearly all software development publications, online and off, with whom he has already published articles: some would vet the stories, some would publish the company's articles.
- He has bought ads in every major and many minor software development websites, magazines, newsletters, conferences and webinars, so he knows the right message for each audience and could probably cut a deal.
- A few mentions on his blog, read mostly by software developers, might beat $10,000 of A/B-tested Google AdWords.
- His prior success would get initial meetings with investors and raise the odds of funding.
- The contrast: while others set up a new blog, blast their LinkedIn network with pleading emails, and pay to test AdWords variants, he would be "years ahead in the marketing war."
