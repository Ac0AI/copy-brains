## The PMF roadmap Jason used at WP Engine

Jason lays out an eight-step progression from idea to Product/Market Fit: personal fit, market fit, customer fit, ship an SLC, marketing and sales, retention-driven product, ruthless prioritization, and managing psychology. He says it took WP Engine from an idea to a unicorn. It is repeatable, but not a guarantee. "Exceptions are not repeatable, and therefore they may not be teaching you anything useful."

## Personal fit: passion plus a unique edge

- "Do what you love and the money will follow" is false. Most artists and the roughly 80% of failed startups had real passion.
- Passion is required as the motivator through dark times: depleted savings, constant rejection, impostor syndrome. It is not a competitive edge, since every founder has it.
- The edge is leverage: big output from little input, drawn from your mix of talents, taste and experience. Unique leverage is the source of differentiation.
- Uniqueness usually sits at an intersection. He cites Scott Adams, who is a decent illustrator, a decent humorist and has tech jobs. That intersection is unique, not being top 1% at any one thing.
- He prefers "different" to "better", because being better for some people means worse for others.
- Leverage plus your chosen path forms the kernel of strategy. The test question: "Why are *you* the perfect person to build *this* company?"

## Market fit: a plausible theory of why they will buy

- Product/Market Fit means fitting the market, not building something fun, interesting or ego-enhancing. Most "indie hacker" and "AI is cool" startups fail because they began as a fun project, not a plausible business.
- "I had the problem myself" is the most common origin story, and all his startups began that way. But your own view of the problem is less likely to be a real business than you think: "You are, in fact, not like your customer."
- Passion projects sometimes become businesses anyway (Smart Bear), but that is luck. The framework exists to reduce reliance on luck.
- Test the market on six traits, answered with Fermi estimation:
  - Plausible: do 10M people or 100k companies have the problem?
  - Self-aware: do they know and care?
  - Lucrative: is there substantial budget?
  - Liquid: can they buy now?
  - Eager: do they want to buy from you specifically?
  - Enduring: will they still pay a year later?
- Write a one-page strategy on "how we will win". Have a specific, falsifiable theory so you can notice when reality contradicts it and pivot. Smart Bear, Slack, WhatsApp and Flickr all found their winning idea this way, by noticing what customers wanted instead of the original idea.

## Customer fit: talk to customers and find the ICP

- "There's only one source of truth for what customers will buy: Customers." Not advisors, analysts, Twitter polls, past data or competitor behavior.
- Customers' "yes" is a "maybe", but their "no" is a "no", which can save months or years.
- Interviews reveal:
  - where customers look for products, so you know where to advertise
  - the language they use, to copy into marketing
  - how their budgets work, for pricing and packaging
  - what they use today
  - the trigger moment when they decide "today I'm going to buy ___" (Bob Moesta's framing)
- Founders avoid this because building is the fun part. Building first doesn't make customers easier to find, and means you never learn what people want.
- How he got WP Engine interviews: he used LinkedIn to find web developers working in WordPress and asked for an hour to discuss a new startup concept they would be the ideal customer for. He offered to pay any amount they wished. Of 50 requests, 40 agreed, only one asked to be paid, and 30 eventually became customers.
- He uses his Iterative-Hypothesis customer development method. About 17 years ago it invalidated an analytics startup idea he thought was good and validated the idea that became WP Engine.
- Find the ICP: a segment "almost comically over-specified" so that the product is the obvious best choice. Aim all messaging at it: website, ads, terminology, writing style, pricing. The ICP should see in three seconds that "you get them".
- Targeting only the ICP doesn't shrink the market. For every ICP, he says 10x more people make similar buying decisions and 100x more take more convincing but still buy. The clear message also earns attention and loyalty.

## Build and ship the SLC, then focus on marketing and sales

- SLC is his alternative to MVP: Simple (so you ship and learn fast), Lovable (love overpowers bugs and missing features), Complete (a v1 that does the job, not a v0.1 that is broken).
- Real learning starts only when customers use, or don't use, the product. Six months of building without customer use is not progress: "What you've done is proved what you already knew--you can build software."
- Founders drift toward what they enjoy, so push yourself to do marketing and sales until it feels like "too much". Daily question: what can I do today to (a) bring more target-market visitors, (b) convert more of them to try, (c) convert more of those to buy?
- The answer is sometimes code, such as a better new-user experience to lift try-to-buy. But more often it isn't, and several steps happen before the person touches the product. His line: "The code is actually your enemy."
- He cites examples of founders whose growth took off after hiring a marketer, such as ProjectionLab going from not enough to quit a day job to a team and $1M ARR, and Pallyy not growing until marketing started.

## Retention-driven product work

- A customer who noticed your ad, tried the product, paid, used it and then cancelled means "You made the right promise, but didn't keep it." Marketing finds the promise; retention shows whether you keep it.
- Low retention can't be offset by growth. Growth slows and halts sooner than expected, and if users don't want the product, the growth is "fake news" and not real fit.
- When building features, build things that increase retention.
- Churned customers rarely talk, though paid interviews (gift cards, charity donations) can help. Better is to spot struggling customers by in-product activity and reach out while they are still in the struggle.
- Look for patterns in unsuccessful customers that successful customers do not share. If feature X predicts success, unsuccessful users may not know about it (promote it) or can't use it due to workflow (add integrations). Sometimes the fix is tuning the ICP by industry, geography, company size, buyer title or use case.
- Retention is a lagging, multi-factor indicator: good as a high-level check but not as a day-to-day operating metric.
- At WP Engine, years passed with no new features because engineering time went to scale, speed and uptime. This produced best-in-industry retention for 16 years, which fed word of mouth and reviews (87% of 1,007 Trustpilot reviews are 5-star).

## Ruthless, systematic prioritization

His system combines Fairytale Planning with Rocks, Pebbles, Sand:

1. **Next milestone:** one thing, written down and read every morning. Examples: finding a business model, public launch, 20 paying customers, trial conversion from 1% to 5%, cancellation from 7%/month to 4%, $10k MRR to quit the day job.
2. **Current obstacle:** why the milestone is hard, slow or expensive. Pick the uncomfortable, existential one, not the comfortable one. Examples: only 15% use it again, 17 Product Hunt customers but no repeatable channel, can't get interviews.
3. **Split work into Rocks, Pebbles, Sand.**
4. **Pick one Rock** that attacks both milestone and obstacle (use Binstack). If none is good enough, brainstorm with friends or customers rather than commit three months to a mediocre plan.
5. **Schedule Pebbles sparingly,** using an ROI framework.
6. **Make almost everything only "good enough".** "Almost everything you do will not determine whether the company is successful." Satisfy most metrics rather than maximizing them, and design matters less than people think. Exception: obsessive details that give a new customer an amazing experience, so pick your battles.

His rule: "Don't stray from the system. You don't have the time."

## Managing psychology, and the trap of comfortable work

The journey demands:

- facing the truth about your abilities, customers and market
- finding yourself
- making clear decisions: ICP, milestone, what you're not good at
- tolerating constant rejection: 99% of visitors don't act, customers leave, employees and investors say no
- changing course fluidly, and telling an anomaly from a real learning
- acting under uncertainty while feeling like an impostor

He calls it a "gauntlet of pain", possibly the hardest thing you'll do, though also the most exciting professionally.

The common failure is retreating to comfortable work: building a fun feature, learning Rust, building with AI (a technology, not a customer problem), while avoiding ad managers, asking for the sale and customer calls. Posting "$1600 MRR after 18 months" while framing it as perseverance is the trap. "Perseverance is a virtue, if you're doing the right work, with the right goals. It's a vice if it means you're moving diligently in the wrong direction."

## Smart Bear: only half the roadmap

- Smart Bear (founded 2002, sold 2007) began with Code Historian, a personal project built to learn .NET, on an assumption (version-control history has value) that sank every product built on it.
- He did not talk to customers beforehand. After they paid, he learned many were using it for something else. He built Code Reviewer, which half-fit the need but had the wrong architecture, then rewrote everything from scratch as Code Collaborator, which made millions.
- He admits interviews up front would likely have stopped everything cold, and he wouldn't have known what to ask. So interviewing isn't the only road.
- Still, he credits steps 1, 4, 5 and 6 (specific passion-driven idea, shipping, marketing and selling, learning through retention) and says skipping those would have failed. He values getting product into customers' hands over conversations.

## WP Engine: the roadmap followed exactly

- **First idea (invalidated):** a marketing analytics tool with a real-time event dashboard, retroactive goal-setting, and capture of partially filled web forms, all via one line of JavaScript. About 20 interviews showed it wasn't strong enough, so he avoided wasting years.
- **Second idea (validated):** managed WordPress hosting, born from his own blog crashing when it hit the top of Hacker News. Bloggers he asked said, "I don't know, but if you find it, tell me." The same interview method showed the need, what people would pay, and how to find them.
- **Market:** WordPress powered 11% of the top 10 million sites then, and 43% in 2023, so large and growing. He knew WordPress as a user and loved code optimization.
- **SLC:** he built the first version in 30 hours. It survived a Hacker News traffic day with almost no server load. It had no customer dashboard; he took cards via the credit card service's default page, and the rest was plain WordPress. The early site was spare, and a later version showed database errors on the home page, which he says shows the market need was overwhelming if customers still paid.
- **Marketing before product:** he did marketing and sales before there was even a login screen and launched with 30 paying customers. His asks to his advisory group in 2010 were for marketing and sales help, not product or engineering.
- **Results:** $1M ARR in 18 months. True Product/Market Fit, a sudden shift to hyper-growth that never slowed, came in early 2012. He credits the right promise, market and product, plus hard work, craft, a large growing market and luck.
- He cites Simon Hoiberg's FeedHive as a bootstrapped example that echoes the roadmap: a large growing market, a focused ICP, an SLC, and marketing and sales over features.
