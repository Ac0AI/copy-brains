## Hiring above your own level means testing for signal, not expertise

- Jason says that if you don't hire people better than you, "the organization gets bigger, not better." Without hiring up, organizations get worse with size: more overhead, more meetings, more communication cost, with no improvement in individual capability.
- He argues a great person is not ten times better than an average one. They are "infinitely better," because they come up with ideas and implementations a hundred average people never would.
- Early on, the CEO is the best person at some things (sales from passion, product from vision). By around a hundred people, if the CEO is still better than everyone at anything, the CEO has hired poorly and the company is suffering.
- The core problem: you hire a VP of Marketing precisely because you can't do the job, so you also can't judge whether the candidate can. Jason compares it to evaluating a pancreatic surgeon.
- Since domain expertise can't be assessed, he looks for other signals: ideas you want to implement, learning from the person, organization-wide leadership ability, and fit confirmed by references.

## Signal 1: you leave the interview wanting to implement their ideas

- Test: after the conversation, do you think "Even if we don't hire them, we've got to do half of the things they said"? Maybe you start working on it right away.
- You can't verify that the advice is wise. But the feeling means the ideas connected deeply, which is at minimum a culture fit, and the ideas are plausible for your situation and constraints.
- Genuine expertise produces specific, actionable ideas that feel tailor-made to your context. People who "talk a good game" say impressive, broadly true things that leave you wondering "is that really what grown-up Marketing is?" instead of "Hell yes that's what I want this company to be like."
- Jason's heuristic: if they change how you think about your own company in an hour, imagine what they could do in a year.
- Red flag: a candidate who speaks fluently about frameworks but never connects them to the challenges you described five minutes earlier is parroting what they've seen. "ChatGPT can do that too." You want someone born for this job, not performing the generic version.
- Competitor test: if they went to work for your competitor, would you think "Uh oh, now we're dead?" Or "I'm not sure what they would do for that competitor?"

## Signal 2: you're already learning from them, and so would others

- Jason reworks Zuckerberg's executive hiring rule, "you should only hire people to be on your team if you would work for them." He notes it isn't literally true of Zuckerberg, but the spirit is a good test.
- Weakest version: if you wouldn't be comfortable working for this person, you're hiring out of expediency, not quality.
- Better: would you learn from this person, and would others at the company? He says this is true of every great executive, and clear enough to tell in the interview because it has already happened. They said something you didn't know, you checked it afterwards, and fell down rabbit holes you never knew existed, ones the candidate already knew about.
- The "sabbatical test": if you took a six-month sabbatical and never checked in, would the department be measurably better under them when you returned? That means improved processes, a stronger team, higher output.
- He calls this "lift": the hire takes work away from you and builds a better department than you were building. "Your job isn't to also know all those things; it's to hire people who do."

## Signal 3: they will elevate the whole organization

- Great leaders improve more than their silo. They are better at people issues, communication, decision-making, goal-setting, annual planning and org structure, which help everywhere in the company.
- Example: a great VP Marketing won't contribute to your TypeScript-specific `CLAUDE.md`, but will have theories on:
  - growing from three to thirty teams
  - setting annual goals while keeping people focused on the present
  - communicating difficult news to the whole company
  - knowing when it's time to adopt "big company" policies
  - structuring a two-day strategic retreat
  - handling a conflict between two employees
  - running performance management as objectively and humanely as possible
- You can test this in the interview by asking how they'd handle these situations, using ones you're facing now, just faced, or fear after reading a forum. Frame it as leadership in a company, not as their domain expertise.

## Interview tactic: give them a real problem from your company

- Pose an actual challenge. Examples he gives: how would you implement goal-setting in a culture that's never had goals; how would you diagnose the cause of high churn; how would you figure out why turnover is high when people won't explain themselves.
- You're not grading the answer's details. You're watching how they think and what direct experience they have.
- Look for:
  - incisive, clarifying questions that show they understand what you're going through
  - acknowledging what they don't yet know
  - naming a framework and explaining how they applied it
  - any sign they did it themselves rather than parroting blog posts

## Reference checks: use them for culture fit, not expertise

- Jason says standard reference checks are useless for expertise because candidates hand over friendlies primed to say nice things. They are powerful for judging whether the person will thrive in this company at this stage.
- He asks references three questions:
  1. "What is the ideal scenario under which this person thrives?" Construct the perfect circumstances (subject matter, goal, team or lack of one, direction or lack of it, incentives) where they are efficient, productive, happy, and make everyone around them happy.
  2. "Construct the scenario where they die inside, mess everything up, and piss everyone off." It's funny, easy to answer, and doesn't feel like negativity, since everyone has a personal hell.
  3. "What are their strengths that they're unaware of because they come so naturally to them?" These are abilities they don't realize are unusual; others do, and find them annoying and endearing.
- Example reading: a reference says the person needs a goal to know what success looks like, then needs you to get out of their way because they explore quickly. That yields attributes like "goal-oriented" and "craves exploration," which suggests an early-stage startup fits better than a mature product.
- Why focus on strengths: everyone has weaknesses. You're not hiring someone without weaknesses but someone with a few special strengths that your company needs right now. "Not just competence, but superpowers," which is the difference between "filling the seat" and making an impact.

## Handling mis-hires and accepting uncertainty

- Jason has hired people who were great in interviews but couldn't do the job, and people who were unimpressive in interviews and "crushed it for ten years."
- Only one error is costly. A false negative costs a continued search. A false positive hurts the team, delays getting the right person, and hurts the hire, who may have quit a job. If it happens a lot, "you have to fix yourself."
- What you control is what happens after. Don't let it linger for a year, annoying your best people (who can most easily find other jobs) and delaying the department.
- Signs you hired wrong:
  - it feels easier to just do it yourself
  - your best people complain constantly instead of being inspired
  - things don't start improving immediately
- The team knows before you do, and only you can fix it. "Make the break cleanly. Act immediately; you're already three months late."
- Even a year later you won't know whether their strategy was optimal, since you never get the counterfactual. What you will know is whether they changed how you see your company, found problems you didn't know about, built things you didn't think to build, and made the people around them (including you) sharper. Those clues are available in the interview, and culture fit is available in reference checks.
