## Why startups need luck, and why "and" kills the odds

Jason argues that luck always plays a role in startups, because success requires many things to go right at once. His list includes:
- A marketing channel that works.
- The right mix of features and usability to trigger product/market fit.
- Employee number one working out.
- A competitor not making a fatal move.
- Enough money in the market.
- Appropriate pricing.
- Market forces not shifting the rules of the game.

He notes this comes on top of the luck of where and when you were born. His conclusion: it is obvious why startups fail so often, because they need a lot of success in a lot of areas.

He opens with a story. As a kid he won a fake stock market competition in elementary school by putting all his money in a few penny stocks. Prices moved in multiples of 1/8, so small moves were large percentage swings. Some days he had the worst portfolio, some days the best, and the contest happened to end on an up-day. That was "high risk, high reward."

## The math of "and": probabilities multiply

Jason's core point: a list of requirements joined by "and" is bad, because each has a probability of success and the total is their product.

- "No matter how optimistic you are about those probabilities, the end product is a small number."
- 70% multiplied by itself six times is only 12%.
- Most of the things on the startup list are below 70%, and more than six have to go right.

He states it plainly: **"'And' is bad!"** Needing a good channel and killer features and great early employees and a healthy market means stacking multiplied odds against yourself.

## First technique: make explicit choices that raise each probability

Before using "or," Jason says you can raise the odds of individual "and" items by choosing deliberately:
- Pick a large, growing, healthy market so the risk of "no one wants to solve this problem" goes to zero.
- Compete in a niche where incumbents don't care or can't move quickly.
- Hire someone you've worked with before, or build something sustainable without hiring.
- Generate Love rather than only Utility or Coercion, to overcome your deficiencies.

He points to a separate long list of common blunders and how to mitigate them. Even so, he says there will be plenty of challenges, which is why a second technique is needed.

## Second technique: leverage "or" instead of "and"

Jason's rule: **leverage "or" instead of "and."** When only one of several options has to work, the combined odds rise even if each option is unlikely.

Marketing channels are his example. Your first few hundred customers could come from any of these:
- Google Ads
- Facebook ads
- Affiliate sales
- Targeted outbound sales
- SEO
- Guesting on podcasts and newsletters
- Partnering with a high-profile reseller
- Great press about your unique brand and message

"Only one of these needs to work! So although the probability of success for each one of these is low, the probability that *something* will work is higher."

He says the same logic applies to product features, website copy for conversions, and the avenues for exiting the company years later.

## Optionality is strength

Jason's general rule: **"optionality is strength."** When there are lots of ways for things to go right, that is a strong position even if you haven't actualized any of them yet.

Practical reading of the idea:
- Structure the plan so success can come from many independent sources.
- Don't bet the company on one channel, one feature set, or one exit path.
- Each added "or" is another way for luck to land in your favor.

His closing line: "Set yourself up for luck!"

## Why marketplaces are the cautionary example

Jason uses marketplaces as the converse: businesses with extra "and" clauses, even more than usual. He says they almost never succeed. When they do, they are often durable and profitable, which makes them a smart bet for a venture capitalist with a diversified portfolio of attempts. For the individual business, he says it is a tough road.

The "and" chain for a marketplace:
- It must thrive with sellers and buyers.
- It can't be too expensive to build up both sides.
- Both sides must realize value.
- It must not be easier, cheaper, or preferable to transact outside the marketplace.
- Many only deliver value at scale, so they must also "scale down," so the first 100 buyers and sellers also see value.

His takeaway: accumulating "and" requirements lowers the probability of success, while stringing together possible solutions with "or" increases the number of ways luck can smile on you.
