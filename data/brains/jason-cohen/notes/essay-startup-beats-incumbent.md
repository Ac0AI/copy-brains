## Taking risks that incumbents can't quantify

Jason argues a startup beats a large incumbent by doing "things that incumbents cannot or will not do." Big-company advantages (money, brand, customers, sales) come with weaknesses, because things at scale are "super-linearly more difficult."

- Incumbents approve risk by asking whether ROI (potential revenue divided by cost) is good and whether the chance of failure is low.
- ROI analyses are usually wrong. Projects run late and over budget, and outcomes come in worse than expected, so both sides of the fraction are off.
- Projects that look low-ROI on paper are unlikely to be pursued by incumbents, even though some are genuinely good. Easy-to-analyze ideas get chosen over better ones.
- Fear of failure motivates more than the desire to innovate. The core business is going well, and no one wants to preside over a multi-million-dollar failure. "Let's gather more data" and "let's wait for consensus" feel safer.
- Startups can pick high-risk or unknowable-risk ideas with large upside, and incumbents are unlikely to follow.
- He applies the same logic to work planning: big "Rocks" should be chosen on potential outcome, while "Pebbles" should be chosen on ROI.
- His blunt aside: the incumbent can buy you later at 10x revenue.

## Going after a niche that fails the incumbent's materiality threshold

Incumbents can't afford niches because new bets must be material to their revenue.

- Rule of thumb: a new market or product should add at least 10% to overall revenue (the "materiality threshold"), and the bar rises with perceived risk or investment. For a company with hundreds of millions in revenue, that means a product with a good chance of making $50-100 million, or they won't try.
- He quotes Warren Buffett: "Given our size, we only see a few good things [to invest in]. If we were smaller, then we'd see lots of good things."
- There is also a P&L ratio. Software companies at scale spend around 20% of revenue on R&D. A fully loaded team (assumed: 8 engineers, a PM, a designer, a manager, in the US) costs upwards of $2 million a year. At 20% of revenue, that team must generate at least $10 million a year, even if it owns a small add-on.
- A founding team of two plus one employee doesn't need $10 million. A $1 million niche is a win.
- A startup can pick a niche and ICP the incumbent can't afford to target. The bigger the incumbent, the less the startup has to worry about competition.

## Doing things that don't scale, and not needing to operate at scale

Jason uses WP Engine's launch of LetsEncrypt support as the example.

- WP Engine had around 100,000 customers who could use it on day one. Before release it had to be built to scale: components split into queues, monitoring and human alerts when a queue stayed broken, at-scale tests with 1,000 simultaneous requests and random failures.
- Hundreds of support reps and hundreds of salespeople had to be trained, marketing had to craft messaging, and the feature needed near-zero bugs. If 10% of thousands of users hit a bug, support gets crushed and people complain on Twitter.
- It took the team most of a year. On launch, thousands of users adopted it on day one and tens of thousands in the first month, and people praised the company on Twitter.
- The upside of scale: 1,000 users on day one, 10,000 by day 30, and $1M or even $10M ARR quickly. A startup can't do that.
- A startup can launch in a few weeks and iterate. Incumbents won't spend a couple of years getting to $1M ARR, a couple more to $5M, and a couple more to $20M. That is too slow to reach material revenue and doesn't use their at-scale assets.
- That ramp is a "highly successful growth rate" for a startup. Nobody knows you exist, which is bad for sales but good for iterating without harming your reputation.
- Heuristic: a product that is naturally easy to scale from day one is easy for an incumbent to copy. One that is naturally hard to scale from day one is ideal for a startup.

## Unsurpassed customer service from founders and engineers

Jason handled support tickets himself every day at WP Engine until the company had around 35 people.

- Talking to customers when things go wrong is the best way to learn how people use the product. It produces feature ideas, ways to streamline the product, and ways to improve retention.
- Customers are impressed by the quality of support and the range of problems you can solve, which a company with a thousand support reps can't match.
- Great support builds love and loyalty. Love brings forgiveness during outages or security incidents, and word-of-mouth advocacy, so it produces inexpensive growth. His line: "Love is the best form of 'willingness to pay.'"
- The advantage fades as you scale. WP Engine objectively still has world-class support (a dozen Stevie Awards, 98% CSAT), but it's not the same as personal attention from the founder.
- Option: never grow that large, optimize for profit and efficiency over revenue and scale, and make support a permanent competitive advantage. Either way, it is a great way to get started.

## Using new technology and making drastic changes

New technology is risky for incumbents, but the startup's constraints differ.

- Incumbents are right to avoid new tech. It may lose support in five years, be hard to hire for, have security problems (he says it will "certainly" have many), not work at scale, or be hard to coordinate across thirty developers.
- A startup is more likely to die from the things that block product/market fit: customers are hard to find, there is no budget for the problem, competition is tough, or it runs out of money. The tech stack is rarely the cause.
- New technology often creates advantage by making things efficient or possible that weren't before (AI is his current example).
- Worst case, if the startup survives, is a rebuild on a different platform five years later, possibly pausing features for a year. If that penalty "buys" a successful company, it was worth it.
- Incumbents struggle to change anything with 100,000 paying customers. Even a slight UI change brings angry tweets, clogged support, and docs and screenshots to update. Customers don't wake up wanting a different UI, and they have workflows to accommodate. Brand positioning, pricing, architecture, integrations and partners are locked in.
- A startup with 100 customers can change any of that drastically and pivot toward market reality and its own strengths. When the market shifts (AI today, mobile before), incumbents can't adapt quickly and the startup can adapt immediately.
- Part of why "big companies are dumb": they can't change even when they know they're doing something dumb.

## Having an opinionated personality

Large companies rarely express a personality, and startups can win on it.

- A big company addresses a large, diverse market, so by speaking to everyone it speaks to no one in particular. It relies on brand trust ("No one ever got fired for hiring IBM"), which means impersonal but solid language.
- Customer communication is spread over hundreds or thousands of people in support, sales, marketing, product and design. A thousand people can't share one genuine human voice, so they default to generic, professional language.
- Startup founders often have strong personalities and views on what's good and bad, which is partly why they started the company.
- Some customers will be attracted and some repelled, which is true of anything "wonderful and different and powerful."
- When a big company injects personality it feels contrived. A startup's feels genuine because it is. He says you will directly win customers because of it.
- Some buyers only want the market leader and nothing a startup says on its home page will change that. Ignore that segment and target customers who want to buy from a plucky upstart that has something to say.

## Playing non-zero-sum games

Incumbents win zero-sum games because they can apply money and expertise, and can even overspend at a loss to make a channel worse for competitors.

- Zero-sum channels arise from power laws or exclusivity:
  - SEO (the top positions get more traffic than all others combined)
  - AdWords (same)
  - Affiliates (the top few produce more leads than all others)
  - Retail shelf space
  - Exclusive distribution deals
  - Government fiat
- Non-zero-sum channels, where an entrenched incumbent can't stop others from winning:
  - Social media
  - Newsletters and content marketing
  - Collaborative promotions
  - Ecosystems like Salesforce and the Apple Store
  - Consumers who buy multiple products (3D animators often use several tools)
- Markets can be zero-sum too. Stagnant or shrinking ones are, because winning one customer means a competitor can't have that revenue. Growing markets have a stream of new dollars, so many competitors can grow. He says startups should target growing markets, whether the goal is a small profitable company or a unicorn.
- Startups needn't never play zero-sum games, but those are harder and sometimes impossible. Invest in the non-zero-sum ones.

## Being worse but acceptable on most dimensions, and low-cost against the profit center

Most customers don't care about most things, so a startup can choose one or two dimensions to care about and win the segment that cares about the same.

- Uptime example: "99.9% uptime" sounds excellent but equals 44 minutes of downtime a month. Most hosting providers promise only 99.9-99.95%. Getting to 99.99% takes an order of magnitude more spend, software and process, yet looks little different on a pricing page.
- Incumbents must invest in these dimensions because "at scale, rare things become common," and because it wins segments (WP Engine has enterprise customers whose policies demand 99.99% uptime).
- Startups don't have the rare-at-scale problem and can pick customers without extreme demands. They should actively avoid customers with myriad difficult demands.
- Dimensions where incumbents must care and startups needn't:
  - **Security:** WP Engine spends tens of millions a year (internal teams, policies, training, SOC and ISO compliance, vendor reviews) because security is a selling point. Most products and customers don't demand it.
  - **Quality:** An entire product that is low quality in every dimension is just a bad product. But a SaaS tool used in one geography's working hours can tolerate an hour of downtime in the middle of the night.
  - **Scalability:** Without "big data," build with simplifying assumptions: PostgreSQL instead of BigQuery, SQLite instead of PostgreSQL, Python instead of Erlang, reading into memory instead of streaming, batch jobs instead of queues and auto-scale groups, off-the-shelf libraries and vendors instead of bespoke.
  - **Compliance:** Internal policies (data access, device management, IP ownership, enterprise and government requirements) win multi-million-dollar three-year contracts but slow everything and raise cost and price.
  - **Legal:** Startups often (unknowingly) don't comply with all laws, such as taxes and acceptable-use policies. He doesn't advocate breaking laws on purpose, but notes it doesn't hurt sales and may speed them. Uber's growth by ignoring regulation is his example.
- Incumbents must charge more to pay for multi-dimensional excellence at scale. A startup can charge less for something objectively "worse" in many dimensions and stay profitable.
- Incumbents can't change their business model. The costs of a global sales team, white-glove onboarding, expensive infrastructure, vendors and shareholder expectations of rising profits mean they can't drop prices, so they can't match "a 'lite' version for 1/10th the price."
- This is a softer version of Disruption Theory (The Innovator's Dilemma): incumbents retreat to their most profitable segments and let the startup thrive.
- "Whatever generates the most profits for the incumbent, is the thing they are least able to change."
- Caveat: incumbents will defend profit centers heavily, but not on price. They might overspend on AdWords to ruin that channel, but can't stop a great content marketing strategy.
- Summary: "The only mistake is for a startup to go head-to-head with an incumbent where the incumbent is strong. Attack where you are strong, and they are weak."
