## Why big-company competition matters less than founders fear

Jason Cohen says every small software company on Earth is under threat from a big competitor, so the threat alone is not fatal and may not even be important. His advice is to redirect that angst toward the real danger, small startups. He says big companies are slow dinosaurs: whatever they do now is probably what they will do in two years, possibly four. The same message, product and pricing, and they still fumble new platforms like Facebook. The scary competitor is "another scrappy, smart startup like yours," the "tree-shrew" that watches from afar, skips your mistakes and copies the good parts.

## Aim to own a piece of the market, not to beat Google

- Jason argues the goal is a solid company you are proud of that "pays handsomely" without needing to be worth $1B.
- The question is "How can you own your little piece of the world", not "How can you wrest $100m of revenue from a big guy."
- He says "It's not your purpose to 'beat' another company." It is to define yourself on your own terms, not by being like or unlike someone else.
- Setting your sights on a competitor is fine, for example discounting for customers who switch to you.
- The only thing that ultimately matters is that you earn more and more customers, whether or not anyone else does too.

## A big player entering your market can raise your value

Jason says a gorilla can be an advantage, especially if it enters after you are established. His example is Smart Bear, which makes a peer code review tool (Code Collaborator).

- Any Microsoft code review tool would only work with Microsoft's own version control system and inside Visual Studio. It would not support ClearCase, run in Eclipse, or serve Java well.
- Even if Microsoft owned 100% of the Visual Studio market, that leaves every other market.
- Microsoft's competitors (IBM, CA, Oracle, Serena, CompuWare, HP) would then need a code review tool right away to stay level on competitive-analysis charts. The fastest route is buying the #1 or #2 code review company, which is Smart Bear, and it is profitable.
- So a Microsoft copy would validate the market and raise Smart Bear's value.
- What actually happened: Microsoft added "shelving" and put the least possible effort into code review, essentially a check-box saying a review happened somewhere. Smart Bear sells a lot of Code Collaborator to Visual Studio shops.
- Jason's lesson: they validated the market by entering, but because they are huge they could not make it good enough to stop him.

## Go where big companies can't follow: small-but-real markets

- Big companies play only in big markets. With expensive machinery and bureaucracy, building something new takes "a dump truck of money and dozens of man-years", so the opportunity must be enormous.
- Even a success in a small market would not move the needle at the top.
- Microsoft can't attack a market unless it can earn at least $1B, yet Jason notes you would be happy in a market where you rake in "only" $100M.
- He is not recommending micro-niches with seven potential customers on Earth.
- Avoid massive general markets like "everyone with a digital camera", "anyone with a smart phone" or "all software developers."

## Do what they structurally cannot do

Jason says big companies' advantages (money, brand, team, large customer base, many customers who never switch even to a clearly better alternative) come with restrictions. Their brand ("eBay is trustable", "Apple is cool", "IBM is safe") is a force you probably can't overcome. But the same attributes limit them:

- They can't release a completely revamped new version because they can't retrain 200,000 users.
- They can't take risks, because protecting the existing revenue stream beats everything, even at the cost of their ultimate demise.
- They can't turn new ideas into released code quickly, because of requirements, documents, designers, approvals, schedules, testing and vetting.
- They can't change their image. If they have a reputation for bad tech support, most people keep thinking so even after it is fixed.
- They can't react quickly to market shifts, with too many layers of people and process and careers tied to the status quo.

## Examples of what incumbents cannot copy

- **Intuit and Mint:** Intuit needs to look solid and timeless, its developers know C++ and desktop apps, and it can't retrain computer-phobic Quicken home users, so it could not create Mint.
- **IBM and small consulting:** IBM's expensive infrastructure, dev teams and sales channels exist to command multi-million-dollar consulting deals. That makes small deals unprofitable for it, so small consulting shops never worry about competing with IBM.
- **The takeaway:** They can't change their product, so you can innovate without competition. They can't change their image, so you can fill the gap. They can't listen to a customer and ship an impact a week later; you can. "Do what they can't do, be what they're not," and you won't have to compete on those points.
