## Show, don't tell: authentic values come from actions, not adjective lists

Jason argues that a company's values are communicated through what it does, not through the adjectives it lists in marketing copy.

- "The cardinal rule of authenticity and believability is that actions speak louder than words."
- Corollary: if you have to tell someone something is true, they automatically don't believe you. An honest person doesn't walk around asking "Hey, did you know I'm honest?"
- Values are "the reason for your actions, the theme behind your words, and the underlying consistency in how you do business", not words to be shoe-horned into tag-lines or stapled onto disingenuous mission statements.
- The rule applies to customer service, the website, how you sell, and even how you hire, not just to commercials.

## Why adjective lists and "Mad Libs" copy backfire

Jason criticizes the common marketing exercise of listing every adjective you want customers to associate with the company or product.

- He cringes at the size and scope of such lists. The bigger problem comes when the marketing department "plays Mad Libs" with them.
- His example is Sun GlassFish Enterprise Server copy: "easy to use, fast, and scalable ... easy to download, develop, and deploy ... robust, highly-available, and cost-effective." He calls it *prima facie* false.
- His test: have you ever used a system that embodied every one of those attributes, with no trade-offs, no compromise, A-plus-plus on every count? No.
- Result: the reader doesn't know which claims are true, so they distrust all of them, and nothing tangible has been communicated.

## The actor's "motivation" analogy

Jason compares company values to a character's motivation in acting.

- A character's motivation is the secret reason she is angry, depressed or indifferent. Actors often invent a detailed back-story for how the character got to this point.
- You don't publish the back-story. His example: you don't announce "Darth Vader is disillusioned with the notion of a Republic." "Explanation ruins everything!"
- Applied to business: values should drive visible behavior and consistency, not be explained out loud.

## Apple as the model of demonstrating values without words

Jason says Apple has mastered conveying values without stating them.

- Apple's values include "design is paramount," "form over function," and even "Apple is cool."
- An iPod wouldn't be cool because a corporation claimed it was, and listing features and benefits wouldn't convey it either.
- Apple's iPod commercial instead shows the product and the feeling. He contrasts this with how "standard" marketing machinery would portray the same features and benefits.

## Replace claims with proof: action versus slogan pairs

Jason gives concrete swaps where a policy or behavior replaces a claim.

- High-quality product: don't say "Your satisfaction is important to us"; have a 90-day no-questions-asked return policy.
- Caring about talking to customers: don't play a recorded message saying their call is important; simply answer the phone.
- Customers love you: don't say "100 companies use our software"; have a web page with 100 stellar testimonials.
- Treating employees as human beings: don't call them "resources" (he links this to his argument that labels matter); mock companies that do, and display testimonials from your own employees.

## Once you walk the walk, you can call it out

Jason says that after the behavior is real, you have earned the right to mention it, and the words then work because they point at something tangible.

- Quality: "We're so confident in the quality of our hammer, if ever breaks we'll swap it out with a brand-new replacement. For free!"
- Service: "At MyCo, a human being always answers the phone. Why? Because business is personal."
- Social proof: "Don't take our word for it, read for yourself what our customers say. Did we pick the best ones? Well yeah, but we have 100 'best ones!'"
- Culture, from Fog Creek: "What if programmers were treated like rock stars? ... management, not coding, is the support function. ... people love working here."
- The underlying values are still words like quality, service, happy customers and great place to work, but they appear as tangible demonstrations, not trite phrases plastered in all the expected places.

## Takeaway: values are the means, not the end

- "Let values motivate action. Values are the means to the end. Get to the end."
- "Actions count; words don't."
- Practical test for any claim in copy: can you replace it with a policy, a behavior, or evidence the customer can see?
