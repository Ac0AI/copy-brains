## Sustainable growth versus "successful" unsustainable companies

Jason argues too many companies funded and celebrated as "successful" are unsustainable. His summary of the pattern: "Build up a business, create impressive 'shareholder value,' then it fails." Growth alone is not the measure of a lasting company: "Growth is necessary, but not sufficient."

- He says the press and the Valley reward companies that raise money and create shareholder value, even when the business later collapses.
- He asks founders to be honest about what "lasting" means before they decide what to build.
- If the goal is a flash-in-the-pan, like a cool mobile app, he says that is fine too, as long as the founder knows that is what it is.

## The Bill Nguyen case: investors acting rationally on a track record of exits that later failed

Jason uses serial entrepreneur Bill Nguyen (from a Fast Company profile) to show how a "successful" track record can hide unsustainable companies.

- Color, his seventh startup, raised $41m for a mobile app before launch. The launch failed and it went bankrupt.
- Jason says the investors backing Nguyen are "acting rationally": before the house of cards tumbles, private equity investors get a tidy return, and Nguyen knows how to keep the magic going long enough for the payday.
- Track record as Jason lists it: Forefront (IPO'd 1995, parent stock fell 85% in 1998); Freeloader (sold for $38m on $3m invested, shut down in 1997); Support.com (IPO'd at $32/share in 2000, now about $2); OneBox (sold for $850m on $60m invested, 18 months after launch); Seven (still private, cancelled IPO); Lala (sold to Apple for $80m on $35m invested, shut down in June).
- He rejects blaming the acquirers: they are not all stupid. His own sold companies did well afterward: Smart Bear grew revenue and profit every year for five years after the sale, even through 2008/2009, and IT WatchDogs did the same after its 2005 sale.
- He treats Nguyen's persuasion ("the Jedi," in a quoted colleague's words) as a skill that keeps the funding flowing, and asks whether such a person is driven by truth or deception.

## The bigger problem: companies everyone honestly believed were sustainable

Jason says the bigger issue is not deceivers but what Dan Lyons calls unsustainable companies that people honestly believed were sustainable.

- **Groupon:** a product everyone called brilliant, with 1,000 copy-cats. Heavy investment in growth and a sales force had no long-term payback, and the product's value to small businesses was lower than claimed. He says simple customer development (asking any restaurateur) would have revealed this. It IPO'd at a $13b valuation, fell month over month to about one-fourth of that, and appeared to be in "constant pivot mode" seeking product/market fit again.
- **Zynga:** financially successful at a certain scale, but individual game successes are fleeting and he doubts they can keep innovating enough games to justify their size. They bought Draw Something for $200m and it lost 5m active users a month after the purchase. Stock was $2.43 against a $10 IPO price under a year earlier.
- These were not like Color. They had real, growing revenues (not just "active users"), a repeatable, scalable business model, ownership of a large market, and had survived the operational challenges of rapid growth.
- His two candidate explanations: the markets were more short-lived than assumed ("Facebook games" a fad rather than an industry), or executives and investors over-valued growth versus sustainability. Groupon's engine that turned capital into revenue growth was "a form of force-feeding rather than building a product."
- He flags this as hindsight-based armchair speculation.

## What sustainable high-growth companies look like

Jason names companies that grow fast and remain sustainable, and what each does:

- **HubSpot:** a revenue curve "as predictable" as a plot on a TI-90, while obsessing over customer satisfaction and retention.
- **Freshbooks:** an enviable corporate culture plus an unflagging revenue curve.
- **SEOMoz:** insistence on TAGFEE wins over employees, customers and investors, giving consistent growth even in the tumultuous SEO tools market.
- **Rackspace:** a "maniacal obsession" with excellent customer service lets them charge premium prices and sustain about 30% annual growth at $1b in revenue.

Financing path does not decide it. Some were bootstrapped, some bootstrapped and raised later, some had huge funding from the start: "It's not about the financing path, it's about what you've decided to build."

## WP Engine's model for sustainable growth, and questions Jason asks founders

Jason states his goal to put WP Engine among those excellent companies. At the time of writing it had thirty employees and millions in revenue, and he describes it as experiencing fantastic growth that he believes is sustainable. He borrows a trait from each exemplar:

- A Rackspace-like commitment to customer service (more WordPress experts per 1,000 customers than anyone).
- An SEOMoz-like commitment to honesty and transparency.
- A HubSpot-like obsession with introspective measurement, covering customer happiness and retention, not just marketing and growth.

Questions he poses for any founder:

- Is your company building something of lasting value?
- Are you valuing growth over sustainable growth?
- Are you articulating and then living up to company cultural values that attract and retain the right people, who then create the right product and service?
