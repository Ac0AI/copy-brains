## Jason treats common "fallacies" as heuristics that can be rational

- Jason rejects the "back on the Serengeti, this made sense, but now it's a fallacy" narrative. He says "not all heuristics are 'fallacies.'"
- He argues that calling something a fallacy implies it breaks "cold, hard, scintillating, pure, perfect logic," and he disagrees with some of that logic.
- He objects to the lizard-brain framing and to buzzwords like "first principles," "maximize expected value" and "Bayesian priors" used to look enlightened.
- His closing advice is to use ideas and tools that fit each person's circumstances, think for yourself, and run your own experiments instead of believing "models and theories with more exceptions than examples."

## "Maximize expected value" is the real fallacy for one-shot, high-stakes decisions

- Jason believes maximizing expected value is "rarely the correct way to make a decision."
- He notes that economists who push it cannot predict major economic metrics or markets, yet call everyone else irrational.
- Thought experiment: a fair coin flip where heads doubles your life savings and tails loses all of it.
  - Expected value is zero (sum of outcomes weighted by probability), so EV says playing and not playing are equivalent.
  - People with little in the bank might play. Most people who spent years building a nest egg would not.
- Change it to 55% win and 45% lose-everything:
  - EV becomes positive (+10% of savings, so $10,000 on $100,000).
  - Very few people would change their answer, because losing everything at 45% is essentially as bad as at 50%.
- Jason calls the "Expected Value Fallacy" using statistics that only apply if you play the game a million times and keep the average, then applying them to a single play.
- Losing everything is the point: the outcomes are not symmetric, so averaging them is the wrong lens.

## Loss aversion and the endowment effect are not irrational

- **Loss aversion** (valuing loss pain above gain joy):
  - It took "a lifetime of pain and sacrifice and luck" to build what you have, so protecting it more than you chase more is rational.
  - This matters most when the loss is catastrophic.
  - If the loss is immaterial (an experiment with 1/1000th of your money), people happily play. So the "fallacy" only gets invoked when the loss is important.
- **Endowment effect** (valuing a thing more because you own it):
  - Owning something carries real value: emotional investment in the decision, identity ("a person who would have this object"), how others perceive the ownership, and sentiment that grows over time. He says economic theory is wrong if it treats sentiment as worthless.
  - Concert-ticket example: a $200 ticket, per a study he cites, wouldn't be sold for less than $300 to $400. Jason's explanation is that you bought it because you value going to the show, so selling the ticket means selling the experience, which is worth more than face value.

## Sunk cost, confirmation bias and status quo bias have a startup upside

- **Sunk cost:**
  - In long-term projects, commitment can lead to eventual success, while "fail fast" can ensure failure.
  - Emotional and financial investment drives perseverance when there's little apparent hope, which can be the key to overcoming obstacles.
  - He says sometimes people claim to avoid the sunk cost fallacy when "really we're justifying bailing out when times got tough."
- **Confirmation bias:**
  - Constant self-doubt leads to inaction, and frequent plan changes confuse people.
  - A leadership team that keeps shifting priorities will confound the whole organization. "A strategy that constantly changes, cannot be executed."
  - A strategy that never changes is also wrong. There is a time to reevaluate, but that time is not "always."
- **Status quo bias:**
  - Stability and consistency build reliability and trust.
  - A startup thrusts constant change on its people, so keeping some things constant gives everyone something to hold on to.
  - If you put real thought into a decision, changing it should take even more thought. It's not a bias to honor your former self unless new information would have changed the decision.

## Availability, recency and overconfidence bias are how startups operate

- **Availability bias:**
  - Startups (and even scaled-up companies) often lack comprehensive or statistically significant data, and extensive research is impractical.
  - You must act on what's in front of you under uncertainty rather than fall into analysis paralysis. "It's not a bias, it's life."
- **Recency bias:**
  - Early in a startup almost everything is being done wrong, so reacting quickly to what's in front of you is how you iterate to doing it right.
  - "This is how we did it at my last company" doesn't matter when that company was 400x larger, 50x older and in a different industry.
  - Agility is one of a startup's few advantages, and "reacting to the latest information is the definition of agile."
- **Overconfidence:**
  - He cites that 93% of drivers believe they are above-average drivers. That is mathematically wrong, but it gives them the confidence to act, and constant second-guessing would make them worse.
  - "Founders must be over-confident." The most likely outcome is failure, so you need overconfidence to stay optimistic.
  - At the beginning, successes and failures look the same, so you need it to push through.
  - Before product/market fit you can't know whether or when you'll hit it, so you need it to keep trying things and treat everything as an experiment, not a failure.
  - Overconfidence is not blindness: "Being confident in the vision, but skeptical about every detail, is how you find the truth."

## Survivorship bias is real, but copying successes still beats avoiding failures

- Jason has been pointing out survivor bias in business advice for more than 15 years.
- He concedes several points:
  - Successes are lucky, not just good.
  - Failed companies often do the same things as successful ones, so those things may not have caused success.
  - Often one or two most important things caused the success despite everything else, not because of it.
- He disagrees that "you learn more from failure than from success." Failure shows what didn't work, but that doesn't point to what does.
- His test: which is more likely to work, copying everything about a success or avoiding everything about a failure? The success, because "not all of it was luck."

## Herd behavior: be different in one place and conventional everywhere else

- Your product and company should differ in one way your target customers believe makes you the best in the world (for them).
- In every other way, "surprises are bad." His examples of unwanted novelty:
  - A unique pricing model where the customer can't work out the bottom-line price.
  - Bizarre controls built because the team didn't want to use menubars.
  - Non-standard icons for things like copy and paste, chosen to be creative.
- Adhering to norms provides "safety, understanding, control, and ease," which are desirable in products and companies except where you make a genuine improvement.
