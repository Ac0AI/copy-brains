## Success comes from valuing the journey, not hitting a peak outcome

Jason argues that startup success cannot be guaranteed by any specific result. It can only be guaranteed by how you define success. His claim: "If you value the journey, your 'success' is guaranteed."

- The Depeche Mode story is his proof. The band sold out the Pasadena Rose Bowl (capacity 60,000) on the 101st show of their 1988 tour. Critics expected humiliation, and the band themselves hoped to sell only the 10,000 floor seats. Instead it sold out, with paid attendance above any Rose Bowl event in the prior eight years.
- Backstage, singer Dave Gahan cried because he didn't know if the group could ever do anything this great again. Jason notes they never did, despite more hits, albums and tours.
- His lesson: "reaching the peak means by definition your next steps must be downhill." The peak is fleeting and often unhappy.

## Why a single "moment of success" is a trap

- The same pattern hits founders. Jason cites well-documented sadness and even depression right after an exit.
- The mistake is believing "the goal is to achieve a single moment of success": a big sale, an IPO, or passing the company to your daughter.
- His reframe: "it is the building, not the result of that building, that matters."
- The math: about 99.99% of your time is the journey and 0.01% is basking in "success" (a sale, ringing the bell at an IPO). Often that moment never comes, so it is 100% journey.

## How to define winning

Jason's test is a retrospective one. You win if you can look back and say:

- It was a wonderful time and you'd do it again.
- You'd change some things, but you're proud and stronger and wiser than ever.
- You created terrific jobs where great people were empowered to build important things together.
- They were comfortable and safe, shared in whatever upside you could muster, and would say they're personally fulfilled too.

## The cost of outcome-based definitions of success

- If success is tied to metrics, money, growth rate, headcount, whether you exited, how much you raised, or how many users you got, "mathematically you're almost guaranteed to fail."
- The failure comes only from your own definition of failure.
- You end up spending years in nervous agony chasing an outcome you think will make you happy, instead of "making all those years be the years you are happy."

## Keep score, but don't confuse the game with life

- Jason is explicit that he is not anti-money. He made plenty from previous startups and hopes to make more at WP Engine, where all employees are shareholders, as are the investors.
- Honoring metrics is part of building a high-performing team and a huge, sustainable enterprise. There is nothing wrong with using metrics and money to keep score.
- The rule: "Keep score, so long as you can distinguish between the game and life."
- Enjoy the journey alongside the score: generating happy customers, launching unique products, and gathering the energy and brainpower of brilliant people tackling interesting problems.

## Journey focus solves the hiring and recruiting riddle

- Jason paraphrases Peter Thiel: the first employee joins because it's the ground floor of an exciting startup, but why will the 20th person join? Or the 200th?
- His answer: "To be part of the journey."
- So valuing the nature of the journey is also what makes a company attractive to the people it needs to hire as it grows, and it is why the founder should be there too.
