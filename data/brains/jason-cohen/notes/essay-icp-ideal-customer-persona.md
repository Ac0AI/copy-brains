## Why speaking to "everyone" fails and the ICP is the fix

- Jason argues most companies make the home page speak to "everyone" (buyers, users, existing customers, large and small companies) for fear that a narrow message makes everyone else bounce.
- He says the result is generic positioning like SAS's trademarked "The Power To Know®". The Penny Group uses the same trademarked line, so there is no defensible differentiation and no information.
- By not speaking to a specific customer, you say nothing to everyone, and everyone bounces and never thinks of you again. "If your ideal customer doesn't know you're selling to them, who will?"
- It is also impossible to speak to everyone at once. Developers want to hear about the extensible API, corporate buyers want to hear it makes them more money, large companies want configurable workflows and reports, and small companies want simplicity. You want to look professional to big buyers without looking "too corporate" to small ones.
- His conclusion: be evocative and specific, you can't speak to everyone, so speak to one type of customer.

## How to define "Carol", the perfect customer

- Pick one concrete perfect customer, "Carol", at a concrete company similar to one of your existing thrilled customers. Give her an official title and say what she does.
- Carol has all the problems your product solves, knows she has them, already has the budget, and is already looking for how to spend it. Write the problems down in her words, as she'd complain to a friend over lunch.
- Carol needs exactly your competitive advantages and has the attributes of people who love you and who keep paying forever.
- Where to find these answers:
  - Sales team: the thing a prospect says that means "we're going to close the sale".
  - Support team: which customers ask great questions and say they love you.
  - Retention data: who stays forever, even if you think they shouldn't.
  - Customer interviews using the Iterative-Hypothesis method.
- Jason says to skip invented back-stories like "Carol has two cats and loves Mahler". They are irrelevant and inaccurate. Stick to professional attributes: what she's interested in, why, why she's looking for a new solution now, how she decides, and her budget.

## The 3-second test for ads and home pages

- You get about 3 seconds to grab Carol's attention and another 5-10 to convince her. A paragraph won't do. Communicate in a picture and a few words.
- Ways to do it:
  - A before/after she can relate to.
  - Three features that make it plain.
  - Your best competitive advantage.
  - A provocative question she identifies with.
  - A phrase she'd laugh at because "that's so true".
- The test, in his words: "If your ad can't grab Carol's attention--your perfect customer--why do you think it will grab anyone else's attention?"
- If you say it's impossible to communicate your message in 5-10 seconds, no one in the world will get it. Simplicity matters in positioning as much as in strategy.
- Examples he cites of speaking to a specific audience:
  - Twilio targets developers even though the buyer isn't one, and the campaign was effective enough to become a book.
  - Dollar Shave Club was unabashedly "cheap but good" and ignored Gillette-style sleek advertising.
  - Subaru knows it isn't cool and highlights its positives.
  - Porsche's message is differentiated from Subaru's.

## Why targeting Carol makes the market 10x to 100x larger, not smaller

Jason's bullseye model, using Subaru. Strengths: low-cost, reliable, safe, rugged. Weaknesses: low performance, not sporty, not cool, ugly.

- **Carol (center):** values everything on the strengths side and actively doesn't want the weaknesses side. She'd never be caught in a Porsche, so the weaknesses are strengths to her.
- **Diana (next ring):** values your positives or is indifferent to them, and isn't dissuaded by the weaknesses. Jason's own example is driving his daughter to school and to work, wanting safe and reliable, and not caring about performance. "There are 10x more of those people than there are of Carol."
- **Eddie (outer ring):** weighs trade-offs differently, may care about looks or not care about off-roading, but still decides this trade-off set is the best available. He supposes about 1/10th of the people in that ring pick you over competitors, and that there are about 100x more of them than of Diana.
- Net effect: the effective target market is at least 10x larger because of Diana and more like 20x-100x larger because of Eddie.
- Why it works: targeting Carol makes the message clear, compelling and emotional. People buy on trade-offs, and because you were clear about yours, you paved the road for many people to make that choice.

## Case study: Subaru in the 1990s

- Sales were declining, so Subaru researched which segments were most likely to buy. It found five: teachers, healthcare professionals, IT professionals, "outdoorsy types", and lesbians. Lesbians were the strongest, four times more likely to buy.
- This was risky then. When Ellen DeGeneres came out in 1997, advertisers including Chrysler pulled out, and Ikea received bomb threats after running an ad with a gay couple. Jason quotes Buffett: "Be greedy when others are fearful."
- Result: the campaigns were a hit, and Subaru grew faster over the following ten years than any other car company founded before 1990, measured as a share of total sales volume from 1995 to 2005.
- The vast majority of Subaru buyers were still straight, buying for the affordable, safe, rugged attributes and caring more about those than about sleek, sporty, high performance.
- His takeaway: target Carol and your positioning is strongest and you win where you deserve to win, but far more people buy because they share similar purchasing preferences.

## Separating Carol from the noise in your data

- Cancellations: unless the customer stopped their project or went out of business, a cancellation signals a gap between what marketing promised and what the product delivered. This is most urgent when the customer is Carol, since you declared her perfect. Either the product must change or your definition of Carol must change.
- Diana's cancellations deserve the same scrutiny. Eddie's are less clear. Eddie may cancel because of attributes you shouldn't change, since they align with Carol and Diana.
- If you treat every customer as Carol and every cancellation as vital signal, "we lose track of building for Carol and we end up building for nobody."
- Some customers aren't even Eddie. They are off the bullseye yet signed up, which happens all the time. Identify them and ignore their data, because the noise pushes you off course.
- Tactics:
  - Identify who is a Carol right from onboarding, with a few questions or activity indicators.
  - Measure Carol's cancellation rate, "the only rate you care about", and spend extra time finding out why she cancels.
  - The extra time comes from the time saved by not pursuing and interviewing other customers.
- Segment Carols from everyone else everywhere: activation funnel, trial or freemium conversion, feature usage, conversion to monthly active users, and tech-support requests for bugs and features. "It's always about the Carols; everything else is noise."

## Prioritize Carol over revenue: turn down the big non-ICP deal

- Typical failure mode: your biggest customer, whom you desperately want to keep for the revenue, constantly occupies support and feature requests. You build features just for them, telling yourself larger customers will want them too, yet they stay unhappy and are obviously an anti-Carol.
- They eventually leave in a huff, having been very unprofitable despite high nominal revenue. You should have recognized they were never in your bullseye and helped them leave early.
- The opportunity cost: you could have been finding and onboarding more Carols, who would still be paying you profitably.
- His marker for doing this right: you turn down a sale, even your largest ever, because the buyer isn't your ICP. He cites Daniel Zarick of ArrowsHQ, who rejected a $48,000 sale for that reason, and John Doherty of EditorNinja.
- "This separates first-time founders from seasoned founders." Both Zarick and Doherty had made the mistake before and avoided it the second time.
