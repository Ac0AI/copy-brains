## Failure to face the truth is a primary blocker of progress

- Jason argues that a primary blocker of progress, from personal lives to corporate strategy, is a "failure to face the truth."
- The advice recurs across many books, frameworks and decades. His view: "When something is so consistent, it must be wisdom."
- The default mode is denial (when intentional) and rationalization (when unintentional). Because people don't realize they do it, it's an "immense, invisible barrier."
- People avoid the truth because they don't like admitting they're wrong, even privately. Saying it aloud can also be annoying, painful or career-altering.
- Once you look for the pattern, it shows up everywhere:
  - In almost every meeting, someone is thinking something and not saying it, even though one of the best uses of a meeting is to unveil and discuss insights.
  - In every strategy discussion there is a monster in the room that no one will name, even though the point of strategy is to identify the monsters and build a battle plan against them.
  - Written plans and strategies are "all optimism and confirmatory data" instead of crystallizing the scary challenges so the team can attack them together.
- Those who break through this barrier are rewarded, as many famous books and frameworks point out.

## Radical Candor: telling the truth is a courtesy

- Jason cites Kim Scott's story. After a presentation to Google executives, Sheryl Sandberg told her, "When you say 'um' every third word, it makes you sound stupid."
- Scott knew Sandberg didn't think she was stupid, so she understood the feedback was meant to help. She was grateful to be made to "face the truth."
- Scott's reaction: "It was like I'd been walking through my whole career with a hunk of spinach between my teeth and nobody had had the courtesy to tell me it was there."
- Jason's takeaway:
  - The truth is a courtesy.
  - Failing to face it is at least impolite, and at worst "ruinous," as when a manager withholds necessary feedback for fear of hurt feelings.
- *Radical Candor* starts from personal trust. The receiver must believe the giver cares about their well-being. Without that trust, the same bluntness becomes what Scott calls "obnoxious aggression," feedback meant to belittle or dominate.
- Jason's analogy: a coach who tells you everything is fine when it isn't, even by omission, is a bad coach.

## Five Dysfunctions of a Team: trust makes conflict safe

- Patrick Lencioni's *Five Dysfunctions of a Team* describes great teams as built in layers, each requiring the one below it.
- Jason's reading: a team must be capable of constructive conflict, "discussing the real issues," which is facing the truth. That requires building trust first, so conflict is safe.
- Failure to face the truth together, in the open, prevents teams from being great.
- Teams need enough trust in each other to face it. If someone can't handle that trust, "maybe they're not right for the group after all."

## Good Strategy, Bad Strategy: name the challenge

- Jason takes his phrase from Richard Rumelt's "Failure to face the challenge." Rumelt lists these hallmarks of bad strategy:
  - Fluff: gibberish masquerading as strategic concepts.
  - Failure to face the challenge: if you can't define the challenge, you can't evaluate or improve a strategy.
  - Mistaking goals for strategy: statements of desire rather than plans for overcoming obstacles.
  - Bad strategic objectives: ones that fail to address critical issues or are impracticable.
- Strategy must identify and then address the most important and difficult facts of the market, competitive space, customers, product and team.
- Example: "Our market is shrinking" is scary to say. If it's true and you refuse to name it, don't write it down that crisply, don't challenge the company to find alternatives, and don't build a strategy that attacks it head-on, then "it will be fatal."

## Fluffy language hides a deeper failure

- Fluffy language is a personal peeve of Jason's.
  - In the benign case it's laziness: filling the page with jargon and generic phrases to avoid crafting prose.
  - In the worst case it shows the lack of having any thoughts in the first place.
- It's rampant in marketing and content marketing, and especially common in vision and mission statements.
- Bad example: "A leading provider of website development for businesses of all sizes."
  - It says almost nothing, so it's wrong. It can't be true of building sites for Ethiopian coffee farmers and also for Tesla's next vehicle launch.
  - Perhaps the company refuses to face what it really does, who it really serves, where it's strong and weak, and is afraid to say "no" to any potential customer.
- Better example: "We are boutique artisans chosen by discerning businesses who demand the highest-quality, completely unique web experiences."
  - "Boutique" signals a small company that charges a lot. "Discerning" and "unique" signal amazing work.
  - Saying "no" to people who don't want that lets you say "yes" to interesting projects at profitable rates.
  - It also lets you beat larger competitors you can characterize as "factories that churn out the same website with different colors."
  - Jason's condition: "But only if you face the truth."

## Kroger vs. A&P: seeing the truth is not enough

- Jason retells Jim Collins's *Good to Great* story about Kroger and A&P, where only Kroger was willing to "confront the brutal facts."
- Starting positions:
  - A&P was the largest retailer in the world and, at one point, ranked behind only General Motors in sales.
  - Kroger was an unspectacular grocery chain, less than half A&P's size.
  - Both were old companies heading into the 1970s (Kroger 82, A&P 111), with nearly all assets in traditional grocery stores and strongholds outside the major growth areas.
- A&P's model was perfect for the first half of the twentieth century: cheap, plentiful groceries in utilitarian stores.
  - In the affluent second half, Americans wanted bigger stores, fresh-baked bread, flowers, cold medicines, forty-five cereals, ten milks, banking and flu shots.
  - In short, they wanted super-stores, not grocery stores.
- Both companies knew the world was changing. A&P even opened a test super-store under a different name, and it outperformed the standard stores.
- A&P didn't want to face those facts. It shut the test store and went back to business as usual.
- Kroger ran the same experiments, got the same results, and pivoted the entire company into the modern supermarket.
- Kroger and A&P grew about the same for twenty years, both underperforming the market. Kroger then exploded after "confronting the brutal facts" while A&P kept faltering.
- Jason's conclusion: "A&P saw the truth, but failed to face the truth." Across Collins's dozens of companies and Rumelt's dozens of strategies the pattern is the same: "Face the truth, or be killed by it."

## Why we avoid the truth: it's hard

- Jason compares it to other obvious things people avoid only because they're hard: diet, exercise, giving feedback, working on relationships.
- Specific hard truths he lists:
  - Telling the truth to someone's face.
  - Realizing your industry has completely shifted, and saying so in front of the whole company.
  - Saying "no" to a customer when you have bills to pay.
  - Making a major strategic choice, "because what if you're wrong?"
- His verdict: that's a good explanation for the failure, "but it's not a good reason." The instruction is simply "Face the truth."
- He closes with d'Alembert's advice to early calculus practitioners, when rigorous proofs were still a hundred years away: "Go ahead. Faith will follow."
