## The journey is the whole book, and most of it is drudgery

- Jason argues we "skip to the last page of other people's books" and forget they took the whole journey, so we feel bad when we're only on "Chapter Four" and ask when we get to the good part.
- The visible success is a small fraction of the effort. For musicians, he says there are "a hundred hours of toil behind each visible hour of glory": practicing in spaces that aren't sufficiently isolated, landing gigs, setting up stages, and getting more than four people to show up.
- His claim is that invention in every field, not only engineering, is mostly drudgery rather than inspiration.

## Edison: roughly three years of trial and error before a salable product

- Edison spent 18 months of trial and error producing the first workable light, which lasted only 13 hours before the carbon fiber filament burned out.
- He spent another 18 months of trial and error before finding that a carbonized bamboo filament lasted 100x longer, which made a practical, salable light bulb.
- Jason quotes Edison: "I have not failed 10,000 times--I've successfully found 10,000 ways that will not work."
- Lesson: persistence through many failed attempts is the mechanism of invention, not a detour from it.

## What drudgery looks like when you build a product

Jason lists the unglamorous work that fills most days:
- Fixing bugs your unit tests "proved couldn't exist".
- Tying off loose ends of development that never stop arising.
- Planning, estimating, communicating and post-morteming.
- Taking the wrong path and backtracking.
- Planning the launch, the training, the targeting, the positioning and the alpha testers.
- Losing five hours on a problem that, on another day with different luck, might have taken five minutes.

## Why people quit, and why that's the trap

- Products have no clear end the way a book does, and it's often unclear whether to continue or quit. He describes the early stage as chaos where you can't tell if you're on the right track.
- People quit the job, thinking the next company will be much better, or quit the startup because it "apparently" isn't working. He acknowledges tiredness is real and "life's too short" is a fair thought.
- His point: it is the drudgery itself that tempts people out, and it is exactly the stretch that has to be endured.

## Who eventually succeeds

- Those who succeed are the ones who "plodded through the 5,999 filaments that didn't work", put in the 10,000 hours to master a craft, and fought through the overwhelming pile of challenges and drudgery that "is always required to create something great".
- Success is not found by avoiding the grind; it is the result of getting through it.

## Luck, credit and meaning

- When you finally succeed, you can say "I really *did* something". That is also the cue for others to dismiss it as mostly luck, and Jason concedes: "They're not altogether wrong."
- He acknowledges it may seem crazy to do all this for a fleeting feeling of accomplishment, but suggests "maybe it's the meaning of life."
- Practical takeaway: expect the work to be mostly drudgery, don't read your own Chapter Four against someone else's final chapter, and don't treat the absence of glory mid-journey as a sign you're on the wrong path.
