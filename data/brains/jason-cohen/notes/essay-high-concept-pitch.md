## Why the high-concept pitch fails, and what Jason uses instead

Jason rejects the "high-concept pitch" (popularised by Venture Hacks): a three-word phrase that borrows a known successful product. Examples he cites: "YouTube is Flickr for video," "LinkedIn is Facebook for business," "Twitter is Blogger for Attention Deficit Disorder." His position: "I like brevity, but I don't like the high-concept pitch." It omits so much that it becomes ambiguous, and it "defines you as a copy of something else."

- He wants a company to define itself on its own terms, with clarity.
- His core objection: "When you use only three words and when you invoke something well-known and complicated, it's not clear what message will be received."
- He calls himself "brevity's biggest fan", but argues "there's such a thing as 'too brief.'"

## The three layers of messaging Jason uses

He separates three artifacts, each with a different job:

- **Elevator pitch:** critical to the business, not just for pitching but to crystallize the company's goals in the founder's own mind.
- **One-line positioning statement:** a single clear sentence defining the company. It is not used on customers. It is something to "tack on your wall", a target that all marketing and communication should work towards.
- **High-concept pitch:** the even tighter analogy phrase, which he declines to use.

He sticks with Eric Sink's definition of the positioning statement as "the fundamental particle in my marketing universe."

## Worked example: a portable projector

Jason illustrates with a hypothetical compact projector.

**Product pitch** (all the details and reasons to buy):
- Weighs under a pound, the size of a cell phone.
- Uses bright LEDs, so you never blow out a $100 bulb.
- Salespeople don't have to haul equipment or plug into venue projectors that are missing "yellow" or can't run at the laptop's resolution.
- Works 100% of the time and is small enough to get through airport security in a jacket pocket.

**Positioning statement:** "A compact, portable, rugged projector that eliminates surprises on the road." Short, yet it still carries the primary features and benefits.

**High-concept version:** "iPhone for projectors." It sounds good at first (easy to use, pretty, coveted, commercially successful, and the analogy fits size and portability), but it fails on the details below.

## How an analogy imports unwanted attributes

Borrowing a famous product imports all of its attributes, including bad ones for your audience. For "iPhone for projectors," Jason lists these risks (as of 2009):

- iPhones had a reputation for not working well with Microsoft Office. That is particularly bad for traveling salespeople, who live on PowerPoint and Outlook, not GMail and Open Office.
- He spoke to a business traveler who avoided the iPhone specifically because it lacked Exchange integration (since fixed).
- iPhones are seen as inflexible and not customizable, and they "bucket you in a culture."
- After the v2.0 software problems, "buggy" could also be implied.

Takeaway: you don't control which of the analogy's attributes the listener picks up.

## On investor advice that "the product is nothing"

Jason pushes back on the Venture Hacks line that "for investors, the product is nothing."

- He accepts the underlying point: product is not strategy, and product strategy interests investors more than feature bullets.
- But he considers "is nothing" an exaggeration to make a point.
- He has met an investor for whom it was literally true, and advises: "steer clear." His reasoning: "Strategy with zero product understanding isn't strategy."
- Despite the disagreement, he recommends Venture Hacks as a must-read for anyone funding or selling a company.
