## Re-adding things you cut from the backlog is a sign you're culling enough

- Jason argues that a backlog grows without bound unless it is culled. "1000 tickets is the same as 100 tickets, except that you haven't identified which 10% are most important," which means you aren't working on the most important ones.
- If you delete feature requests or bugs and later find some of them were needed after all, that is a sign you're handling the backlog well.
- His rule of thumb: "If you're not adding back feature-requests or bugs you cleaned out of the backlog, you're not cleaning out enough."
- He recommends keeping multiple backlogs, except for the work being done right now.

## Pivoting a strategy right after writing it is expected

- Jason says a strategy that never changes is wrong, and the most likely time to find out it's wrong is just after you wrote it down, because you have the least practical experience with how it meets real life.
- Not pivoting a wrong strategy costs the company months or even years of useless work, followed by rework. That can put the whole future of the company at risk.
- Strategies are released with fanfare (documents, presentations, speeches, reorganized teams), so saying "just kidding, we were wrong about something important" feels costly. He says you have to take that risk, because executing the wrong strategy is far worse.
- His communication tactic: say it out loud to everyone. Explain that a new strategy is unlikely to be right the first time, that you're putting the company first, and that no one should be doing work you secretly know is the wrong work.

## Refactoring infrastructure after 10x growth means you didn't over-engineer

- If you're not refactoring your infrastructure after a tenfold increase in growth, you over-engineered the original.
- Jason scaled WP Engine to millions of websites serving tens of billions of requests daily. His lesson: you don't know what will break under scale until you're already scaling, because you must handle things you can't even measure yet.
- Over-engineering for massive scale up front means you aren't shipping your SLC (simple, lovable, complete product) fast enough. He calls it a form of premature optimization.

## Release early and expect a pile of bugs afterward

- If you're not fixing lots of bugs after a major release, you released too late.
- He says releasing garbage is a bad policy, but waiting until "everything's perfect" is also bad.
- Example: Windows 95 shipped with tens of thousands of known bugs (an entire book explained how to work around 1000 of them) and was still heralded as one of the most successful product releases of its time.
- Contact with customers shows which bugs matter most to fix next, and reveals bugs you would never have found on your own.

## Keep support and sales in-house longer than feels natural

- If you held onto support and sales rather than hiring a team, you learned a lot about your customers and about how to do support and sales.
- Scaling either too soon is a classic funded-startup mistake. Without a system (materials, knowledge bases, scripts), new hires don't know how to do the job, and distributed work makes this 10x harder.
- "The second you put someone else between you and a customer, your pace of learning and understanding falls off a cliff."
- His rule: wait until it's breaking for lack of scale, then scale. Once the company is truly at scale, this rule no longer applies, and he says you're mismanaging the company if you still follow it.

## Letting someone go soon after hiring is a good outcome

- If you kept someone on when you knew it was never going to work, you did a disservice to that person, the team, the company and yourself.
- Don't do it capriciously, but dragging it out has costs: good people leave because they don't want to be on that team, meetings, complaints and side-conversations multiply, morale drops, and competent people cover for the incompetent, lowering productivity.
- Face the truth and act quickly. If it happens a lot, fix your hiring process, but the rule still applies in the meantime.

## Ignore most competitor moves, and reject lucrative deals that don't fit

- **Competitors:** "If you're not ignoring most of your competitor's moves, then you're playing their game, not yours." Stay focused on your unique value proposition. His example: when a major competitor launched a similar service at a lower price, Dropbox stayed the course on user experience and brand loyalty rather than fighting a price war.
- Sometimes ignoring a move turns out to be wrong. He says the signal to react should come from customers: asking for features "because so-and-so has it," or cancelling and going to a specific competitor. React then, because you're seeking what's best for customers, not reacting to everything competitors do.
- **Deals:** "If you're not rejecting lucrative deals that don't align with your strategy, then you don't have a strategy." The same goes for relationships that conflict with core values.
- He admits money is tempting and is a main reason to build a company, and that it's often wise to say "yes" so long as there's enough money in it. But money can't outrank strategy or values, or you're saying you have neither.

## Messaging and removed features: the other "good errors"

- **Terse messaging:** If you're never adding words back because messaging got too terse to understand, your marketing is too verbose and fluffy and probably doesn't know what it's trying to say. He says it's "100x more likely" that messages aren't punchy or specific enough than that they're too brief. People don't read: tweets, Google Ads and email titles are short, and visitors bounce off home pages in three seconds. He notes ChatGPT prompts can get you 80% of the way to tighter copy.
- **Removed features:** If you're not adding back features you removed, you're not removing enough. Products that never remove features aren't weeding the garden, learning what customers really want, or admitting when they got it wrong. Occasionally a removed feature turns out to matter, which is a natural consequence of weeding.
- **Overall frame:** Not all errors signal progress (some are negligence or bad luck), but these are by-products of good decisions. Celebrate them, because they mean you have a strategy and the ability to make tough, wise decisions.
