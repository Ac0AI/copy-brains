## Why a "1% better per day" goal can't make Jason 37x more productive

Jason rejects the Zappos COO's claim that improving 1% a day makes you "37x better" in a year. The compounding arithmetic is fine. His objection is that nobody can keep raising daily output by a full percent every day. Things "rarely compound like that, even at slow rates," and other factors overwhelm the simplistic idea.

- His push-up example: if you can do 1 push-up and improve 1% a day, you do 37 a day at the end of year one and 1,369 a day at the end of year two. "Yeah right."
- He calls the 37x claim "poppycock" and impossible.
- He argues that "2x better is possible", but it is a one-time doubling, not a repeating compounding rate.

## The speed puzzle: low-velocity time wipes out high-velocity gains

Jason uses a drive-to-your-mother's-house puzzle to show why gains in your fast periods barely move your overall average.

- The setup: the house is 60 miles away. You hit traffic and average 30 mph over the first 30 miles, which takes an hour.
- The question: how fast must you drive the second half to average 60 mph for the whole trip? It is impossible. Averaging 60 mph requires covering all 60 miles in one hour, and that hour is already gone. Even at 1000 mph, the trip average stays under 60.
- Doubling your speed in the second half raises the trip average only from 30 to 40 mph.
- Quadrupling it raises the average only to 48 mph.
- His takeaway: "periods of low velocity wash away gains of high velocity."
- He says the same math applies to anything measured per hour: emails processed, code written, prose written, any "gettin' stuff done" per hour.

## Why productivity is stuck: half the day moves at 30 mph

Jason says most people's day is full of low-velocity activity, and that is what holds the average down.

- Examples he gives: dealing with emails you didn't need to see, dawdling in a meeting that hasn't started, reading blogs, managing the calendar.
- "When half your day moves at 30 mph, it's impossible to make up the time during the other half."
- Even a 37x improvement in some areas might not make you 2x more productive overall, because the slow stuff drags the average down.
- This is also why the 1% idea fails: low-velocity time makes it too hard to improve even 1% overall, every day, for a year.

## The fix: eliminate the low-velocity work

Jason's conclusion is to cut low-velocity activity instead of speeding up what you already do fast.

- "The thing to do is eliminate the low-velocity stuff."
- Learning to type faster is good, but cutting the time spent processing useless email may help more.
- To get roughly 2x, "identify the biggest perpetrators of low-velocity activity and eliminate them."
- He also says to make sure your "big rock" tasks are the right ones, linking his essay on rocks, pebbles and sand.
- None of this requires working later or harder: "Just stop averaging down!"

## Measuring where time goes with RescueTime

Jason uses RescueTime, a free tool, to find the low-velocity activity. He shares his own numbers from one week.

- Almost 8 hours of email in the week, which he describes as "a solid, uninterrupted, full day of nothing but email."
- About 45 minutes in "Calendars." A drill-down showed almost an hour in Google Calendar. The week was packed with events, but time in the calendar app means managing meetings, not attending them.
- An average of 5.5 hours of real activity per day, even though he was in the office over 8 hours every day. The rest went to meetings, office chatter and lunch.
- His read on the "mythical eight-hour workday": you are starting from 5-6 productive hours, and even then much of that goes to fielding email.

## What he changed after seeing the numbers

- He now notices sooner when an office conversation has gone past the point of being productive.
- He points to the many existing tips for processing email more efficiently (he links 43folders' inbox-zero material).
- His stated principle: once you see the numbers, the correction is easy.
