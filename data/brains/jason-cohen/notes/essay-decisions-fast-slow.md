## Default to fast, and slow down only when specific signals say so

- Jason's premise: startups should usually decide quickly, because fast decisions speed up action, which speeds the loop of production and feedback. That is how you "outpace and out-learn a competitor, even one that already has a lead."
- Some decisions deserve slow, contemplative, data-driven treatment: a key executive hire, pricing strategy, whether to raise money, whether to invest millions in a new product line, whether to enter a new market.
- He frames it as a set of questions rather than a rubric: "important decisions aren't a Cosmo Quiz." Don't score items 1-5 and add them in a spreadsheet. Answer honestly, and the quick, light-touch decision stays the default unless several signals point the other way.

## Signals that you'll decide better later, or can't take it back

- **You'll make a much smarter decision later.** "Today we know the least that we're ever going to know." Waiting doesn't always add information, but sometimes it does. Before launch you can't judge which features come next; launch and learn from customers. Don't even think hard about it now, and make no firm decision, when you know a better one is coming.
- **Can't undo.** This is the one-way versus two-way door distinction. If a decision can't easily be reversed, invest more effort in analyzing the upsides and risks.
- **Can't measure incremental success.** If you can objectively see whether things are going well after implementation starts, course-correcting (or reversing) is easy. If progress will be invisible or subjective, you could sink person-years before knowing, so build confidence in the path up front.

## Signals about effort, urgency and cost of deliberating

- **Huge effort.** Some things take less time to implement than to estimate or debate (his "Sand" category). Two engineers for a week might be cheaper than a team debating for a week. This is why small teams without process can out-produce larger teams with process. If implementing costs less than deciding, "just knock it out." If you're picking a path that takes six months to yield results, research up front is worth it.
- **No compelling event.** If the status quo isn't bad, there may be no reason to decide quickly, or at all, so more deliberation is justified. With time pressure, the opposite holds: every extra day deciding is a day less for implementation and surprises, so dragging it out adds risk.

## Signals about your team's experience and information

- **Not accustomed to this kind of decision.** Online marketing teams throw creative things at the wall daily, so they don't agonize over trying a campaign on a new social platform or which headlines to test. Most organizations have never made decisions like a pricing change or an acquisition, and most founders don't know how to hire a great executive. They also don't know when to shift from "just throwing something out there and iterating" (right at the start of a company) to a deliberate multimillion-dollar product bet. First-time decisions are riskier, so add research, data, debate or outside advice.
- **Don't know how to evaluate the options.** If strategy is clear and detailed, relevant data is at hand, goals are clear and the team is confident, the decision can be fast. If not, deliberate to clarify those things. Tools he points to: Fermi Estimation to simplify evaluation, and Binstack to find the best option among many.
- **Imperfect information.** Buying a house is nerve-racking because it's expensive, hard to undo, and you know little about the goods (what the seller isn't telling you, what you'll discover a year later). When more information is obtainable, buy it, such as reports or experts, as with a house. When objective data is impossible to get, the opposite applies: don't burn time debating subjective points. Decide from experience and gut, since that's all you have, and "experts are often wrong."

## Cross-team decisions and what to do with the answers

- **Multiple teams that haven't worked together.** At WP Engine, Jason notes, the company is extremely collaborative across support, sales, marketing, engineering, product and finance, which avoids solving one department's goals at another's expense. But it makes decisions harder, because good solutions need compromise and creativity, and that takes time. If the teams or members lack rapport, common language and shared experience, give the decision more time. You're really giving people time to build relationships and find good solutions, which is a long-term investment in organizational intelligence.
- **Use problem signals to guide next steps.**
  - If "Can't undo" is the problem, rethink the decision so it can be undone: apply it to one segment only, invest more in planning, create a disaster recovery plan, or split the decision so part of it is undoable.
  - If "No compelling event" is the problem, the best answer may be to "not decide." Some people will be disappointed, but it's better to honestly say "we can't figure out the answer right now" than to make a rash decision that does more harm than good, or to spend time on a decision that doesn't need making at the expense of work that does.
