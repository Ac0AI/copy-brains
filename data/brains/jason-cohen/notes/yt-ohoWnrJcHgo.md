## Why post-product-market-fit is a different game

- Jason (WP Engine founder, "the Smart Bear") says WP Engine hit $1M ARR in its first 18 months, then went from $1M to $5M in one year in 2012. It reached $100M in a little less than 7 years.
- He argues the company changes more than founders expect. Before product-market fit you explore: who is the customer, what is the unique thing, which channel. Experimentation and pivoting fast are right then.
- After fit, "just keep experimenting" is not how you scale something that works. The work becomes hiring and managing humans, defending customers and growth you can lose, and fixing the "kind of crappy product" you rightly built first.
- Rare problems become common. A bug hitting 1% of customers is zero or one people early on. At scale it means 30 support tickets and 30 negative tweets in a day, so you need processes to detect and handle rare things.
- Predictability becomes required. If hiring and ramping support takes 6-9 months, you must know how many people you need 6-9 months out.
- "What got you here won't get you there." He stresses the emotional cost: founders were proud of being scrappy experimenters who beat stupid big companies, and now have to change that identity. He thinks this is underestimated.

## Don't dilute scale across many initiatives

- Having 30 people versus a competitor's 3 does not make you 10x faster. Extra people mostly add extra things to do: a YouTube channel, micro projects, moving up-market to enterprise.
- Jason argues against that. Splitting everyone across initiatives means each one lacks the people and money to be good, so you waste the scale advantage.
- His example: two people on YouTube against a startup's two people on YouTube means you are no better. Put 10 people and heavy investment on it and you might dominate YouTube in your area.
- His rule: keep only a few things and point all of the company's scale at them.

## Quarterly process for picking the few priorities

Jason runs this at WP Engine with teams and groups of teams, once a quarter:

1. **Ideal moves.** "If we weren't encumbered by problems, challenges, constraints, how would we advance our strategy right now?" Examples: a feature customers keep asking for that competitors lack, opening a new sales channel. Brainstorm broadly and bring as many people into the room as possible, so they buy into the answer.
2. **Obstacles.** Ask what stops you. It could be churn of 5% per month (customers fleeing, which matters more than a new geography), or losing a key person. Pick the top couple of obstacles.
3. **Plan.** Either attack each obstacle or work around it. Example: not enough money can be attacked with debt or customer financing (annual prepay), or worked around by treating it as an "enabling constraint" and making progress without money.

- The output is a Google Doc of bullets, about one page. Linking to Jira epics is optional.
- The first run is messy (some use Miro to brainstorm and cluster). His corporate data team's first pass took about 3 weeks. A later one took 2 days because most of it matched the prior quarter.
- Why quarterly: about every 100 days this falls out of people's heads, like the social fabric of a distributed team weakening. More often than that is too much overhead.
- It also gives a ready answer when someone claims a top-priority request: "Here's our priorities. Is it really?"

## Reflect the focus in the org chart

- If the whole company works on a few things, projects cross teams, which causes meetings, turf fights (marketing wants the product redesigned to match the site, engineers want to refactor) and escalation to the CEO. He says this is inefficient and bad for morale.
- Two options:
  - **Matrixed.** One person runs the project across teams and sets priorities and metrics, while direct managers (and the CEO) don't direct that work. Better for careers, since a designer sits with designers and has a manager who makes them a better designer. The cost is org chart complexity.
  - **Initiative-based.** A place in the org chart is the initiative, and everyone under it is who you need to get it done. Simpler, but worse for the designer case.
- "There's no free lunch." Pick the one that fits your culture and whose downsides you can live with, but the org chart has to reflect the focus.

## Hiring managers is not hiring your best individual contributor

- Early on you have a "hero developer" doing 100 tickets a day. If they leave or have a baby, that work grinds to a halt. At scale anything important must be a team, so you need managers.
- Engineers (and founders new to this) assume a good engineering manager is the best coder. Jason says that's wrong. A manager must speak the language but their job is to build the team, handle conflicts, set priorities and work with other teams. Engineers rarely interview their manager on any of this.
- He calls building people a different skill from building products.

## How to hire executives who are better than you

- Jason's premise: at C-level every hire must be better than you or the company doesn't progress, and fast scaling outpaces anyone's growth. The puzzle is "how do I hire a pancreatic surgeon" when you aren't one.
- **Giddy test.** After the call you should be excited, with notes on things to go do whether or not you hire them. Their ideas may not be perfect, but your excitement signals culture, skill and process fit.
- **Up-level test.** Ask whether they will up-level peers, not just their own team. He says that is part of an executive's job description, not a manager's. Example: someone in support who is strong on metrics could help everyone.
- **Reference calls, reframed.** Asking "how are they?" gets "great" and tells you nothing. Instead ask: "What is the perfect scenario where this person is an absolute superstar?" Then ask the negative: construct a scenario where you'd crush their spirit. Some people say "tell me exactly what to do and I will crush it", others will leave if told what to do. Neither is wrong, they are different modes. The references don't know your company, so the answers are unbiased. Then map those modes to your culture and the specific slot.
- **Someone who has done it before.** He says there's a big difference between zero people who have done it and one. At WP Engine it was the CEO. He became CTO and hired Heather Brunner as CEO, "the second best decision I've ever made" (after marrying his wife, married 20+ years). He has seen it be a COO or CFO elsewhere, often forming a trinity with the founder CEO and the product or engineering co-founder.

## Deciding to hire a CEO as a founder

- Jason framed it with an ikigai- and Dan Pink-style test (mastery, autonomy, purpose): you are in a good place when you do work you love, are good at, and the company needs.
- At about 70-80 people and growing fast, the company needed a global sales team, more fundraising, a more serious board, and a plan to keep culture at scale. These were things he was either unskilled at, didn't like, or both. Lacking skill but liking it is a growth path, but not liking it means doing a poor job.
- That intellectual conclusion took about 3 months. What got him over the line emotionally was a founder-investor at a wine bar who said he was thinking he'd one day ring the NASDAQ bell and want the credit. Jason agreed it was ego. The reply: "You're the founder. You'll always get the credit."
- His title today is Chief Innovation Officer. He says he has an article on hiring a CEO at smartbear.com.

## Measuring your own growth as a leader

- Jason recommends a 360 review: anonymous feedback from reports and peers, processed by someone who knows what they're doing so you can't tell who said what.
- He is unsure about AI summarizing it. Summaries drop the detail, and "you need to listen more" only lands when you hear the specifics of what you did to someone.
- It also surfaced strengths. People consistently said he gets them excited about the company's vision, which he hadn't considered. So a 360 shows what to lean into as well as what to fix.
- He also notes he knows some of his weaknesses and tries to stay out of those positions or not stay in them long.
