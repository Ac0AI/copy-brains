## How Jason decides whether a new product or market is worth pursuing: the adjacency matrix

- Jason says good strategy leverages the assets you already have: strengths, existing customers, brand, culture, a unique advantage. Doing something you are weak at is usually a bad idea unless there is an overwhelming reason, because it takes heavy investment and carries high risk of failure.
- His first question is whether to expand at all. If the current market has real headroom, stay put. Going elsewhere too quickly is a common mistake.
- If the current business is at about 0.1% of its market, the issue is that it is not working yet, or there is plenty of growth right in front of you.
- If the current business is at about 2% penetration, that is actually a lot for a small company. Getting to 3 or 4% is plausible over years; getting to 20% is "nuts". That is a reason to look at adjacent products.
- Diversification is another valid reason, but not for a young product. Early on, when the first product is "still getting legs", it is not the time to diversify.
- He calls his tool the "adjacency matrix" and says the name sounds more complicated than it is.
  1. List the functional buckets of the company, such as marketing, sales, support, product, and design if relevant.
  2. For each candidate idea, rate how adjacent it is in each bucket using only three options. **Trivial**: almost no change, such as one email or one slide. **Adjustment**: manageable with current people and processes, about the size of a large feature release. **Overhaul**: you lack the people or expertise, need to hire for specialty or capacity, or face big retraining or a completely new market.
  3. There are no scores. He dislikes rubrics and numeric scoring ("this is not a Cosmo quiz").
- The output is an understanding of the impact and risk: which parts of the business get shaken up and which stay easy. It also narrows the field, because some ideas are clearly too far afield.
- Easiest is not necessarily best. An easy idea may also be low-value or uninteresting.
- Which buckets are overhauls matters. An engineering-led company may accept a product overhaul but treat a big sales overhaul as a deal breaker because sales is a weakness.
- A key hire can change what you can say yes to. His example: poaching a VP of Sales who has spent seven years selling podcast ads could make an ad business feasible in a way competitors can't follow.
- Applied to Craig's podcast-hosting company: going horizontal (CMS, blogging, email) or vertical (recording, editing, promotion) would each be analyzed bucket by bucket. A CMS would change every competitor, all marketing and all sales, which makes it an overhaul.

## Real WP Engine example: Enterprise was an overhaul in sales but trivial in engineering

- WP Engine deliberately expanded into new market segments. It invested "tens of millions of dollars" in sales, marketing, customer service and account management to create "hundreds of millions of dollars of revenue" in a new segment.
- Jason describes WP Engine's pricing position as depending on the segment. It is expensive relative to GoDaddy for low-end customers, but cheap relative to Adobe for Enterprise customers. He calls it "the cheap but Enterprise grade alternative for McDonald's" and "the expensive but worth it" option for a dentist's office.
- Enterprise scored as an overhaul in sales and marketing but trivial in engineering and product. Existing customers already had sites bigger than McDonald's' site, since a five-person media site can get far more traffic than IBM.
- Diversification paid off during COVID. Small-business customers canceled or got invoice suspensions, while Enterprise customers flocked to WP Engine because Adobe became too expensive.
- Enterprise means millions of dollars a year, so $100 a month is nowhere near Enterprise.

## Justifying premium pricing: the value the customer perceives

- Jason says to be premium you must be better at something customers will pay more for, and be specific about what. The test is whether the value the customer perceives is about 10 times the cost, even at the premium price.
- WP Engine examples he gives:
  - Better, more knowledgeable, friendlier tech support that spends more time.
  - A visibly faster WordPress site. You can see it when you log in, and Core Web Vitals scores rise.
  - Security with a bullet-point list that other security people agree is good.
- For a non-technical customer, doubling site speed means a lower bounce rate and better Google SEO. For media or e-commerce sites that earn per page view or traffic, that is worth a lot, so paying $100 instead of $50, or $30 instead of $5, is an easy decision if it changes the revenue curve.
- Value is not only features. Community can be the differentiator: Genesis developers have hundreds of thousands of peers on Facebook groups and Slack, which is not a line in the Genesis roadmap. Mission can too: Patagonia, or customers who pay ten times more for sustainable coffee beans.

## Brand versus the real thing behind it, and when to invest in each

- Jason says Patagonia's brand is not a logo, colors or name. It is the actual mission and how the company is run; for example, the company bails employees out of jail if they are arrested protesting. "That's not brand, that's company culture and policy and genuinely believing in the mission." The brand is just the handle for it.
- Traditional brand work (logo placement, typography consistency) comes later. Before product-market fit you don't know your target customer or right products, and nobody knows you yet, so obsessing over brand is a mistake. The exception is that founders often pour their identity into the name and font, which he understands.
- After product-market fit, customers flood in and you are fighting bugs, scalability, tech debt and support load. Once you are on top of that, brand consistency starts to matter because many people now know you and you are placing a specific feeling in their minds.
- Authentic elements like community and mission can't be bolted on later. The Genesis community started on day zero. A jacket company that later declares it cares about the environment would be inauthentic to customers and employees, and he says he can't name a company that built a genuine community only after years of operating.
- It should come from who the founder is. If you are an introvert who hates community, don't do it; there are dozens of other marketing tools. He notes that podcasting is a big, fast-growing market, and "there isn't just one way to grow."

## Joy, skill and need: choosing your role as a founder

- Jason uses a three-part test for whether he is in the right role: does he love the work (joy), is he good at it (skill), and does the company need it (need). He says he made the decision to stop being CEO about ten years ago using this, and has kept using it as he moved to CTO and then to Chief Innovation Officer. He notes it is one of several possible frameworks, mentioning Ikigai.
- Each missing piece has a specific trap:
  - **Missing joy**: you do work you are good at and the company needs, but come to resent it. He burned out at SmartBear this way without realizing it.
  - **Missing skill**: founders like the work and the company needs it, but they are bad at it, and as founder nobody tells them no. His example is messing with AdWords for three months with no experience, concluding AdWords sucks when the real conclusion is "you suck", and thinking you're an expert after wasting the time. Even once at some scale, "we don't have time for people to just be messing about."
  - **Missing need**: common with indie hackers. You love making things and are good at it, but the company needs customers who actually want the product, and only people on Twitter tell you otherwise.
- It is an ideal state, and nobody is in the middle of all three every day. The point is to notice when you are drifting too long or too often.
- His roles: CEO for four years, CTO for roughly the next eight, and recently Chief Innovation Officer. He has worked on corporate strategy, helped teams with their strategy, and in the last year worked on corporate data, including data teams and machine learning models. He says the CIO title is a bit silly but common among founders.

## Hiring someone better than you, and diagnosing flat growth

- When a founder says they can't afford someone better than themselves, Jason pushes back: if that person did the job well you could afford them, so the math doesn't add up.
- He suggests hiring someone who is ahead of you but earlier in their career, so they are cheaper. They have a higher slope and the company becomes a place for them to crush it. Look for someone better in both "point and slope."
- If a hire doesn't change the growth trajectory, they can't stay. If they do, you can afford them. This is true of every position.
- If several hires have not changed a flat growth curve, the hires may not have been effective.
- When interviewing a growth hire, ask why predecessors didn't change the growth rate, what it is about this market or the work, and how specifically they know it will be different this time. If they can't answer, that is bad; if they can, you still evaluate the answer.
- Look at competitors: if they all grow at the same rate, maybe there is nothing to figure out. If they grew a lot, check what they are doing (partners versus advertising) without assuming that copying works.
- If getting new customers is just too hard, a valid strategy is to grow from existing customers: raise ARPU through segmentation, different kinds of value, underpricing, new products, or acquiring something and feeding it into the customer base. He calls that "a great strategic statement" and says to ask where growth can come from if not acquisition.

## Making big, scary decisions and the limits of "two-way doors"

- Jason says big decisions are hard because they cost time, attention, energy, money and expectations from employees and customers, and fear of them is reasonable.
- "Two-way door" thinking is overused. A Google ad you took down is a two-way door, but many big bets technically reversible are painful to reverse, and the team wonders whether leadership knows what it is doing.
- Experimenting at scale is different in kind from small tests. Running a pricing test for a month differs from a bet that could lead to laying off 30 people. "This is people's lives and we all know it."
- He recommends setting culture expectations ahead of time: a company that never tries big bold things is going to die; some attempts will fail; we will call it out after three or six months instead of three years; people should support each other when it fails and celebrate when it works. If you want a company that never admits mistakes, work elsewhere.
- Frameworks like the adjacency matrix don't give the answer but give confidence that you have thought it through, with five decent options and the risks of each known. You will never know what would have happened with another choice.

## Thinking about AI at WP Engine: solution space, not problem space

- WP Engine uses AI in three places: internally in operations such as support and sales, with an internal tool not yet shown to customers; in developer tooling such as Copilot; and in products.
- He argues that building a standalone AI product doesn't make sense, because there will be a billion AI products and a team or two can't overtake 100 other startups building the same thing.
- The first product step is to add AI to products WP Engine already has, which fits the adjacency idea. Example: AI-powered e-commerce search where a shopper can type "a shoe that's good for people with plantar fasciitis" and get the right results. He says people who use the search bar are three times more likely to convert, so improving that experience is where to invest.
- On uncertainty: "there's zero people who know what AI looks like in five years." In that situation you don't make five-year plans; you play, explore, act and see what happens. He cites the Cynefin chaos domain, where the correct response is to act first. Play now so you are present as the future unfolds.
- Customers don't wake up wanting more AI. They want what they always wanted, such as media companies wanting more visitors, longer visits, more newsletter sign-ups and more ad revenue. AI "should always be thought of as the potential solution or potential execution" that delivers the thing the customer always wanted in a new way. "It's in the solution space and not the problem space."
