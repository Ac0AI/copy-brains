## The core claim: Startup Weekend is a startup contest, not a code or design contest

Jason told Startup Weekend teams in Austin that the code does not win. He had judged the previous event, and tech and design had nothing to do with the decisions. Judges don't ask "which had the best algorithm" or "which had the cleverest logo."

- Everyone in the room can code an app and make a clean home page, so that output cannot separate teams.
- He extends this to real life: customers don't pick companies for code difficulty, unit test coverage, or which typeface is on the home page.
- He says he has made "millions of dollars on companies with hideously ugly websites and buggy code."
- Builders have a natural weakness: they do the work they like and are good at, then end up with "a curiosity, a hobby, some trivial code and a typical website." That makes a website, not a company.

## Sharpen the pitch until it is brutally short

Jason's first non-coding priority is a pitch so clear that a customer sees you are relevant to their pain and might solve it. Other intelligent people, such as judges or investors, should see the same.

- The verbal pitch should be under 60 seconds.
- The home page should entice in three words and explain in under fifty.
- The value is in the choices: what you're building, why, who cares, and what they'll pay. A short pitch only forces those choices.

## Get five strangers to react before you build more

Jason's second priority is feedback from 5 strangers, even if only as interviews and without a prototype.

- Validate the pain, the minimum needed to address it, and what they would pay.
- Even if they disagree, or you change your story each time, you learn what is critical and what is unresolved.
- His ranking: "A concept validated by five people is far more valuable than a working application that no one has critiqued."

## Build a plausible business model in a simple spreadsheet

Jason says to build a business model, not a business plan: a simple spreadsheet.

- Include the pricing model, how money flows in and out in normal operation, and customer acquisition cost.
- From that, work out how many customers you need to break even on operating costs, and how many more to cover human costs.
- He notes it is surprisingly hard to build a company where revenue beats marketing, salaries, and operations, even at scale. Showing yours can work puts you ahead of the group.

## Find evidence that people are already looking for a solution

Jason says too many companies build cool tools that nobody in particular wants. Counter this by producing evidence of potential customers already searching.

- Examples of evidence: people complaining on Twitter, asking on forums, musing on blogs, or paying too much for poor alternatives.
- This shows need and also hints at how to reach those people.

## Need beats product: a landing page with 50 emails beats a working app nobody asked for

Jason argues that identifying a need is the scarce skill, because a team that finds a need can build the solution, but a website cannot create interested people.

- A landing page that describes what the company will do and asks for an email, with 50 emails collected, is "far more of a real company" than a working product no one requested.
- Teams that make real progress on even one or two of the points above are the likely winners and the likeliest to have a viable small company.

## Practice what you're not already good at

Jason's closing advice is to push out of the comfort zone. You can write code or make logos any time, so use the weekend to get better at the other skills every startup needs.

- Do some coding and design, but force yourself into pitch, validation, and business-model work.
- His line: "You can go back to building things for the sake of building things on Monday. This weekend, build a company."
