## The Important Thing: one overwhelming strength overrides every deficiency

- Jason argues startups win despite crushing disadvantages: "No brand, no features, no customers, no money, no distribution, no search engine rankings, no efficient advertising, no incredible executive team, no NPS, no strategy."
- They do not win by solving all those problems, or solving them quickly. He gives two answers:
  - Pick battles where the startup has advantages over incumbents.
  - Get one thing to go really, really right, enough to overcome the torrent of things going wrong.
- He calls this universal pattern **The Important Thing**: break-out products deliver something so fantastic, game-changing and important to the customer that it overrides the deficiencies of the rest of the product and company.

## What The Important Thing buys a startup

When the one thing is compelling enough, it neutralizes the usual startup weaknesses:
- **No marketing:** people tell their friends or force colleagues to use it.
- **No operational excellence:** they use it even if support is slow and releases have bugs.
- **No brand:** they are excited to support a promising new company instead of worried about depending on a wobbly one.
- Net effect: despite lacking brand, service, features, stability, integrations and social proof, the startup can still beat the incumbent.

## Case study: the first iPhone

- The first iPhone launched with a mountain of issues. As a phone it was worse than a cheap Nokia flip-phone, which everyone agreed was "ten times better at being, you know, a *phone*."
- It had no copy/paste in its operating system.
- Its Important Thing: you could use the real Internet, with full websites. The web worked (even if slowly), and email worked, in your pocket.
- That was compelling enough that all the other problems didn't matter.

## Case study: Heroku

- Early Heroku was full of deal-breakers developers complained about, and the complaints were valid:
  - Forced use of Bundler, which was broken half the time.
  - No changing the filesystem at run-time, forcing algorithm changes.
  - No MySQL support, so queries could break.
  - Slow websites.
- Its Important Thing: type `git push production` and the site goes live. A knob on a web page sets how scalable the site is (also connected to your wallet). You never see a server, think about backups, or worry about stashing API keys, and you always get a staging area to test in a real server environment.
- DevOps became a thing of the past for a large class of applications.
- The evidence of its power: developers changed their workflows and code around Heroku and "12-Factor" apps; Heroku did not change to suit developers.
- It has been the dominant way Ruby on Rails developers launch public applications for more than ten years.

## The Important Thing isn't always a feature

- Jason's examples of non-feature Important Things:
  - **Reputation and values:** Fogbugz was never the leading bug tracker, but he used it for most of the 2000s because he was a fan of Joel Spolsky's blog. Using a product from a company whose values and behavior he respected and learned from felt good. The same happened with Basecamp and the 37signals blog.
  - A **lifestyle** the product enables, such as remote-work or with-kids-work.
  - **Solving a problem in an industry others overlook:** "any solution, even with problems" beats no solution.
  - **Culture that resonates with an audience,** for example an informal voice in an otherwise formal market.
  - **A cause or higher purpose,** so customers are supporting a movement rather than just buying a shirt or software. His example is Patagonia: a company policy to bail employees out of jail if arrested for peaceful protest, and only 4% annual employee turnover.
- Feature example: disappearing messages with Snapchat.
- On Basecamp, he rejects the idea that 37signals' work-style explains success. Their many later products used the same work-style, people and even code base, yet were dramatically less successful and have all been discontinued. The company was renamed "Basecamp" to emphasize that only the first experiment worked. (Editor's note, 2024: they now seem successful with hey.com.)

## How to apply it to your own business

- It is easy to be overwhelmed by your many inadequacies. Attacking all of them at once, "with no weapons," leads to burn-out and a company that is excellent on no front.
- "You don't need to solve all those problems. You need to solve almost none of them."
- Focus on the one thing (maybe two) that is your Important Thing: the thing where, if you are extraordinarily good at it, customers overlook everything else.
- Pick something that meets three tests:
  - You *want* to obsess over it for the next five to ten years.
  - It gets your customers excited.
  - You have at least a possibility of executing it.
- Then do that, maybe only that. Let the other fires burn.
- If you become excellent at it, the other disadvantages still exist but are not fatal. In his words, that is all you can hope for at the beginning, "And all you need."
