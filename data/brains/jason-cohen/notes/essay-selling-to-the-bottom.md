## Bottom-up selling: solve the pain for the people in the trenches

- Jason sold Code Collaborator, peer code review software, to Fortune 500 companies from the bottom up, more than a decade before "Product-Led Growth" became a term. It was one-time-license software, not recurring revenue.
- The pitch is that the tool "removes pain." Developers carry the burden of review: gathering files and diffs, packaging and sending them, tracking comments across email and continents, chasing fixes, and timing reviews with stop-watches and line-counters for metrics.
- His sales model: "Genuinely solve a genuine problem for the folks in the trenches, then they beg their bosses to buy the tool to alleviate pain."
- Managers like peer code review because it finds bugs, especially documentation, maintainability and mentoring issues that no automated tool or unit test catches. They also like the reports that "prove" productivity.
- He says removing pain is "really the only reason anyone buys our tool."

## Why a metrics-and-reports-only tool fails

- If the product only generated metrics and reports, managers would like it but developers would "(rightly!) revolt and circumvent the system."
- Circumvention makes the reports meaningless anyway, so the manager's value depends on the user's value.
- He distrusts top-down mandates ("Hey we just bought a new tool! Time to do code reviews!") because they land on users as an imposition.

## Selling top-down to a CTO without changing the pitch

- When he did pitch a public company's CTO (not his usual route), he used the same message he gives developers: "This will save your developers time and increase happiness."
- He contrasts this with tools sold only top-down, built on ROI charts promising something like "$142,245 saved in the first 9 months," metrics reports without interpretation, and a $75k entry price.
- He doesn't think an ROI formula is the point: "If you don't believe that proof-reading code will uncover defects, an ROI formula isn't going to convince you otherwise."
- If the buyer already believes review finds defects, the cost of the tool is trivial next to an undiscovered defect, especially one shipped to a customer.

## Principle: tell the same truth to every level of the org

- "The sales books say otherwise, but I think you have to stick to the truth and stick by your mates in the trenches."
- His test: "If we're not truly making software better and people's lives better, screw it!"
- He argues executives "aren't as insensitive as the sales books would have you believe," so an honest, user-centered pitch can work on the brass too.
- In a 2024 editor's note he observes that this bottom-up approach predated Product-Led Growth, "So, maybe it always was!"
