## Time is zero-sum, so only a few tasks deserve your best hours

Jason Cohen argues that time is a hard, zero-sum limit: an hour spent on one thing is an hour not spent on everything else, and every minute is a choice. He says there "isn't enough time for more than two big things" in your life. Because we can only do one thing at a time, whatever we're doing now is definitionally our "highest priority", so the real choice is whether it's thoughtful.

- Every company proves the point with its issue tracker: thousands of valid items accumulate (ideas, differentiating features, customer-visible bugs, design tweaks).
- Completing even 10% would be a miracle, and the ratio worsens every week as more items are added than removed.
- Listening better to customers, more creative teams and more hires all make the ratio worse, not better.
- He puts the fixed constraint at 1%-10% of what you "need" to execute, and says the fraction shrinks with scale.
- "Urgent, but not important" tends to win over "Important, but not urgent" if you aren't paying attention. Teams end up doing only the things that happen to have deadlines.

## The 10x / 0.1x prioritization framework

Jason offers one overarching framework for any prioritization: sort work into 10x tasks and 0.1x tasks.

- A 10x task returns an order of magnitude more than you put in, even if you valued your time at about $1000/hour.
- Most other activities are worth far less than $1000/hour, even if mandatory. These are 0.1x tasks, to be minimized.
- If you don't know what your one or two 10x tasks are, "identifying that is your highest priority". Otherwise "the entire company is misusing their time."
- He recommends acting as if there were no 1x tasks, to force clearer decisions: "we must be ruthless, final, and precious with our time."
- He admits the binary is reductive. You will mislabel some 1x work as 10x and spend too much time on 0.1x work, but the forced choice helps you be ruthless.
- If you insist on a middle tier, he points to his Rocks, Pebbles, Sand framework, which segments work by size and prioritizes each size differently. He also credits Shreyas Doshi's LNO framework as another take on the same exponents.

## What counts as a 10x task

Jason's examples of 10x work:

- The few features that win most sales. Rule of thumb: impactful features are actively used by at least 40% of customers, or are a critical reason-to-buy or reason-to-stay for at least 15%.
- Finding the marketing positioning and wording where ads convert 2x higher and home-page visitors buy 2x more often. He says "Not 10% higher", because you can't actually measure that.
- Finding the pricing model that maximizes profitable growth while staying fair to customers.
- Correctly identifying the next strategic objective or biggest obstacle. Working on the wrong thing is "a 100% waste of time, even if efficiently- and perfectly-executed."
- Hiring the next critical employee who raises throughput, work quality and decision quality, and adds a missing skill needed for the current obstacle or next objective.
- Applying energy where small changes have a large impact on growth or profitability.
- Addressing the single biggest drag on growth.
- Deciding how to expand into the next adjacency.

## How to test whether something is really 10x

Jason says it's rare to have too many 10x candidates, and if you think you do, you're probably being too generous with the label.

- It must literally 10x a key metric, or be the difference between survival and bankruptcy.
- The upside must survive the usual misses. Work always takes about 2x longer and delivers about half the impact you expected. "To take that 4x hit and still have a great outcome requires starting at 10x."
- Treat 10x work as an investment: significant time, outsized expected return, real uncertainty. He has a separate Investment Criteria guide for this.
- If there truly are too many 10x choices with incomparable inputs, he points to his Binstack framework, built for maximizing impact when dimensions can't be compared.
- Easy is not the same as low-impact. Conflating effort with impact is a fallacy: easy things can be 10x, and complex things can have no impact on revenue or employee happiness.

## Minimizing 0.1x tasks

Jason lists four strategies for tasks that are worth far less than $1000/hour:

- **Eliminate them** by structuring your life, product, target-customer selection or company strategy to avoid them.
- **Delegate**, even if the result is worse than you'd do yourself. His example: grocery delivery is more expensive and doesn't pick the apples you would have picked. You need to be "in command instead of in control."
- **Batch or automate**, accepting minor penalties. Examples: paying bills once a month and risking occasional late fees, or batching security patches since each one is unlikely to be exploited in the next few weeks.
- **Archive loose tickets older than 100 days.** More than 90% of ideas never get done, so these are already in that group, and deleting them helps you prioritize the rest. If something is truly important it will come up again. He says "archive", not "delete".

## "Life by a thousand sparks": when many small tasks add up to 10x

Jason makes one exception to dismissing small tasks. In some regimes many small tasks sum to a 10x impact, which he calls "life by a thousand sparks" (a facetious inverse of "death by a thousand cuts").

- His example is great design. Amazing design isn't required for success, but some products win primarily because of beloved design.
- Great design is not one thing. It combines macro-scale architecture with many details: color choices, pixel-perfect layout, font and word selection, aspect ratios, white space, snappy interactivity, a design system for consistency, fixing every aesthetic bug, and harmony across website, product, emails and marketing, sales and support material.
- These tasks shouldn't be dismissed as individually 0.1x if they serve a 10x concept that is also the main way you win customers in a competitive market.

## The dog peeing on the floor: handling the fallout of ruthless prioritization

Jason tells a parable he has retold many times at WP Engine. A dog pees on the floor of a lit living room while a man sits reading a book. An observer at the window thinks he's crazy. In fact he has the most important meeting of his career in an hour, and everything he needs is in the book. Cleaning up the pee is worth it.

The observer misjudges for two reasons:

- They don't know the complete story.
- They struggle to accept that something bad or stupid can still be the right decision because the alternative is worse.

The same thing happens inside a company that prioritizes ruthlessly:

- Important fires are intentionally left burning because the Most Important Thing matters more.
- Others see the fires and peeing dogs for months or years, lose faith in leadership, and lose morale. Loss of trust and respect leads to talent leaving, "which leads to the death of an organization."
- Prolific idea-creators complain "They never listen to my ideas." Jason says they're right in effect: you could mathematically never implement more than a few percent of those ideas. It's "inexorable math", not a failure to listen.

## Communicating priorities so the team keeps trust

Jason's fix for the dogs-peeing problem is to tell the whole story, repeatedly.

- Share not just the few top priorities but why they are the top ones.
- Acknowledge the twenty other things that also deserve attention and explain why you're letting those fires burn on purpose.
- Keep a "not doing" list as an explicit part of the plan.
- Retell the dog parable and admit it feels bad to watch the dogs pee, especially when you know how to prevent it.
- The pee is tolerable only when people see it buys time for the most important things, which leverage "precious, woefully limited time, for out-sized results."
- He says this takes constant, repetitive communication, and that the full picture is what prioritization actually looks like, not social-media admonitions to "focus!"

## Jason's dislikes in prioritization frameworks

Jason lists what he avoids when choosing or building a prioritization method:

- Rubrics or computed scores (he has a separate piece on why, "Fermi ROI").
- Any "confidence level", because "your confidence scores are bullshit".
- Models built for symmetry on consultants' slides rather than reflecting the messiness of real work.
- Anything that compares incomparable things, such as scoring "more growth" on the same made-up number as "don't run out of money" or "make employees happy."
- He adds that our ability to measure impact even after the fact is "laughably poor", and our ability to predict it is "even more pitiful."

His own frameworks, each for a specific job: Fermi ROI, Binstack (incomparable dimensions), Adjacency Matrix (expanding an existing product), Investment Criteria, Rocks/Pebbles/Sand, Satisficing vs Maximizing, Cleaving (separate upside from downside), Fairytale Quarterly Planning (work against strategic objectives and obstacles), and JIT streams (separate prioritization from work-planning).
