## Define the target market by use-case and shared traits, not demographics

- Jason dislikes target-market definitions built on demographics and firmographics ("small businesses in the UK", "females 19-29"). For most companies these dimensions describe the market incorrectly.
- Demographic definitions both over-target and under-target. Most people in the named demographic never buy, and many outside it do. The real target is whatever the buyers share that non-buyers lack.
- Ad copy shows the real target. An ad for a TV show won't say "if you're a female 19-29, watch this." It appeals to the genre, the actors, the aesthetic, the writing style, or the kind of person who watches it. That appeal is the target market.
- Demographics are legitimate only when the product is inextricably tied to them. His example is back-office software for dentist offices under Norwegian law.
- An email client's target market has nothing to do with B2B vs B2C, company size, or geography. It depends on use-cases: people with hundreds of emails a day who need processing workflows, people whose assistants handle mail, people who get few emails, security-conscious people, outbound campaigners, or shared support desks.

## Why Google and other large companies buy "small business" software

- At WP Engine, early on, the website looked like "a small company selling to SMBs." Mid-sized and large companies bought anyway, a little in the first two years and substantially in the next two.
- They bought the same product the small customers bought, not Enterprise-sized multi-million-dollar contracts.
- Many founders see Google, Facebook, Salesforce or Oracle become customers and ask whether to "go up-market" with a sales team. Jason's short answer: "No."
- Individual teams inside large companies buy things too. Not everything at Google is purchased top-down and forced on its roughly 27,000 engineers.
- Those teams buy for the same reason a small business's only team buys. That reason defines the target market, and in turn the advertising, positioning, pricing and features.
- The exception: if your software really is purchased top-down and forced on users, you are selling to the enterprise, and that belongs in the target-market definition.

## WP Engine's real target market: a worked example

- Enterprise use-cases of WordPress hosting at WP Engine:
  - One of the world's largest banks let branches publish their own content.
  - One of the largest software companies ran its developer-relations portal.
  - One of the largest software development tools companies hosted its public technical documentation.
  - One of the largest fast-food chains published social content in small countries, where local agencies could get culture, language and color right.
- The target market was not "small businesses and individuals with WordPress." It was WordPress users who valued:
  - Enterprise-grade quality: speed, scale, security, 24/7 high-quality tech support.
  - A flexible, open-source, inexpensive-to-build platform where employees or agencies were easy to hire anywhere in the world.
  - Pricing 10x or 100x above "cheap hosting" but 1/10 or 1/100 of "enterprise CMS solutions".
- To go deeper, keep asking "why do they value those things?"
  - They valued enterprise-grade quality because the website mattered, unlike throw-away or fun sites where price is the issue.
  - They valued flexibility because they wanted to change things themselves or outsource anywhere.
  - Some chose WordPress because marketers already knew it from personal projects.

## Turning the definition into positioning

- A segment of "mid-sized companies" would produce a home page like "WordPress for mid-sized companies": soulless, boring, and unclear about what "mid-sized" means.
- Naming what the customers value gives something like "Enterprise-Grade WordPress" or "Affordable Enterprise-Grade WordPress."
- He says it could be made more authentic and interesting still, but it is already an improvement because it is specific about strengths (affordable, reliable, high-quality).
- The chain of whys gives internal clarity and a path to effective marketing.

## How to find the actual target market

- Customer interviews are part of this: find customers and interview them to learn how they view the problem, how they describe it, their constraints, and what would compel them to buy.
- Use that information to build an ideal customer profile and target everything at only them.
- Don't add company size, industry or geography unless it is tied to the nature of the product so that it truly can't be sold or used outside that boundary.
- Jason also points to his Needs Stack framework (where you sit in customers' hierarchy of needs) and his Love and Utility dimensions. The intersection of your personal motivation and what delights customers is the target market.
- Shortlist your best customers. "Best" means high retention, propensity to upgrade, a "10" NPS, proactive positive reviews and social advocacy, and engagement with beta-testing or feature ideation.
- Their shared characteristics define the target market. Ask which types of organizations and people would care about their testimonials.
- The customers may not know why they fit; it's your job to work out the defining characteristics through observation and interrogation.
- Then use those characteristics in marketing, sales identification and product optimization.

## The testimonial test

- To check whether customer profile B belongs to the same target market as existing profile A, ask: would B be swayed by a genuine, effusive testimonial from A? The reaction he wants is "that person's situation is just like mine, so I'm interested, I'll give it a try."
- It can be tested directly: see whether testimonials in sales calls light up people's eyes, or whether adding them to landing pages increases conversions.
- It echoes Geoffrey Moore's definition in *Crossing the Chasm* (1991): a market is a set of actual or potential customers, for a given set of products or services, who have a common set of needs or wants, and who reference each other when making a buying decision.
- Moore's definition mentions no demographics or firmographics. Jason prefers a slightly looser version than Moore's, since Moore says two buyers who don't communicate are in different markets (a Boston doctor and one in Zaire). Jason thinks that is less true in today's connected world, especially in software.
- His summary: your target market is the set of potential customers your best customers self-identify with, in the sense that they would find each other's testimonials compelling.

## Better segmentation dimensions for the modern age

- Old constraints that forced demographic segmentation are gone:
  - TV could only know gender and age, via a few firms like Nielsen.
  - Stripe, PayPal, Paddle and FastSpring made multi-country, multi-currency payments and tax compliance accessible, breaking geography and currency constraints.
  - AI support and translation may soon erode language barriers.
- Dimensions he suggests considering, though most won't apply and you should invent new ones:
  - Individual vs team (e.g. a free single-player mode that hooks people while teams pay, as with Asana or Notion).
  - Integrations and other software used daily.
  - Processes and workflows they use or aspire to.
  - Job-to-be-done success: which numbers they are held to, vital vs operational, satisfied vs maximized, what change would get them promoted or fired.
  - Personal success (their Needs Stack) and professional success (how they are evaluated and promoted; early-career priorities differ from late-career regardless of company size).
  - Corporate goals: revenue growth, revenue size, profit dollars or percentage, market share, Rule of 40, cost savings.
  - Role inside the company, title, and problem ownership (who owns the problem, and whether problem-owner and solution-owner are the same person).
  - Cultural attributes, such as productivity vs work-life balance, or external innovation vs internal efficiency.
  - *Crossing the Chasm* lifecycle phase: innovators (tinkerers), early adopters (take risks for competitive advantage), early majority (wary, want well-known vendors and support), late majority (dragged in by investors or market pressure).
  - Work style, technical acumen, risk tolerance (uncorrelated with company size), tech-stack philosophy, update cadence, communication culture.
  - Organization size (SMB is often bought by larger firms but not vice versa; it matters most for top-down sales or products like HR software), growth trajectory, regulatory environment.
  - Budget type, including whether a clear budget threshold changes the sale.
  - Sales process (top-down vs bottom-up PLG), decision cycle (hours, weeks, quarters), emergency vs deliberate buying, maker vs administrator, business model, learning style.
- Most answers will be "don't know" or "don't care," and that is expected.
