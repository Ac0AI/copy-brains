## Sunk costs are gone whatever you do next, so ignore them

Jason defines a sunk cost as "money you've already spent and cannot get back." He says a rational actor ignores it completely, because the money is gone whichever action comes next. He says many of his own mistakes trace back to failing to recognize sunk cost.

- **Clear case:** a banner ad cost $1000 to design and $2000 to run for three months. Results were horrible: barely any clicks and no purchases. Nobody spends more on that ad. Whether $30 or $30,000 had been spent, continuing is still not worth it.
- **Harder case:** a company burns $20m on a new plant and needs another $10m to finish. A $2m retrofit of a different plant appears. The $20m is unrecoverable, so the real choice is $2m versus $10m, and they should abandon the original project.
- **Why it usually goes wrong:** the person who owns the project would have to stand up and say "I'm responsible for this hugely wasteful endeavor, and now I want you to trust my judgment as I pull the plug and do something completely different."
- He rejects the idea that this is a big-company problem: startups are not too smart or agile to make the same mistakes ("that's crap").

## Why people hold on: loss aversion and the endowment effect

- Jason says "it's not just politics, it's human nature." The related terms are Loss Aversion and the Endowment Effect, which are subtly different but close enough for his purposes.
- Core idea: "We place excessive value on that which we own, or which we perceive we own."
- **Duke tickets:** students who lost the lottery for scarce game tickets would pay an average of $170 for one. Students who won would sell for an average of $2,400. Both had made the same effort, but owners valued the randomly assigned ticket far higher.
- **Horse bettors:** people who have already placed bets are more optimistic about winning than people still in line.
- **Movies:** most people won't walk out of a movie they hate because that "wastes money," even though the money can't be recovered and they could be doing something more enjoyable.
- **Touching items:** just touching an item in a store makes you more likely to buy it.
- It is so deeply ingrained that gut reactions don't change even when you know about it. That is why he says you must be especially vigilant.

## Killing pet features and recycled ideas

- **Pet feature:** you built a hard-to-implement feature and you're proud of it. Customers don't care, and it causes confusion and bugs. Even if customer XYZ says it's neat, the effort is sunk whether or not the feature fits. He says to kill the feature.
- **Clever marketing slogan:** it came out of hours of brainstorming and arguing, but it doesn't quite fit the business. Don't try to "waste not" it by forcing it in somewhere. As with all writing, "you have to learn how to throw things out."

## Firing the hire who isn't working out

- The scenario: the newest hire passed the interview and tests, but isn't a culture fit, isn't learning fast enough, and others are resentfully covering the slack.
- The trap is thinking "it was so much work finding them and we've put all this effort into training them; maybe they'll change?" Jason says they won't, and deep down you know it.
- The time is sunk. He calls this one of the most expensive mistakes you can make, but says prolonging the inevitable is worse.
- It is also bad for the employee, who deserves a chance to find a job where they can succeed.

## Endless marketing iteration and A/B testing

- A marketing effort doesn't work, so you A/B test a pair. The second is a little better but not much. You iterate again, and again.
- His question: when do you stop and realize that incremental iteration isn't always enough, and that repeated experimentation can produce false positives?
- The sunk-cost pull is continuing to tweak something because you've already invested in it, instead of trying something new.
- He links to his related pieces on creativity over optimization and p-hacking.

## Walking away from the big deal that never closes

- Example: a 500-seat sale to a big-name company, with a 9-month trial. They keep finding reasons not to buy: "deal-breaker" features, "critical" bugs, budget allocations that never materialize.
- The pull to continue: the sale would mean so much, you've spent hundreds of hours with them, and you've added special features, so surely they'll buy eventually.
- In his experience they often won't, or they buy just 10 seats so you can't claim they're "still trialing."
- His read: this is a sign of poor fit, and the time-suck won't vanish after they buy. "Let it go."
- His rule: you need profitable customers and advocates, not merely customers.

## Sunk costs in personal choices

- Example: you studied Biology as a teenager, before you knew what makes you happy or where your talents lie. Friends and family expect a lab career. Should you throw away the work and expectations to become a caterer?
- Jason's point is that attachment to sunk costs is natural. It hurts to admit wasted time, money, energy and reputation.
- His conclusion: "it's even worse to irrationally prolong the waste."
