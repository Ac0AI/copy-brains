## Mission, vision and purpose: Jason's view on what is useful

- Jason is skeptical of "mission" and "vision" as terms. Pundits can't agree on definitions, and he notes two nearly opposite meanings of "mission":
  - A higher purpose bigger than the company (Patagonia: "to save our home planet"; Tesla: "to accelerate the world's transition to sustainable energy").
  - A current execution goal (an army battalion's mission; McDonald's "to be our customers' favorite place and way to eat and drink"; FedEx's "superior financial returns for its shareowners").
- He says that in early-stage startups these are mostly irrelevant, because founders are busy making things and discovering customers wanted something else. In large companies they become marketing-written fragments on "About Us" pages that "no one reads and no one believes".
- He still sees real value: Khan Academy ("a free, world-class education for anyone, anywhere"), TOMS, DuckDuckGo and Patagonia (the founder put the company in a trust) take their words seriously.
- Because the terms are untethered, he prefers three plainer words: Purpose, N-year Vision, Next Milestone.

## Why a higher purpose can help

Jason lists the benefits Simon Sinek (_Start with Why_) and other researchers claim, in his own words:
- Loyal, vocal customers who tolerate high prices and missing features because the company is part of their identity.
- Passionate employees with lower turnover.
- Differentiation against profit-only companies that consumers dislike.
- Resilience in downturns and bad publicity.
- Consistency in messaging, prioritization and goals.
- Easier fundraising from angels who expect you'll probably fail but want to make a difference.
- Higher profits (he flags weak evidence: a study found "purpose + execution" beat the market but "purpose alone" did not; "purpose-washing" may hurt, though hindsight bias is possible).
- Legacy that outlasts any person or product.

He adds that you don't have to be mission-driven to succeed. Most successful companies aren't, and you can simply "make a dollar and get out of here".

## Purpose: the transformation bigger than the company

- Definition: "The transformation in the world that is bigger than ourselves, that we hope everyone involved is excited to help bring about."
- Examples: Khan's "Everyone deserves a free, world-class education"; Tesla's "Accelerate the world's transition to sustainable energy."
- It is never fully achieved, so it is not an operational goal. It answers "Why are we doing this at all? Why should anyone else care?"
- It is about others, not about us or what we do, and it matters even if the company didn't exist.
- Others adopting the same purpose is an ally, not competition. Jason's examples:
  - Khan Academy gives away its AI teaching assistant to other educators.
  - TOMS shares sustainable-materials R&D with other shoe makers.
  - Tesla open-sourced its core battery patents.
  - Netlify coined "jamstack"; rivals adopted it and eclipsed Netlify in revenue.

## N-year Vision: a specific future state

- Definition: "Our desired future state: what we will have achieved?" It must be different from today but not so far out that it isn't actionable.
- Rule of thumb for N: divide the company's age by 3 and round up. Smaller for new companies, larger for established ones.
- It must cover three things, in one or two sentences:
  - What we built (the product or service).
  - For whom (a subset of customers, can be larger than the ICP, never "everyone").
  - The insight (what makes this company "this company", in the product or how it gets there).
- His reductive examples:
  - Tesla: "Create the first high-performance luxury sports car for Americans that is powered only by batteries."
  - Patagonia: "Build the most durable, low-environmental-impact equipment for serious rock-climbers."
  - Amazon: "Create the largest and most affordable bookstore on Earth, by selling exclusively online with just-in-time inventory."
- Original audiences: Tesla started with rich Americans, Patagonia with serious American rock-climbers, Amazon with only 2,000 titles. Amazon's insight was operational: no physical stores and almost no inventory ("Your margin is my opportunity").
- Because it is over-simplified, it is the starting point for a fuller strategy document. The short version aids comprehension and communication.
- It should be clear, concise, declarative, specific, "bold, but plausible."

## Next Milestone: the specific, operational step

- Definition: "The most important thing to achieve next, and how we'll know when we've arrived."
- Method: work backwards from the N-year Vision. Template: "If we're going to achieve [final result] by [far-future-date], we will have to accomplish [1-5 critical projects or results]. So, right now what we need to accomplish next is [near-term project], and we'll know we've achieved it when [objective observable]."
- Unlike Purpose and Vision, the Milestone is specific, tactical, decisive and metrics-driven or at least metrics-influenced. Not everything important is a number.
- Examples he gives:
  - Pre-raise founder: wants $1M in 10 months, needs 200 paying customers before raising in 6 months, has only happy beta-testers. Next milestone: 20 paying customers, "enough that it's not a fluke."
  - $5M ARR, stalled: customers cancel at 5%/mo, so he mathematically cannot grow; some cohorts cancel at only 2% and pay 5x as much. Mixture is 10/90 in the wrong direction. Next milestone: shift new customers to a 35/65 mix.
  - Burned-out founder (5 years in): hire a new CEO and manage the transition so he can stay ten more years. He was in this position at WP Engine and calls Heather Brunner "our late-joining co-founder."
  - Growth-stalled but healthy unit economics: brainstorm adjacent spaces and run five experiments, then pick one or two, before committing millions of dollars.
  - Indie game studio: needs $600K for an 18-month dream game; makes about $12K/month profit. Milestone: ship a small high-margin DLC in 4 months, lifting profit to at least $20K/month.
  - 35-person agency growing 15%/yr: wants recurring revenue at 40% of business in three years. Milestone: package the most repeatable service as a fixed-price subscription and sign 10 recurring customers, even at a steep discount.
  - Open-source project with 50,000 GitHub stars: launch a hosted premium version, 50 paying customers, $10K MRR, enough for two people to quit their jobs.
  - Acquired 15-year-old manufacturer: modernize operations, implement an ERP, cut COGS 12% in six months to fund e-commerce.
  - Series B AI startup at $12M ARR, 90% gross margins, custom solutions: extract common patterns, build a self-serve platform covering 80% of use cases, migrate 3 existing customers.
- To operationalize it, he points to his quarterly strategic planning method, Rocks/Pebbles/Sand prioritization, and a sensible set of product metrics.

## Purpose-driven vs purpose-derived

- Purpose-driven: fulfilling the Purpose is the origin and number-one goal, shown by advancing it even at the cost of sales, share or profit.
- Purpose-derived: the Purpose is real and good for others but a by-product of success, not the driver. He believes most companies fall here.
- His own companies were derived, not driven:
  - Smart Bear began as an idea to data-mine a software project's history, then became a code review tool. Derived Purpose: increase software quality. Evidence: after he sold it over 16 years ago, it accumulated nearly a dozen quality tools, many open-source. He also wrote a free book on doing reviews well that didn't require buying the software.
  - WP Engine began because his blog crashed when it hit Hacker News's front page, and other bloggers said "if you find it, tell me." Derived Purpose: enable everyone to run a superior website (fast, scalable, secure), since 99.9% of business owners shouldn't become technology experts. Customers have told him unprompted that it matters.
- Apple sold computers to hobbyists and took 21 years to reach "Think Different." It was purpose-derived: Apple "did not, in fact, start with why. But it got there."

## Making it real

- Words on an "About Us" page don't create a Purpose. You create one by:
  - Figuring out the positive difference you can make.
  - Writing it succinctly.
  - Building it into strategy, positioning and goals.
- Jason's test: on your deathbed you can honestly say "What I did, mattered."
- Best case is being driven by a higher purpose from the start and building a sustainable organization around it. He notes this is an era where "it's possible to do good as well as become rich."
