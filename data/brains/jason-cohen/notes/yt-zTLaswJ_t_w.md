## Honesty as a competitive differentiator

Jason argues that lying in marketing, sales and social media is so common that being brutally honest makes a business stand out. He treats this purely as a way to make more money, not as ethics.

- Most websites sound like "the People's Republic of North Korea": claims that fool no one, so visitors stop believing anything. Examples he gives are "the leading provider" language and Smuckers' "100% fruit spread" jar that is only 30% strawberries.
- People say they want honesty (the standard "be authentic" social media advice) but rarely do it. A sentiment check on Gary Vaynerchuk's and his own Twitter followers found almost no real negativity.
- Jason says James Altucher told him that after he decided in November 2010 to tell only the truth on his blog, readership went from about 10,000 to 100,000 people a day within a couple of months. Jason notes that honesty alone does not make you a celebrity.
- The key is being proactively honest, not just answering truthfully when asked. Volunteering the bad news earns believability for everything else you say.

## Showing the bad with the good: the Canon reviews experiment

Jason cites a field experiment by Bazaarvoice, a company in Austin. Canon added customer reviews to its camera pages, and Bazaarvoice says it saw the same pattern across about 500 companies over roughly seven years.

- Highly rated cameras roughly doubled their sales.
- Low-rated cameras gained almost as much, which surprised Canon.
- Profit rose more for the low-rated products than for the high-rated ones, because returns fell about 20%.
- Why it works: buyers of cheaper products expect flaws, and knowing exactly what the flaw is lets them decide to buy without shopping around. Jason's example is his daughter's toy spaceship. A relative bought it after reading reviews that said the door pops open but everything else is good, and it was the best option under $40.
- Returns are very costly. On a $100 camera, Canon's profit is about $10, so one return wipes out the profit on roughly ten more sales. Software returns also cost support time, bad Twitter comments and a wasted bad-fit customer.
- Jason's summary: if you say both the good and the bad, you sell more and profit more.

## Rethinking the competitor comparison chart

Jason calls the usual feature-comparison chart (your column all green checkmarks, competitors' columns mostly crosses) a lie by omission. Buyers know the competitor has features you left off.

- Skip the chart. Instead state the two or three things you uniquely do better and say "we're the best at the following three things". This promotes you without attacking anyone or naming competitors.
- Also say who you are bad for. For his WordPress hosting company, he tells small-traffic bloggers to use wordpress.com or shared hosting because they would overspend with him, and tells huge sites like CNN to use a different, larger provider. Telling people "we're no good for you, go over there" makes the people who do fit trust you more.
- Rewrite the About page the same way. Contrast Web Methods' buzzword-filled blurb with Balsamiq's early page, where Peldi wrote that he was one dude and that supporting a whole software company alone "sounds iffy" and "remains to be seen". Jason says Balsamiq's first-year revenue was about a million dollars. He is clear that honesty does not guarantee that, only that it does not stop sales.

## Cancellation data: owning outages

Jason shares a trailing 30-day chart of his hosting company's cancellations within the first 30 days of signup. The normal rate was about 6-8%.

- After a one-hour full outage that the company did not announce, the early cancellation rate jumped to around 20%.
- After a 12-hour outage handled differently, there was no visible increase at all. The team tweeted and posted on its status blog immediately and kept updating as the problem developed.
- Both outages were data-center-wide and not the company's fault. Jason says that does not matter, because customers' sites were still down.
- His conclusion: proactively talking about every problem neutralized essentially all of the damage.

## Don't copy the page, copy the principle: TOMS vs. Skechers Bobs

Jason warns that copying a tactic that worked for an honest company does not work if you are not the same kind of company.

- TOMS gives a pair of shoes to a child for every pair bought. Skechers launched "Bobs" with the identical one-for-one model in 2010 and pulled it within 24 hours after a backlash. The campaigns were identical except that TOMS was honest and Skechers copied it as a marketing trick.
- Copy the idea behind Balsamiq's page: be forthright about who you are and what you value.
- Jason's hosting site lists the whole team and says "when you host with us, you're betting on us personally". He argues that in a commoditized market, where servers and RAM look the same, the people are the differentiator.
- He also publishes his company's values on the careers page: how far the company goes for customers and where it stops, including when it decides a customer is abusive. He says a company will have a culture either way, so you should decide it, hire to it and act on it. Customers have told him they are impressed that he said it.
- Presentation is still fine. His analogy is picking your best true profile photo. OkCupid data suggested that angled, from-above "MySpace" style photos got the most messages for women, while for men an animal or muscles beat travel photos. Selecting the best true thing is not lying.

## Turning small-company weaknesses into advantages (sales scripts)

Jason's approach is to admit the limitation first, which earns credibility, and then pivot to the specific advantage only a small company can offer. The examples below come from his own sales conversations.

- **No 24/7 support:** "You can go to a competitor and get 24/7 support." Rackspace will answer at 2am Sunday, but that person is not a WordPress expert. Jason says his team are true experts who can solve deep problems and are unlikely to be awake at 2am. He says he never lost a customer on the 24/7 point using this argument.
- **"You'd be our biggest customer":** agree and say it could cause problems because the company has not done this before. Then add that the customer will be so important that everyone will work on it day and night, because the company cannot grow unless customers like them succeed, and a company where they are the hundredth-biggest customer won't care the same way. Offer to connect them with the current biggest customer to ask how it has gone. Jason says he never lost a biggest-customer deal with this approach, and that he has told prospects they should go with someone else, which makes them keep talking.
- **Enterprise sales without suits:** at SmartBear he sold five-figure deals when it was just him and six-figure deals with a handful of employees and no salespeople. He opened demos by saying he didn't have the expected 44 PowerPoint slides about the mission statement, only a demo. He told prospects to include his tech support in their trial, and one buyer pulled out his wallet and bought on the spot because that honesty implied the support would be good.
- **Lean on small-company advantages:**
  - Take on risk that incumbents can't, such as doing a special project that you might not get paid for if it fails.
  - Move faster and structure unusual deals, like lower rates in exchange for paying upfront or later payment.
  - Have no legacy customers or code to protect.
  - Be fully committed. Jason's line to customers was that if it didn't work "we all have to go back to working at Sonic", and people laughed and wanted him to succeed.

## Why people lie about size, and why it doesn't pay

Jason says that when he said "three" employees when it was just him, there were two motives: continuing a lie already implied on the website, and fear of losing the deal.

- The upside is tiny. A Lockheed Martin team of a dozen people won't treat three employees differently from one, so you can't stretch the lie far enough to matter. The downside is huge, because you lose all your credibility if it is discovered.
- He cites a book by two New York women who interviewed hundreds of women entrepreneurs. They said small white lies about the business consistently backfired and left them feeling bad about the work that was supposed to be fulfilling.
- He sold SmartBear to AutomatedQA in about four months. The buyer asked how much he wanted in the bank, and they simply worked backwards from that number instead of the usual haggling. He says 17 advisers reviewed it and all but one called the deal generous. He gives this as another case where honesty worked, not as a template.
- He points to Howard Stern, who says his secret is simply being honest. Jason's view is that shock jocks who copied only the shock never matched his popularity.
- His closing view: honesty is hard to do in every sales call, page and phone answer, so almost nobody goes all the way. That is why doing it is a large competitive advantage.
