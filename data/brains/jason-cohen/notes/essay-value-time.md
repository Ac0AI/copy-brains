## Why Jason says a startup founder's time is worth $1000/hour

Jason argues that founders undervalue their time. Consultants know their rate ("what the market will bear"), but a founder in a startup is working on very different terms. His claim: "Your time is worth $1000/hour, and you need to act accordingly."

- Valuing yourself at a consultant rate ($150/hr in his example) is a mistake once you are in a startup.
- The reason is that the math of a startup is not the math of a consultant.

## The thought experiment: a client who pays in four years

Jason builds the $1000/hr figure from a hypothetical.

- A consultant who normally charges $150/hour meets a client who says: "I can't pay you now. I promise to pay you four years from now, at which time I will pay everything I owe in one lump sum."
- The rate must go up for the delay alone. 4% interest isn't the whole story, because you can't live off money you don't have, so you also have to work for other clients to cover your expenses. That inconvenience demands a hefty premium.
- The bigger problem is the risk the client never pays. If the client is an early-stage startup, even a funded one, the most likely outcome is that it dies and stiffs you.
- He assumes a 25% chance the company exists in four years and pays its bill.

## Why 1/odds isn't enough

- The naive fix is $150 ÷ 0.25 = $600/hour.
- Jason says this "merely brings you back to break-even."
- Test: split your time across four companies on these terms. Probably all but one fold, and the survivor pays 4x your rate. That leaves you where you'd be charging $150/hour on standard terms, without the risk and without waiting four years for cash.
- Like gambling or investing, steeper odds require a bigger upside. To be worth the risk you must charge a premium over break-even, so he lands on "more like $1000/hr."

## Founders are already accepting those terms

- Jason says this isn't hypothetical: it is "exactly the terms you're accepting for yourself when you create a startup."
- Because the risk is high, the financial reward must outweigh it, so "you have to value your time at least $1000 per hour."
- Platitudes like "my time is worth what the market will bear" don't apply.

## What valuing your time at $1000/hr changes day to day

- Skip projects that have potential only for small, incremental results.
- A personal assistant is worth the money.
- Focus on a few things that really matter, the ones worth that hourly rate, and delegate far more than you think.
- Follow the rules of finding product-market fit: talk to customers rather than assuming you know what to build, and work on marketing and sales at least as much as the product.
- Spend money on a bookkeeper and CPA instead of messing with receipts, QuickBooks and taxes. Pay for help at home with cleaning and chores.
- Build things more valuable than money, ones that generate customer Love.
- Work harder than is healthy, and obsess about your own productivity.
- Be smart at prioritization, and make sure you are actually doing it.

## The filter for how to spend your hours

- Jason's summary: stay in the intersection of your zone of maximum excellence and the zone of importance.
- "Because at $1000/hr, you can't afford to do anything else."
