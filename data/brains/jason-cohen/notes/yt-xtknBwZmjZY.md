## The ceiling comes from math: new customers divided by churn rate

Jason's "max MRR" or growth-ceiling idea: marketing channels add a roughly fixed number of customers per month, but cancellations are a percentage of the whole base, so they grow with you.

- Formula: new customers per month ÷ monthly cancellation rate = the most customers you can ever have if nothing changes.
- Example: AdWords adds 100 customers a month and 5% cancel monthly, so growth stops at 2,000 customers (100 ÷ 0.05). At 2,000, 5% churn is 100 leaving, matching the 100 arriving.
- Marketing "doesn't care how big your company is." Each channel tops out fast (only so many searches, words and profitable ROIs), and most companies get only a few channels that really work.
- If nothing changes, you "will never ever" exceed that number. Without action you grow slower and slower until cancellations win.
- He says everyone should compute this for their own company, charted over time and ideally by cohort or segment.
- It is visceral ("Oh crap, this is it") and it skips the abstract argument over whether 5% churn is okay. He asks instead: "You're never going to have more than 2,000 customers. You good with that?"
- If a competitor selling to similar consumers has 2% churn, your high churn is a choice of market segment, and you control it.
- Cancellation also matters emotionally and financially. People saw everything you offer, believed the promise, and left anyway, so find out why.
- When growth slows for everyone at once, that is a secular shift (AI), not coincidence. The ceiling math is true regardless.

## A strategy is a unique set of strengths and weaknesses, not one unique thing

Jason says seeking one thing nobody else does is a platitude "said by people who aren't operators." Occasionally it exists, but typically the whole set is unique.

- No one copies your weaknesses. What matters is that the weaknesses are trade-offs that enable the strengths, not just weak spots.
- WP Engine example:
  - It over-invested in the fastest CPUs, the fastest memory and attention across the whole stack, so customers think "what the heck is going on?" about the speed.
  - It charged more than everyone else and staffed capable support people, which is expensive.
  - It disallowed slow WordPress plugins. "That's a massive weakness. If you have a website, you depend on this plugin, it won't work here... You don't want my business. I want it, but no."
- Competitors could copy all of it, but low-priced shared hosts at $2 a month can't afford it, it doesn't match their model, and their customers don't need it.
- His summary: "You can copy our strengths but are you going to copy our weaknesses?" That special shape is the strategy, and some customers will prefer it and some won't.
- He has been building for 25 years and says he can't remember anything he did that was uncopyable from a code perspective.

## Copyability and competition in the AI era

- Everything has always been copyable (successful software had clones before AI). AI makes copying faster and cheaper, but you and the copier both speed up, so the strategic rules hold on a multi-year timeframe.
- He concedes absolute time does change: 6 months vs 3 months differs from 3 months vs 2 weeks.
- Markets with lots of money are busy and noisy, and AI floods them with mediocre marketing that makes it harder to be seen, but it also lets you build faster. The need to be special in a way customers pay for remains.
- Big companies are not asleep this time, unlike mobile or the internet. HubSpot, Salesforce, Google and Microsoft are all investing in AI. You can't discount them, and a startup chat product must fight Intercom, HubSpot and Salesforce.
- Incumbents aren't necessarily succeeding, and startup AI products often don't work well either.
- He was wrong in his earlier article that data is an incumbent advantage. One person with a strong set of LLM skills (skills as markdown files) can do amazing things without data. He allows that some applications may still need it.

## Urgency without chaos on AI

- Jason pushes back on "chaos." Experiment and move with urgency, but don't overreact, because the tooling changes constantly (Claude Code ships updates daily, sometimes major ones, and "the way we're doing AI now is nothing like what we did six months ago").
- Since nobody knows what AI will look like in 6 to 12 months, he asks what there is to be chaotic about.
- The real risk is staying in the "chat with a model" mindset. That sees a dumb one-shot, context-free chat and concludes AI is dumb. Structured tools with skills, iteration and agents can be 5 to 10 times better in accuracy and productivity.
- Learn the stable concepts: prompting, then skills, then agents, then agent coordination. The details of a given agent setup will change.
- Start now to build the mindset. If you don't, you don't know when you'll start or how far behind you'll be.
- Much AI simply doesn't work, but when it does it is "magical," and a competitor who gets there first while you don't is "really bad." Anthropic overtaking OpenAI in revenue and growth because of Claude Code is something few predicted six months earlier.
- Deep truths that held 10 or 20 years ago still hold. Use AI as a new tool to attack them, and drop ideas that are no longer relevant.

## Why startups usually fail, and what to do about it

- Starting any company is net negative expected value, so applying probability to the decision means never starting one. Do it if you feel compelled, you have an idea, or "this is who you are."
- Failure is common because many things must each go right enough: the product works, you can build and maintain it, you find customers, it's cheap enough, customers convert, keep paying and find value, and you hold up against competition. Even at 70% each, the product of many is low.
- The model is not literally true, but it helps you find your weak or unknown areas. Then act on them:
  - Learn the skill.
  - Add a co-founder with complementary skills.
  - Test earlier so you find out in two months that it's a bad idea instead of twelve.
- Bootstrapped founders still have a shot. Most bootstrapped and VC-funded AI companies will fail, and too much money is going into AI.

## Using AI as a facilitator, and sharing ideas freely

- Jason's book "Hidden Multipliers" (hiddenmultipliers.com) covers jump-starting growth with your current budget and team. It is strategic, not tactical, with no SEO or GEO tactics, only understanding how businesses work. Examples illustrate deeper concepts and shouldn't be copied exactly.
- ICP: write for one bullseye customer, because focus drives decisions and makes the homepage clear. Many people outside the bullseye still buy. Most buyers aren't your perfect customer, yet it stays your north star. His own ICP is a bootstrapped founder with about $2M ARR in SaaS whose growth has slowed or stopped and who doesn't know which of 10 options to pick.
- AI can't tell you who your perfect customer is, it would just generate something that sounds good. Where it helps is facilitation: it prods you, brainstorms and plays devil's advocate. You can't be your own devil's advocate, and a coach's value is facilitation, not answers. He built skills for each step of the process.
- He wants people to take and remix his ideas. His blog (18 years) has an RSS feed under "special links" with every article in markdown for ingestion. He isn't trying to make money from the ideas, only to cover the book's cost.
- Best way to learn from a book: read it, then build the skill yourself. "If you tell me, I forget. If I do it, I might remember, and if I teach it, I understand." Teaching the LLM gives you both understanding and efficiency. Pick which tasks to automate (those that don't matter to you) and which to stay engaged in (your company's strategy).

## Joy, craft and Claude Code

- Jason finds Claude Code magical and fun to tune, but finishes days drained rather than energized, likening it to doom-scrolling. Other people report the same.
- Productivity gains are real: one thread is often faster, two or three threads accelerate things further, but beyond that he can't keep it in mind. Context switching is draining, and managing an AI that says "you're right" patronizingly is annoying.
- It's great for scaffolding and prototyping, but for tiny edits it's faster to open the file and change the number.
- He loved the craft of code optimization (reading how libraries implement cosine and sine, for example) and that part is gone. He says it's sad when a craft is lost, even when the technology is a net good.
- He is not going back to coding the old way, but he writes his book by hand because the human ideas and style are the product. He writes long, thoughtful sentences, not "fifth-grader" marketing prose, and articles take dozens of hours.
- Chess analogy: computers have beaten the best humans for 25 years, yet chess is more popular than ever, because the human struggle is the point. He thinks human expression is insulated, though AI changes styles and study methods. He clicks off YouTube videos that are clearly AI, and would rather watch a human do a bad job.
