## Three pricing strategies, pick one and commit to the consequences

Jason argues there are three viable strategies in the interplay of value and price, and that each drags along a set of consequences you must accept:

- **More for more** ("the best"): luxury, advantage, high price.
- **More for less** ("high ROI"): everything you actually need at a reasonable price.
- **Less for less** ("incredible deal"): not much, but incredibly affordable.

The pattern holds across industries, consumer and business. In clothing: a cashmere sweater or Miu Miu cardigan is more for more, an L.L. Bean sweater is more for less, and a fast-fashion hoodie is less for less. The only wrong choice is not choosing. Then the market message is confusing, the product is a "Frankenstein," the pricing model is wrong, and "your product is the right choice for no one." Because all three are valid, he says to listen to your gut and pick the one that best matches your personal strengths and what you would be proudest to execute, since that is the one you will run best and find most fulfilling.

## More for more ("the best"): what it requires

Being "the best" is good for profit and for enjoying your work: pride in craft, customers who love you, and a high price that funds further investment. But the best is an outlier. It is "special" and "different," not the average of what already exists, so your ideas must be substantially different from what is available.

- **Best at what?** It must be specific and customers must agree it is worth the higher price. Options include design (simplest, most pleasant, most beautiful), quality ("always just works"), a superior workflow, integrations with all the tools the customer uses, or personal tech support.
- **Best for whom?** Billions of people disagree on what "best" means. Founders go wrong by assuming "I built it for myself, I am the customer, so others will pay to solve it this way." Treat your initial insight as a motivation that is half right and half wrong, then interview potential customers and use a structured customer-development process to morph your definition of "best" to fit a specific ideal customer.
- **Who buys it?** Customers who care about something more than price: mission-critical products where failure causes outsized internal cost or reputational damage, urgent top priorities (emergency or strategic mandate), "toothbrush products" used at least twice a day, or things tied to personal identity. Luxury brands make being "far too expensive" the point. His WP Engine example: hosting eCommerce sites (all of the customer's revenue), media sites (slowness loses ad revenue), and corporate home pages (downtime loses leads and reputation). It can also be cover-your-ass buying ("no one ever got fired for buying ___").

Customers have their pick of options and every other product is a competitor, so the customer is in control. Hence the importance of being "the best" for some subset of the market.

## More for more: aligning the rest of the business

Positioning as the best must shape every other decision:

- Marketing focuses on excellence and exclusivity, not affordability.
- The team is smaller and higher caliber, passionate artisans who celebrate perfectionism. Hire the top 1% and pay premium salaries.
- Support is measured in seconds, not hours, often white-glove with dedicated account reps.
- Product cycles may be slower, emphasizing perfection before launch and building without cutting corners regardless of cost.
- Because the number of customers is small and limited, market share matters and upgrading existing customers is vital for growth at scale. He notes many public enterprise software companies get the majority of revenue growth from upselling existing customers.

The high price tag funds all of this. If you pull it off it is the best outcome for you: fulfilling, validated by customers, and attractive to great employees. "Most products are not 'the best,'" but for those that are, it creates a virtuous cycle of excellence, satisfaction, and profitability.

## More for less ("high ROI"): the largest and most popular category

Jason calls this the right value proposition for most things in life, especially business, where you need to get the job done as cheaply as possible "but not cheaper." Buyers have a budget and seek "the best that I can afford." Price is the primary constraint; within it they prioritize features by preference. The product's goal is not to be best on every dimension but to offer a set of trade-offs that enough customers prefer.

Apparel evidence he cites:

- Global apparel revenue is about $1,600B; more for less is about $1,200B, luxury about $250B, fast fashion about $150B.
- Profit margins: more for less roughly 5%-15%, fast fashion under 5%, luxury 20%-30% (Prada about 23% net profit). L.L. Bean is private, so its range is estimated from other retailers.
- Software can have much better margins than apparel, so reasonable prices plus the largest market segment is a great business model.

Where it fits best: back-end systems invisible to customers (no competitive advantage from being the best), areas where downtime is not disruptive in real time (a reporting tool can be down an hour; a customer support tool cannot), products where a missing feature like one chart type is not a deal-breaker, and products that just need to "tick a box" so requirements are lenient. Not being mission-critical is a cost but also gives leniency on quality and features.

## More for less: how to win the ideal customer and everyone else

- **You can be "the best" for your ideal customer.** Target the "perfect" customer with marketing and features. They cannot believe they are getting a more-for-more product at a more-for-less price. If you cannot win them from your home page, "you're not doing your job."
- **Leniency gives you far more sales.** Jason says you will make 100x more sales than you expect, because for every ideal customer there are a hundred others who get most of what they want, hit no deal-breakers, and like the price. You get the specificity and emotional connection of talking to one ICP while still winning the broader market.
- **Position on value, not savings.** "Save money" is weak positioning; show how much value you generate. Even with a more-for-less pricing strategy, marketing should still be as strong as possible and emphasize that it is really more for less.
- **Marketing must be honest about trade-offs.** Make the "smart choice" feel obvious; do not waste money on useless branding. Do not claim to be best for everyone, which "quietly alienates everyone." Comparison charts should not be a solid column of green checkmarks but show where you are strong and where you are not the right solution.
- **Product rule of thumb:** build only features used by at least 50% of customers, or where at least 15% of customers buy you specifically for that reason. The price does not allow special exceptions or brittle integrations that only 5% use.
- **Team and costs:** hire people who excel at identifying and eliminating waste without sacrificing core value. Internal costs and decisions must match the external promise.
- **Against Porter.** Michael Porter's *Competitive Strategy* (1980) listed differentiated best, low-cost leader, and niche leader; later thinkers noted differentiated and niche are the same, differing only in market scope, leaving more for more and less for less. Jason argues customers have spoken: more for less wins most customers, revenue, and profit. It is not "a balance" between value and cost but a third, independent strategy to leverage fully.

## Less for less ("incredible deal"): why it works

Jason rejects "never compete on price": many wildly successful companies, from solo bootstrappers to some of the largest, most profitable companies in the world, won on rock-bottom prices. His examples: Amazon, Walmart, Vanguard, Costco, Dollar General, IKEA, Southwest Airlines, H&M, Zara, Xiaomi, ALDI. "Sometimes, 'cheapest' is 'best'."

- **Why customers accept the trade-offs:** "the alternative is to not have the product at all." The choice is binary (any hoodie versus no hoodie), so no one quibbles over features, quality, or service. At $5 per month people expect crashes, no enterprise integrations, and knowledge-base-only support. A $2/month web host can have spam and slowness and tech support that can only reset a password.
- **Positioning:** extol democratization, participation, simplicity, affordability, and accessibility. The real mission behind the company, empowerment or exploiting desperation, shows in how it is run.
- **Consequences accepted:** low prices mean little money for marketing, sales, development, support, design, or culture perks.

## Less for less: making it profitable and surviving competitors

The hardest internal challenge is profitability, since there is so little revenue to be profitable with. Costs must be extraordinarily, unconventionally low.

- **Scale helps:** bulk purchasing from suppliers, and structural effects like support per customer being lower at 100,000 customers than at 100, because most do not have a question in any given month. Still, it takes many $2/month customers to afford one support person.
- **Acquisition must be nearly free.** If a customer is worth $2/month and maybe $50 over their lifetime, you cannot pay much to acquire them: no heavy ad spend, no sales team. Rely on word of mouth and reviews, and encourage them. Affiliates and influencers paid only after you are paid are an affordable form of marketing. Organic marketing is slow at first but scales over time.
- **Funding:** revenue lags costs, so the company will burn cash from savings, friends and family, a loan, or investors. Annual billing helps a lot. Jason (and other pundits) tell bootstrappers to "raise their prices," which really means switching from less for less to more for less or more for more. "If you don't want to raise money, don't build a business that burns money on purpose."
- **You do not need exorbitant funding.** Each of the four case studies in his "never compete on price" article grew to multiple billions in revenue on only a small amount of raised money, because strategic decisions were aligned to low cost. VC-funded companies often assume "we can work the costs out later," but processes, technology, and culture solidify around what acquired customers, and change means layoffs, rewrites, and forced marketing and sales motions. Some bootstrapped companies never raised money thanks to an extreme cost advantage, such as being based in a very low-cost country and selling to the US and Europe.
- **Do not just lower the price.** Inexperienced founders think "my product sucks, so I'm not comfortable charging a lot." That is not a good reason, and you should not build a crappy product. Ask instead: how do I build a simple yet lovable product that a specific ideal customer would pay 10x more than I charge now, even with few features and occasional failures? He says that is what he did with WP Engine.
- **Biggest external threat:** a competitor undercutting you on price, because of newer technology, a different location or cost structure, or a larger company using a loss leader. His examples: Microsoft killed Netscape with a thousand-plus developers on a free Internet Explorer, and over a decade later Google killed Explorer the same way with Chrome. The antidote is constantly improving cost efficiency and possibly proactively lowering prices, as Amazon did in retail ("your margin is my opportunity") and later in cloud. He notes that once Microsoft and Google were the only other serious cloud competitors, all three stopped lowering prices at the same time ("Surely a coincidence?").

Less for less is "brutal," with relentless competition and no spare money, but it reaches the largest number of potential customers and can truly bring goods and services to people who could not otherwise afford them.
