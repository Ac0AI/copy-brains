## Why solving a real problem is not enough

- Jason says many startups fail after finding a real problem and building a product that solves it. The founder talks to three people, they agree the problem exists (and they're correct), the founder builds the product (and it works), then sales stall and the company dies within 6-24 months.
- His claim: "solving a problem is--perhaps surprisingly--not nearly enough to build a successful company."
- He models the path from "Problem" to "Viable Business Model" as a chain of ANDed conditions: Plausible, Self-Aware, Lucrative, Liquid, Eager, Enduring. Each can be a deal-breaker even when the product is excellent.
- He says there is an exception to every rule, but you have to really want that harder path and have a specific answer for the weakness.

## Plausible: market size math (10M consumers or 100k businesses)

Jason uses Fermi estimation to show how big the market must be, even for an indie company:
- Impression to visitor converts about 1%. A very good AdWords campaign gets about 2% click-through, display ads 0.3%-0.5%, and a top SEO position 3%-5% (almost impossible for a competitive keyword).
- Visitor to paid converts about 1% for a typical successful product website (sign-up or trial forms show 2-5%, of which only a fraction pay).
- So 10,000 impressions gives about 1 paying customer, and 10M impressions gives about 1,000 paying customers.
- 1,000 paying customers is his minimum for a sustainable small company, at roughly $30-$100/mo. Below that price you need more customers; above it you need fewer, but they're harder to find and convert.
- It takes about two years, 10M impressions, and luck. WP Engine, which hit $100M ARR fast and now has over 200,000 customers, took two years to get its first 1,000 customers. Competitors that started later took that long too. It's a rule of thumb: some founders take 52 hours, 6 months, 4 years or forever.
- Consumer products need about 10M people with the problem. Business products need about 100k, because there are far fewer businesses but they pay orders of magnitude more and convert better.
- Exceptions: a high-price product in a small niche, or a frugal solo business making about $100,000/year post-expenses post-tax. He notes 80% of small businesses do this and it's valuable; there are two kinds of companies, those replacing a salary and those trying to become huge.

## Self-Aware: they must know and care they have the problem

- People who don't believe they have a problem won't search for a solution, and you can't get them to pay to fix it.
- Website security example: everyone has the problem, since hackers want your server for spam, ad-click fraud, remote-controlling visitors' browsers, or hiding their tracks. But millions think "I'm nobody," so they don't search for or buy security software. WP Engine blocks hundreds of millions of nefarious requests daily.
- Second version: they know but genuinely don't care because it's their ninth priority. Accessibility is his example; many agree they have the problem but few act.
- Timing matters. Instacart worked after 80% of Americans had smartphones; WebVan had the same idea before the market was ready.
- Sam Altman on Loopt (raised $30M, failed): "The market wasn't there. You can't force a market."
- Exception: founders who are natural evangelists on a mission to educate and create the market. He calls it difficult (changing minds is almost impossible), expensive and slow, because you create demand then fulfill it.

## Lucrative: a substantial allocated budget

- Typical objections: needs someone else's approval, budget closed for the year, the internal team says they don't need help.
- College-student startups at Capital Factory: in 17 years none of them worked, even though the problems were real. Students have no money and don't buy SaaS, and the businesses serving students charge low prices so they have no budget for new ideas.
- Large companies fund only their top few problems each year. They also add compliance approval, vendor comparison and pilots, and internal teams may resist outside tools that make them vendor-managers. Target companies that already outsource the problem.
- Consumers pay little even for high-value productivity apps. Examples he cites: Bear Pro at $15/year, Remember the Milk at $40/year, and an Evernote user leaving over a rise from €29 to €43/year.
- Consumers "pay" with attention and data, so he argues self-funded companies should target businesses, which spend money to solve problems and to make more money. He flags this as a personal bias: he doesn't build or invest in B2C because he doesn't understand that customer.
- Exception: a huge number of customers, extremely low cost basis (no direct support, little infrastructure, near-zero acquisition cost, tiny team, low switching costs), a simple product, ideally against poor, expensive incumbents. You then need far more than 10M customers.

## Liquid: able to buy right now

- Blockers: contract not up for 15 months, the problem is already covered by an all-in-one like Workday, deep integrations with other systems, or a one-time need ("I'll let you know when I get married again").
- At WP Engine, Jason's own team cancelled several SaaS products because Workday did it all in one system.
- Lock-in forces include contracts, hard data extraction, retraining thousands of employees, and re-implementing integrations, workflows and reports. "All these forces have nothing to do with your product or its price," yet they can overwhelm the best product at the best price.
- Flip it into strategy: how will you create lock-in so competitors can't kick you out of your customers?
- Temporary-need products struggle. From his Smart Bear days, founders of code profilers and load-testing tools struggled to keep even small companies alive: customers didn't have the problem, then suddenly needed it right now, then didn't again.
- Exceptions: pay the customer's contract-breaking penalty (if still profitable), do the migration as a service or for free (common in WP Engine's market), target buyers who can't afford Workday so it isn't a competitor, or offer a cheap or free way to stay on while not using the product.

## Eager: they want to buy from you specifically

Two hurdles:
- **Trust:** that the product works, that you'll be around for years, keep up development pace, avoid security issues, deliver real support, and scale as they scale. Typical objections: "only a year in business," "will you handle 100x growth," "SOC 2 required."
- **Differentiation:** being unique isn't enough. If only 10% of the market cares about your feature and 30% cares about a competitor's feature you lack, you lose.
- Over-serving: with ten features where the market cares about three, the simpler or cheaper product wins even if your feature matrix looks more complete.
- A sales team helps by building trust and positioning so the customer accepts your mix of pluses and minuses. He points to "Love" and "Utility" willingness-to-pay as the best way to overcome both.
- Exceptions: build a product that needs little trust (no private data, not time-critical, local, or for freelancers who like supporting startups); mitigate with open source; compete on price (works but hurts margin and customer quality); specialize in a niche in a large market; or sell into a small market with few competitors. A mission bigger than the company can differentiate, mostly in consumer products. His example is Patagonia, bought for its conservation spending and treatment of employees.

## Enduring: churn kills the business

- Jason has seen countless indie developers reach $15k MRR and stall at 7% monthly cancellation, with many thinking 5% would be a success. It isn't: at 5%/mo only half of customers remain after a year.
- The deeper issue is that customers don't want the product enough: it lacks features, has bugs, lacks integrations, is too expensive for the result, or the problem isn't important.
- The math: top-line growth is linear (or quadratic in hyper-growth), but cancellation is exponential because it's a percent of the base, so it catches up with new sales.
- Worked example: $15k MRR, adding $2k/mo of new customers (a healthy 15% per month), at 7% churn. A year later MRR is about $27k and growth has stopped, since $27k × 7% ≈ $2k cancelling each month. Each of those new customers cost money to attract, sell and onboard.
- One-time-revenue businesses still need repeat revenue, from customers buying again and from happy customers referring others.
- Even Unicorn goals are impossible with high churn. He cites Gainsight CEO Nick Mehta on launching a second product to avoid the inevitable stall.
- Exception: Shopify has about 8% cancellation (only 34% of new customers last one year) and still wins, because (a) survivors grow forever and super-linearly, (b) the market is huge and growing, and (c) people cancel because their own business failed, not because the product is bad. If the product itself is the problem, he believes there is no exception.

## The viability scoring model and worked examples

Jason's Fermi-style score multiplies seven inputs (power-of-ten only where noted), scored for one specific target market, buyer, problem, trade-off set and price:
- **Plausible:** potential customers with the problem (1k to 1B).
- **Self-Aware:** 0.01 few agree or care; 0.1 thought-leaders evangelize; 0.5 industry standard practice; 1.0 almost no one doesn't care.
- **Lucrative:** annual allocated budget, net revenue ($1 to $1M). Net revenue means after pass-through costs, not after marketing, support or infrastructure.
- **Liquid:** purchase-decision frequency: 0.01 every few years; 0.1 annual; 1.0 always in the market.
- **Eager (identity):** 0 cannot buy from you; 0.1 structural challenges; 0.5 indifferent; 1.0 mission-level desire to choose you.
- **Eager (comparative):** 0.1 no differentiation; 0.5 some features good enough for some buyers; 1.0 one-of-a-kind.
- **Enduring:** 0.01 one-off without loyalty; 0.1 one-off but repeat and referrals; 0.5 recurring revenue from recurring problem; 1.0 strong lock-in.
- Multiply, because the conditions are ANDed, then divide by 625,000. A score of 1 is the indie-startup threshold; 2 or more suggests a scale-up. (625k comes from 100M × 0.5 × 0.1 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5, with 100M being 10M consumers at $10/mo or 100k businesses at $1000/mo, e.g. dentist practice management.)
- He warns it's "dangerously close to a silly quiz": use it as guidance, since "having to think through the answers and trade-offs is most of the value."

Examples:
- **WP Engine:** 100M × 0.1 × $100 × 0.01 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 1.0 = 2,500,000, so score 4, a scale-up (as happened). Market logic: 334M businesses, 71% with websites, 43% of sites on WordPress.
- **ConvertKit** (email for creators monetizing newsletters): 10M × 1.0 × $100 × 0.01 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 1,250,000, score 2. A good model, bootstrapped to large scale though not a Unicorn. Focusing on higher-paying customers (1M people, $1000 budget) gives the same score but is a different product and market.
- **Consumer security software:** 1B × 0.01 × $10 × 0.01 × 0.5 × 0.1 × 0.5 = 25,000, score 0.04. A bad model.

## What to do with a negative score: niche down or move on

- Re-score a narrower target. Security for high-net-worth individuals: people fall to 1M, but willingness to solve rises to 1.0 and budget to $100 or $1000.
- Security for mid-sized businesses: fewer organizations than consumers, but very high willingness (policies, fear of incidents) and substantial budgets.
- His rule: "targeting a niche often results in a better business model," because it can raise several other numbers even as it cuts customer count. It works whether you stay in the niche forever or use it as a beachhead to expand later.
- If no target scores well, "your business idea might simply not be viable." Facing that early is better than spending years on it.
- This path isn't the only factor: you still need reach, attention, cost-effective acquisition, manageable costs, skills and runway.
