## Founders carry two jobs: win on growth and win on "best"

- Jason separates "survival of the fittest" from "survival of the best." "Fittest" means "most prolific at becoming grandparents." The grandparent test is the only one: offspring that can't reproduce don't count (a horse-donkey cross makes a sterile mule).
- Nature has no ethics. "Bad" things can be as fit as "good" ones. Viruses are amazingly fit: they spread at prodigious rates and are hard to stop once numerous.
- He argues it is the founder's responsibility to solve for both growth and the betterment of the world, because the market will not do the second job on its own.

## "Growth at all costs" turns companies into viruses

- Jason is not against obsessing over growth. In winner-take-all markets, "growth is objectively the correct goal."
- His complaint is "growth *at all costs*." Investors and founders hold up viral growth as the best kind, since exponential curves overwhelm all others. Once the mechanism is stable and the company is big enough to be noticed, competitors can hardly catch up.
- A virus colony grows with no care for the host, and can destroy the host and itself.
- His rule: "When 'growth rate' becomes the *only* important metric for company 'fitness,' other metrics are left unsolved."
- Winning the market this way proves you are "an excellent virus in a Darwinian sense," not that you have "an excellent business model."

## Profitability is the metric most often left unsolved

- Many companies that won the growth battle never convert from a virus into a business.
- Jason names companies he sees as already past the point of "can never be profitable unless something drastically changes": GroupOn, Zynga, SoundCloud.
- Others continue on massive investment, and he says the jury is out on whether they ever produce profits: SnapChat, Pinterest, WeWork, InstaCart, Square, FourSquare, and Twitter, which has impressive revenue but has never been profitable.
- His read: some will find a business model and some won't. Either way, their market-winner position came from growth, not from a proven model.

## Betterment of humanity is the second unsolved metric

- Jason says it is the entrepreneur's duty to build companies that leave the world better than they found it, or at least not worse.
- He says it is also employees' duty to put most of their waking hours and most productive years into companies that make the world better, "not just 'hit a number.'"
- He gives examples of market-winners that do make the world better:
  - Biotech advances, which have extended and improved life.
  - WordPress (25% of all domains), which made owning your website orders of magnitude cheaper.
  - Amazon's cloud services, which enable products and services that were impossible a decade ago, for startups and 100-year-old companies alike.
- He also lists winners that are a mixed bag:
  - Uber turns under-paid employees into so-called "entrepreneurs," but they are still indentured and still under-paid.
  - Twitter connects people and ideas but kills attention spans and stokes hate more than debate.
  - Google puts the world's information at our fingertips but destroys personal privacy in ways more insidious than most people want to know.
  - Self-driving cars will solve traffic, help the environment, serve people who can't drive, and make parking lots obsolete, but will destroy millions of transportation jobs, many well paid relative to the holders' formal education.
- Darwinian growth wins by definition. He says there is no use crying about it, and regulation would only shift where the winners are located.

## Make values drive growth instead of trading growth for values

- Jason asks founders and employees whether growth is *sufficient*, and whether the company will treat other values as "*also* inalienable and unassailable," letting them drive the most important decisions and requiring growth to be compatible with them.
- His framing: "The real question is not how to retard growth in submission to other values, but rather how to use our insistence values to drive growth."

## Three examples of growth driven by values

- **Small, Buffer:** a 5-year-old company, profitable and growing, in the overcrowded "Social Media Tools" space with a product Jason calls not particularly special or unique. Talent still beats down the door to work there, even knowing they'll take a pay cut, because salaries are set by a formula. The pull comes from complete transparency and positivity, honesty about struggles and successes, and care for employee health and well-being.
- **Mid-sized, TOMS shoes:** a massive success due to a viral story people want to support and retell, "exactly because its mission is so commendable and pure," not because of a superior product.
- **Large, USAA:** a company with the boring homepage title "insurance, banking and retirement solutions." It is the 49th most valuable company on Earth, with $24 billion in annual revenue and $3 billion in profits. Its customer-service NPS runs consistently between 75 and 80, among the highest in the world. It is known as one of the best places to work, even in traditionally low-paying, low-formal-education roles like customer service, and for its unwavering commitment to US Armed Forces families.
- Jason's conclusion: you can "grow and be profitable exactly because of goodness and love." He points to his willingness-to-pay essay for ways to do it.

## The takeaway: build the virus with beneficial effects

- A virulent company is wonderful only if it carries beneficial effects: customers who love the service, employees who enjoy spending most of their waking hours at work, and net-positive effects on economies, communities and societies.
- His closing line: "When you build your unstoppable company, make it an unstoppable force for good."
