## Get feedback from lost sales, not from the customers who already bought

Jason argues the number one way to raise revenue is learning why people check out the product but don't buy. Adding features, polishing the website and even talking to existing customers are all weaker. Existing customers "bought in spite of your faults"; the 99.9% of potential customers who didn't buy are the ones who can tell you what blocks sales. In his 2009 essay he lists 11 ways to gather this data, most doable in under a day.

## Collect emails before downloads and run an opt-in newsletter

- Jason adds a short, optional form before a download, eBook or whitepaper so he can follow up. Objection: forms reduce downloads. At Smart Bear, adding the form had "no impact in the number of downloads. None."
- If a hundred people download in silence and don't buy, "it doesn't matter that they downloaded."
- Follow-up matters because, in his experience, about half of trial downloaders never started the trial, about half got stuck and stopped silently, and about half don't realize you're a friendly small company that wants them to succeed.
- Asking non-buyers "why" often gets answers.
- He recommends an honest, opt-in, no-spam newsletter list even if you have no time or content yet, since someone will want it later. Most people on such lists aren't paying customers, so it gives you reasons to ping interested non-buyers.
- Newsletter ideas: praise new release features, a tip about a lesser-known feature, a customer success story, an article about you or why services like yours are valuable, a partnership announcement, a time-limited coupon, or an offer to demo when you're in their city.

## Pay non-buyers for 15 minutes of feedback

- Find contact info for people who trialed but didn't buy and offer something free if they talk for 15 minutes about why.
- At Smart Bear the company gave $2010 to Wikipedia, $5 at a time, as the incentive. He calls it "amazingly successful."
- Incentive options: cool stuff that does good for the world (e.g. something from Etsy), a charity donation ($25 to Kiva, Wikimedia Foundation or Free Software Foundation), a $25 Amazon coupon, company swag (T-shirt, mug, mousepad), or desirable unrelated items (an XKCD cartoon calendar, Despair posters).
- Worth $25 at least, probably $50. Run it until you hear the same things repeatedly, or budget $1000 and run until it's gone.

## Interview one existing customer about the buying experience

Existing customers can't explain why others don't buy, but they can describe the trial and purchase process. Carve out at least 15 minutes (a customer discovery call is a good excuse) and ask:

- How did they hear about you? (shows which marketing sources deserve more spend)
- What website information convinced them to download? (shows which messages matter; make buried ones obvious and use them in ads)
- What was the main reason they bought? (their primary pain point, to put on the home page and ads)
- What part of the trial was confusing or difficult? (where others probably drop out)

## Mine testimonials for the real reasons customers buy

- Testimonials are marketing material and also reveal the real pain you solve and the actual impact on customers' lives.
- Check whether what customers highlight is obvious to new website visitors and trial users. If customers don't mention something you thought was great, reconsider whether it matters. "Listen to your customers' point of view, not yours. Listen to their stories, not your vision."
- Example: a Siemens program manager said Code Collaborator "was like someone broke the ice in our group" and that people "came out of our cubes and actually talked to each other." It says nothing about features or competitors; it's about a social change from isolation to collaboration. That is reflected in the product tagline: "When code review is easy and fun, it actually gets done."

## Add in-product feedback hooks: uninstaller, Help menu link, quit prompt

- Uninstaller: ask why they didn't buy. People like giving opinions; a WindowTabs founder said it was "eye-opening." Firefox even asks when you cancel an install. Most responses are empty or "You suck," but some are "gold."
- Don't build custom forms. Pop open an email client or a web form. Add an optional field: "If you'd like to be notified when we address the problems you've raised, put your email address here."
- Link or Help menu item: use human wording like "Complain" or "Yell at us"; plain "Feedback" is too corporate. At Smart Bear a sizable share of all feedback email came from this link, and it takes about an hour to add. No proxy servers or complex forms needed; open a Contact Us page or the email client.
- A prompt on exit during a trial is no worse than any other buy-now box but has more upside (suggestion credited to Keith of Redcort).

## Trade free software for reviews or feedback sessions

- Free copies for reviews give both feedback and publicity. Bad publicity isn't a big risk: if the product is dead on arrival, nobody writes about it, because "I couldn't get it installed" isn't an interesting article. Mixed but detailed reviews can make people more likely to try a product than pure rave reviews. Small bloggers are the most willing to help; even one with 17 RSS subscribers gives useful feedback.
- For trial users who won't buy (the boss won't release money), give them the software free only if they get on the phone for 30 minutes about why. You lose no sale, since they weren't going to buy; the cost is unpaid tech support.
- Limit it (e.g. one free seat per company) and end the program once you've had about 10 meetings and know how to convert their peers. Recipients also tell friends, which is free publicity.

## Pitch locally and use public feedback boards

- Pitch to a local startup, entrepreneur or user group. Even without great feedback, preparing the pitch forces you to rethink what's interesting about the product, which often reshuffles ideas on the home page and ads. A user group of potential customers is a friendly place to practice a sales pitch.
- UserVoice or GetSatisfaction (free or cheap) collect feedback and let people vote on each other's ideas. Allow anonymous responses so people aren't discouraged by account creation. At Smart Bear, hundreds of entries and comments and thousands of votes never required deleting anything, so moderation is easy.
- His closing advice: if you aren't getting data about lost sales daily, "you're in the dark." Even if you think you already know the answers, try it; being wrong could decide whether you build something people pay for.
