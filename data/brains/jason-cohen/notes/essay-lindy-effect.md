## Growth doubles by assumption but 10x has to be proven (the Lindy Effect applied to startups)

Jason borrows the Lindy Effect: for non-perishable things like technology, companies, books and ideas, the expected remaining lifespan is about twice the current age. Applied to a project with an unknown endpoint, "on average, you're halfway there." He ties this to Tom Cargill's line: "The first 90% of the code accounts for the first 90% of the development time. The remaining 10% of the code accounts for the other 90% of the time."

His startup version of the rule: "However large you've gotten, you can probably double it." If you have 10 customers, you can probably get another 10 the same way. Most companies that reach 10 customers never reach 2000.

- Doubling sounds plausible because you've done it once and can do it again, even faster.
- 10x or 100x is not obvious at all.
- "You get 2x by assumption but 10x you have to prove." If you're raising money, you actually have to prove it.

## Why 2x is easy but 10x needs innovation

The actions that got you the first 10 customers are probably not enough to get 100, though they can probably get another 10. His summary: "You can probably double your size by doing what you're already doing, but 10x will require innovation."

Examples of tactics that don't scale 10x:
- Scratching and clawing through your social network for the first 10 customers does not scale to 100.
- Converting customers one at a time through hour-long Zoom calls does not mean you can convert 100 no-sales-touch customers via Google ads.

The alternative to innovation is time and luck. Time means waiting a long time for the existing mechanism to keep working. Luck means nothing stops the slow, predictable growth: the channel doesn't saturate and get worse, no new competitors arise, market conditions don't make the product less desirable, and the economy doesn't slow. He calls this possible but unlikely in the fast-moving world of tech.

## Marketing channels have limited inventory

Jason's channel version of the argument:
- If one marketing channel got you to 1000 customers, it can surely produce another 1000.
- It's unlikely that channel holds 10x the inventory to reach 10,000 customers.
- You will need other channels to work, which is hard, and to reach 10x you'll probably need multiple new channels.
- Possible, but not with the confidence you have in 2x.

He links this to his separate essay on exponential growth and channel inventory.

## Hard ceilings on 10x

At some point 10x is strictly impossible.

- **High end:** market size limits and running out of acquisition channels. Facebook has 2B users and there aren't 20B humans. GoDaddy's customer growth rate was 13% per year since 2009 (per its SEC filing); at that rate it would take 18 years to 10x, assuming market size and conditions even allowed it.
- **Low end:** maybe there is no market, or the market doesn't want the product at a profitable price. You can squeeze out some early sales, but it can't get substantially bigger.

**2024 editor's note on GoDaddy:** annual revenue in 2014 was just under $1B and in 2024 is about $4B. It did not 10x, and did not even 5x, over ten years. GoDaddy also expanded into several additional product lines; had it done "what we've always done," growth would have been even lower.

## How to prove 10x: very fast growth

Jason's first answer to "how do you prove something we agreed is unlikely" is very fast growth.

- If you're doubling every six months, you clearly have line-of-sight to more than 2x. The trajectory shows intense market demand coupled with an ability to find and serve it.
- If the underlying market is large or growing, you have a good case that 10x is within reach, and innovation could potentially get 100x or 1000x.
- Companies that showed this growth at "interesting size" went on to 100x or 1000x thereafter.
- "Interesting size" does not mean going from 10 customers to 20 in six months and being proud of "rapid growth." Going from 1000 to 2000 in six months is more like it.
- Example: his company WP Engine experienced hyper-growth after hitting $1M ARR and reached 100x that six years later.

This is why investors, and founders who want to build enormous companies, fixate on hyper-growth. He says it's the only way to have even the potential of building something enormous. He adds that "exponential growth" is a misnomer for it.

## How to break the Lindy Effect: change something substantial

Jason's second answer is to change something substantial, which he thinks of as expanding into an adjacency.

- Keep one foot planted in the areas where you're already strong.
- Expand into something new and risky, with much higher upside than incremental improvement on the existing business.

He notes that not everyone wants to build a company serving a million customers or expand just for growth, and they shouldn't have to. Even so, the Lindy Effect helps you see what will be easy (doubling with what you already do) and what will require something new (10x and beyond).
