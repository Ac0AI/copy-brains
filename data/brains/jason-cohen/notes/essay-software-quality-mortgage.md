## The "quality mortgage" analogy for tech debt

Jason frames software quality as a mortgage. Shippable, salable software is the house you want today, and the debt is the quality and maintainability of the code. You can't build a near-bug-free application outright (he says that would take ten years), so you take out a mortgage to build v1.0 in six months.

- Early on, taking on this debt is the right move. When a company is new and still seeking its niche, "the most important thing is to get the software out the door fast, bugs and all."
- The emphasis eventually shifts from time-to-market to reducing tech support calls and not upsetting tens of thousands of existing users with a dud release.
- He calls the debt a "necessary evil in early software development," and says "just plan on paying it back."
- He notes a "fine line" separates debt as a lever for acceleration from debt as an insurmountable drag.

## How the debt accrues and what it costs

Jason says you eventually pay the debt back "with interest," and the interest is paid in bugs.

- Many of those bugs were preventable had there been time for unit tests, good design, manual tests and use-cases.
- Fixing surface bugs doesn't fix the underlying problems in the code. He compares this to the first years of a mortgage, where you pay interest without reducing principal.
- Fixing the most heinous bugs and moving on is still the right choice at first.
- Symptoms of a company deep in quality debt: years of squeezing in "must-have" features for big customers stretch code beyond its original design, core modules are "riddled with landmines," engineers are afraid to change anything even with unit tests, and the tacit assumptions of the two founders aren't obvious to the next ten hires.

## Why slow, incremental paydown often isn't enough

Jason describes the "pay principal slowly" approach and its limits.

- Examples of incremental paydown: refactor one file while adding a feature, add full unit test coverage for a handful of core methods, write a manual test plan for one complex dialog box. He calls this "all good."
- At that rate, though, it can still take ten years to pay back.
- Unlike a house, software keeps expanding with new features and gets reused beyond its original conception. Without fixing the underlying mess, and the process that produced it, you may never catch up. He likens this to an interest-only mortgage.
- At some point it becomes intolerable, and "it's time to pay down the principal in earnest," which requires allocating time for major rework.

## Winning the political fight to refactor

Jason says getting the right to refactor can be politically tough, especially with non-technical stakeholders. He gives rebuttals to three common objections.

- **"Clean-up is invisible to users; we need new features."** The bugs messy code produces are visible to users. Time spent fixing those bugs could have gone to features, and the longer you stay in quality debt, the longer each new feature takes.
- **"We don't have time for clean-up."** Fixing bugs generated by the problem instead of the problem itself is like "whacking weeds every weekend instead of pulling them up by the roots." He cites Ben Franklin's "an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure," which he renders as prevention being sixteen times more valuable than cure.
- **"Developers made this mess; they should fix it on their own time."** He argues that shipping fast and responding quickly to early-adopter feedback, even as the product morphed from its original conception, is what produced the current customers and revenue.

## Advice to CEOs on handling tech debt

- Don't finger-point; he says it "impedes resolution."
- Instead, challenge developers to reduce bug reports. This is easily measured, so you can track time versus results.
- He observes that developers prefer building new features to fixing bugs, so "if they're begging for time to fix bugs, it's serious."
- He adds that Martin Fowler made the same point in his "TechnicalDebt" piece.
