## Why "better" is never a valid business idea

- Jason says "we can do it better" is "always an invalid 'idea' for a business." Founders say it constantly, and he thinks they should be thinking something like it. But it isn't an idea until it says who the product is better for.
- "Better" is "always in the eye of the beholder," so he asks one question: "Better for whom?"
- His analogy: a home builder says "I looked at all the other houses in this city and thought, 'I can do better.'" Better for a 19-year-old bachelor who never cooks and has no money? A millionaire with a wine collection? A family of six? A family of rats?
- His conclusion: "there's no such thing as 'better.' There's only such thing as 'better for a specific market segment.'"

## Spotting the empty pitch in crowded categories

- The trigger for the essay was a founder in project-management (PM) tools who said they looked at the existing products and thought they could do it better.
- Jason notes why this is tempting: almost everyone dislikes some aspect of their PM tool, which may also explain why there are so many of them.
- A typical empty pitch is "All the things you love about Microsoft Project, but none of the things you hate." His reaction: "Uh-huh. Who's 'you?'"
- If your product were inferior in every way to even one incumbent, there would be no reason to build it. Saying it is better only restates that baseline and defines nothing.

## What a real "better for whom" pitch looks like (PM tool examples)

Jason says he doesn't know whether these are good ideas, but each is an actual proposal because it defines "better":

- **Accidental PMs:** Existing tools assume you already know project management. A tool for people thrust into a PM role who need help with nomenclature, process, best practices, and getting the most from the tool and team.
- **Large, complex projects:** Modern tools follow the 37signals and Lean Startup mantra of building the simplest possible thing, so they fail on large, complex, distributed, multi-user projects. Older tools understood that use case but are clunky install-only products where online collaboration, mobile and even the Mac are bolt-ons. The pitch is a tool for large, complex projects with modern technology requirements.
- **iPad-first:** Existing tools were built in the installed or web-based eras. A tool built from the start for the iPad is best for managing projects from meetings and on the go.
- **Open, large-scale projects:** Existing tools are built for a small, fixed set of workers. A tool for distributed, open projects with thousands of members that still need central management.
- **Constantly changing requirements:** Existing tools assume tasks, goals and projects are fairly fixed. A tool for open-ended projects where requirements change constantly and the tool changes with them, and even measures the change.

## Defining the audience tells you what to do next

Once you can say who it's better for, the next steps in the business follow:

- **Marketing:** You can run targeted advertisements, go to specific conferences, and even cold-call people on LinkedIn.
- **Messaging:** You know what words to put on your homepage and what to highlight to differentiate from competitors.
- **Product:** You know which features will especially tickle that audience's fancy.
- He frames this as the usual benefits of identifying your ideal customer, applied to the product rather than to the customer's identity.

## Using "better for whom" at the ideation stage

- If you're still working out the idea, the answer becomes the basis for customer development.
- It tells you where to find potential customers to call.
- It also tells you what you're verifying once you have them on the phone.
- His closing test: "Surely your product is 'better' than the rest... for someone. Whom?"
