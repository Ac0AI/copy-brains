## Useful models can be wrong about how the world works

Jason argues that a model can be useful (it makes accurate predictions) while being incorrect (its explanation of why is false). His anchor example is the Mayans:

- Their cosmology was wrong: Earth at the center, thirteen tiers of heaven, nine levels of underworld, eclipses caused by spiritual beings consuming the sun or moon.
- Their priests still predicted eclipses with impressive accuracy. They knew eclipses happen roughly every 173 days, adjusted by a 405-month cycle and smaller corrections, and they refined the model over centuries.
- In his words, "their theory yielded accurate predictions, even though the theory's explanation of why was entirely incorrect."

He uses this to restate the bromide "All models are wrong, but some models are useful": the Mayan model was wrong in describing the universe and useful in predicting eclipses.

## Quantum mechanics shows the same tension in modern physics

Jason uses Richard Feynman's view that "nobody understands quantum mechanics" to show that even modern physics wrestles with useful versus true.

- Feynman won a Nobel Prize for a model of how particles interact. It says all possible interactions happen simultaneously, each weighted by probability, and a measurement resolves one of them.
- Einstein ("God does not play dice") and Schrödinger both found this absurd. Einstein built the EPR paradox and Schrödinger built the cat thought experiment to show QM must be only a sophisticated Mayan-style calculation.
- Those experiments have been run many times over decades, and the model has been correct every time.
- Schrödinger said of the Copenhagen interpretation: "I don't like it, and I'm sorry I ever had anything to do with it."

The point he draws is that usefulness is judged by predictions holding up, not by whether the explanation feels sensible.

## Business models are far weaker than physics models

Jason lists the models used for companies and markets: economics, management theory, strategy theory, product management (pain-points, delighters, JTBD, willingness-to-pay) and startup theory.

- They work some of the time and explain the past retroactively, but often fail to predict the future.
- A company is not an experiment with controlled variables and many trial runs, so these are arguably not "theories" at all.
- He says even the term "expected value" is a fallacy in this setting.
- He asks whether these models are like the Mayans (occasionally useful, not representative) or like QM (representative but noisy because of faulty or missing inputs). He also points to authors more interested in selling consulting off their HBR articles and books than in admitting the limits of simple models in an irreducibly complex world.

## The core test: does the model fit messy reality or a slide?

Jason's stated filter for any framework: "Does the framework attempt to model the messy real world, or does it seem like a pretty fantasy that looks nice in presentations, HBR articles, and consultants' sales materials?"

- He trusts diagrams that are imbalanced, asymmetrical or even ugly, because they are probably trying to capture the truth rather than please the eye.
- His conclusion: "The most useful are the ones that genuinely attempt to model the real, complex, ugly, asymmetric world, not the ones made to look pretty on brochures for consulting services."

## Red flags that a framework is a pretty fantasy

Jason lists concrete tells:

- **Perfect symmetry in a model of people.** He immediately disbelieves any human framework in a symmetric diagram with identical numbers of bullets and sub-categories. His example is the DISC personality assessment: four categories, each with exactly six subcategories, each with identical components. "No, that's never how it is with people."
- **Rotational symmetry as the goal.** A model seems to be built so the picture has 90 degree symmetry rather than to describe reality. He cites a marketing "periodic table of elements" graphic as an example.
- **Exactly 10 items on a list.** That is how many fingers we have, not how many things belong on the list.
- **People treated as fungible worker-units.** A model of organizations with predictable responses to stimuli, built by "a professor who has never managed a team beyond six sycophantic grad students."
- **One rubric for everyone.** Strategy as 2x2s and symmetric diagrams with the same questions for all companies, stages, industries and markets, instead of grappling with interacting, dynamic, partly unquantifiable systems (markets, customers, competitors, alternatives, employees, technology, products, global trends).

## What a realistic model looks like

Jason's examples of models he trusts more, and how he builds his own:

- **Craigslist's category structure.** It has "however many" categories containing "however many" subcategories, which is how human activity actually organizes.
- **Plutchik's feelings wheel (1980).** He calls it quite useful for helping patients identify feelings. It is somewhat suspicious because every second-level category has exactly two third-level emotions, but the first-level categories vary in number.
- **Wardley maps.** They show which activities support growth and differentiation versus which are necessary but commoditized, so teams can invest appropriately.
- **Roger Martin's strategy slide.** It has "five boxes," but the instructions use however many questions are useful, and the order of operations is non-linear.
- **His own frameworks.** His lists on what goes into a great strategy and on deciding whether an investment is worthwhile contain however many items make sense. His analysis of why startups fail has imbalanced categories and unequal bullet counts. His system for product-market fit has steps of varying length and detail, and gives counter-examples to show it is "an interesting guide but not a law."
