## The metric you pick depends on stage, circumstance and goals, not on a universal ranking

Jason Cohen's essay answers "what is the single most important SaaS metric?" by arguing that the question itself should be un-asked. He makes a strong case for seven different metrics, then says all of them are true "for some companies, at some times, in some stages, in some industries, in some markets." His larger point is that "companies are more complex than 'a single most important metric.'" A metric is a tool in service of your goals, timeline, size, circumstance and even philosophy, "not a master that you thoughtlessly obey."

## Retention and top-line growth: why each can be called the most important

**Retention.** Jason argues cancellations signal a lack of Product/Market Fit, whatever the cause (price, features, severity of need, duration of need). If that can't be fixed, the business is a failure even with otherwise stellar metrics, because customers are rejecting the product.
- At scale (past Product/Market Fit, into tens of millions in revenue), cancellation grows with total customers, so it scales exponentially.
- New-customer growth scales only linearly with sales and marketing cost.
- So cancellation necessarily and always outgrows inbound activity, and growth stops.
- Retention matters at every stage.

**Top-line growth.** High revenue growth implies many other things are going well, even unmeasurable ones: Product/Market Fit, ability to reach and retain customers, and a large addressable market.
- Tech market dynamics follow a "winner take all" power law, and the only way to be the biggest is to grow the fastest.
- Fast growth proves every other part of the company is healthy, or at least healthy enough not to be fatal.
- High-growth companies keep all options open: stay independent, raise money, or sell.
- It is also the biggest determinant of equity value, because it maximizes both revenue and the revenue multiple.

## Net-profit growth and sales efficiency: the counterweights to growth-at-all-costs

**Net-profit growth.** Jason says the era when equity value was determined almost entirely by top-line growth "died in 2015." Public and private markets now reward profitable growth.
- Profitable growth means building sustainable, value-creating companies, not companies that "grow like a virus," without purpose and possibly killing the host.
- In his words, unicorns are dying "not for lack of revenue, but for lack of profit."

**Sales efficiency.** If acquiring new revenue is expensive, the product is being stuffed into the market rather than embraced by it. That means you are growing artificially and haven't found real Product/Market Fit and longevity.
- Merely replacing natural cancellation becomes too expensive, which caps your maximum size.
- Too much of a customer's lifetime revenue is spent on costs before they write the first check, so long-run profit is hard.
- It suggests you are "buying customers" rather than building a product people organically want, enjoy and talk about, which questions the product's fundamental value.

## LTV and Rule of 40: combined metrics that avoid optimizing one number at a time

**LTV.** Looking at component metrics individually never gives a complete picture.
- High growth fails if margins are too low for a profitable business at scale.
- High retention fails if MRR is so low that meaningful revenue needs too many customers.
- Optimizing one number in LTV often moves the others the wrong way. Example: raising price increases MRR but also increases cancellations because people can't afford it.
- Only combining the metrics gives a true picture of the business model's health.
- To critics who say LTV isn't valid because the future is unpredictable, he replies that this is "exactly why it's so important to try."

**Rule of 40.** The sum of year-over-year revenue growth rate and bottom-line profit margin.
- A company either needs to grow fast (so being unprofitable now creates long-term value) or be very profitable (value is cash coming out of the business over many years).
- He calls it the "super-metric" covering both the growth and profit phases of a successful company's life.

## NPS: customer satisfaction as the root of other metrics

Jason presents NPS as the ultimate measure of value creation, which feeds every other important metric.
- **Growth:** only high-NPS customers spread word of mouth, the cheapest form of marketing and one of the few that keeps scaling with the business.
- **Retention:** high NPS implies customers stay.
- **Intangibles:** brand quality, Product relevancy, and customers who love you even when you screw up.
- **Scope:** it covers Product Management, Tech Support and every other function.
- His framing: "The customer is the fundamental unit of the company, and NPS measures customer health."

## How to choose: focus on what matters right now, then simplify

Jason says focusing on one thing is valuable because it creates focus, especially in a young company that doesn't have time to maximize multiple goals. Larger companies like WP Engine can have three or four, "but still not many."

His process:
- Figure out what is most important right now.
- Find what's on fire.
- Identify what matters most for reaching the company's next milestone.
- Lean into what is working well.
- Then simplify and clarify the few goals and metrics the entire company should align on.
