## Assuming you know your market blocks the learning that kills bad assumptions

Jason argues that expertise hinders learning, and that insight comes from curiosity, not certainty. The worst case is the self-declared "expert," who is least likely to challenge his own assumptions. He says the goal is to leverage expertise without being blinded by it.

## Test the "I know my customers" claim by asking them

At a startup incubator, a founder dismissed several mentors' pricing advice with "I know our industry, I know how our customers think."

- Jason asked whether the last dozen prospects had all said "Heck yes" to her pricing. She hadn't asked them, because "I know what they're going to say."
- He did not argue. He asked her to include the pricing scheme in her pitch at the next week's convention, since she was sure it would be well received.
- She expected an "I told you so" moment, and so did he, because she was the expert.
- She was wrong. She emailed: "In the past week of quick phone calls to vendors, I've learned more about this market than I did in the past year."
- Her earlier "lean" discovery had been hour-long calls, relationship-building meetings and vague answers to poorly formed questions.
- His takeaway: "whenever an assumption is kicked out from under you, that's when you learn the most." Betting on an assumption that is actually tested is what exposes it.

## Resist pitching in customer calls

Jason says the defect is human nature: thinking we know more than we do, and so missing chances to learn. He still struggles with it.

- In every customer call he consciously restrains himself from pitching.
- He asks questions instead and tries to understand what customers mean, rather than mapping their words onto what he wants them to say.
- Experts' "laws" about what makes their market unique are largely based on feeling, not fact.

## Markets change faster than your experience

Fifteen years of experience in a sector is not a defense, and Jason applied this to himself too: "I built a company and forged dozens of customer relationships in the software development tools sector; I know exactly how to sell into that market." He gives several reasons it fails.

- You can't rely on five-year-old information about customers, even in stodgy big-company markets and especially in mass-market consumer ones.
- Non-technical people now use iPhones and Facebook. Industries built on controlling process and information, such as real estate and book publishing, are now out of control.
- Methods of reaching consumers change yearly, for example SEO or AdWords strategy in 2003 versus 2010.
- Expectations also shift constantly: what software should do, what it should integrate with, whether data can live on someone else's server, whether it must work from a cellphone.
- Old experience can cloud judgment. A competitor not shackled by old ideas can beat you with innovations they achieve because of that freedom.
- Jason's 2024 editor's note on AdWords: in 2003 at Smart Bear he was often the only bidder on a keyword at $0.05/click, which let him bootstrap the company with AdWords. By 2010, at WP Engine, AdWords was too expensive to start with anything but long-tail phrases. It became a staple only once the company could afford it, and was still one of the lowest-ROI marketing activities. By 2024 many bootstrappers call advertising prohibitively expensive, partly from inexperience with paid ads, but also because AdWords ROI keeps worsening.

## Selling a different product into the same industry is a different market

Experience selling one product to a market doesn't transfer automatically to another product in that market. Jason's example is an entrepreneur who had sold a standard medical device to doctors for years and had an idea for practice-management software.

- He assumed his Rolodex let him line up ten alpha testers before writing code, but he hadn't done it.
- He didn't know whether doctors could install new software, since he had sold hardware.
- He had never asked whether the software could depend on an internet connection inside a secured hospital.
- He assumed that because doctors pay a lot for the device, software would fetch a lot too. Jason doubted that front-office software budgets relate to device budgets.
- Zero people had said they wanted this software, even for free, and that didn't worry him.
- Jason's point: you aren't starting from scratch, but you need an attitude of re-learning and questioning everything.

## How to use expertise without being blinded by it

Jason says combining special knowledge with an open mind could be unbeatable. It won't happen unless you force it.

- Set your ego aside and explore anew with a "child-like" mind.
- Use your Rolodex to set up meetings and sales calls, but don't assume you know what prospects will say.
- Use experience to generate plausible theories, not to make decisions.
- Some prior experience is invaluable, an unfair advantage worth leveraging, but the old assumptions in it should be treated as hypotheses to test.
- His closing line: "After all, you're the expert."
