## Early adopters are the only customers a tiny company can win first

Jason's core claim is that a pre-traction company should not try to look big. His next sale will not be "a 1000-seat order from Lockheed Martin", and he tells founders to repeat that line. He says he has sold software to every size of company, from indie hackers to IBM to Lockheed Martin.

- Today's product is a "shaky version one-dot-oh" with undiscovered bugs.
- It is "missing 90% of the features" big companies require.
- It has no case studies, no proper manual, no ROI model and no large, referenceable customer.
- That makes it a mismatch for big enterprises, but a perfect match for Early Adopters.

## Who Early Adopters are and why they buy from a small company

Early Adopters want to live on the bleeding edge.

- They like new technology even if it is buggy, and don't mind "a hundred bugs so long as they get fixed fast".
- They like small companies where they have a personal relationship with the founders, get lots of attention, and see their ideas implemented quickly.
- They want to be involved in the process.
- They take the risk because new technology can help them beat competitors, through features only they have, distribution channels they can win in, or another competitive advantage.
- Most companies won't take risks for any reason, so Early Adopters are a winning niche for founders willing to serve them.

Jason's example is Tom, a customer. At Smart Bear he had ten or twenty Early Adopters like him before the product was stable and rich enough to attract bigger companies.

## Why Early Adopters are what a young company needs

This is the stage when the company needs help building the right product. It doesn't need people who download, get discouraged and never call back. It needs "a chatty Cathy who wants to dive in and help out."

His conclusion is to define the ideal customer persona tightly around the people who love the company as it is today, because that is where it will succeed.

## The mistake: acting like a big company in public

Jason made this mistake himself before he had a single customer. He believed he had to look like a "real company":

- culture-neutral language
- stock photos of cheerful coworkers around a laptop
- standard marketing-speak

His original About page said Smart Bear was "the leading provider of enterprise version control data-mining tools" whose Code Historian software was used "for risk-analysis, root-cause discovery, and software development decision-support." He admits he wasn't sure what "leading provider" or "data mining" even meant there.

He says the fear that looking small loses sales is backwards: the big-company persona alienates the best customers, the Early Adopters.

## What the small-company persona looks like

His replacement About copy was: "Hi, I'm Jason and I built an inexpensive tool for visualizing what's in your version control system. It's useful for answering questions like 'When was the last time we changed this file?' Check it out and tell me what sucks!"

The persona should signal to Early Adopters: a cool new company with a fresh product and attitude, a product rough around the edges but ripe for feedback and collaboration, a company that is small today but thinking big. His checklist:

- **Show you understand her pain.** Don't use content-free phrases like "Provide the ability to quickly and easily do XYZ so you can go back to accomplishing high-value tasks."
- **Come across as a passionate small team that wants to make a difference,** not a big, established, safe company.
- **Display your phone number and Twitter account on the home page** instead of hiding behind "Contact Us" forms.
- **Promote real things:** forums, a blog, and a weekly all-customer virtual meeting where everyone gives feedback. Don't promote features and benefits you haven't implemented yet.
- **Be specific and meaningful, be human, be yourself.** His closing line: "Stop hiding."
