## Meaningless marketing language costs you customers

Jason argues that vague corporate marketing language (buzzwords, "solutions," "a leading provider of") signals nothing and pushes readers away.

- His test: does "a leading provider of" make you think "Awesome, I found the leader!"? It doesn't. At best you skim past an "overused phrase, devoid of meaning." At worst you lose interest and click "Back."
- His example is Omniture's description: "a leading provider of online business optimization software, enabling customers to manage and enhance online, offline and multi-channel business initiatives." It turns out they do web analytics.
- His fix: just say "web analytics," then spend the remaining words on how you differ from other web analytics companies.

## The word "solution" is a red flag

Jason singles out "Solution" as perhaps the worst offender.

- Many sites make visitors choose between "Products" and "Solutions" in the main menu, and a visitor can't tell which to pick. He asks what a "solution" is and how it differs from a product.
- His favorite example is AT Systems, whose motto, "The system is the solution," is printed on the side of an armored truck. The company moves money around, and the motto says nothing about that.
- The company name plus a motto still leaves the question "What do they do?" unanswered. That is the failure.

## Why marketing teams write meaningless phrases

He offers three reasons and rejects all of them as valid.

- **Fear.** Generic, widely-used phrases don't offend and don't invite lawsuits, since "it's hard to sue over meaningless words." That may work at a big company, but "in a startup you can't afford to be bland and wishy-washy."
- **Laziness.** "A leading provider of business solutions" is easier than nailing your message. Doing it right means knowing your customer, boiling the company down to its essentials, and being succinct, evocative and specific. "It's hard! ... It's easier not to."
- **Incompetence.** If you don't know what sets your product apart, can't articulate the niche you own, or can't describe your perfect customer, you fall back on generic phrases.

## The test: say it in a few specific words

- His rule: "If you can't articulate your product in a few, choice, specific, words, your potential customers won't get it either."
- A company description should say what the product is, for whom, and how it differs, in concrete terms.
- A weak description leaves a reader unable to guess what the company does. He uses the webMethods boilerplate as the "tragic example": "business integration software to integrate, assemble and optimize available IT assets to drive business process productivity," plus "innovative, enterprise-class" and "tangible business value."
- His closing instruction: "Take a scalpel to your marketing content. Make every word count."
