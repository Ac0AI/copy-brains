## Diagnosing stalled growth: work through the questions in order

Jason has built four companies (two unicorns, WP Engine being one; the earlier ones included a bootstrapped company, and one was VC-funded) and invested in about 60 startups. He says growth usually doesn't stop suddenly. It "slowly gets slower," like running through mud: lots of work, less impact.

He diagnoses it with a series of questions, asked in a fixed order. If an earlier one is broken, fixing a later one won't help enough. The order:

1. Are customers leaving (logo churn)?
2. Is pricing and positioning right?
3. Are existing customers growing (NRR)?
4. Are marketing channels saturated?
5. Do you actually need to grow?

He notes the same list also works as a "how do I grow more" checklist, not only a stall diagnosis. The rules hold across B2B and consumer because they come from the mechanics of finance, though the levers differ by segment.

## Churn: why cancellations cap your size, and how to find the real reason

**Why it comes first**
- Once a customer leaves, nothing can save them or grow their revenue.
- Churn often travels with negative reviews that hurt growth further.
- Emotionally: the customer found you, didn't bounce off the homepage, survived the pricing page, had the budget, bought and onboarded, then left. "Wait a minute, that's terrible."

**The ceiling math**
- Cancellations grow automatically with your customer count; marketing does not. Triple your customers overnight and ads/SEO still deliver the same new leads, but cancellations triple.
- Maximum size = new customers per month ÷ monthly cancellation rate. Example: 100 new customers a month at 5% churn is 100 ÷ 0.05 = 2,000. The company never exceeds 2,000, and growth slows sharply as it approaches that number.
- The same math works with dollars instead of customers. He uses customer counts because "we will never have more than 2,000 customers" feels visceral.
- Vague rules like "anything above 3% a month is terrible" get argued about; the ceiling number is concrete.
- For SMB, 2% monthly cancellation is good, so he'd check whether there's still much to gain.

**Getting the real reason**
- Dropdown reasons are mostly noise. At Smart Bear, one option got picked a lot; he realized it was the first on the list. After randomizing the order, all options were picked equally.
- Ask open-ended questions, and ask "What made you cancel?" rather than "Why did you cancel?" He took this from a Groove case study: the same email went from 10% to 20% usable responses.
- "Too expensive" is never the real reason. The customer already saw the pricing page and chose to buy. Something else failed: an unmet promise, a missing integration (for example, it only works with Jira and they use Linear).
- "Project ended" isn't a dead end either. A more successful product might have kept the project alive, or you may have picked a target segment whose projects end (small businesses, consumers).
- He dislikes the idea of a single root cause. Complex systems have several interlocking causes. His analogy is cause of death: stopped breathing, car crash, passed out, undiagnosed diabetes.
- Talk to customers who are in trouble but haven't canceled yet. Signals include never uploading data, calling support too much or too little, or not logging in. Guessing at signals is fine without much data.
- Good customers share traits with bad ones. Look for what good customers have that bad customers don't.
- If unsure where to start, work on onboarding. Most cancellation happens early, and small onboarding changes have big effects. In his YouTube retention analogy, about 50% drop in the first 30 seconds; moving that to 55% could lift the final 20% retained to roughly 25%.
- AI (LLMs) is good at themes in cancellation feedback but weak at actionable details, because it averages. He asks it to pull every specific detail under each theme with the customer who said it, then reads them himself.

## Pricing and positioning

- Quoting Patrick Campbell's data on 4,200 startups: "Your prices are way too low because you just guessed and you haven't changed them." Often prices were copied from competitors or tweaked arbitrarily.
- The textbook demand curve (raise price, demand falls) often fails in practice. Often you raise prices and signups don't change, or go up, because pricing selects the market.
- Larger companies (his example: 1,000 employees, $400M revenue) see a $2 or $100 a month product and assume it isn't good enough, with weak support and governance. A higher price puts you in their consideration set. The real curve is more like a mesa than a line.
- Example: a founder selling to enterprise and government charged $300 per year and got 1-2 signups a week. Jason had him switch to $300 per month (12x). Still 1-2 signups a week. His advice: raise prices again.
- Price is also structure and positioning (per site, per usage, per seat), not just the number.

**The "Double Down" positioning story**
- Pitch 1: "cut your AdWords cost in half." For a customer spending $40K a month, they save $20K and would pay maybe $5K.
- Pitch 2: "double your leads for the same money." The customer already accepts spending $40K for that volume of leads at the same cost per lead, so doubling the leads is worth up to $40K.
- Same product, 8x the price ($40K vs $5K). The CMO can also tell the CEO "I increased the growth rate" rather than "I saved money."
- Lesson: sell more of what the company values (growth, retention, competitiveness) rather than saving time or money, which caps the price.
- Caveats: moving up-market brings new demands (SOC 2, governance, integrations, professional services), and may not fit your culture (his example: Buffer is for "the little people"). Pricing is a strategy decision, not a knob.

## Net revenue retention and expansion

- Cancellations always overtake direct marketing, so a way to offset them is existing customers paying more through upgrades, usage or seats. NRR is the revenue from today's customers one year later, and can end above 100% if upgrades exceed cancellations and downgrades.
- NRR can mislead. If logo churn is high, too few customers remain to upgrade. Also percentages are asymmetric: a 20% loss needs a 25% gain to recover (100 to 80 needs 25% to return to 100; a 20% gain only reaches 96).
- Public SaaS evidence: of over 100 public SaaS companies, about two have NRR below 100%, and those have poor financials. Median NRR at IPO is 119%.
- To raise NRR, the customer should feel they get more value as the price rises ("the price doubles, but I'm getting five times the value"). Measure the value the customer gets, not only usage. Use proxy or qualitative measures if no number exists. His framing: "create more value for the customer and then split that with them."
- Ask whether a change is good for the customer, not only for you. Don't let investors push policies that aren't.
- If a segment doesn't expand (consumer, SMB), other levers exist: a second product sold to the same customers (his comparison: AG1's added supplements), or existing customers referring new ones. Referrals also offset exponentially growing cancellations.

## Channel saturation and the "elephant curve"

- Marketing channels saturate and then decline. He calls the pattern the "elephant curve": an S-curve with a sagging "butt" rather than a flat top. Audiences have seen your message, and channels themselves shrink (magazines, conferences). It affects Adwords, Facebook ads, SEO and affiliates. AI's effect on channels is unknown.
- Key question: "Do you know right now which channels are saturated and which aren't?" If not, "flogging" the ads or SEO team harder or adding one feature won't fix it.
- Adding adjacent channels (SEO and ads) can work, but in his experience a product that sells well direct often does poorly in SEO or social, and vice versa.
- More creative options:
  - Constant Contact held in-person workshops in cities teaching small businesses email marketing, using power users who were also agencies. It restarted growth despite the $20-a-month price.
  - HubSpot tested selling through agencies; about 50% of revenue after four or five years.
  - WP Engine sells many sites through agencies that build WordPress sites.
  - A new product or new market that plants one foot in an existing strength or asset and the other in a risky new area (he has a written framework for this).
- When channels are full, he says it forces more drastic bets, since the marketing department can no longer be the answer.

## Do you need to grow? Plus other views

**Whether to grow**
- "If you're not growing, you're dying" may be investor rhetoric. Bootstrapped companies (his example: 37signals) can choose profit over revenue growth, and growth might mean a bigger organization or a market segment you don't want.
- He still thinks the idea applies to people. Ask whether you and your team are fulfilled doing the same thing every day, and if you're stuck, something dramatic (leaving, selling, changing the company) may be needed.
- He rejects growth at all costs: grow as much as possible within things you're proud of. Early on you can be looser, then tighten standards as you can afford to.
- On decisions under uncertainty (quit, sell, move), he says probability and expected value don't help, because the probabilities are unknowable and it's one life. This is the topic of a book he'd like to write next.

**Common thread**
- Customers must actually get value: the product promises the right thing, delivers it, customers onboard, they realize they're getting it, and you measure it. Many failures are targeting the wrong customer or segment, or poor positioning, rather than lack of value.

**A/B testing**
- His contrarian view: A/B testing "doesn't work very well and it doesn't work on most things." It can't test strategy or vision. On details, most "wins" are false positives, because the true effect is rare, so even a 95%-accurate tool produces more false positives than real wins. Teams stack winners for a year and see no change in conversion rates.
- He cites the Shopify CTO's account of holdout groups: roughly a third of effects disappear. Unless you're very sophisticated, "you're the sucker at the table."
