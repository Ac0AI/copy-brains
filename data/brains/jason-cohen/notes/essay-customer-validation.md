## Ten buyers, not opinions, are the validation test

Jason's core rule: if you cannot find ten people who say "If you build this, I'll give you $X," your company is "bullshit." He saw hundreds of pitches at the Capital Factory incubator in Austin, Texas, and almost none had found even ten people willing to pay. Founders were ready to quit jobs, burn savings, risk their reputation and work 70-hour weeks without that evidence.
- He wants hard evidence that customers exist in your "future light cone," not mantras repeated at Lean Startup meetings.
- He says founders resist because they fear being proved wrong, especially after telling friends and family.
- He points to his own guides on how to find customers to interview and how to run customer interviews.

## "I'm scratching my own itch" is a starting spark, not validation

Jason rejects the idea that being your own target customer means you already know what to build.
- By definition, a startup founder is not the typical customer. A typical customer rarely has the observation, creativity, persuasion and passion needed to spot monetizable pain, invent a product, recruit free help and quit a job.
- All three of his companies started by scratching his own itch, "but it's only that--the start." In his words: "the spark of inspiration, not the strategy. It's the grain of sand tickling the oyster, not the pearl."
- He cites Evan Williams: be a user of your own product, "but don't trick yourself into thinking you are your user."
- He challenges readers to find one founder of a real business who says "I'm the customer" is the only market validation needed.

## "There are millions of customers" makes ten easy to find

Jason counters the argument that ten opinions don't matter when the market is huge.
- If there are millions of potential customers, finding ten who say they'll buy is trivial. If you can't find ten, either the millions don't exist or they aren't interested in you.
- Businesses start with one customer, then ten, then a hundred, then a thousand. "But most don't get past ten."
- If you haven't got ten people to even say they'll buy, he asks where the hubris comes from to claim thousands actually will.

## You don't need a product to sell the concept

Jason rejects "my customers can't understand mock-ups, so I have to build it first."
- If you can't describe the concept to someone in your target market in 30 seconds at a cocktail party, it is too complex or you don't understand it yourself.
- WP Engine example: he got thirty people to say they'd pay $50/mo before he had a company name, website, product, co-founder or employee. He notes he had earlier ideas that proved to be "crap," so it wasn't just easy for him.
- Your first customers are early adopters who are fine with alpha software. If you can't find a few and get them excited, maybe the product isn't exciting.

## A great product does not sell itself, so attack the riskiest part first

Jason argues against "I suck at sales/marketing, so I'll build something so compelling it sells itself."
- His guess for the top Twitter clients (150+ existed in 2010): about 80% are decent software that works, about 5% earn any revenue, and under 1% earn enough after hosting and marketing to let the owner skip a day job.
- Conclusion: if the goal is a business rather than a hobby, "building charming, novel software isn't enough."
- Founders default to coding because they love it. He says to do the opposite: attack the part you are least sure of and least qualified for.
- He frames it as project risk management. In a big software project you tackle the high-risk, ill-defined work first. For a startup the "high-risk unknown" is everything that isn't code: finding customers and getting them to open their wallets.

## Whose opinion counts: only people willing to pay

Jason dismisses feedback from friends, family and dentists.
- Non-entrepreneurs don't understand product/market fit, tight budgets or how customers actually spend time. As he puts it, "Your mother thinks you're smart and good-looking, but that doesn't mean I do."
- Even real entrepreneurs matter only slightly. They may lack expertise in your domain, hold outdated notions, be biased against certain ideas or technology, and carry baggage from good and bad experiences shaped by timing and luck.
- "The only thing that matters is that people are willing to give you money!"
- Example: experts could argue that buying shoes online makes no sense, but while people give Zappos $1 billion per year, it doesn't matter.
- "When ten people say they'll give you money, that's the only validation that counts."
