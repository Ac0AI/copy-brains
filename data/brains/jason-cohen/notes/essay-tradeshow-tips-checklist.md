## Why Jason attends tradeshows: set a goal first

Jason treats tradeshows as expensive: a booth runs $2k-$20k, travel about $1,000/day, plus a week at the show and weeks of prep. Because of that cost he insists on one primary goal, which drives every other decision and tells you afterward whether the show was worth repeating. Example goals he lists:
- Make a sale on the floor
- Get at least 20 genuine prospects
- Talk with 10 industry leaders
- Find 10 good recruiting prospects
- Find 3 serious investors
- Ask potential customers 3 specific things (market research)

His experience: running booths at dozens of shows. He also says "Quality not quantity": six solid conversations with likely buyers beat giving away 200 pieces of branded swag to people who won't remember you.

## Planning 3-6 months out: presentation, message, booth, print, staffing

- **Always schedule a vendor presentation.** Even 20 people for 45 minutes is worth far more than 100 people at the booth for 5-60 seconds. He often gets a few sales from the talk alone.
- **Pick one short main message.** You get about 3 seconds to make someone stop, like a home page. The goal is to make people stop, not explain everything. Boil it to one short sentence.
- **Pick the booth early; location matters.** Good spots: next to the bathroom (everyone passes it), near the front doors, nearer the room center than the ends, and aisle corners (more traffic, and your stuff can spill over the edge).
- **Start print work early.** He has "never" gotten what he wanted from a print shop on the first try. Screen colors and Pantone swatches don't match the print. Ask for the techie in the back (who knows InDesign, DPI, CMYK, vector vs. raster), not the counter salesperson.
- **Staff at least 3 people.** Two at the booth (busy times, restocking, breaks) and one roaming and attending meetings.
- **Book all travel early** for cheaper fares and no sold-out surprises.
- **Make the booth different.** Most booths have a logo, banner, a "clever" phrase and adjectives like "fast" and "scalable." Do something different; it needn't be amazing.
- **Order shirts and swag early** since customization takes time. At least have a "tradeshow shirt."

## One month out: postcards, email, meetings, promotion, the box

- **Postcard mailers work.** Offer something cool or expensive at the booth, redeemable only by bringing the postcard. Attendees keep it handy (seeing your marketing repeatedly), seek you out, and you collect their name, company and address for follow-up. Put the booth number on it.
- **Emails probably work** because you can put the show's name in the subject line.
- **Set meetings proactively.** Aim for at least 5 per day, using clearly non-spam subjects like "At [Tradeshow]: Can we chat for 5 minutes?" Targets:
  - Magazine editors (online and offline). He says he has "been published in every major programming magazine," almost all traced to talking with editors at tradeshows.
  - Bloggers you like
  - Existing customers
  - Prospects trialing your product
  - Vendors
  - Competitors
  - Potential partners
  - Dinner or drinks after the show also works.
- **Promote the show:** add the show info and booth number to everyone's email signature, plus any giveaway.
- **Bring a cheap plastic "box of everything"** (he says you'll use an unpredictable subset every time): multiple pens, Sharpie, Scotch and masking tape, extension cord, plug bar, post-its, rubber bands, tiny stapler, highlighter, paper clips, scissors, all-in-one tool, medicine (Tylenol, Advil, Motrin, DayQuil), zip-ties, generic business cards.
- **Wear comfortable shoes.** You stand far longer than usual. Use floor pads or pay the venue to pad your booth carpet.

## At the booth: how to engage people

- **Ask questions instead of pitching.** Be genuinely interested in what they do. They'll then ask for your pitch as reciprocation, which gives you permission to sell and a listening audience.
- **Don't open with "how are you doing?"** Open with something specific you offer; even "Are you interested in [thing you do]" is better, though still weak.
- **Use it as market research.** Prepare 3-5 questions to ask passersby. No need to record carefully: "the big trends will be obvious and the rest is noise."
- **A/B test your pick-up line** all show long, like a landing page. After the 100th pitch you'll know what gets people to stop, laugh, or say "tell me more," and he says to put that on your home page.
- **Stand, don't sit.** Sitting looks like you don't want to be there, and the head-height difference is off-putting.
- **Get into the aisle** rather than standing behind the table; one person inside the booth, another in the aisle directing people in. During high traffic, being a barrier forces people to squeeze past, giving a chance to engage. He links the "guy in a bear suit" lesson.
- **Quality of staff:** sales people aren't enough. Attendees want to "geek out" with peers, so bring people who don't normally go on sales calls.
- **Note the jokes.** People will make fun of you; if no one does, they may not understand what you do.

## Booth attractions: demos, video, food, raffles, parties

- **Moving pictures:** use a big monitor or, better, a bright projector with a demo movie playing, not a static screenshot or a stuck demo. At Smart Bear other vendors said "OMG we have to do that next year."
- **Always be able to demo.** "Nothing is more sticky than a live demo," more than swag, brochures, phrases or raffles. Prefer a projector so passersby get hooked; at least a large monitor, "not your laptop screen."
- **Free food** works better than most freebies; the more "real" the better (cookies over candy). Put it at the center of the booth so people must talk to take it.
- **Raffle something.** Not a sales tool, but it draws a crowd, and crowds make others think the booth is interesting. Some people returned the next day saying they hadn't stopped earlier because the booth was swamped. Require contact info to enter (form, badge scan, business card); the leads are weakly qualified but better than nothing.
- **Host your own happy hour.** Rent a room at or near the venue with wine, beer and basic food; invite people at the show and in pre-show mailers. It is cheaper than you'd think, and people are relaxed and willing to talk about your product to reciprocate.
- **Don't depend on the internet.** Show internet is spotty; demos and note-taking must work offline.

## Capturing leads and networking during the show

- **Take names, not brochure handouts.** Attendees get stuffed with paper that gets lost. Scan badges or take business cards and mail something afterward so it's waiting on their desk without tradeshow distraction.
- **Write notes on their business card** (you'll talk to hundreds and forget). If the card can't take notes, stick a post-it to it.
- **Add contacts on LinkedIn every night.** Most accept if you add them the same day and mention the conference.
- **Walk the floor and talk to every vendor.** Commiserate about the show, look for ways to work together (usually nothing results, but they remember you), skip their salespeople and meet the founder.

## After the show: follow up and mine what you learned

- **Follow up.** He estimates a 99% chance attendees have forgotten you, even if they took swag or a brochure. Remind them and take them up on offers of a demo, trial, or reviewing an article draft.
- **Apply what you learned about selling:**
  - Which one-liners got attention, and how to use them on the home page
  - New AdWords text to try
  - How to change the 2-minute demo
  - What people said about competitors and your best retorts
- **Apply what you learned about the product** from demoing it about 50 times:
  - Existing features that weren't obvious
  - Features people kept asking for that you lack
  - Demo parts that dragged or confused viewers
  - Terminology newbies didn't understand
  - What people hated about competitors (keep that advantage) and loved about them (close the gap)
