## The ideal bootstrapped business is a "cash machine"

- Jason prefers "self-funded" to "bootstrapped" or "lifestyle business" (a term he got from Heaton). Small friends-and-family money or modest debt is fine if it doesn't cost meaningful equity.
- He frames the target as a "cash machine": a predictable way to make profit every month. His test is whether you could do nothing next month and still be certain of $10,000 in revenue. Under 20% of a room of founders could say yes.
- The goal is $10K a month per founder in revenue, ideally $20-40K.
- He says most startups fail by building something nobody wants, and also by building something wanted but structured badly for low money, low time and small-team constraints.
- He bootstrapped four companies, each past $1M a year and profitable, two of them sold. WP Engine was bootstrapped to $1M+ before he raised money to swing for the fences.
- He passed on a company idea he couldn't fit to these constraints.

## Recurring revenue and the "150 customers" model

- His third company, Smart Bear, sold one-off downloaded software. Even with millions in revenue he woke up at night panicked about covering payroll, with 1 person or 12. "It never got better ever."
- He rejects Kevin Kelly's "1,000 true fans". Kelly retracted it after artists said they couldn't get 1,000 fans or $100-200 a year from them, and $200K wouldn't cover costs anyway. Seth Godin's popularization stuck.
- Jason proposes 150 customers instead, paying about $70 a month on average, which gets you to $10K a month.
- You can scratch together the first 50 through outreach and conversations. Then add some guest posts and social, then paid marketing such as AdWords or ads on relevant sites.
- If you can't scrape together 150 over months, he doubts it can become a company.
- Customer-development outreach that worked for WP Engine: he found WordPress consultants on LinkedIn and emailed them. He said he was founding WordPress hosting designed for them, wanted to learn their pain, and would pay whatever they thought fair for an hour, even above their hourly rate.
  - He sent 42 emails and got a 100% response rate.
  - He talked to 38, and none asked for money.
- Before WP Engine had a name, deck, employees or servers, he got 30-40 people to commit $50 a month. A previous idea failed because he couldn't find enough payers.
- At Capital Factory (the Austin incubator he helped start) he tells every company to try this. About half do, and many get 20-30 people to give a check for something that doesn't exist yet.

## Charge more: price, tiers and the "boutique" frame

- Many self-funded founders charge $9-19 a month because the product feels rough, then need thousands of customers. He says that's the harder path.
- WP Engine's tier prices produce an average revenue per customer above $100 a month.
- Tactics:
  - Highlight the middle tier. Add a big tier on the end for people who always want the most.
  - Add a "business" plan; Erica Douglas's lesson is that businesses sign up for it automatically.
  - List $99 and run coupons all the time. Hand bloggers a 30% off for a year coupon. Everyone feels they got value, but you average about $70.
  - Cross out a $79 list price and show $49. Sales went up.
  - Double the price and watch for a week. A Capital Factory company at $19 a month doubled to about $49 and signups didn't change. He told them to double again or go up at least 50% until something bad happens.
- He treats revenue per customer as the most important early metric, more than cancellation rate.
- "Boutique" is the mental model: one or two people, expensive, sometimes slow to reply, but with attention, unique work and the good feeling of backing a founder making a go of it. Customers will pay about 3x for a dress or a consultant like Patrick McKenzie.

## Annual prepay and cash flow

- He says annual prepay is mandatory for self-funded companies. His example:
  - Spend $300 to acquire a customer paying $50 a month; one blog post or a few AdWords clicks might do it.
  - $60K of spend gets you to a $10K a month run rate.
  - With annual prepay at 2 months free, those signups produce about $100K in cash up front, versus $120K collected over a year. That is $40K in the bank now to spend on ads, development or a designer.
- WP Engine numbers: about a quarter of website signups choose annual prepay by revenue. They pay 10 months' worth instead of 12. That gives over 3x the cash flow per signup cohort.
  - Because of this, cash collected each month exceeds acquisition cost. Card revenue lands today while ad bills hit next month, so cash flow is negative one month on ads. Marketing was never limited by money; it stopped when support got overwhelmed.
- Ways to push annual:
  - Give affiliates and bloggers a coupon for 3 months free that works only on annual plans.
  - Raise the monthly price and make the annual discount large, like 6 months free. A Capital Factory company moved from $99 a year to $49 a month plus a big annual discount, and signups didn't change.
  - Another Capital Factory company charged $50 a year. He told them to charge monthly instead; signups didn't change, then add annual.
- On free trials: he dislikes them for self-funded companies. Most people who enter a card stay, so a 15-30 day free trial gives away money you never recover. WP Engine switched from a 15-day free trial to a 60-day money-back guarantee, charging the card immediately.
  - Signups went up. People emailed to say 15 days wasn't enough time.
  - He showed a chart of customer count with a visible change point around January of the previous year, when they also reworked tiers and added the business plan.
- He avoids "picking up pennies" models such as Kickstarter. Kickstarter moved over $100M of funding but earned about $6M and lost money.

## Which markets suit a cash machine

- B2B only: "you should absolutely never not not sell to consumers."
  - Consumers won't pay $70 a month; Gmail charging $5 a month looked crazy, and a $1.99 app was called overpriced.
  - Backblaze-type consumer backup was only $40 a year, so they moved to business.
  - Every self-funded speaker at the event, including Patrick, Rob and Heaton, sells B2B.
- Avoid temporary pain or one-time moments.
  - A wedding-photo video tool had happy customers but you only marry two or three times, and you must catch brides during planning among many sellers.
  - Event tools sell once a year.
  - A code profiler he acquired: users fixed the slowness during the free trial, so no reason to pay.
- Favor value that is naturally recurring:
  - Ongoing costs, like hosting with servers and bandwidth.
  - Financial cycles: invoicing, reconciliation, taxes, reporting, compliance, HR admin. A mobile-app study showed finance apps averaged $6,000 a month and IT apps $1,500 a month.
  - Fields that keep changing: SEO, AdWords, email marketing, content, social measurement and A/B testing.
  - Support tiers. A premium support package is $500 a month at WP Engine. A simple version is $100 a month where a paid queue gets worked first; it's nearly free money since you answer all tickets anyway.
- Avoid viral-dependent products: they rarely work, and with 100 users and 1% inviting a friend you get rounding error.
- Avoid real-time products. Hosting woke him up at night for outages. Prefer decision support, analytics, monitoring, finance, project management and content, which can tolerate delay.
- Avoid marketplaces: you have two chicken-and-egg businesses (buyers and sellers). They are defensible once working (the asset is people typing etsy.com, not the code), but too risky self-funded.
- Prefer products that can be "done," so you aren't in a feature war with better-resourced rivals. Example: Nico Mak made about $1M a year in the 90s with WinZip shareware.
- Prefer aftermarkets that attach to an incumbent product with a following:
  - Smart Bear added code review to Perforce, Subversion and CVS.
  - Balsamiq started as a Basecamp add-on.
  - Woo Themes, WP Engine (WordPress), Alien Skin (Photoshop plugins) and QODBC (an ODBC interface for QuickBooks, around since 1984, bootstrapped).
  - Benefits: obvious pain points, obvious keywords, and vendors may help. He gave talks at Perforce's user conference while promoting Perforce, with no money changing hands.
  - Ecosystems like Salesforce and Heroku (plugins, AppExchange) are even better because the platform intentionally avoids competing with you; the App Store is riskier.
- Pick a big market, even though you start in a niche. Reasons:
  - Optionality if a niche fails or succeeds.
  - You needn't be the best; there are hundreds of CRMs, project tools and invoicing tools, and many earn well.
  - The pain and spend are already validated.
  - It keeps the option to raise money and swing for the fences, as he did with WP Engine.

## Acquiring customers: paid, measurable, not social

- He doesn't like social media as the main channel: it's time-heavy, ever-changing, and not a repeatable "put in $1, get $4" machine.
- His blog had 40,000 readers; when he launched WP Engine he expected 30-50 signups from it and got 2.
- Heaton, internet-famous, told him it's always like that. He allows that Buffer succeeded through great social execution, but says it's the exception.
- Rule of thumb for what to pay per click: average revenue per customer divided by 25. At $50 a month, pay about $2 a click. He admits it rests on assumptions and wrote a blog post deriving it.
- Rule of thumb is for when you have no data. Below roughly 1,000 signups a month you can't get statistical significance.
- WP Engine actually spends more:
  - It measures cost per acquisition by channel (affiliate, sales-assisted, coupon), retention, server cost and support cost per customer, and payback period.
  - Most signups come from word of mouth, social and the consultant channel without advertising, so paid can be priced aggressively; a customer has extra value via referrals and upgrades.
  - At the time they got four-figure new customers per month.
- Affiliates:
  - Work well for hosting and WordPress; not for enterprise.
  - You have to pay a lot to get big affiliates' attention (hundreds of dollars per sale, versus a typical $30). That works only with high price points and annual prepay, since affiliates are paid in about 45 days.
  - Pair with annual-only coupons.
  - Affiliates are predictable because it's their job to send new customers every month; you pay only on a sale.
  - Vet them. WP Engine avoids shady affiliates and gray-hat SEO to protect its reputation for service.

## Enterprise sales on a bootstrap

- Enterprise SaaS is still recurring: annual contracts, one PO and invoice a year, auto-renewal. Budgets are annual anyway.
- At Smart Bear almost all sales were enterprise with no salespeople. Jason did the demos and sales calls, and staff were smart generalists who could code, talk to customers and do support. Revenue was about $1M per head.
- Harder to bootstrap because of long sales cycles, legal, slow payment (a PO with net 130), and cold outreach.
- Tactics:
  - Charge a lot to make it work.
  - Use commission-heavy pay for salespeople since you can't float a $100-150K package.
  - Run pilots (like 30 seats) and land and expand. Smart Bear's first deals averaged $12K and lifetime deals about $60K, sometimes six or seven figures over 18 months or so.

## Growth dilemma and regrets

- Success causes problems: if it keeps growing you either give bad support or hire and become a manager, which may not be what you wanted.
- Options he lists, none perfect:
  - Sell it: hard, usually modest money. A sale to a partner or employee can use an earn-out (a few grand a month or a percent of revenue over 3 years).
  - Raise prices: changes the clientele.
  - Raise money, which is what WP Engine did, but if you knew that was the goal you should have optimized for it from the start.
- He says there's no great answer. His example: IT Watchdogs built a device for a client that earned them millions; the client asked why not sell it to other power-strip makers, and the client bought the company four months later.
- His advice: "know thyself" (Thales). Work out what you want from the company and revisit it as it changes; talk it through with people who've done it.
- His regrets, which he says he keeps repeating:
  - Not removing someone fast enough. It's almost always cultural, not skills. "No one ever says we fired that person too quickly."
  - Not standing up for what he believes is right about how people are treated and the standards inside the company. Because the company's identity is so tied to the founder in a self-funded business, he always regrets staying quiet.
- His summary formula: predictable customer acquisition, recurring revenue, annual prepay to solve cash flow, a market that fits your constraints and advantages, and being a boutique rather than working a million hours a week.
