## Fulfillment needs Joy, Skill and Need together

Jason's framework for what makes work (and a life) fulfilling, which he built about ten years ago when deciding to step aside as CEO of WP Engine, the company he founded. He still uses it and recommends it to others. Fulfillment sits where three circles overlap:

- **Joy:** You love doing it. You forget to eat and pee while doing it, and at the end of a long day you still want to keep doing it.
- **Skill:** You're great at it. Your work is good enough that even you are proud of it, others notice, and people who don't see the effort call you "a natural."
- **Need:** The company needs it done. It's a top-three priority, doing it well makes a critical part of the strategy succeed, and not doing it is crippling.

He stresses that all three are required, and that any two without the third create a specific, common trap. The traps matter because it can feel good to be inside them.

## The three traps: Useless Flow, Indulgent Failure, Burn-out

**Joy + Skill − Need = Useless Flow.**
- This is "being in the zone." It is intoxicating and recharges the batteries, but it is unproductive because the company doesn't need it.
- Nobody is willing or able to tell a founder to their face that they aren't contributing, so they stay in their happy place.
- The classic example is the technical founder writing code instead of making sales, fixing the website, handling accounting, or hiring the next great team member.

**Joy + Need − Skill = Indulgent Failure.**
- Example: the company needs to start advertising. You've never done AdWords but always wanted to try it, and you tell yourself you can't hire or manage someone without understanding the job.
- You waste three months, and may wrongly conclude "AdWords doesn't work" because that is easier than "I don't know what I'm doing."
- Then you tell yourself that three months of flailing makes you an expert, so now you can hire.
- As founder, no one contradicts you, so the company falls further behind.

**Skill + Need − Joy = Burn-out.**
- You do the work all day and feel drained rather than energized. You do it because the company needs it and you're undeniably great at it.
- You take it on yourself to "protect others from the drudgery," or because you think they can't do it as well, or because you can't afford to hire.
- Because the results are great, it doesn't look like a problem to you or your team. But you grow to resent it, eventually can't face it, and you're finished.

## Why Joy is the missing piece in Pink's model

Daniel Pink argued that carrots and sticks are poor, often counter-productive motivators, and that internal motivation comes from three conditions:
- **Autonomy:** deciding what to do and how. Counter-examples are micro-management and command-and-control; positive ones are self-managed scrum teams, work-from-anywhere schedules, and agreeing on goals rather than dictating details.
- **Mastery:** the chance to do great work around others doing the same. Experts like deploying expertise; eager novices like learning.
- **Purpose:** being part of something bigger, from a noble cause to genuinely helping another person (for example world-class customer service).

Jason agrees with this and says it matches his own experience. His point is that it is possible to have autonomy, to use your expertise, and to be proud of the cause, and still "dislike every day of your existence." Moving from Pink's question (what motivates people?) to his own (what is fulfilling?) requires adding Joy.

He explains how his framework relates to Pink's:
- It adds the missing Joy and reinforces Mastery under the label Skill.
- It lacks Autonomy, probably because he built it for founders, who by definition have autonomy, even to their detriment.
- "Need" is more tactical than "Purpose"; it is really about being useful.

## Why founders stay stuck: ego and the CEO myth

Jason says burn-out creeps up like boiling a frog. He quotes an anonymous founder who spent months fantasizing each morning about working at WalMart or as a grocery bagger because it "seems so stress free," and who locked himself in the bathroom with the shower running to cry.

- The ego says "being the CEO is the best," and Silicon Valley says the founder being CEO is the only way. That makes it hard to let go of the role even when it creates unhappiness.
- He notes the usual examples cited (Apple, Facebook, Amazon) ignore counter-examples like Google, LinkedIn and Intel.
- He burned out at his previous company, Smart Bear, and says even selling the business didn't immediately fix it, though it was the start of the answer.
- He used the framework to change roles at WP Engine from CEO to CTO, so that both the new CEO and he could operate in the center of their Venn diagrams. He says the thousands of people who have since worked there agree it was a fantastic decision for both of them and for the business, which is now a unicorn.

## Hiring and delegating around the framework

Several lessons follow from the traps:
- Don't hold onto a job you hate to "protect" others. He notes no one wants the boss doing all the drudgery, especially if the boss visibly hates it, and some people actually like what you hate. By keeping it you prevent them, and yourself, from being happier.
- Don't delay hiring for a skill because you want to learn it first when the company needs results now. Playing with it yourself can cost months.
- Be wary of a company where nobody will tell the founder they aren't contributing to what the company needs.
- To decide what to keep and what to hand off, ask of each task whether it gives you Joy, whether you have the Skill, and whether the company has the Need. Give away what fails any of the three, and move yourself toward the center.

## A higher purpose helps even mercenary leaders (Sinek and ikigai)

Jason ties the framework to a higher purpose and recommends identifying it, then fulfilling your own part at the center of the three circles.

- **Simon Sinek's "Start with Why":** companies that produce fanatics, not just customers, have a clear, simple, compelling reason for being that they won't contravene even if it hurts sales or profit. Employee turnover is low and productivity high, so every part of the business is stronger.
- **Examples he gives:** Patagonia (saving the Earth, with a formal policy to bail employees out of jail if arrested protesting peacefully), SpaceX (a multi-planetary species), the Gates Foundation (eradicating malaria), Apple's "Think Different," Salesforce's "1-1-1," WP Engine's "Engine for Good," and Rackspace's "fanatical support," which turned customer service from a cost center into the honor of serving others and a differentiator in a commoditized market.
- **Not just a Gen Z thing:** those companies were created and run by Gen X and older. Even a skeptical, cold-blooded leader should see that having a higher purpose is more useful than not having one, since the freshest talent skews younger.
- **Ikigai (生き甲斐):** the Japanese concept of a reason to be alive, and the shokunin (craftsman) ideal of mastering a craft while bettering the community. He frames this as already covering two of Pink's three: Mastery and Purpose.
- **Purpose can be small:** being useful, needed or wanted by one person can be enough.
- Quote he cites from Sinek: "When people are financially invested, they want a return. When people are emotionally invested, they want to contribute."

## Why this matters for managers and leaders

- Managers ask the question because the Great Resignation showed that people are more mobile than expected when insufficiently motivated. He calls your best talent "volunteers" who can always cross the street for more money.
- Enlightened leaders ask it proactively, because intrinsically motivated people do great work that is as fulfilling for them as it is productive for the company. He calls this "capitalism on its best day."
- Creating a work environment where others can find fulfillment already improves the world substantially, and caring enough to learn what fulfills each person helps it materialize.
- The closer you are to your own center, the better you can serve others.
- For working out what drives you and what stops you, he points to a separate system he has used for himself and others (his "pivot points" essay), which he also uses to figure out who a whole product or company is.
