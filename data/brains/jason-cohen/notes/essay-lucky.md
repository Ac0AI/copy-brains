## Luck is always present, but how you use it is not luck

Jason argues that people who call his success "lucky" are being dismissive: the hours, the decisions and the product sense did not happen by accident. He also accepts that luck sits underneath every part of a business. His resolution is a distinction between luck and what you do with it.

- "Good luck and bad luck are constantly swirling around you. How you *use* it, is not luck."
- Success in business doesn't mean you "got lucky." It means you used luck: "taking advantage of the good, identifying and ejecting the bad."
- Jason says "both things can be true": the effort was real and luck buoyed it.
- He answers the "it was luck" line with the work behind Smart Bear:
  - 60-hour weeks with no pay for years, at the cost of his health.
  - Inventing a unique product in a market that didn't exist yet.
  - Cultivating relationships with the best customers and listening to their needs with a specific framework.
  - Negotiating a sale that put more money in his employees' pockets than any other job would have.

## Every decision at Smart Bear had both an intentional part and a lucky part

Jason says he can pick any decision in Smart Bear's history and the same pattern appears: success tied to something intentional that also leveraged something lucky.

- Customers: he cultivated relationships with them, but it was lucky that customers showed up at all.
- Google Ads: they found him through ads, and he started right when AdWords were new and cheap. Everyone used Google, but AdWords weren't yet "saturated with garbage."
- Marketing: he chose "effective, brief marketing messages" with an "authentic voice," but he was lucky to have a mentor who had already taught him how.
- WP Engine, in a 2024 update (founded a year after this essay):
  - It became a unicorn about fourteen years later and is 100x larger than Smart Bear ever was.
  - It took a great idea, great execution and immense effort from thousands of people.
  - It also took luck: the WordPress market stayed healthy and fast-growing throughout.
  - The WordPress community gave the company the benefit of the doubt at first and supported it later.
  - He was able to hire many talented people.
  - Serious competitors took ten years to understand how big the market was.
- Even intentional choices rarely work first time. You have to "experiment and fail" before finding success. Pushing through is tenacity, but there is luck in how quickly you find success, whether you ever do, and whether you can tell the difference.

## Personal luck and privilege are tailwinds, not a reason to discount effort

Beyond operational luck, Jason names personal luck, which many call "privilege" and Warren Buffett calls "winning the genetic lottery."

- He lists the components:
  - Being born in this era rather than the Middle Ages.
  - Being born in the US, "easily the most supportive place to start a company."
  - A stable household.
  - Certain talents, such as building software, problem-solving, tenacity and focus.
  - Ethnicity, gender and having all body parts in good working order.
- None of this negates the value or necessity of hard work and good choices. Neither can you ignore these tailwinds in the final equation of "success."

## Failure is mostly bad luck, but the response to it is a choice

Jason applies the same lens to failures, using Smart Bear's advertising.

- Many marketing and advertising attempts flopped.
- Ads in certain magazines bombed. In some cases he spent many thousands of dollars, then a significant percentage of revenue, on ads that didn't net a single sale.
- An ad that fails utterly in one magazine after working in another is bad luck.
- The choice to cancel some ads and not others is not luck.
- Making sure individual print ads could be measured was also a choice. Without that, he says, "we wouldn't have been able to distinguish success from failure, and then indeed our destiny would be controlled by luck alone."
- 2024 note: every magazine he used to advertise in has gone out of business.

## Treat every decision as an empirical test

Jason's practical conclusion is a way of operating that lets you capture good luck and shed bad luck.

- "Your best bet for success is to treat all your decisions as empirical tests."
- "Try anything, measure everything, and follow what works, even if that means changing everything."
- He says confidence and experimentation are not contradictory (he links this to "strong opinions, weakly held").
- Measurement is what separates luck from skill. If you can't tell which ad worked, luck controls your outcome. If you can, you keep winners and cut losers.

## Ask "how did you know" questions, not "what inspired it"

Jason notes that luck rarely comes up when he talks to other entrepreneurs, who want stories, tips and how things work. He says they want to know how to think, not how to copy.

- The wrong question: "What inspired that idea?"
- The right question: "How did you know when an idea was right?"
- An even more specific version: "How does one know whether a print ad is working?"
- The useful lesson from a success story is the method for evaluating ideas, not the origin of the idea.
