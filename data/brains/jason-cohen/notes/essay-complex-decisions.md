## Separate upside from downside before deciding

Jason argues that complex decisions stall because people argue in logical circles, with every point true but none converging. His example is "should we build feature X?":

- Feature X makes the company unique in the market.
- But the company is behind on feature Y, which three competitors have.
- But once Y is built, those competitors will have Z.
- But deals are being lost today for lack of Y.
- But a competitor with Y could copy X.

All of those statements are true, which is why no decision comes out of them.

His fix is to split "how could this go right" (upside) from "how could this go wrong" (downside) and state the outcome of each in a line:

- Upside of X: unique market position leads to deals won through differentiation, plus some less measurable brand benefit.
- Downside of not building Y: deals lost to one specific competitor.

Building Y also wins more deals, but only in a subset of sales calls and with no brand benefit, so X looks better.

He adds a caveat: if you can quantify it, quantify. If the company is losing $10m/yr without Y and expects to gain $1m/yr from X, Y wins. Even without data, which is common for early-stage startups, the separation still gives a clear and sensible answer.

## Decide on the upside, use the downside only as a veto

Jason applies this to hiring a senior leader such as a VP Marketing. The "perfect candidate" (vision, strategy, operations, people, org structure, internal and external communication, speed, mentorship) is mythical, so it is easy to go in circles.

His rule: hire on exceptional strengths, then fill weaknesses through the rest of the team. "Only through exceptional strength will the person make an impact on the business."

He tests two questions:

1. For the most critical problems this leader is expected to solve over the next year, is she world-class at solving those particular problems? If not, it is a no-hire, however much you like the other upsides or ignore the downsides.
2. Do you know her weaknesses (otherwise you can't plan for them), and are they either low-impact or something you or she can concretely mitigate? Everyone has weaknesses. If any is impossible to ameliorate, it is a no-hire. Otherwise, that is the plan.

So the primary decision rests on the magnitude and likelihood of the upside, and the downside is only a veto when it is unworkable. He adds that it is your job to fill the company with people much better than you at every position.

## Over-invest in strengths rather than shoring up weaknesses

Jason says people naturally focus on weaknesses ("we're getting killed for not having F," "customers complain we're bad at E," "20% of customers leave because we're bad at D") and spend time and money bringing them up to neutral. His argument: "developing a 10x strength is far more valuable than shifting a weakness to neutral." This holds personally, professionally, and in product.

His examples:

- **iPhone v1:** it mattered more to be 10x on strengths (form factor, browser and email experience) than to fix weaknesses (no copy/paste, poor call experience).
- **Engineers:** you are more effective being very deep in one language, framework or problem domain than passable at ten.
- **Heroku:** it won Rails developers by being 10x better at deploy/stage/production, so developers (begrudgingly) accepted a read-only file system, having to use Bundler, having to use PostgreSQL, overpaying for CPU, and tying their fate to a platform.
- **Counter-example, WP Engine:** web design was a weakness, so the company invested and "got zero return," because words and product/market fit turned out to be 100x more important than design.

Practical rule: invest most in your greatest upsides and strengths. Treat downsides as constraints to design around, or as a veto in extreme cases. He suggests running the same exercise on your own personal strengths and weaknesses.

## Deliberately over-simplify to at most three dimensions

Jason says the feature X/Y summary ("both increase sales, but only one also increases differentiation") quietly ignored many real factors, such as implementation time, team fit and likelihood of failure. That is intentional. "If you don't intentionally over-simplify, you will never reach a firm and clear decision."

He compares it to voters in the 2016 US presidential election. There are too many issues to weigh (climate, taxes, trade, war, education, civil rights and more), no candidate matches on all, and it is unknowable which few issues will actually change. So voters pick a few issues they care about most and decide on those. He says this kind of reductive simplification is logical and necessary, and links it to his writing on SaaS metrics and on great strategy.

Why it is safe: if you treat all the considerations as important, you end up with a set of good options. Any one of them is objectively good, so almost any process for picking among them is acceptable. His guidance is to decide using one, two, or at most three key dimensions.

He points to two frameworks for doing this as a group:

- Binstack, for multi-dimensional choices.
- Fermi ROI, a better way to run rubrics or "ROI" calculations.

## Ignore the social pressure of the options you did not pick

Jason warns that whichever option you exclude, people with a vested interest (even just that it was their idea or they got attached) will push back with "how is your choice really better?" He says they are right, because you had several equally good choices, and someone will say it whatever you do. His conclusion: do not include this "social pressure" in your decision.

## Decide how to decide, then move on

Jason's closing rules:

- Making decisions quickly is valuable, because you can usually make a different one if the first proves wrong. If you can't reverse it, that is a reason to spend more time evaluating.
- Pick a process, use it, and move on.
- Separate upside from downside, clarify the choice with one to three dimensions, base the decision on the magnitude and likelihood of the upside, and use the downside to veto untenable options.
- Use Binstack or Fermi ROI as frameworks that guide a group to a joint, explainable decision.
- "Defining the conditions of the decision is 80% of the decision," so spend your time there.
- Don't be too hard on yourself afterwards even if it goes badly. Life is an experiment with little predictive power, no control group, and N=1 that cannot be re-run.
