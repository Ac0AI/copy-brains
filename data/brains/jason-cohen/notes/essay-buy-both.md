## Why enterprises buy a cheap second product that does the same job

- Jason describes visiting a hospital's network operations center with a co-founder while selling ITWatchDogs, whose web-enabled hardware measured temperature, humidity, door status and power in server rooms. The hospital already ran HP OpenView, a multi-million-dollar monitoring suite.
- It also ran What's Up Gold, a bootstrapped, shrink-wrapped product with a silly name and an ugly logo, at about $500-$700.
- When they asked why, the buyer said: "What's Up Gold has a couple of features we like. There's this thing we do many times per day, and it's really quick with What's Up Gold. And it's only like five hundred dollars or something. We can just run that on our credit card without asking permission. So we just bought it."
- What's Up Gold never beat HP OpenView, IBM Tivoli, CA Unicenter or BMC Patrol head-to-head. It did not need to; it sold to the same customer anyway.
- Jason's point: a small product can sell into an enterprise that already solved the problem, if it adds even a little incremental value at near-zero cost to buy.

## Price for procurement pain, not buyer psychology

- When selling to mid-sized and large businesses, Jason argues pricing is less about whether $49.99 feels cheaper than $50 and more about how much of a pain in the ass the purchase is.
- Procurement friction is the real price. "$500 is not the same as $499.99; it's the same as $0."
- He compares it to voting theory. Board shares of 7%, 16%, 17% and 60% give voting power of 0%, 0%, 0% and 100%, because the 60% holder always wins.
- Applied to price, $500 can be the same as $0 (a credit card, no approval), while $1,000 might be the same as $60,000 (it triggers the approval process).
- For What's Up Gold the product was effectively free, so any incremental value justified buying it.
- The price solved for procurement. It was "notably _not_ 'price to value' nor perception tricks."
- Jason notes this does not always work, and points to his essay on purgatory for the alternate cases.

## What a small product needs to win alongside an incumbent

Jason lists what made What's Up Gold thrive:

1. A product with a few unique and delightful features. It was fast at one task the buyer did many times a day.
2. A price that solved for procurement.
3. A target market defined by the needs of an individual IT person, not "SMB" vs "Enterprise." The customer was an enterprise, but the buyer was one admin with a credit card.
4. A way to get the right person's attention, meaning distribution.

He adds that name, logo and website design clearly did not block the sale here.

## Distribution: find where the buyer actually pays attention

- The IT admin said he probably saw the product in an ad "in the back of one of the ones in the bathroom," gesturing at a table of manuals, wires and CD-ROMs.
- Jason's takeaway: while you worry about SEO and AdWords positioning, it is easy to overlook the magazine the buyer reads for 20 minutes of solace.
- His team went to the bathroom to see which magazines were there, then advertised in them, and it worked.
- Sub-lesson from his footnote: always ask about adjacent products and behaviors. Wherever the buyer found What's Up Gold, he could have found ITWatchDogs too.

## Customer interviews: ask about their life, not just your product

- Jason says to proactively seek out customers and talk to them, and that "whenever you see something surprising, that's a signal that there's something to learn. Dig in and learn it."
- They learned a lot by asking about other products, as long as the questions were about the life of the customer. He suggests customers may be more honest precisely because it is not about you.
- From that one conversation they learned:
  - which magazine to advertise in;
  - that customers will buy two pieces of software to solve the same problem;
  - that the buyer had a credit card limit.

## Land and expand: shrink the first deal to fit the buyer

- They walked in expecting to pitch a $10,000 package. After learning about the credit card, they changed the pitch on the fly: "You could just buy $500 worth of stuff from us today. See whether you like it."
- The hospital bought a little more each month, because the product was good and they could buy in a way that was convenient.
- The hospital was part of a larger group. After it worked well at one site, word spread internally, and the global IT center eventually placed orders of tens of thousands of dollars, going through real procurement.
- Jason's summary: enterprise sales is not always a large top-down deal. It "can start small, start accessible, start simple, start affordable, and then expand outward once you're a proven quantity."
- He acknowledges this is 20 years old, with SaaS, markets, budgets and AI all different, but says the fundamentals hold: learn from your customers and make things they want to buy, "twice."
