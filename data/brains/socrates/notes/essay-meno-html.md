## Virtue needs a single definition before it can be sought or taught

- Meno answers "what is virtue?" with a list: one virtue for a man (to administer the state, "benefit his friends and harm his enemies"), another for a woman, a child, a slave. Socrates replies that he asked for one virtue and got "a swarm of them."
- He uses the bee: bees do not differ as bees. He looks for the common nature that makes all virtues virtues, the "simile in multis."
- Health, size and strength are the same in man and woman, and so is virtue. Every good person is good through the same temperance and justice.
- He rejects definitions that list parts or use unexplained terms. "Virtue is the power of governing mankind" fails because a child cannot govern a father. "Doing everything with justice" fails because justice is only a part of virtue. "Can any one who does not know virtue know a part of virtue?"
- He insists the whole of virtue be delivered "whole and sound, and not broken into a number of pieces."
- Priority: he would settle "what it is" before asking whether it can be taught, and he says he does not know what virtue is.

## How to define: shape and colour, and answering a friend differently from an opponent

- To model a good definition he defines figure as "the limit of solid," after first trying "the only thing which always follows colour."
- He says the first definition is true but useless to someone who does not know what colour is. Terms must be ones the questioner already admits.
- With an eristic, antagonistic opponent he would simply state his answer and leave the refuting to the opponent. With friends he uses the dialectician's way: speak the truth, and use premises the other person is willing to admit.
- He notes that his plain answer about figure beats the "orthodox solemn" Gorgias-style definition of colour as an "effluence of form." The solemn one only seems better because Meno is used to hearing it.

## Nobody desires evil: virtue as the power to attain the good

- Meno proposes "the desire of things honourable and the power of attaining them." Socrates tests it.
- Argument: people who say they desire evils either think those evils are good, in which case they really desire what they take to be good, or they know the evils harm them. Harm makes the possessor miserable, and nobody wants to be miserable. So "there is no one who desires evil."
- Since everyone desires good, no one is better than another in desire. Virtue must lie in the power of attaining good.
- Attaining silver, gold, office or honour counts as virtue only if done justly and piously. Acquiring wealth unjustly is vice, and not acquiring it dishonestly may be virtue. So justice is a part of virtue, and the definition goes in a circle.

## Perplexity as a benefit: the torpedo fish and the slave boy's mistake

- Meno says Socrates numbs people like a torpedo fish. Socrates answers: "I perplex others, not because I am clear, but because I am utterly perplexed myself."
- The boy asked to double a square of 4 feet confidently says double the side. Socrates shows this gives 16, not 8. The boy then tries 3 feet and gets 9, and finally admits "I do not know."
- Socrates asks Meno: "Is he not better off in knowing his ignorance?" The "torpedo's shock" did the boy no harm.
- Before the shock the boy "would have been ready to tell all the world again and again" that a double space has a double side. Nobody inquires into what he fancies he already knows. Perplexity is what creates the wish to learn.

## Meno's paradox and recollection: inquiry as remembering

- Meno's challenge: a man cannot inquire into what he knows (no need) or what he does not know (he does not know what to look for, or recognise it if found).
- Socrates calls it a "sophistical argument" that makes us idle and "is sweet only to the sluggard." The alternative makes us "active and inquisitive."
- He cites priests, priestesses and poets such as Pindar: the soul is immortal, is reborn many times, and has seen all things. So "all enquiry and all learning is but recollection," and one recollection can lead to the rest "if a man is strenuous and does not faint." People should live in perfect holiness.
- Demonstration: Socrates only asks questions and does not teach. He asks the boy to say what he thinks. The boy finds that the double square is built on the diagonal. He was never taught geometry, so these were true notions already in him, "stirred up... as in a dream." With repeated questions in different forms he would know as well as anyone.
- Since the knowledge was neither acquired in this life nor always known, the soul must have had it before. Socrates concludes the soul is immortal, but says he is not fully confident of some of this.
- What he will fight for "in word and deed": we will be "better and braver and less helpless" if we believe we ought to inquire into what we do not know.

## Is virtue knowledge? The argument from hypothesis

- Socrates proposes arguing from a hypothesis, as a geometer does: if virtue is knowledge it can be taught; if not, not.
- Virtue is good and profitable. Health, strength, beauty and wealth help only when used rightly, and harm otherwise. Soul qualities such as courage, temperance and quickness are profitable only with wisdom. Courage without prudence is "only a sort of confidence" and does harm.
- Argument: all else hangs on the soul, and the soul's goods hang on wisdom. Under wisdom everything the soul attempts ends in happiness, and under folly the opposite. So virtue is wholly or partly wisdom.
- If so, the good are not good by nature. If they were, there would be discerners of character who would spot future good people and keep them safe, stamped "far rather than upon a piece of gold."

## Why virtue seems unteachable: no teachers, and the sons of great men

- Test: anything teachable has teachers and disciples. He has often searched for teachers of virtue and found none.
- Anytus calls the Sophists a "manifest pest." Socrates objects that Anytus condemns them without acquaintance. Protagoras earned more than Pheidias and any ten sculptors and kept his reputation for more than forty years. A cobbler who ruined shoes would be found out within thirty days.
- Gentlemen as teachers fail the test too. Themistocles taught his son Cleophantus horsemanship, but no one calls Cleophantus good. Aristides, Pericles and Thucydides had the same result. Thucydides paid for the best wrestling trainers for his sons but could not make them good men. If virtue could be taught, he would have taught it.
- Meno's own circle disagrees on whether virtue can be taught. Theognis contradicts himself on it. Socrates says people who disagree this much cannot be called teachers.

## Knowledge versus true opinion: Daedalus's statues

- Socrates corrects his earlier claim that only a person with knowledge can guide well. A person who has never been to Larisa but holds a true opinion about the road guides just as well as one who knows it.
- True opinion is as useful in action as knowledge. The difference is that knowledge always stays right and opinion is sometimes right.
- Image: the statues of Daedalus run away unless tied down. True opinions are "beautiful and fruitful" but flee the soul until "fastened by the tie of the cause." This tying is recollection, and it makes opinion into knowledge. That is why knowledge is "more honourable and excellent."
- Of the claim that knowledge differs from true opinion, he says: "There are not many things which I profess to know, but this is most certainly one of them."

## Conclusion: virtue as a divine gift, and the unfinished question

- Statesmen like Themistocles did not govern by knowledge, since they could not pass on their virtue. They governed by right opinion, "in politics what divination is in religion." Diviners, prophets and poets say many true things "not knowing what they say." Socrates calls such statesmen "divine."
- His conclusion: virtue is "neither natural nor acquired, but an instinct given by God to the virtuous," not accompanied by reason.
- If someone existed who could educate statesmen, he would be among the living what Tiresias was among the dead, "he alone has understanding; but the rest are flitting shades."
- He says the certain truth cannot be known "until, before asking how virtue is given, we enquire into the actual nature of virtue." He leaves asking Meno to calm Anytus.
