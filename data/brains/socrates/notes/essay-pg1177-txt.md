## Educating the gifted, the rich and the self-satisfied

- Socrates "fell in love" with young people whose souls showed promise: a readiness to learn what they attend to, a memory that retains lessons, and a passion for studies serving the good management of a house or state. Such people, educated, become happy themselves and able to make others happy.
- To those who trusted natural talent and despised learning, he argued that the most gifted nature needs the most training. A spirited colt properly broken in becomes superb; left unbroken it is intractable. A well-bred puppy trained becomes an excellent hunter; neglected, a stupid brute.
- The same holds for people. The strongest, most determined youth, if taught what is right, becomes superlatively useful. Left in ignorance, he does the worst on the grandest scale, because without knowledge of what is right his vehemence cannot be reined in.
- To those proud of wealth ("money makes the man"), he argued that without instruction a person cannot tell what is gainful from what is hurtful. Without that discrimination, wealth alone cannot get a man what he wants. Nor can wealth without true knowledge buy a reputation for excellence.
- He did not rush students toward skill in speech, action or invention without a foundation of self-control. Power without self-control, he believed, only makes men worse and more mischievous.

## Curing the proud scholar: Euthydemus and the saddler's shop

- Euthydemus had collected a large library of poets and philosophers and believed he already out-topped his peers in wisdom. Socrates went to the saddler's shop where he sat, and first raised the point indirectly: it is naive to think minor arts need teachers while leading the state, the greatest concern, "drops into the lap of anybody."
- He mocked the self-taught politician with an imagined opening speech: "I have never learnt anything from anybody." He set it beside a candidate for state physician announcing he learned no medicine and will "acquire skill by experimenting on your persons." The listeners laughed.
- He observed that people will sit at the feet of the best teachers to learn harp, flute or riding, yet some expect to master politics, the harder field with more competitors and fewer successes, "by inspiration" with no preparation.
- Once Euthydemus was listening, Socrates spoke with him alone. He praised him for prizing wisdom over gold and silver, then asked what he wanted the books for. Euthydemus ruled out doctor, architect, mathematician, astronomer and rhapsodist. He wanted statesmanship, which Socrates called "the noblest of virtues and the greatest of the arts," the "royal" art.
- After being refuted (see the next section), Euthydemus left in despair. Many of Socrates's refuted interlocutors never returned, and he judged them dullards. Euthydemus instead saw that his best course was to stay close to Socrates and imitate him. Socrates then taught him plainly and simply what is needful to know and practise.

## Testing justice: the R and W columns

- Socrates asked whether anyone can be a good statesman without justice. Euthydemus said no and claimed he could "hold his own against all comers as an upright man."
- Socrates proposed writing R (right) and W (wrong) on two sides and sorting actions. Lying, deceit, mischief and enslaving free men all went to W.
- He then tested the sorting with cases:
  - A general who enslaves a wicked, hostile city, or deceives and plunders an enemy, does right.
  - A general who invents a story of reinforcements to revive demoralised troops does right.
  - A father who deceives a sick son into taking medicine does right.
  - Taking a knife from a despairing friend who might kill himself is right.
- Each case forced Euthydemus to revise the rule. Deceit is not simply wrong; what matters is its object and effect. He withdrew his claim that one must always be straightforward with friends.
- Socrates pressed further: is voluntary or involuntary deception worse? Euthydemus said voluntary. Socrates compared it to the man who misspells on purpose, who is more literate than the one who errs from ignorance. If justice is a science like letters, the intentional wrongdoer would then be more just. Euthydemus saw the absurdity but could not resolve it.
- Socrates's test for knowing: a man who says the road is east and then west, or whose sums keep changing, "does not know what he thought he knew."
- Slavishness, he said, is not ignorance of smithing or carpentry (many skilled craftsmen are "little better than slaves"). It is ignorance of "the beautiful, the good, the just," and everyone should strain every nerve to avoid it.

## Know thyself, and the questionable goods

- Socrates asked Euthydemus whether he had heeded the Delphic inscription "Know thyself." Euthydemus said he assumed he knew himself, since otherwise he would know nothing.
- Socrates rejected knowing only your name. A man buying a horse tests whether it is tractable or stubborn, strong or weak, quick or slow, for its use. Likewise a man must examine his own nature and capacities for what a man needs.
- Benefits of self-knowledge:
  - He knows what is advantageous to himself and discerns the limits of his powers.
  - He does what he knows and avoids what he does not, so he makes fewer mistakes.
  - He gains fame and honour, and friends and neighbours seek his counsel.
- Costs of ignorance of self: a man neither knows what he wants, what he is doing, nor the people he deals with. He fails, loses reputation, and ends in contempt.
- States are the same: one that ignorantly wars against a stronger state is uprooted or enslaved.
- Applying this, Socrates showed that commonly named goods are not unconditionally good:
  - Health and strength can kill those who sail or fight while the weak stay safe.
  - Wisdom did not save Daedalus and Palamedes from slavery and murder.
  - Beauty, strength, wealth, reputation and political power are among "the commonest sources of mischief": beauty draws ruinous admirers, strength tempts men beyond their powers, wealth softens and invites plots.
- A thing that sometimes benefits and sometimes harms should not be called strictly good or evil.
- He also showed the vagueness of "rich" and "poor": some with a pittance find it enough and save, while kings driven by want act like paupers. So good economists with little can be wealthy and rulers can be poor.

## The gods' care and honouring them

- Socrates's first aim with companions was a wise spirit toward the gods. With Euthydemus he listed divine provision for man: light, night for rest, stars and moon to mark time, crops and seasons, water, fire ("nothing serviceable to the life of man worth speaking of but owes its fabrication to fire"), and the sun's gradual approach and retreat so we are neither scorched nor frozen.
- He added animals that serve man for food, commerce, and work and war, and man's own gifts: senses matching nature's variety, reasoning and memory, and speech for teaching, forming societies and laws. Divination helps where human foresight fails.
- The gods themselves are unseen, so one must "behold their works" and honour their authors. Their ministers are hidden too: thunderbolt, winds, and the soul of man, which shares in the divine yet is invisible.
- To Euthydemus's worry that no one can repay the gods enough, he cited Delphi: honour the gods "according to the law and custom of your city," with offerings according to one's ability. The best honour is doing what they bid, without falling short of one's ability.
- The reasoning: expect great blessings from those able to help most, by pleasing them with the amplest obedience.
- This teaching made companions "more devout and more virtuous."

## Justice as lawfulness: Socrates and Hippias

- Socrates showed justice in deed: he refused an unconstitutional vote as president of the assembly, refused the Thirty when they forbade him to talk to the young and ordered him to help arrest a man for execution, and at his trial refused the customary flattery and pleading even though bending slightly would have won acquittal. He "preferred to abide by the laws and die rather than transgress them and live."
- He told Hippias it is strange that people know where to send a horse or ox to be trained, yet cannot tell where to find a teacher of right. Hippias complained he repeats the same old talk; Socrates replied that on a settled subject one says the same thing, just as one always spells a name the same way.
- Challenged to say what justice is, he first pointed to his conduct: never bearing false witness, informing maliciously, stirring strife or doing wrong. Hippias objected that this says only what the just man avoids. Socrates then defined "the lawful is the just."
- Arguments that law-abidingness is just:
  - Laws are enactments citizens agree on, so the lawful citizen does what is just.
  - Laws being repealed no more discredits obedience than the fact that war ends in peace discredits discipline in war.
  - Lycurgus made Sparta distinguished chiefly by instilling obedience to the laws.
  - Concord, the greatest blessing of a state, is sworn across Hellas. It means obedience to laws, not shared tastes in choruses, flute-players or poets.
  - The law-loving man is protected from penalty, wins honours and is trusted with deposits, command and treaties by friends, allies and even enemies. Hatred and hostility melt before him.
- He then added the unwritten laws, which Hippias said must come from the gods since they hold everywhere: worship of the gods, honour to parents, and returning kindness for kindness. Their transgression carries its own penalty (the ungrateful man ends isolated; incest brings ill-begotten offspring), pointing to a higher than human legislator.
- Hippias agreed the gods' laws are just, so the gods are pleased that "the lawful" and "the just" coincide.

## Self-control as the root of freedom and pleasure

- Socrates told Euthydemus that freedom is the noblest possession of a man or a state. A person ruled by bodily pleasures, hindered from the best and driven to the shameful, is not free but enslaved to "the worst of masters."
- Incontinence shuts out wisdom, drags men to the pleasant, and makes them choose the worse even when they know good from evil. It undermines modesty and carefulness. Self-control, its opposite, is the best thing a man can have.
- It also yields more pleasure. Only those who can endure hunger, thirst, sexual appetite and lack of sleep until each is at its height get the full joy of eating, drinking, love and rest. The incontinent are tied to the nearest pleasure and miss the joy of learning what is good, of managing body and household well, and of serving friends and state.
- A man who gives up aiming high and only gratifies pleasure is no better than "the silliest of cattle."
- The self-controlled discover hidden treasures, choosing the good and rejecting the evil. He derived "dialegesthai" (discussion) from "dialegein," picking things out by kinds, so the self-controlled become best at reasoning and debate and fittest to lead.

## Definitions by stepwise agreement

- Socrates held that whoever knows what each thing is can explain it, and that those who do not will stumble and cause others to stumble. So he constantly inquired into the nature of things.
- Definitions reached with Euthydemus:
  - Piety: knowing the laws about honouring the gods and honouring them accordingly. A man honours the gods only as he thinks he ought.
  - Justice: knowing the lawful dealings among men. Nobody obeys laws without knowing them, and nobody who knows what he ought to do does otherwise.
  - Wisdom: knowledge. Each man is wise only in what he knows, and no one can be all-wise.
  - Good and beautiful: relative. The good is the beneficial to whom it benefits, and the useful is beautiful for the use it is applied to.
  - Courage: not mere fearlessness, since madmen who do not know dangers would count. It is knowing how to meet terrors and dangers well. Those who fail utterly are cowards.
- Governments: a kingdom rules willing subjects by law; a tyranny rules unwilling subjects by the ruler's whims. Aristocracy has offices filled from those who meet legal requirements, plutocracy by property, democracy by all citizens.
- When a disputant merely asserted that a person he admired was wiser or a better citizen, Socrates returned to the underlying question. He asked what a good citizen's work is: increasing the state's resources while lightening expense, winning wars, making friends from enemies abroad, and ending party strife at home.
- His method: advance step by step from points of general agreement. "Herein lay the real security of reasoning." He admired Homer's praise of Odysseus as a safe orator for leading discussion from one accepted opinion to another.

## Useful learning only, and the examined life

- Socrates sought to find what each companion really knew and taught gentleman's knowledge himself or sent them to those who knew.
- Geometry: enough to measure, divide or take over land. He disapproved of pursuing intricate diagrams, which would wear out a lifetime and crowd out more useful studies.
- Astronomy: enough to tell the hour of the night and the season for travel, march and watches, which night hunters and pilots can teach. He opposed studying planets, distances and causes.
- He opposed trying to work out how the gods produce celestial phenomena: it is beyond human power and displeasing to the gods. He cited Anaxagoras, who claimed the sun is a red-hot stone and lost his head over it, ignoring that we can look at fire but not at the sun and that plants grow in sunlight but are parched by fire.
- He urged study of reasoning only up to the limit of utility.
- He urged attention to health: learn from experts, but also observe yourself for a lifetime to find the food, drink and work that suit you.
- For help beyond human wisdom, he counselled turning to divination.

## Facing death and the judgment of his life

- The "divine sign" opposed Socrates each time he tried to prepare a defence. He told Hermogenes that his whole life had been his defence, spent "distinguishing between what is just and what is unjust" and doing right.
- He considered his death timely: no one had spent a better or happier life than his. "Theirs I regard as the best of lives who study best to become as good as may be, and theirs the happiest who have the liveliest sense of growth in goodness."
- He reasoned that old age would bring dimmer senses, weaker intellect and forgetfulness, and a life with consciousness of such decline would be "a death in life."
- If put to death unjustly, the shame belongs to those who slay him, since injustice is base. Posterity will judge him differently from his killers: "I never at any time did wrong to any man, or made him a worse man, but ever tried to make those better who were with me."
- He lived the thirty days before execution, delayed by the Delian embassy, with the same cheerfulness and calm.
