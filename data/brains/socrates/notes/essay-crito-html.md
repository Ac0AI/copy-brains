## Why Socrates Refuses to Escape: Reason Over Fear and Opinion

- Socrates sleeps calmly the morning before his execution. He says "when a man has reached my age he ought not to be repining at the prospect of death."
- He is "one of those natures who must be guided by reason, whatever the reason may be which upon reflection appears to me to be the best."
- His rule for a crisis: principles honored in good times are not dropped because fortune turns. Unless "other and better principles" can be found "on the instant," he keeps the old ones.
- He will not be moved by threats, "not even if the power of the multitude could inflict many more imprisonments, confiscations, deaths, frightening us like children with hobgoblin terrors."
- He asks whether the earlier argument was "talk for the sake of talking; in fact an amusement only, and altogether vanity," and tests it again with Crito instead of assuming it holds.
- He asks Crito to refute him or stop pressing: "I am extremely desirous to be persuaded by you, but not against my own better judgment."

## Whose Opinion Counts: The One Who Understands

- Crito warns that the many will think he cared more about money than his friend, and that the many can do great harm to those who lose their good opinion.
- Socrates replies that only good men are worth considering, and they will see the truth of events.
- The many "can do neither good nor evil: they cannot make a man wise or make him foolish; and whatever they do is the result of chance."
- Analogy from gymnastics: an athlete heeds the praise and blame of his one trainer or physician, not everyone. Ignoring that one man ruins the body.
- The same holds for just and unjust, fair and foul, good and evil. He follows "the one man who has understanding of just and unjust," and "what the truth will say," not the crowd.
- Crito concedes that the part of a person improved by justice and ruined by injustice is far more honored than the body. Life is not worth living with that part corrupted.
- To "the many can kill us," he answers that the old principle stands: "not life, but a good life, is to be chiefly valued," and a good life is "a just and honorable one."

## Practical Worries Are the Crowd's Doctrines

- Crito offers many reasons to escape:
  - Simmias the Theban has brought money.
  - Cebes and others will pay.
  - Friends in Thessaly will protect him.
  - He would be betraying his children.
  - Staying is the "easier part," not the "manlier."
  - His friends will look cowardly.
- Socrates files money, reputation and the education of children under "the doctrines of the multitude, who would be as ready to call people to life, if they were able, as they are to put them to death - and with as little reason."
- The only question is whether escaping would be right. If it is wrong, "death or any other calamity which may ensue on my remaining here must not be allowed to enter into the calculation."

## Never Do Wrong, Never Retaliate

- Socrates restates the premise of the whole inquiry: injustice is always an evil and a dishonor to the one who commits it, whatever the consequences.
- He draws a chain of conclusions:
  - We must do no wrong.
  - We must not return injury for injury, "as the many imagine."
  - Doing evil to someone is the same as injuring him.
  - So no evil is to be rendered for evil, whatever we have suffered.
- He admits that few hold this view and that those who accept it and those who reject it "have no common ground, and can only despise one another."
- He checks that Crito truly agrees: "neither injury nor retaliation nor warding off evil by evil is ever right." Crito says he has not changed his mind.
- The next premise: a man ought to do what he admits to be right, not betray it.
- Escaping would wrong those he ought "least to wrong" and abandon principles they had both agreed were just.

## The Laws Speak: Obligation to the City

Socrates imagines the laws and the State confronting him, using the question-and-answer style he practices himself.

- **Overturning the State.** A State cannot survive if court decisions "have no power, but are set aside and overthrown by individuals." Replying "the State has injured us and given an unjust sentence" does not answer the point.
- **Parent and nurse.** The laws regulated his parents' marriage and his upbringing and education, so he is their "child and slave." He is not on equal terms and cannot return harm for harm to them, just as he could not strike a father who struck him.
- **Country above family.** The country is "more to be valued and higher and holier far than mother or father." Its punishments are endured in silence.
- **Persuade or obey.** A citizen must do what the city orders "or he must change their view of what is just." The laws "do not rudely impose" commands but offer the alternative of obeying or convincing them. He does neither.
- **Leave if dissatisfied.** Every Athenian may, once grown and acquainted with the laws, take his goods and go elsewhere. Staying means "an implied contract that he will do as we command him."
- **Disobedience is a triple wrong.** It disobeys parents, defies the authors of his education, and breaks an agreement.

## Evidence That Socrates Consented

- He was the most constant resident of Athens, left only once for the Isthmus games, and otherwise only on military service. He showed no curiosity about other States and their laws.
- He begat his children there.
- At his trial he could have proposed banishment as his penalty but "pretended that you preferred death to exile."
- He had seventy years to leave. Sparta (Lacedaemon) and Crete, which he often praised, were open to him.
- The agreement was made "in deed, and not in word only," not in haste, compulsion or deception.

## What Escape Would Cost

- His friends would face exile, loss of citizenship or property.
- In a well-governed city like Thebes or Megara he would arrive as an enemy of the laws, and would confirm the judges' verdict: "he who is a corrupter of the laws is more than likely to be corrupter of the young."
- He could not talk about virtue, justice and law there without shame.
- In lawless Thessaly he would be the flatterer and servant of all, "eating and drinking in Thessaly, having gone abroad in order that you may get a dinner," mocked for fleeing in a goatskin disguise "from a miserable desire of a little more life."
- Taking his children to Thessaly would deprive them of Athenian citizenship. If their guardians are true friends, they will care for them whether he is in Thessaly or in the other world.
- The laws' counsel: "Think not of life and children first, and of justice afterwards, but of justice first, that you may be justified before the princes of the world below."
- He departs "in innocence, a sufferer and not a doer of evil; a victim, not of the laws, but of men."

## Conclusion

- He hears the laws' voice "like the sound of the flute in the ears of the mystic," humming so loudly he cannot hear anything else.
- Crito has nothing left to say.
- Socrates ends: "Then let me follow the intimations of the will of God."
