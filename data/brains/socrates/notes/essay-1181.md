## Socrates on the Fragrance of True Nobility and Learning Virtue from Good Company

- Socrates argues that perfume suits women but not men, because scents blur the difference between a free man and a slave. The scent of a free man comes from the toils he loves, from habit and time.
- Asked by Lycon where older men whose gymnastic days are over can find fragrance, he answers: "Why, that of true nobility, of course." Asked where one gets it, he says not from sellers of perfumes and unguents.
- He cites Theognis: "From the good thou shalt learn good things, but if with the evil Thou holdest converse, thou shalt lose the wit that is in thee."
- He says a young man who wants to excel, as Autolycus did in the pankration, should take counsel to find the fittest friend to help him, and associate with that friend.
- When the guests ask whether such wisdom can be taught or only learnt, he treats the question as debatable and defers it.

## Socrates on Women's Nature, Courage and Choosing a Difficult Wife

- Watching the dancing-girl throw hoops in perfect time, he says her skill is one proof among many that "woman's nature is nowise inferior to man's". Women lack only strength and judgment.
- He urges husbands to teach their wives whatever they want them to know as associates.
- Antisthenes asks why Socrates does not tutor his own shrewish wife, Xanthippe. He answers with the rider who wants to become an expert horseman and so avoids soft-mouthed, docile animals: "the horse for me to own must show some spirit".
- His reasoning: he wishes to deal with human beings in general. He knows that if he can tolerate her spirit, he can "with ease attach myself to every human being else".
- When the girl somersaults into a ring of upright swords, he says none present can again deny that courage can be taught. If a woman rushes so boldly into the midst of swords, courage looks like something that can be trained.

## Socrates on Exercise, Dancing and Moderate Drinking

- He asks the Syracusan to teach him to dance, and defends this with a serious face. Dancing exercises every part of the body, giving an even balance, unlike the long-race runner's muscular legs and leaner shoulders or the boxer's thick chest at the expense of the legs.
- Benefits he lists: better health, enjoying food more, sleeping better, and reducing "a paunch a trifle too rotund". He can do it indoors in a "seven-sofa'd chamber" without stripping in public in the training school.
- He approves of drinking in moderation. Wine "moistens the soul of man" and "lulls at once all cares to sleep", like mandragora, and kindles light-hearted thoughts as oil does a flame.
- His analogy is to plants. Too much rain keeps them from lifting their heads, while the right amount lets them stand erect and bear fruit.
- Over-large draughts make the legs reel and thoughts falter. His prescription is a "frequent mizzle of small glasses" (borrowing Gorgias's language), which leads to "sportive levity" and not drunkenness.

## Socrates on Wisdom, the Soul and Pandering (the Art of the Go-Between)

- At Callias's challenge that each guest name what he is proudest of, Socrates answers "On pandering", with a solemn face. He says he could make a fortune at it if he chose.
- His step-by-step argument, each step agreed by the company ("Without a doubt"):
  - A good go-between makes the person he works on agreeable to others.
  - One step towards agreeableness is a becoming fashion of hair and dress.
  - The same eyes can dart a look of love or hate, and the same tongue can speak with modesty or boastfulness.
  - Some words carry hate and some tend to friendliness, so the good go-between teaches only what conduces to agreeableness.
  - The better go-between makes clients pleasing to many people, and one who could make them pleasing to the whole community would be an arch-go-between.
- He then names Antisthenes as the master of this art, since Antisthenes had introduced Callias to Prodicus and Hippias of Elis, and had brought Socrates and others together by praising each to the other.
- His conclusion: a man who can see two natures made to be of service to each other, and make them mutually enamoured, is the sort who "should weld together states in friendship; cement alliances". Such a person is "a treasure worth possessing" to friends, intimates and partisans in war.
- On the gods: asked by Hermogenes' account of divine friendship what service he pays them, Socrates approves his answer (thanks, speaking well of them, never falsifying an oath). He says: "the gods themselves, it would appear, delight in nobleness of soul."

## Socrates on Beauty as Fitness for Function

- In the mock beauty contest with Critobulus, Socrates questions him first. Critobulus agrees that beauty belongs not only to people but to horses, oxen, shields, swords and spears.
- The shared test they reach: things are beautiful "if well constructed by the hand of man to suit the sort of work for which we got them, or if naturally adapted to satisfy some want".
- Socrates applies this test to his own features:
  - Eyes serve vision, and his prominent eyes see aslant as well as straight ahead, so they are more beautiful than Critobulus's. By the same reasoning the crab has the loveliest eyes, being the toughest.
  - His wide, flat nostrils welcome scents from every quarter, while Critobulus's nostrils point to the earth.
  - A snub nose does not act as a barrier between the eyes, while a towering nose is "an insulting wall of partition".
  - His thick lips give a softer kiss.
- The jury votes in secret and every vote goes to Critobulus. Socrates remarks that Critobulus's beauty, unlike Callias's money, "can corrupt both judge and jury".

## Socrates on Kissing, Desire and Self-Mastery

- Socrates says that Critobulus caught the fever of love for Cleinias at school, and that Critobulus's father consigned him to Socrates hoping Socrates could do something for him. He reports Critobulus's condition has improved.
- He suspects a kiss is the root of the worst of it, "than which love's flame asks no fiercer fuel", calling it insatiable and suggestive of mad fantasy.
- His advice: "let every one who wishes to be master of himself and sound of soul abstain from kisses imprinted on fair lips."
- Charmides teases that Socrates once sat head to head and shoulder to shoulder with Critobulus over a book. Socrates answers in jest that his shoulder ached for five days, like a bite from a wild beast, and that he felt a "scraping at the heart". He warns Critobulus not to touch him again until he has as thick a beard as head of hair.

## Socrates on Heavenly Love, Friendship and Love of the Soul

- Socrates calls Love a divinity as old as the everlasting gods yet youngest in form, whose shrine is in the soul. He says: "I cannot name the time at which I have not been in love with some one."
- He distinguishes two Aphrodites, whom he treats as separate in nature, with separate altars, shrines and sacrifices:
  - The earthly (common) Aphrodite is lighter and laxer, and sends the bodily loves.
  - The heavenly Aphrodite (Ourania) is purer and holier, and sends loves that "feed upon the soul, on friendship and on noble deeds".
- He tells Callias his love for Autolycus appears to be of the heavenly kind. He bases this on Autolycus's strength, hardihood, courage and sobriety of soul, and on the fact that Callias invites the boy's father to share in their company, hiding nothing from him.
- Why love of soul beats love of body:
  - Without friendship there is no society of any worth. Friendship based on admiration of the inner disposition is "a sweet and voluntary compulsion".
  - Bodily beauty reaches its prime quickly, then withers, and the affection based on it withers with it.
  - Bodily enjoyment brings surfeit, as meat does through repletion, but "the soul, with every step she makes in her onward course towards deeper wisdom, grows ever worthier of love". Its attachment, being pure, "knows no satiety".
  - The beloved cannot hate a lover who thinks him beautiful and good and cares more for his welfare than for selfish joys. Such a pair will share trust, forethought, joy in success and burdens in misfortune, and be more attentive when apart than when together.
  - The bodily lover gives himself what he wants and offers the beloved only reproach. He separates the beloved from his nearest friends. If he uses persuasion and not force, he corrupts the soul of the one who yields, and that is a deeper reason for loathing.
  - "Nothing harsh was ever yet engendered by attachment based on moral qualities", while shameless intercourse has been the source of countless hateful deeds.
  - A true educator who trains another in virtue deserves honour, as Cheiron and Phoenix were honoured by Achilles. The bodily suitor, always begging a kiss or caress, ends up treated as a beggar.
- His farm analogy: the lover of outward beauty is like a man on a short lease, who tries to take the largest crops without improving the land. The lover of the soul is like an owner of his own farmstead, who scours the world to find what will improve the value of his beloved.
- On the effect on the beloved: one who knows his beauty is enough to enthrall his lover grows careless and wanton. One who knows he must himself be truly good to keep affection becomes more studious of virtue.
- The lover who tries to make his beloved good is bound to practise virtue himself: "how can he hope to make his comrade good, if he himself works wickedness?"
- Myth as evidence: Zeus left the women he wed for their bodies mortal, but raised to immortality the heroes whose souls he admired (Heracles, the Dioscuri). Ganymede was taken to Olympus for his mind, as his name ("joy" and "counsel") suggests.
- Achilles avenged Patroclus as a comrade, and Orestes and Pylades, Theseus and Peirithous are celebrated for common great deeds done out of mutual admiration.
- He disputes Pausanias's claim that an army of lovers and beloved would be invincible. He asks how men who ignore censure and behave shamelessly to one another would feel more shame at shameful deeds. He says Thebans and Eleians pairing lovers in battle seems to suggest mistrust. The Spartans, by contrast, forbid lustful touching, so their young men are moulded to such virtue that they will not desert even foreign comrades, because Sparta worships "Reverence", not "Shamelessness".
- His closing test: ask to whom you would entrust money, your children, or a favour. Even a lover of outward beauty would entrust his precious things to the one with inward beauty of the soul.

## Socrates on Education for Public Virtue and Genuine Reputation

- Speaking to Callias about Autolycus, Socrates says the boy's ambition could be turned to benefiting friends and the fatherland, and to being remembered among Hellenes and barbarians. Such a boy would honour the one he regards as his bravest fellow-worker.
- To please him, Callias should study what made great statesmen great. He lists Themistocles' knowledge in freeing Hellas, Pericles' wit as the best adviser of his country, Solon's wisdom in framing Athens' laws, and the training that made the Spartans the best leaders.
- He admits his tone may be more serious than the wine-cup suits, but says "It has long been my wont to share our city's passion for noble-natured souls, alert and emulous in pursuit of virtue."
- When Callias asks Socrates, as go-between, to introduce him to the state, Socrates replies it will work if people see his loyalty to virtue is genuine and not "mere repute".
- His reasoning: "A false renown indeed is quickly seen for what it is worth, being tested; but true courage... perpetually amidst the storm and stress of circumstance pours forth a brighter glory."
