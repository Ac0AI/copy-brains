## The ideal city and the limits of what words can prove

Socrates treats the model city as a pattern, not a forecast. He compares it to a painter who draws a perfectly beautiful man and is "none the worse" for being unable to show that such a man ever lived.

- The earlier inquiry into justice aimed to build an ideal, so the just man could be judged by how closely he resembles it. It was never meant to prove the ideal could exist.
- He asks: "whether ideals are ever fully realized in language? Does not the word express more than the fact?" The actual always falls short of the ideal.
- So he asks only for an approximation. If a city can be governed nearly as described, the possibility has been shown.
- He asks for the fewest and slightest changes, ideally one thing, or two at most.
- He admits the proposals are hard to defend. He is "a hesitating enquirer", and to carry on an argument that way is "a dangerous and slippery thing". His fear is not ridicule but that he will "miss the truth where I have most need to be sure of my footing, and drag my friends after me in my fall".
- He says he would rather risk being "a deceiver about beauty or goodness or justice in the matter of laws" among enemies than among friends. He thinks deceiving about these things is worse than involuntary homicide.
- He compares those who daydream about their wishes with those who skip the question of possibility. They assume the wish is granted and plan from there, which does little good for "a capacity which was never good for much".

## Different natures: why women can be guardians

Socrates argues that women can take the same pursuits as men by examining what "sameness of nature" means.

- The objection is that different natures need different pursuits, and men's and women's natures differ. Socrates replies that this is a verbal contradiction. Many people argue from words because they "cannot define and divide, and so know that of which [they are] speaking".
- He says "glorious is the power of the art of contradiction!" Disputing in a spirit of contention is not reasoning.
- He gives a test case: bald and hairy men are different, but it would be a jest to forbid hairy men to be cobblers if bald men are. Nature differs only where the difference affects the pursuit in question.
- A physician and one who is "in mind a physician" share a nature. A physician and a carpenter do not.
- The sexes differ only in that women bear children and men beget them. That is no reason to give them different education.
- The real tests of a gifted nature are these: one person learns easily and another with difficulty, one discovers much from a little learning, and one has a body that serves the mind rather than hindering it.
- Some women have a gift for healing, music, gymnastic, philosophy or spirit, and others do not. Men and women have the same qualities of a guardian, "they differ only in their comparative strength or weakness".
- Women who have these qualities should be chosen as companions and colleagues of men like them.
- Socrates grants Glaucon the general claim that women are weaker, but not that any pursuit is closed to them. The lighter labours go to the women.
- His dog analogy: guard dogs, male and female alike, share in hunting and watching, and the only difference is strength. The same duties require the same nurture and education, namely music and gymnastic.

## Custom, ridicule and the standard of the good

Socrates expects the proposals to look ridiculous, such as women exercising naked with the men. He answers by pointing out that the standard of laughter changes while the standard of the good stays fixed.

- The Hellenes once thought a naked man ridiculous, as barbarians still do. When the Cretans and then the Lacedaemonians introduced the custom, wits mocked them.
- Experience showed that uncovering was better. Reason prevailed over the "ludicrous effect to the outward eye".
- The man who mocks anything but folly and vice is a fool. A man should not weigh the beautiful by any standard but the good.
- Of the one who laughs at women exercising "from the best of motives", he says he is "plucking a fruit of unripe wisdom". He does not know what he is laughing at.
- His maxim: "the useful is the noble and the hurtful is the base."
- He argues the practice is beneficial as well as possible. The same education makes men and women good guardians, the guardians are the best citizens, and "there can be nothing better" for the State than that men and women be as good as possible.
- The present practice of excluding women is "a violation of nature". Their virtue "will be their robe".

## Unity as the greatest good: common wives, children and property

Socrates asks what the legislator's chief aim should be. He answers that discord is the greatest evil and the bond of unity the greatest good.

- A city is unified when citizens share pleasures and pains. Disunity comes from disagreement about "mine" and "not mine".
- The best-ordered State is the one where the greatest number apply "mine" and "not mine" to the same things.
- His model is the body. When a finger is hurt, the whole frame feels it and we say the man has a pain in his finger. Likewise, when one citizen fares well or badly, the whole State should rejoice or grieve with him.
- Names matter, but only if they are meant. In other States rulers are masters and the ruled are slaves. In his city the people are "saviours and helpers" and the rulers "maintainers and foster-fathers". Guardians call one another fellow-guardians and treat every citizen as brother, sister, parent or child. They must act in the spirit of those names, "for what can be more ridiculous than... to utter the names of family ties with the lips only".
- Common wives and children, together with common property, are meant to make the guardians "more truly guardians". The proposal is that no parent knows his own child, nor any child his parent.
- Socrates says this would end suits, quarrels over money or children, and flattery of the rich by the poor. He also says it would end the burdens of finding money to raise a family, and trials for assault.
- Equals defending themselves against equals is honourable. Elders rule and chastise the younger, and the younger are held back by shame and fear. Shame restrains them from striking those like parents, and fear that others will rescue the injured one.
- He claims the guardians' life will be blessed "as the life of Olympic victors and yet more blessed", since their victory is the salvation of the whole State.
- He repeats that the city aims at the happiness "not of any particular class, but of the whole". A guardian who tries to appropriate the city for himself will learn Hesiod's saying, "half is more than the whole".
- He admits his main proposals are open to doubt, and he handles utility first and possibility second. He notes that the possibility of common wives and children "will be very much disputed".

## Breeding, marriage regulation and the use of useful falsehood

Here Socrates describes a controlled system of mating for the guardian class, modelled on the breeding of dogs and birds.

- If breeders take care to pair the best with the best, "what consummate skill will our rulers need if the same principle holds of the human species".
- He says rulers will need "a considerable dose of falsehood and deceit" for the good of their subjects, as a doctor uses medicine rather than mere regimen. The best of either sex should unite as often as possible and the inferior as seldom as possible.
- The arrangement must stay secret to the rulers, or the guardians may rebel. Mating takes place at festivals with sacrifices and hymns. "Ingenious... lots" are drawn by the less worthy so they blame luck and not the rulers.
- The braver youth get more access to women, and such fathers "ought to have as many sons as possible".
- The offspring of good parents go to a nursery with nurses in a separate quarter. Inferior offspring, or those that are deformed, are put away "in some mysterious, unknown place". Mothers are not allowed to recognise their own children.
- The prime of life is about twenty years for a woman (bearing children from age twenty to forty) and thirty for a man (from twenty-five to fifty-five). Those outside these ages who beget children without rulers' sanction are said to act unholily, raising a bastard "uncertified and unconsecrated".
- After those ages people may range freely, except for parent-child and grandparent-grandchild unions. Any embryo conceived must not be allowed to see light, and any child that does is not to be maintained.
- Kinship is by birth timing. Children born in the seventh to tenth month after a wedding are called sons and daughters by the husband of that wedding. Those born then are brothers and sisters and generally may not marry, though the lot and the Pythian oracle may permit it.

## War and the conduct of Hellenes towards Hellenes

Socrates sets rules for guardians in war, built on the distinction between discord among kin and war against foreigners.

- Children should be taken to war as spectators, as a potter's boy watches before touching the wheel. The presence of their young is "the greatest incentive to valour" in parents. Risk is acceptable if it makes them better.
- They go only on safe expeditions under experienced veterans, and are mounted on tractable, swift horses so they can escape.
- A soldier who deserts his rank or throws away his arms is degraded to husbandman or artisan. One who is captured is given to the enemy. A hero is crowned by comrades and given the right hand of fellowship. Anyone he wishes to kiss may not refuse while the expedition lasts. He is also honoured with seats of precedence, meats and full cups. Those who die gloriously are "of the golden race" and are revered at their graves.
- Hellenes should not enslave Hellenes, and should spare Hellenic cities, "considering the danger... that the whole race may one day fall under the yoke of the barbarians".
- They should not strip the dead beyond the armour. Despoiling gives cowards an excuse to skulk, and robbing a corpse is "illiberality and avarice". He compares it to a dog quarrelling with the stones that strike it. The dead should not be hindered from burial, and spoils taken from Hellenes should not be offered at temples.
- He forbids devastating Hellenic land or burning houses, and would "take the annual produce and no more".
- His distinction: Hellenes and barbarians are natural enemies, so their fighting is "war". Hellenes fighting Hellenes are natural friends, so it is "discord". In discord they act as "correctors, not enemies" and fight "as those who intend some day to be reconciled".
- He argues the guilt of war is confined to a few and the many are friends. Hostility lasts only until the innocent sufferers compel the guilty few to give satisfaction.

## Philosophers must rule: the third wave

Socrates names the proposal he thinks will be received with the greatest laughter and holds that it is the one change that could reform the State.

- His statement: "Until philosophers are kings, or the kings and princes of this world have the spirit and power of philosophy, and political greatness and wisdom meet in one, and those commoner natures who pursue either to the exclusion of the other are compelled to stand aside, cities will never have rest from their evils,—nor the human race, as I believe."
- Only then will his State "have a possibility of life and behold the light of day". He believes that in no other State can there be happiness, private or public.
- He braces for anger. Glaucon warns that many respectable people will run at him with weapons, and he agrees to defend the claim by saying whom he means by a philosopher. Some natures should study philosophy and lead, and others are born to be followers.

## Who is a true philosopher: knowledge, opinion and the many beautiful things

Socrates defines the philosopher by love of the whole of wisdom and by the ability to see the one form behind the many particulars.

- A true lover loves the whole of what he loves, not a part. Socrates gives parallels: a lover of the young finds a reason to like every flower of youth, a lover of wine takes any wine, and an ambitious man takes any honour. Likewise the philosopher desires "the whole" of wisdom, has a taste for every sort of knowledge, is curious to learn and "is never satisfied".
- Someone who dislikes learning, especially in youth, is not a philosopher, like one who refuses food and is not hungry.
- Glaucon objects that sight-lovers and music-lovers are curious too. Socrates says they are "only an imitation". True philosophers are "lovers of the vision of truth".
- The distinction is between the many and the one. Beauty and ugliness are two, and each is one. Just and unjust, good and evil are also each one, but in combination with actions and things they appear many.
- Lovers of sounds and sights love fine tones, colours and forms, but their minds cannot see or love absolute beauty. Few can attain it.
- The one who knows beautiful things but not absolute beauty is "a dreamer", one who "likens dissimilar things, who puts the copy in the place of the real object". The one who distinguishes the idea from the objects that participate in it is "wide awake".
- Knowledge and opinion are separate faculties with separate subject-matters. He defines a faculty by its sphere and its result, and the same sphere and result make the same faculty.
- Knowledge is "the mightiest of all faculties" and is infallible. Opinion can err. Knowledge is of being, and the utterly non-existent is utterly unknown.
- Opinion is neither knowledge nor ignorance. It lies between them: "darker than knowledge, but lighter than ignorance". Its objects lie between being and not-being.
- The many beautiful things are always also ugly in some respect, and the many doubles are also halves. These are like riddles told at feasts, which cannot be fixed as being, not-being, both, or neither. This is the "intermediate flux" caught by opinion.
- So those who see many just things but not absolute justice have opinion but not knowledge. Those who see the "absolute and eternal and immutable" know.
- He calls the former "lovers of opinion" and the latter lovers of wisdom. He tells them not to be angry, because "no man should be angry at what is true".
