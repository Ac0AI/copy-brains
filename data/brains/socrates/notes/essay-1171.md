## Why Socrates did not rehearse a defence and welcomed death

- Asked by Hermogenes why he was not preparing his defence, Socrates answered: "What! do I not seem to you to have spent my whole life in meditating my defence?"
- His reasoning: "By a lifelong persistence in doing nothing wrong, and that I take to be the finest practice for his defence which a man could devise."
- Hermogenes warned that Athenian juries often kill the innocent and acquit the guilty, swayed by pity or a charming phrase. Socrates still would not craft a plea.
- He said twice he began to consider his defence and twice his divine sign (the "divinity") hindered him. He took this to mean it seemed better to God that he die at once.
- He claimed no man had lived better than he had: "what can exceed the pleasure, which has been mine, of knowing that my whole life has been spent holily and justly?" His friends' opinion of him echoed this self-approval.

## Old age as the evil that death spares him

- If his life were prolonged, he expected the penalties of age: dimmer sight, duller hearing, slower learning, faster forgetting.
- He added the "sting of self-reproach" of conscious failing powers, and asked what joy in living would remain.
- He read the divine hindrance as kindness: God letting him close his life "in the ripeness of age, and by the gentlest of deaths."
- Had he secured acquittal, he would have prepared for himself not release but an end "wasted by disease, or by old age, on which a confluent stream of evil things most alien to joyousness converges."

## How a good death benefits friends

- Socrates held that a death by sentence is the easiest kind and gives friends the least trouble.
- It also produces the deepest longing for the departed. A man is remembered with regret and longing when he leaves nothing unseemly behind, but, "sound of body, and his soul still capable of friendly repose, fades tranquilly away."

## Refusing to beg for life

- He would display no ardent zeal for acquittal. If openly stating the blessings he owed to gods and men, and his honest opinion of himself, wearied the court, he would "choose death rather than supplicate in servile sort for leave to live a little longer merely to gain a life impoverished in place of death."
- When asked to name a counter-penalty, he refused and would not let friends do it, saying that proposing one is like a confession of guilt.
- When friends planned to steal him out of prison, he treated it as a jest, asking whether they knew "some place outside Attica where death was forbidden to set foot?"

## Answering the charge of impiety and the divine voice

- On not recognising the state's gods: he was regularly seen sacrificing at common festivals and public altars.
- On "novel divinities": his divine voice is no more novel than birds' cries, human utterances, thunder or the priestess at Pytho. All are voices through which the god, who has foreknowledge, premonishes whom he will.
- He says he speaks "of a divinity" more exactly and reverentially than those who ascribe the power to birds.
- His proof: though he reported heaven's counsels to many friends, he was never shown to be "a deceiver or deceived."
- He cited Apollo's oracle through Chaerephon (no man more liberal, upright or temperate), and urged the jury to test it point by point rather than accept it on the god's word.

## The evidence he offers for his own virtue

- Least enslaved to bodily appetites; takes no gifts or pay, so more independent of spirit.
- Most just, being so content with what he has that others' possessions excite no craving.
- Wise, because from the time he understood speech he never stopped inquiring into and learning every good thing in his power.
- Many citizens pursuing virtue, and many strangers, choose his company, and many give gifts though he cannot repay them. Many acknowledge debts of gratitude to him and none dun him.
- During the siege he lived no worse than in the city's prosperity, and he has the "dainties of the soul more sweet" than market delicacies, procured without expense.

## Answering the charge of corrupting the young

- He challenged Meletus to name anyone changed under his influence from religious to irreligious, sober to prodigal, moderate to drunkard, or hardworking to effeminate.
- Meletus said he persuaded young men to obey him over their fathers. Socrates admitted it for education, which he has made his study.
- His analogies: a man obeys his doctor over his parents in health, the assembly obeys the wisest arguments over relatives, and generals are chosen for military wisdom, not kinship.
- He found it strange that the best get exclusive preference everywhere else, yet he is prosecuted on a capital charge for being chosen as an adept in education, "the greatest treasure men possess."

## After the verdict: serenity, and who is the real loser

- He did not hold his head lower: nothing proved he sacrificed to novel gods or swore by other gods. Habituating the young to "manliness and frugality" could not corrupt them, and no accuser alleged a capital crime like temple robbery or betrayal of the state.
- Dying innocent is "a blot not upon me but upon those who condemned me." Perjurers and those who heeded them must be conscious of deep impiety and injustice.
- He took consolation in Palamedes, a nobler theme of song than Odysseus who wrongly killed him, and expected time to testify that he "never wronged another at any time or ever made a worse man of him," teaching gratuitously every good thing he could.
- To weeping followers: "Do you not know that for many a long day, ever since I was born, sentence of death was passed upon me by nature?" If he dies prematurely amid life's blessings, grief is fitting, but dying on the eve of troubles is good fortune to rejoice in.
- To Apollodorus ("hardest to see you put to death unjustly"): "Would you have been better pleased, my dear one, to see me put to death for some just reason rather than unjustly?"
- Of Anytus: whichever of the two "has achieved what is best and noblest for all future time is the real victor in this suit." He prophesied, as the dying do in Homer, that Anytus's son would not stay in his father's tanning trade and would fall into base passion without a guardian.
