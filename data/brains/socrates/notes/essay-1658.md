## Why Socrates will not rely on the argument that the soul outlasts the body

Simmias and Cebes raise two objections after the first arguments for the soul's survival. Socrates invites them rather than resisting: if they still doubt, "do not hesitate to say exactly what you think," since "there are many points still open to suspicion and attack."

- **Simmias (harmony objection):** the soul might be like the harmony of a lyre. It is invisible and divine, yet it perishes when the strings break, so it may dissolve when the body's hot, cold, wet and dry elements fall out of balance.
- **Cebes (weaver objection):** a weaver outlives many coats yet is outlived by the last one. By analogy, a soul may wear out many bodies and still perish at last. Strength and longevity do not prove immortality, and Cebes says confidence about death is foolish unless the soul is proven "altogether immortal and imperishable."
- Socrates calls Simmias's point forceful ("there is force in his attack upon me") and asks Cebes to state his difficulty before answering either.
- He compares his own calm to the swans, who sing most "lustily" before dying, and says he would not go out of life "less merrily than the swans."
- Simmias gives a standard for hard questions: either discover or be taught the truth, or take "the best and most irrefragable of human theories" as a raft to sail through life on, "not without risk."

## Avoiding misology: hatred of arguments

When the company is shaken by the objections, Socrates warns against becoming "misologists," haters of ideas. He says nothing worse can happen to a man.

- **Misanthropy comes from inexperience.** A person trusts others too much, is betrayed again and again, and ends by hating everyone. Experience shows "few are the good and few the evil, and the great majority are in the interval between them."
- Extremes are rare in everything (very large or very small men, swift or slow, fair or foul), and "if there were a competition in evil, the worst would be found to be very few."
- **Misology works the same way.** A person without skill in dialectic believes an argument, then finds it false, then another, until he decides all arguments are unsound. Great disputers think they alone see that everything is "going up and down in never-ceasing ebb and flow," like the currents of the Euripus.
- The error is to blame arguments instead of oneself: "Rather say that we have not yet attained to soundness in ourselves." A man who does this "should lose truth and the knowledge of realities."
- Socrates calls the loss of faith in reasoning "very melancholy" and urges that one struggle "manfully" to gain health of mind.

## Arguing to convince himself, not to win

Socrates says he does not have "the temper of a philosopher" here but is like a partisan, and explains the difference.

- A partisan cares nothing for the rights of the question and only wants to convince his hearers.
- Socrates seeks first "to convince myself; to convince my hearers is a secondary matter."
- He argues that he gains either way. If the soul survives, he does well to believe the truth. If nothing follows death, he will not distress his friends with lamentations in the time left, and "my ignorance will not last, but will die with me."
- He asks the others to think "of the truth and not of Socrates": agree if he speaks truly, and "withstand me might and main" if not, so that he does not deceive them and himself "like the bee, leave my sting in you before I die."
- He restates each objection accurately before answering, to make sure nothing escapes the discussion.

## Refuting the soul-as-harmony theory

Socrates answers Simmias with several arguments, using Simmias's own commitments.

- **Contradiction with recollection.** Simmias still holds that knowledge is recollection and that the soul existed before the body. A harmony comes after its parts (lyre, strings, sounds exist first, "and then harmony is made last of all, and perishes first"), so the soul cannot both pre-exist the body and be its harmony. Simmias keeps recollection because it rests on demonstration, while the harmony theory "rests only on probable and plausible grounds." He adds that arguments from probability are "impostors."
- **Harmony follows, the soul leads.** A harmony cannot act against its parts or "lead" them, only follow. Yet the soul opposes the body constantly: she resists drinking when the body is thirsty and eating when it is hungry, and she disciplines desires, passions and fears.
- Homer is cited as seeing this: Odysseus "beat his breast, and thus reproached his heart: Endure, my heart."
- **Degrees.** A harmony can be more or less fully harmonized, but no soul is more a soul than another. If virtue were harmony and vice discord, no soul would have more vice or virtue than another, and every soul of every creature would be equally good. That is plainly false.
- Socrates concludes the soul is no harmony, but "a far diviner thing," ruler of the body and especially "the wise soul."

## His intellectual history: from natural science to the Forms

To answer Cebes, Socrates tells how he came to his method.

- As a young man he pursued the study of nature: what the causes of generation and destruction are, whether we think with blood, air, fire or the brain. He became so confused that he "forgot" things he thought self-evident, such as that eating makes a man grow.
- He could no longer explain simple things: why one plus one makes two (addition?) while dividing one also makes two (separation?).
- He hoped Anaxagoras, who said mind orders all things, would show that each thing is as it is because that is best. He "would not have sold [these hopes] for a large sum of money."
- He was disappointed: Anaxagoras fell back on air, ether and water. Socrates compares this to explaining why he sits in prison by his bones and muscles and joints, forgetting the true cause: "the Athenians have thought fit to condemn me, and accordingly I have thought it better and more right to remain here and undergo my sentence."
- **Cause versus condition.** Bones and muscles are conditions without which he could not act, but they are not the cause. The many, "feeling about in the dark," mistake one for the other. They ignore "the obligatory and containing power of the good."
- Failing to find the cause of things, he took a "second best" course: avoid blinding the soul by looking at things directly through the senses, as one avoids damaging the eyes by staring at an eclipse, and look at them through thought and reasoning (the "image reflected in the water").

## The method of hypothesis and the safe answer about causes

Socrates explains the method he adopted.

- Assume the strongest principle (an absolute beauty, goodness, greatness), affirm whatever agrees with it, and reject what disagrees.
- **The "safe" answer:** things are beautiful only by participating in absolute beauty: "by beauty beautiful things become beautiful." He is unsure how participation works but is "assured" it is the cause. Colour or shape as the source of beauty only confuses him.
- The same applies to size: A is not taller than B "by a head," since the head is small and the same for both. Ten exceeds eight by number, not by two, and two comes about by participating in duality, not by addition or division.
- When challenged, do not argue with the challenger until you have checked whether the consequences of the principle agree with one another. If asked for an explanation of the principle, assume "a higher principle, and a higher, until you found a resting-place in the best of the higher."
- Do not confuse principle with consequences "like the Eristics," who enjoy turmoil in their ideas and do not care about reality.
- A refinement: instead of the "safe and stupid" answer (heat makes things hot), give the "far superior" one: fire makes things hot, fever makes the body diseased, the monad makes odd numbers.

## The final proof that the soul is immortal

Socrates builds the proof on opposites that exclude each other.

- Essential opposites never admit their contrary: greatness will not become small. Some things that are not themselves opposites still carry an opposite and will not admit its contrary. Three is not oddness, yet it is always odd and "will endure annihilation or anything sooner than be converted into an even number." Fire resists cold, snow resists heat, and each either withdraws or perishes.
- The soul brings life to whatever she possesses. Life's opposite is death, so the soul will never admit death. What does not admit death is "the immortal."
- If the odd were imperishable, three would be imperishable, and the snow and the fire would retreat unharmed. So if the immortal is also imperishable, the soul, "attacked by death," cannot perish but retires safe and sound.
- Cebes is convinced. Simmias still feels uncertain "when I think of the greatness of the subject and the feebleness of man."
- Socrates answers that first principles, "even if they appear certain, should be carefully considered," and then one may follow the argument with "a sort of hesitating confidence in human reason."

## Care of the soul and the myth of the afterlife

Socrates draws the practical consequences of the soul's immortality.

- If death were the end, the wicked would have "a good bargain" in being quit of their evil. Since the soul is immortal, "there is no release or salvation from evil except the attainment of the highest virtue and wisdom," and the soul takes with her only "nurture and education."
- The care owed to the soul covers eternity, not only "the portion of time which is called life."
- Souls are guided after death. The wise and orderly soul follows the straight path. The soul that desired the body "wanders in extremity of evil" and no one will be her companion.
- Judgment divides souls: the incurable (great sacrilege, foul murders) go to Tartarus and never leave. Those with great but curable crimes, such as violence to a parent done in anger and repented for life, suffer a year in Tartarus and must then win pardon from the victims on the Acherusian lake. Those pre-eminent for holiness go to a purer earth, and those who purified themselves with philosophy live "altogether without the body."
- He does not claim the details are exactly true: "A man of sense ought not to say, nor will I be very confident." But given that the soul is immortal, he may think that "something of the kind is true. The venture is a glorious one."
- Hence he tells a man to be of good cheer if he has cast away bodily pleasures and adorned the soul "in her own proper jewels, temperance, and justice, and courage, and nobility, and truth." His conclusion: "Fair is the prize, and the hope great!"

## His last instructions and his death

Socrates' final words and conduct show the same convictions in practice.

- To Crito, who asks what to do for his children and how to bury him: "take care of yourselves; that is a service which you may be ever rendering to me and mine." Burial may be as they like, but Crito mistakes the dead body for Socrates. He should say he is "burying my body only," since "false words are not only evil in themselves, but they infect the soul with evil."
- He bathes beforehand so that the women need not wash his body. He sends the weeping women away, saying "a man should die in peace."
- When Crito urges delay because the sun is still on the hills, he refuses: he would be "ridiculous in my own eyes for sparing and saving a life which is already forfeit."
- He treats the jailer kindly, calling him "charming," and prays to the gods to prosper his journey, then drinks the poison "quite readily and cheerfully."
- When his friends break into tears, he rebukes them: "Be quiet, then, and have patience."
- His last words: "Crito, I owe a cock to Asclepius; will you remember to pay the debt?"
- Phaedo's verdict is that he was "the wisest and justest and best" of the men of his time.
