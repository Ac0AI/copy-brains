## Why Socrates says he is "wise": the oracle and human wisdom

- Socrates traces his reputation to "a certain sort of wisdom which I possess": "such wisdom as is attainable by man."
- His friend Chaerephon asked the Delphic oracle whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The Pythian prophetess answered that no man was wiser.
- Socrates says he knows he has "no wisdom, small or great." He reasons that the god "cannot lie," so he treats the riddle as something to test.
- His conclusion: "God only is wise." The oracle means that human wisdom is "little or nothing." The wisest person is one who, "like Socrates, knows that his wisdom is in truth worth nothing."
- He contrasts his own claim with the "superhuman wisdom" the Sophists profess, which he says he does not have.

## The investigation: testing politicians, poets and craftsmen

- Socrates tried to refute the oracle by finding someone wiser. He went to those with a reputation for wisdom.
- **Politicians:** The first seemed wise to many and wiser still to himself, but was not. Socrates told him so and made an enemy. His reasoning on leaving: "he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows. I neither know nor think that I know." That small difference is his advantage.
- **Poets:** He asked them to explain their own most elaborate passages. Nearly any bystander could have explained them better. He concluded poets write "not by wisdom" but "by a sort of genius and inspiration," like diviners who say fine things without understanding them. They also thought themselves wise in other matters.
- **Artisans:** They really did know many fine things he did not. But because they were good at their craft, they believed they also knew "high matters," and that error overshadowed their wisdom.
- Result: those most in repute were "all but the most foolish," and some lesser men were wiser. He calls the labor "Herculean" and says it left the oracle "irrefutable."
- Cost: many dangerous enemies, and a reputation for wisdom, because listeners assume he possesses the wisdom he finds missing in others.

## Why the accusations exist, and how he answers them

- He separates old slanderers (Aristophanes' comedy, rumors spread since the jurors' childhood) from the formal accusers Meletus (for the poets), Anytus (for the craftsmen) and Lycon (for the rhetoricians).
- The old charge: he studies the heavens and earth and "makes the worse appear the better cause." He denies any interest in natural philosophy and asks the audience to say whether they ever heard him discuss it.
- He denies teaching for pay. His proof is his poverty ("my poverty is a sufficient witness"). He notes Evenus of Paros charges five minae and says that if he had such knowledge he would be proud.
- Young men of leisure follow him and imitate his questioning. Those they refute blame Socrates. When asked what he teaches that corrupts, they repeat stock charges about "things up in the clouds and under the earth," since they do not want to admit their pretended knowledge was exposed.
- He says he has no disciples. Anyone, rich or poor, may listen, and he never taught anyone anything in private that the public did not hear.
- He argues that the hatred he provokes is itself evidence he is telling the truth.

## Cross-examining Meletus: logic against the indictment

- **Who improves youth?** Meletus says everyone improves the young except Socrates. Socrates compares this to horses, where one trainer improves and most others harm. Experts are few. Meletus has shown he has never thought about the young.
- **Intentional corruption is absurd.** The good do their neighbors good and the bad do them harm, and no one wants to be harmed. So Socrates would not knowingly corrupt those he lives among. If the harm was unintentional, the law does not punish it. Meletus should have privately taught and warned him, since a court is "a place not of instruction, but of punishment."
- **Atheism versus "new divinities."** Meletus calls him a complete atheist, which contradicts the indictment's claim that he introduces new gods. Socrates points out that the claim that the sun is stone is from Anaxagoras, whose books were sold cheaply (one drachma at most).
- **Spirits imply gods.** No one believes in horsemanship without horses. Believing in spiritual agencies means believing in spirits, who are children of gods. Denying gods while affirming demigods is like affirming mules while denying horses and asses.
- He says the real danger is not Meletus or Anytus but "the envy and detraction of the world, which has been the death of many good men."

## Duty over safety: the philosopher's mission

- A good man "ought not to calculate the chance of living or dying" but only whether he is acting rightly or wrongly. He cites Achilles, who feared "to live in dishonor" more than death.
- He kept his post as a soldier at Potidaea, Amphipolis and Delium. He says he would be a deserter if he abandoned the god's command to philosophize out of fear.
- If acquitted on condition that he stop philosophizing, he would refuse: "I shall obey God rather than you, and while I have life and strength I shall never cease from the practice and teaching of philosophy."
- His standing exhortation: why care so much for "money and honor and reputation," and so little for "wisdom and truth and the greatest improvement of the soul"? He examines and cross-examines anyone who claims to care, and reproaches those who overvalue the less and undervalue the greater.
- Core claim: "virtue is not given by money, but ... from virtue come money and every other good of man, public as well as private."
- He calls himself a gadfly given to the state by the god, which is "like a great and noble steed" made sluggish by its size. Killing him means the city will "sleep on" unless the god sends another.
- His neglect of his own affairs and his poverty over many years show he acts from duty. He compares himself to a father or elder brother urging people toward virtue.

## Why he avoided politics, and two acts of courage

- His divine sign (a voice since childhood) only ever forbids him and never commands. It stopped him entering politics.
- He believes a man who honestly fights injustice against a multitude cannot survive long. To fight for the right even briefly, "he must have a private station and not a public one."
- **Arginusae:** As a senator, he alone among the presiding Prytanes opposed the illegal collective trial of the generals. He took the risk of imprisonment and death rather than join an injustice, "having law and justice with me."
- **The Thirty:** The oligarchs ordered him and four others to arrest Leon of Salamis. The other four went; Socrates "went quietly home." He says his only fear was "the fear of doing an unrighteous or unholy thing." He might have died had the Thirty not fallen soon after.
- He cites many witnesses: Crito and Critobulus, Plato, Apollodorus and others, including relatives of the supposedly corrupted, who support him "for the sake of truth and justice."

## Refusing to beg, and his reasoning about the sentence

- He will not bring his three sons or weep before the jury. He says it is dishonorable for a man of reputation, shames the city, and is wrong in principle, because a judge's duty "is, not to make a present of justice, but to give judgment" under oath. Begging would teach jurors to break their oaths, which would undermine the very piety he is accused of lacking.
- After conviction (he says a shift of thirty votes would have acquitted him), he proposes as his "due" free meals in the Prytaneum. He argues an Olympic victor gives "the appearance of happiness" while he gives "the reality."
- He refuses to propose imprisonment, exile or death. He does not know whether death is good or evil, so he will not propose something that is certainly an evil. He does not wrong himself, because he believes he never wronged anyone.
- Exile would not help: wherever he went, young men would follow and their elders would expel him.
- He cannot "hold my tongue" because it would disobey the god. He states: "the greatest good of man is daily to converse about virtue ... and the life which is unexamined is not worth living."
- He finally offers a fine of one mina, then thirty minae with Plato, Crito, Critobulus and Apollodorus as sureties.

## Death, injustice and his parting words

- Fear of death is "the pretence of wisdom," because it is "the appearance of knowing the unknown." No one knows whether death "may not be the greatest good." He calls this conceit of knowledge "a disgraceful sort of ignorance."
- What he does know: "injustice and disobedience to a better, whether God or man, is evil and dishonorable." He will not fear a possible good rather than a certain evil.
- He says he was convicted not for lack of words but because he refused to speak as juries expect, with weeping and wailing. He would "rather die having spoken after my manner, than speak in your manner and live."
- "The difficulty, my friends, is not in avoiding death, but in avoiding unrighteousness; for that runs faster than death." Age let death overtake him, while unrighteousness overtook his accusers.
- Prophecy to his judges: they killed him to escape having to account for their lives, but more and harsher accusers will follow. "The easiest and noblest way is not to be crushing others, but to be improving yourselves."
- His divine sign did not oppose him that day, not on leaving home, at court or in his speech. He takes this as evidence that what has happened is good.
- Two possibilities for death:
  - **Nothingness:** like a dreamless sleep, which even the Great King would rank above most of his days and nights. Then "eternity is then only a single night."
  - **Migration of the soul:** he would meet true judges (Minos, Rhadamanthus, Aeacus, Triptolemus), converse with Orpheus, Musaeus, Hesiod and Homer, and meet Palamedes and Ajax, who were also unjustly condemned. He would keep examining who is wise and who only pretends, and says they do not put a man to death for that there.
- Conclusion: "no evil can happen to a good man, either in life or after death." The gods do not neglect him.
- He is not angry with his accusers and condemners; they did him no harm, though they did not intend good.
- Last request: when his sons grow up, his friends should trouble them as he troubled others if they care for riches or anything more than virtue, or pretend to be something when they are nothing.
- His final line: "I to die, and you to live. Which is better God only knows."
