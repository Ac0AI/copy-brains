## Why Socrates censors tales of the afterlife and of grieving heroes

- Socrates argues that no one can be courageous while fearing death. Someone who believes the world below is "real and terrible" will not choose death in battle over defeat and slavery.
- He would strike lines such as "I would rather be a serf on the land of a poor and portionless man than rule over all the dead who have come to nought", plus the shrieking-shades and bat-in-a-cave images and names like Cocytus and Styx.
- The reason is not that these passages are unpoetical. Socrates says "the greater the poetical charm of them, the less are they meet for the ears of boys and men who are meant to be free, and who should fear slavery more than death."
- Poets should be asked to "commend the world below" rather than revile it.
- He grants that such tales may have some use, but warns they could make the guardians' nerves "too excitable and effeminate".

## The good man's equanimity and why laments are cut

- Socrates' principle is that a good man will not think death terrible for a good comrade, so he will not mourn a departed friend as if something terrible had happened.
- Such a man is "sufficient for himself and his own happiness", so he needs other people least. The loss of a son, a brother or his fortune therefore hits him least.
- He will lament least and bear misfortune with the greatest equanimity.
- Socrates would give the laments of famous men to women (not even good ones) and baser men, so that the guardians scorn to do likewise.
- He objects to Achilles rolling in ashes, Priam grovelling in the dirt, and gods lamenting over Sarpedon or Hector.
- If youth watch such unworthy scenes instead of laughing at them, they will excuse the same behaviour in themselves. They will lose shame and self-control and become men "always whining and lamenting on slight occasions."
- Excessive laughter should also be avoided, since it produces a violent reaction. Gods overcome by "inextinguishable laughter" are excluded too.

## Truth, the physician's lie and obedience to rulers

- Socrates says truth should be highly valued. A lie is useless to the gods and useful to men only as a medicine, so its use is restricted to physicians.
- If anyone may lie, it is the rulers, and only "for the public good", whether with enemies or their own citizens.
- A private man lying to the rulers is a worse fault than a patient hiding his illness from his physician, or a sailor hiding the state of the ship from the captain.
- Any craftsman caught lying (priest, physician or carpenter) is to be punished, because lying subverts a ship or a state.
- Temperance, as he defines it, consists mainly of obedience to commanders and self-control over sensual pleasures.
- He approves of "Friend, sit still and obey my word" and of soldiers marching in silent awe of their leaders. He rejects insults to rulers such as "O heavy with wine, who hast the eyes of a dog".
- He also rejects praise of feasting, Zeus overcome by lust, and tales of gods in adultery.
- He approves of tales of endurance, such as "Endure, my heart; far worse hast thou endured!"

## Against love of money and divine misconduct

- Guardians must not be "receivers of gifts or lovers of money". Socrates rejects the line "Gifts persuading gods, and persuading reverend kings".
- He rejects Phoenix advising Achilles to take the Greeks' gifts. He also rejects the idea that Achilles took payment for returning Hector's body, or defied Apollo and the river-god, or dragged Hector round the tomb.
- Achilles, he says, cannot be both mean and avaricious and also contemptuous of gods and men.
- Poets must not claim that heroes such as Theseus and Peirithous did dreadful things. They must say either that the deeds were not done, or that the heroes were not sons of gods.
- Stories that gods cause evil or that heroes are no better than men let people excuse their own vices and "engender laxity of morals among the young."
- On the happiness of the wicked, Socrates says poets who claim injustice pays when undetected, or that justice is "a man's own loss and another's gain", must be commanded to say the opposite. He will not settle this until it is found "what justice is, and how naturally advantageous to the possessor, whether he seem to be just or not."

## Imitation, narration and the single-role principle

- Socrates splits poetry into three styles: simple narration, where the poet speaks as himself (the dithyramb); pure imitation, where characters speak (tragedy and comedy); and a mix (epic).
- He rests his rule on the earlier principle that one man can do only one thing well. No one can imitate many things as well as one, and the same people cannot succeed at both tragedy and comedy.
- Guardians should imitate only the "courageous, temperate, holy, free". They should avoid imitating baseness, "lest from imitation they should come to be what they imitate".
- Imitations that begin in youth "grow into habits and become a second nature, affecting body, voice, and mind."
- They must not imitate women quarrelling or grieving, slaves, cowards, drunkards, madmen, smiths, or the neighing of horses, thunder or the sea.
- The good man will narrate mostly in his own voice and imitate a good man freely. He will imitate a worse character only briefly, or in jest.
- The worse a man is, the more he will imitate anything, including thunder, hail, dogs and sheep, "in right good earnest".
- The wholly imitative "pantomimic" poet would be anointed with myrrh, crowned with wool and sent to another city. The city keeps the "rougher and severer" poet who imitates only the virtuous.

## Music: harmonies, rhythms and the soul

- A song has three parts: words, melody and rhythm. The melody and rhythm must follow the words, not the reverse.
- The mournful Lydian harmonies are banished. The "relaxed" Ionian and Lydian are also banned as drinking harmonies.
- Socrates keeps only the Dorian (the strain of necessity, misfortune and courage) and the Phrygian (the strain of freedom, good fortune and temperance).
- He therefore needs no multi-stringed, many-scaled instruments and no flute-makers. Only the lyre and harp are allowed in the city, with a pipe for shepherds in the country. He prefers Apollo's instruments to Marsyas'.
- For rhythms he defers to Damon. He says rhythms should express "a courageous and harmonious life", and the foot and melody should fit the words.
- He holds that grace or its absence follows from good or bad rhythm, and style follows the temper of the soul. Beauty of style, harmony and grace depend on "the true simplicity of a rightly and nobly ordered mind", not the "simplicity" that is a euphemism for folly.
- The same standard applies to painting, weaving, architecture and sculpture. Guardians should not "grow up amid images of moral deformity, as in some noxious pasture", gathering "a festering mass of corruption in their own soul."
- Musical training is the most potent because "rhythm and harmony find their way into the inward places of the soul". A well-trained youth will love the good and hate the bad before he knows why. When reason arrives he will "recognise and salute the friend with whom his education has made him long familiar."
- The musical person must learn the forms of temperance, courage, liberality and magnificence, and their opposites, wherever they appear. Socrates compares this to knowing the letters of the alphabet so that one recognises them in any size or reflection.

## Love of beauty and the limits of pleasure

- Socrates says a beautiful soul in a beautiful form is the fairest of sights. The man of harmony loves the lovely, but he will tolerate a merely bodily defect in someone he loves, though not a discordant soul.
- Excess of pleasure is no ally of temperance or virtue. It deprives a man of his faculties as much as pain does.
- Sensual love is the keenest pleasure and the "madder".
- "True love is a love of beauty and order—temperate and harmonious", and intemperate pleasure must never come near the lover and beloved.
- He would make the lover treat the beloved as a father treats a son, for a noble purpose and with the other's consent. Going further would be coarseness and bad taste.
- Music should end in "the love of beauty".

## Gymnastic, simple living and why a city full of doctors and lawyers is a sign of decay

- Socrates believes the good soul improves the body, not the reverse. So he leaves detailed bodily care to the trained mind.
- Guardians must not get drunk. Ordinary athletes, who "sleep away their lives" and are endangered by small departures from regimen, are the wrong model. The guardians should be like "wakeful dogs", able to withstand changes of food, water and climate on campaign.
- His model is Homer's soldiers, who eat roast meat with no fish, no boiled dishes and no sweet sauces. He rejects Syracusan dinners, Sicilian cookery, Corinthian girls and Athenian confectionery.
- Simplicity in music breeds temperance of soul, and simplicity in gymnastic breeds health of body. Complexity breeds licence in the one and disease in the other.
- When intemperance and disease multiply, "halls of justice and medicine are always being opened". Needing first-rate lawyers and doctors even among the liberally educated is a disgrace. Prideful litigiousness is worse, because "to order his life as to be able to do without a napping judge is a far higher and nobler sort of thing."
- Men who fill themselves "with waters and winds, as if their bodies were a marsh" force doctors to invent names like flatulence and catarrh. Herodicus, a sickly trainer, invented "lingering death" and spent his life tending his illness.
- A carpenter wants a quick cure (emetic, purge, cautery or knife) because he has "no time to be ill" and his work gives his life its profit. Wealthy people think they have no such work.
- Excessive care of the body is "most inimical to the practice of virtue". It blocks management of a house or office, and "study or thought or self-reflection", since every headache is blamed on philosophy.
- Asclepius cured only those who were basically healthy and left the incurably sick, as he did not want to "lengthen out good-for-nothing lives". Socrates concludes that the city's medicine and law should serve better natures and let the bodily diseased die, and "the corrupt and incurable souls they will put an end to".

## Good judges, good physicians and the harmony of spirit and philosophy

- Socrates separates physicians from judges. The best physicians learn through experience of disease, even their own, because they cure the body with the mind.
- A judge governs mind by mind, so he should have had no experience of evil in his own soul when young. Good men seem simple in youth and are easily fooled for just that reason.
- The judge should be older and know evil "not from his own soul, but from late and long observation of the nature of evil in others: knowledge should be his guide, not personal experience."
- The clever criminal is shrewd among his own kind. Among honest men he looks foolish, because he has no pattern of honesty in himself. As Socrates puts it, "vice cannot know virtue too, but a virtuous nature, educated by time, will acquire a knowledge both of virtue and vice."
- Music and gymnastic are not for soul and body respectively. Both aim chiefly at improving the soul.
- Exclusive gymnastic yields hardness, ferocity and ignorance, a man who hates philosophy and "never uses the weapon of persuasion". Exclusive music yields softness and effeminacy.
- Spirit, if rightly educated, gives courage. Gentleness, rightly educated, makes a man moderate.
- The harmonious soul is temperate and courageous, and the inharmonious one is cowardly and boorish. Soft, melancholy music first tempers spirit like iron, then melts and wastes it.
- Two principles in human nature, the spirited and the philosophical, are matched by the two arts. The person who blends music and gymnastic in the fairest proportions is "the true musician and harmonist" and is always needed for a lasting government.

## Choosing rulers, testing guardians and the noble lie

- The elder should rule the younger, and the best should rule. The best guardians are those with the character of guardians, who love their city most because they regard its interests as identical with their own.
- Guardians are chosen as those who "show the greatest eagerness to do what is for the good of their country". They must be watched at every age to see that they never lose that conviction.
- A conviction can be lost willingly (one learns better) or unwillingly (one is deprived of truth). Socrates names three causes of unwilling loss: theft (argument or time steals it), force (pain or grief), and enchantment (pleasure or fear).
- Candidates are tested in youth with actions where they might forget or be deceived, then with toils and conflicts, then with terrors and pleasures, "more thoroughly than gold is proved in the furnace."
- Those who pass at every age become full guardians (rulers) and receive the highest honours in life and death. Those who fail are rejected. The younger soldiers become auxiliaries.
- Socrates proposes "one royal lie" which he is ashamed to tell. Citizens are told that their upbringing was a dream and that they were formed in the earth, so they must defend their land as a mother and treat fellow citizens as brothers.
- The second half is the myth of metals. God mixed gold into rulers, silver into auxiliaries, and brass and iron into farmers and craftsmen. Children usually resemble their parents, but sometimes a golden parent has a silver child, or the reverse.
- Rulers must guard the purity of the race, and if a child has an admixture of brass or iron, they must demote him without pity. Likewise, children of artisans with gold or silver in them are raised. The oracle says the state is destroyed if a man of brass or iron guards it.
- Socrates admits the present generation will not believe the tale, but their sons and descendants may. The belief will make them "care more for the city and for one another."

## Guardians' way of life: no property, no gold

- The auxiliaries must not become wolves among sheep. Socrates compares them to watch-dogs that, from poor discipline or hunger, turn on the flock. The best safeguard is true education, which should "civilize and humanize them."
- Their way of life is communal. They own nothing beyond what is necessary, have no private house or store closed to others, and live together in a mess like soldiers in a camp.
- They receive from the citizens a fixed rate of pay, enough for the year and no more.
- They are told that they have divine gold and silver in their souls, so they need no earthly coin. They alone may not touch or handle gold or silver, wear it, drink from it, or live under the same roof with it.
- This will be "their salvation", and they will be "the saviours of the State".
- If they acquire homes, lands or money they will become "housekeepers and husbandmen instead of guardians, enemies and tyrants instead of allies" of the citizens.
- They would then live in greater terror of internal enemies than of external ones, and ruin would follow.
