## Socrates's claim: no one can advise a state without knowledge, and Alcibiades lacks it

- Socrates tells the young Alcibiades he is about to advise the Athenian assembly. He asks whether Alcibiades knows the matter better than they do.
- A man is a good adviser because of knowledge, not riches, beauty or birth. Whether the counsellor is "rich or poor" does not matter. Athenians deliberating on health only require that he be a physician.
- Alcibiades learned only writing, the lyre and wrestling. None of these is the business of the assembly. He admits he cannot advise on building, divination or shipbuilding, since others know those better.
- Socrates says anyone who tries "teaching what you do not know, and have not taken any pains to learn, is downright insanity."
- Almost all Athenian statesmen are in the same position, "with the exception, perhaps of your guardian, Pericles."

## How Socrates exposes conceit of knowledge: the justice test

- Socrates asks what "better" means in deciding war and peace. Alcibiades cannot say, though he would readily say "more wholesome" about food. Socrates calls it disgraceful to profess knowledge and fail to answer.
- The answer comes to justice: whom to fight, when, and for how long. Alcibiades has no teacher of justice and never discovered it.
- Alcibiades thought he knew justice as a child, shouting that a playmate had cheated at dice. He never passed through a time of knowing he was ignorant, so he never inquired.
- Learning and discovery both require admitting ignorance first. A man will not examine what he thinks he already knows.
- The "many" cannot teach justice. Good teachers must agree with one another, and the many agree about wood and stone but quarrel about justice. Socrates cites the Iliad and Odyssey, and the battles of Tanagra and Coronea, where Alcibiades' father Cleinias died.
- Men do not go to war over the principles of health. They kill one another over justice, so the many clearly do not understand it.
- Socrates points out that in a question-and-answer exchange the answerer is the speaker. Alcibiades, not Socrates, said he knew nothing of the just and unjust.

## Perplexity as a sign of ignorance, and the worst kind of ignorance

- Alcibiades is asked whether the just is expedient. Through his own answers he concludes that it is:
  - All just things are honourable.
  - Honourable things are good, since a rescue in battle is honourable as courage and evil only in the wounds or death it risks.
  - The good is expedient.
- Alcibiades then admits: "I do not know what I am saying." He is "of different minds in successive instants."
- Socrates says the cause of self-contradiction is ignorance. Nobody wavers about whether he has two eyes or two hands.
- People are not perplexed about what they know they do not know, such as cookery or steering a ship. They leave it to the cook or the pilot.
- Only those who do not know and think they know make mistakes. That is "ignorance of the disgraceful sort which is mischievous."
- It is most disgraceful and harmful when it concerns the greatest matters: the just, the honourable, the good and the expedient.
- Socrates says Alcibiades is "wedded to ignorance of the most disgraceful kind" and rushes into politics before he is educated.

## Why Alcibiades must train: his real rivals are Sparta and Persia

- Alcibiades says he can beat the uneducated politicians "by the light of nature." Socrates replies that the contest is not with fellow citizens.
- A pilot must look to the enemy, not to the crew. Alcibiades' true rivals are the Spartan and Persian kings.
- Socrates describes the training of a Persian prince:
  - At seven he rides and hunts.
  - At fourteen he is given four royal teachers, the wisest, the justest, the most temperate and the most valiant.
  - They teach him the worship of the gods and the duties of kingship, to speak the truth, never to let any pleasure be lord over him, and to be fearless.
  - By contrast Pericles gave Alcibiades a slave, Zopyrus the Thracian, "past all other work."
- Spartan and Persian wealth far exceeds Athenian. Socrates says Alcibiades' mother's wardrobe was "not worth fifty minae" and Alcibiades has a 300-acre patch at Erchiae.
- Socrates imagines Persian queens being astonished that an uneducated youth, not yet twenty, relies on beauty, stature and birth against kings.
- He ends the passage by urging: "hear the Delphian inscription, 'Know thyself'," since rivals can only be overcome "by pains and skill."

## What it means to take care of oneself

- Socrates says all people greatly need "pains and care," himself included, and the pair must take counsel together.
- To take care of something means to improve it. Care of a thing differs from care of its belongings: shoemaking tends the shoe, gymnastic tends the foot.
- Taking care of one's possessions is therefore not taking care of oneself.
- One cannot know the art that makes a man better without knowing what a man is. So self-knowledge is the necessary start.
- Socrates admits it is unclear whether self-knowledge is easy or hard, but says "there is no other way."
- He argues that the user of a thing differs from the thing used. A shoemaker is not his tools or his hands, and a man uses his whole body.
- A man is therefore not his body, and neither the body nor the body and soul together can rule. So "the soul is man."
- Following this, Socrates says "he who bids a man know himself, would have him know his soul."
- It follows that:
  - A physician or trainer, as such, knows only the things of the body, not himself.
  - He who cherishes his body cherishes not himself, and he who cherishes money is further removed still.
  - These bodily arts are "vulgar" and not what a good man would practise.

## True love loves the soul, and warnings about the people

- Whoever loves Alcibiades' body loves only what belongs to him, and leaves when youth fades.
- The lover of the soul "goes not away, as long as the soul follows after virtue."
- Socrates says he loves Alcibiades for his own sake, "whereas other men love what belongs to you." Alcibiades' beauty is fading "just as your true self is beginning to bloom."
- Socrates' main fear is that Alcibiades will "become a lover of the people" and be ruined by them, as many a noble Athenian has been. The demus looks fair but "you should see him naked."
- His remedy is to learn what he ought to know before entering politics, which works as an antidote.

## Self-knowledge, wisdom and the mirror of the soul

- Socrates uses an analogy to sight. The eye sees itself only by looking into another eye, at the pupil where sight resides.
- The soul likewise must look at the soul, especially the part where wisdom and knowledge reside. That is the most divine part, so one who looks there and at things divine will best know himself.
- Self-knowledge is wisdom. Without it, one cannot know one's own good and evil.
- Not knowing oneself means not knowing one's belongings, other people's affairs, or the affairs of states. Such a man "can never be a statesman" or economist, will not know what he is doing, will err and be miserable, and so will those he governs.
- Socrates concludes that "not he who has riches, but he who has wisdom, is delivered from his misery."

## Virtue, not power, should be the aim of men and cities

- Cities do not need walls, triremes, docks, numbers or size "without virtue." A man cannot give citizens virtue he does not have.
- Anyone who wants to govern himself and the state must first acquire virtue. He needs "not power or authority" but "justice and wisdom."
- Power without understanding is dangerous, like a sick man free to do as he likes but without a physician's mind, or a ship's captain with no skill in navigation. All aboard will perish.
- Therefore "not tyrannical power... should be the aim either of individuals or states, if they would be happy, but virtue."
- Until a person has virtue, being commanded by a superior is better. Vice suits a slave and virtue a free man, so the slavery to be escaped is the slavery of ignorance and vice.
- Socrates says a man who acts justly and wisely acts "according to the will of God," and that he will be "security" for Alcibiades' happiness if he does.
- When Alcibiades says he will escape by Socrates' help, Socrates corrects him: it is "by the help of God."
- Alcibiades vows to follow Socrates as a disciple. Socrates replies that his love "breeds another love," then adds that he fears "the power of the state, which may be too much for both of us."
