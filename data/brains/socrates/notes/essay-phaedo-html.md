## Why Socrates calls philosophy the practice of dying

- Socrates tells his friends that true philosophers are "always pursuing death and dying," so they should not grieve when death arrives.
- He defines death as the separation of soul and body. The soul exists "in herself," released from the body, and the body is released from the soul.
- He says it would be a "ridiculous contradiction" to spend a lifetime trying to live as near as possible to a state of death and then complain when it comes.
- On his last day, he says he goes on his way "rejoicing." He hopes to reach what he has pursued all his life, and he believes this holds for anyone whose mind has been made ready and "in a manner purified."
- He defines purification as the soul gathering herself into herself out of the body, dwelling alone, and being released from the body's chains, in this life as far as she can.
- He treats reluctance to die as a test. A man who "repines" at death is "not a lover of wisdom, but a lover of the body," and probably a lover of money or power too.
- He would still be sad about death if he did not believe he is going to wise and good gods, and perhaps to departed men better than those he leaves behind (he is less sure of the second). He has "good hope" that something remains for the dead, "some far better thing for the good than for the evil."

## Why the philosopher turns away from the body

- Socrates holds that the true philosopher is concerned with the soul and not the body. He does not care for eating and drinking, sexual pleasure, costly clothes or sandals, or anything "more than nature needs."
- He says sight and hearing are "inaccurate witnesses," and that the soul is deceived whenever she inquires through the body.
- Thought is best when the mind is gathered into herself and nothing troubles her: no sounds, sights, pain or pleasure. She "takes leave of the body" and aspires after true being.
- Absolute justice, beauty, good, greatness, health and strength are never seen by the eyes or reached by any bodily sense. The nearest approach to knowing them comes from the person who goes to each "with the mind alone."
- He lists the body's costs to the search for truth:
  - It needs endless feeding.
  - It falls ill.
  - It fills us with "loves, and lusts, and fears, and fancies."
  - It takes away "the power of thinking at all."
  - It breaks in on every inquiry with "turmoil and confusion."
- He traces war to the body. "Wars are occasioned by the love of money, and money has to be acquired for the sake and in the service of the body." Factions and fighting come from the same source.
- His conclusion is that pure knowledge needs the soul to behold things in themselves. We make the nearest approach in this life when we have the least possible intercourse with the body and keep ourselves pure until God releases us.
- He adds that the impure are not permitted to approach the pure.

## Virtue is real only when bought with wisdom

- Socrates argues that ordinary courage and temperance are contradictions. Most men face death only because they fear greater evils, so they are "courageous from fear," which he calls strange.
- The temperate are the same. They give up some pleasures only because other pleasures, which they fear to lose, rule them. He says they are "made temperate through intemperance."
- Trading one fear, pleasure or pain for another, "as if they were coins," is not the exchange of virtue.
- There is "one true coin for which all things ought to be exchanged," and that coin is wisdom. Only in exchange for wisdom, and in company with it, is anything truly bought or sold, whether courage, temperance or justice.
- True virtue is the companion of wisdom, whatever fears or pleasures come with it. Virtue made of those goods, severed from wisdom, is "a shadow of virtue only," with no "freedom or health or truth" in it.
- Temperance, justice, courage and wisdom are themselves a purging away of fears and pleasures.
- He cites the mysteries: "many are the thyrsus-bearers, but few are the mystics." He reads "mystics" as the true philosophers, and says he has spent his life trying to find a place among them.

## Why a man must not take his own life

- Socrates says a philosopher will be willing to die but will not kill himself, because suicide is held to be unlawful. He admits this looks inconsistent, since he also says death can be better for some people.
- He gives an argument from a doctrine "whispered in secret" that man is a prisoner who has no right to open the door and run away. He says he does not fully understand this mystery.
- He also believes "the gods are our guardians, and … we are a possession of theirs."
- He asks Cebes whether he would be angry at an ox or an ass that put itself out of the way without being told to. Cebes says yes, so a man should wait until God summons him, "as he is now summoning me."
- Cebes objects. If the wise gladly serve the best of masters, a wise man should be sorry to leave them, and only a fool would be glad to. Socrates is pleased by the objection and treats it as an indictment he must answer in a better defence than he gave the judges.
- He praises Cebes as "always inquiring" and not "easily convinced by the first thing which he hears."

## Pleasure and pain, dreams and poetry

- Socrates rubs his leg after the chains are removed and observes how strange pleasure is and how it relates to pain. They are never present together, yet whoever pursues one is compelled to take the other. "Their bodies are two, but they are joined by a single head."
- He says Aesop would have made a fable of God trying to reconcile their strife and instead fastening their heads together.
- Cebes asks why he has started writing poetry in prison, turning Aesop's fables into verse and composing a hymn to Apollo. Socrates explains that in his life he has often had a dream saying "Cultivate and make music."
- He had taken this to encourage his philosophy, "the noblest and best of music," like spectators urging a runner already running. Under sentence of death, he decided it was safer to obey the dream in the popular sense too.
- He says a poet must invent stories and that he has "no invention," so he used the Aesop fables that were at hand.
- He sends a message to the poet Evenus: be of good cheer, and come after me "if he be a wise man."

## Arguments that the soul survives death

- **Opposites.** Socrates asks whether things with opposites are generated from their opposites.
  - The greater comes from the less, the weaker from the stronger, the swifter from the slower, and the more just from the more unjust.
  - Between each pair of opposites runs a process in both directions, such as waxing and waning, or falling asleep and waking.
  - Life and death are opposites like sleep and waking. If the living come from the dead as the dead come from the living, then souls must exist in the world below.
  - He says dying is visible, and that nature should not "walk on one leg only." So there must be a return to life.
  - If generation ran in one direction only, everything would end in the same state. If all living things died and stayed dead, all would at last be "swallowed up in death."
- **Recollection.** He holds that learning is recollecting what the soul knew before birth.
  - Cebes offers the proof from questions: put a question rightly and a person answers truly from himself, as shown with a diagram.
  - Socrates adds that lovers who see a lyre call up the image of the beloved, and a picture of Simmias can recall Cebes.
  - His main example is absolute equality. Equal pieces of wood or stone look equal at one time and unequal at another, but the idea of equality is never unequal.
  - The material equals only "strive to attain" the idea and "fall short of it." To see that, we must already have known equality before our first sense perception.
  - The same holds for beauty, goodness, justice and holiness. We acquired knowledge of these ideas before birth, so the soul existed before it took a human form.
  - He ties the two: if these ideas do not exist, the argument for the soul falls with them. Simmias agrees the ideas are as certain as the soul's prior existence.
- **Combining both.** The soul existed before birth, and she can only be born from death, so she must exist after death too.

## The soul is like the unseen and unchanging, and need not be scattered

- Socrates says Cebes and Simmias fear, like children, that the wind may blow the soul away on leaving the body, especially in a storm. He tells them to charm away the fear "daily" and to look for a good charmer among all of Hellas and among themselves, "sparing neither pains nor money."
- What can be scattered is the composite. Only the uncompounded can be indissoluble.
- The uncompounded is always the same, like the essences of equality and beauty. The compound, such as the many beautiful horses or garments, is always changing.
- The changing is seen and perceived by the senses; the unchanging is perceived only by the mind. The body is akin to the seen, and the soul to the unseen.
- When the soul uses the body to perceive, she is dragged into the changeable and "wanders and is confused," like a drunkard. When she reflects by herself, she passes into the region of purity and eternity, which is her kindred. This state of the soul is called wisdom.
- Nature makes the soul rule and the body serve, and ruling is akin to the divine. So the soul is like the divine, immortal and unchangeable, and the body like the mortal.
- Even the body lasts a long time after death, for example when embalmed in Egypt, so the invisible soul is unlikely to be blown away at once.
- A soul that is pure at departing goes to the invisible, divine world. A soul that loved the body is weighed down and drawn back to wander near tombs, and is then imprisoned in another body fitting its former life: gluttons and drunkards become asses, tyrants and the violent become wolves, hawks or kites. The merely civic virtuous, whose temperance and justice came from habit "without philosophy and mind," become bees, wasps, ants or men again.
- He describes philosophy as releasing the soul from a body she is "fastened and glued" to. The cause is that each pleasure and pain is "a sort of nail which nails and rivets the soul to the body," so she comes to believe whatever the body affirms.
- That is why philosophers are temperate and brave, he says: not for the world's reasons, but so as not to be left "weaving instead of unweaving her Penelope's web."
