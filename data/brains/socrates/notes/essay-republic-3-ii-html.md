## Glaucon's challenge: why Socrates must praise justice for itself

- Socrates first ranks goods into three classes: those wanted for their own sake (harmless pleasures), those wanted for themselves and their results (knowledge, sight, health), and those that are burdensome but done for the reward (gymnastic, medical treatment, money-making).
- He places justice in the highest class, "among those goods which he who would be happy desires both for their own sake and for the sake of their results."
- Glaucon says the many put justice in the burdensome class, pursued for rewards and reputation, and that Socrates' answer to Thrasymachus has not shown what justice does "inwardly... in the soul."
- Glaucon asks Socrates to praise justice with rewards and reputation set aside. Socrates answers that he would "wish really to persuade you, if I could."
- Adeimantus adds that parents, poets and teachers only commend justice for its reputation and rewards. He asks Socrates to show "what effect they have on the possessor of them," "whether seen or unseen by gods and men."
- Socrates calls the brothers' ability to argue for injustice without being convinced by it "truly divine." He says he cannot refuse to help: "I am afraid that there would be an impiety in being present when justice is evil spoken of and not lifting up a hand in her defence."
- He warns that the inquiry "would require very good eyes."

## The challenge Socrates agrees to answer: Gyges, the unjust man and the just man

Socrates does not state these arguments himself, but he takes them on as the challenge to be answered.

- **Origin of justice:** Glaucon presents the common view that justice is a compromise between doing injustice with impunity (best) and suffering it without redress (worst). It is "tolerated not as a good, but as the lesser evil."
- **Gyges' ring:** a shepherd finds a ring that makes him invisible, then seduces the queen, kills the king and takes the kingdom. Glaucon claims no one would "stand fast in justice" with such a ring, so people are just only of necessity.
- **Isolating the two lives:** the perfectly unjust man has the reputation of justice, while the perfectly just man "wishes to be and not to seem good" and is thought the worst of men. Glaucon says the second will be scourged, racked and impaled.
- Adeimantus notes that the young learn that appearance "tyrannizes over truth." Hence, if they can hide their wrongdoing and buy off the gods with sacrifices, they have no reason to be just.
- Glaucon and Adeimantus ask Socrates to show that justice is the greatest good of the soul and injustice its greatest evil. Without that, each person must watch others, whereas "every one would have been his own watchman."

## Method: find justice in the city first, then in the individual

- Socrates compares the inquiry to a short-sighted person asked to read small letters from a distance, who is lucky to find the same letters written larger elsewhere.
- Justice is the virtue of an individual and also of a State. The State is larger, so "the quantity of justice is likely to be larger and more easily discernible."
- He proposes to inquire into justice "first as they appear in the State, and secondly in the individual, proceeding from the greater to the lesser and comparing them."
- He proposes building a city in thought, since watching it grow should show how justice and injustice grow.

## Origin of the city: need and the division of labour

- A State arises "out of the needs of mankind; no one is self-sufficing." Necessity is "the mother of our invention."
- The first needs are food, then a dwelling, then clothing. The minimal city has four or five people: a husbandman, builder, weaver and shoemaker.
- Principle: people differ in nature, so each is suited to a different occupation. A work is done better "when he has only one" occupation, and it is spoilt if not done at the right time. Things are produced "more plentifully and easily and of a better quality when one man does one thing which is natural to him."
- The city grows as each trade needs tools and trades. Carpenters, smiths, herdsmen, importers and exporters, merchants, sailors, a market-place and money-tokens follow. Retailers (often the physically weakest) and hirelings who sell their labour are added.
- Socrates guesses justice and injustice arise "in the dealings of these citizens with one another."
- Their way of life is simple: barley and wheat cakes, wine, garlands, hymns to the gods, and families kept within their means. Socrates takes salt, olives, cheese, roots, herbs, figs, peas and beans as the relish. He says they would live "in peace and health to a good old age."

## The healthy city versus the luxurious city

- Glaucon objects that this is "a city of pigs" and wants sofas, tables, sauces and sweets.
- Socrates calls the simple city "the true and healthy constitution of the State" but is willing to examine "a State at fever heat." Luxury here means furniture, perfumes, courtesans, cakes, painters, embroiderers, gold and ivory.
- The city swells with callings "not required by any natural want": hunters, actors, poets, rhapsodists, dancers, tutors, nurses, barbers, cooks and swineherds. It also needs many more physicians.
- The land then becomes too small, and the city must take a slice of the neighbours' land. They will do the same to it, once they "exceed the limit of necessity" and give themselves up "to the unlimited accumulation of wealth." So war arises.
- Socrates says war springs from "the causes of almost all the evils in States, private as well as public." He leaves open whether war does good or harm.

## Guardians: why they need training and what nature they need

- War is an art as demanding as shoemaking. By the same principle that no one can practise many arts well, a soldier must be a full-time guardian, not a part-time husbandman or artisan. Tools will not make a skilled workman of someone who has not learned to use them.
- The higher the duty of the guardian, the more "time, and skill, and art, and application" it requires, and natural aptitude as well. So the guardians must be selected.
- A noble youth is like a well-bred dog: quick to see, swift to catch, strong to fight. He must also have spirit, which makes the soul "fearless and indomitable."
- Difficulty: spirited natures turn savage toward everyone, but guardians must be "dangerous to their enemies, and gentle to their friends." Socrates resolves it by noting the well-bred dog is gentle to familiars and fierce to strangers.
- He calls the dog "a true philosopher." It tells friend from enemy "only by the criterion of knowing and not knowing." Since loving learning is loving wisdom, the guardian must unite "philosophy and spirit and swiftness and strength."

## Education of the young: stories, the censorship of poets, and the nature of God

- Traditional education has gymnastic for the body and music (including literature) for the soul. Music comes first, because stories are told before gymnastics can be taught, and "the beginning is the most important part of any work." Character is formed when the child is "young and tender."
- Socrates would set up a censorship of fiction. Mothers and nurses may tell only approved tales, "fashion the mind with such tales, even more fondly than they mould the body with their hands."
- He faults Homer and Hesiod for telling "a bad lie" about gods and heroes, as a painter might paint a portrait with no likeness. His examples are Uranus and Cronus, and Hephaestus bound and thrown out by Zeus.
- A young person "cannot judge what is allegorical and what is literal," so such tales must not be told, because they become "indelible and unalterable." The tales should be "models of virtuous thoughts" and teach that quarrelling is unholy.
- As founders, Socrates and Adeimantus set only the forms poets must follow; making the tales is not their business.
- **First rule:** God is good, the good does no harm, and what does no harm cannot cause evil. So God is "the cause of a few things only," the good ones. The causes of evil are to be sought elsewhere. Poets may not say God is "the author of evil to any one," and punishment may be shown only as benefiting the punished.
- **Second rule:** God does not change. Whatever is best is least altered from without, and God would not change himself for the worse. So no tales of gods who "take the disguise of strangers." Such stories also make cowards of children.
- **The true lie versus the lie in words:** the "true lie" is ignorance and deception in the soul about the highest realities. Everyone hates it. The lie in words is only "a shadowy image" of that state. It can be useful against enemies, as medicine for a friend gone mad, or in myths about ancient times. God has no such reason to lie, so he is "perfectly simple and true both in word and deed."
- Guardians should be "true worshippers of the gods and like them." Socrates would deny a chorus to poets who say otherwise, such as Aeschylus on Apollo's false promise to Thetis, and would not let teachers use them.
