## Why Socrates examines a teacher before handing him your soul

- Socrates tests Hippocrates, who rushes to Protagoras at dawn, by asking what each teacher *makes* of a pupil: a physician makes a physician, a sculptor makes a sculptor. Hippocrates cannot say what a Sophist makes of him, and he blushes at the answer "a Sophist."
- He says knowledge is "the food of the soul." Sophists who sell wisdom praise all their goods alike, without knowing what helps or harms the soul, and so do most of their customers.
- Food bought for the body can be carried home in another vessel and shown to an expert before eating. Knowledge cannot: "when you have paid for them you must receive them into the soul and go your way, either greatly harmed or greatly benefited."
- Practical rule: consult elders, friends and kin for many days before committing the soul, as one would before entrusting the body. Unless you can tell good from evil yourself, "do not hazard your dearest interests at a game of chance."
- His standing test for any teacher is to ask "in what shall I become better, and about what?" Protagoras's vague promise to make pupils "better every day" is not an answer.

## Why Socrates doubts that political virtue can be taught

- He asks Protagoras to answer concretely what the pupil will improve in. Protagoras replies: prudence in private and public affairs, which Socrates restates as "the art of politics" that "makes men good citizens."
- Evidence against teachability:
  - The Athenian assembly consults specialists on building and shipbuilding, and shouts down any unskilled adviser. On affairs of state it lets carpenter, tinker, cobbler and sailor speak, as if the subject cannot be taught.
  - Pericles taught his sons every subject masters could teach but not virtue. He let them wander "in a sort of hope that they would light upon virtue of their own accord."
  - Cleinias, Alcibiades's younger brother, was sent away by Pericles, and the new guardian Ariphron returned him within six months, not knowing what to do with him.
  - He says he could name "numberless" good men who never made anyone else good.
- Socrates admits he wavers: he first said no human care could make men good, then says he knows better after hearing Protagoras's speech. He wants the question settled by argument, not by speeches.

## Speeches versus short questions and answers

- Socrates criticizes long orations: great speakers are "like books" or "brazen pots," which keep ringing after being struck, and cannot answer or ask a question.
- He praises question-and-answer as a rare skill, and insists on short answers because of a "bad memory." Discussion "is one thing, and making an oration is quite another."
- He compares himself to a slow runner: "if you want to see Crison and me in the same stadium, you must bid him slacken his speed to mine."
- When Protagoras refuses to keep to short answers, Socrates rises to leave rather than force the conversation. He refuses an umpire: anyone chosen would be inferior, equal, or better than Protagoras, and none of those helps.
- He prefers partners to poets: poets "cannot be interrogated about the meaning of what they are saying," so their interpreters never settle a dispute. Educated people use "their own voices" and "put one another to the proof in conversation," not flute-girls or recited verse.
- He says he asks only to "clear up my own difficulties": "when two go together, one sees before the other," and a discoverer wants someone to confirm his finding.

## Is virtue one, and are its parts distinct?

- Socrates asks whether justice, temperance, holiness, courage and wisdom are five names for one thing or separate parts. Protagoras says they differ like the parts of a face (eyes and ears), not like pieces of gold.
- Socrates secures agreement that justice is just and holiness is holy, so "justice is either the same with holiness, or very nearly the same."
- An argument from opposites: each thing has only one opposite. Folly is the opposite of wisdom and also of temperance, so wisdom and temperance must be the same.
- He tests the claim that an unjust man can be temperate, using "good sense" and "good counsel." He is also willing to be tested himself: "I who ask and you who answer may both be put on our trial."
- His method: never accept "if you wish" agreements. He wants a conclusion proven for the speakers themselves, "if there be no 'if.'"

## Courage is knowledge of what is and is not to be feared

- Protagoras claims courage differs from the other virtues, since some very unjust, unholy or ignorant men are courageous. Socrates examines this.
- Divers, skilled horsemen and peltasts are confident because they have knowledge. Ignorant but confident people are mad, not brave, so confidence alone is not courage.
- Socrates concludes that cowards are cowards through ignorance of what is dangerous. Courage is therefore "the knowledge of that which is and is not dangerous," and wisdom is courage.
- Everyone, brave or cowardly, goes toward what he thinks is better. No one willingly goes toward what he believes to be a danger or an evil.
- Protagoras goes silent and will not assent to the last step, then concedes that an ignorant yet courageous man is impossible consistent with the argument.

## No one does wrong willingly: the "power of pleasure" is ignorance

- Socrates asks whether knowledge is a slave to anger, pleasure, pain, love or fear, as most people think. He and Protagoras hold that knowledge is "a noble and commanding thing" that cannot be overcome if one truly knows good from evil.
- He argues that acting against one's knowledge makes no sense. Pleasures count as evil only because of later pain or lost pleasures, and pains as good only because of later pleasure. Good and evil are measured by pleasure and pain, differing in "more and fewer, greater and less."
- Being "overcome by pleasure" then means choosing a smaller good over a larger one. This is an error of calculation, so it is simply "ignorance, and that the greatest."
- The saving art is measurement. Near pleasures look larger, as near objects do, so "the power of appearance" deceives us. A "weighing" of pleasures and pains by nearness and distance would "teach the soul at last to find rest in the truth, and would thus save our life."
- Conclusions he draws: "no man voluntarily pursues evil, or that which he thinks to be evil." To prefer the worse to the better is not in human nature. The inferiority of a man to himself is ignorance, and superiority is wisdom.

## The upshot: virtue is knowledge, so it can be taught

- Socrates notes the dialogue's irony: he began by denying that virtue can be taught, yet his argument that all virtues are knowledge implies it can be. Protagoras, who began by claiming it can be taught, argued it is not knowledge, which implies it cannot.
- He wants to continue until they learn "what virtue is, whether capable of being taught or not."
- He prefers Prometheus (forethought) to Epimetheus: he applies "Promethean care of my own life" to these questions.
- He tells the Sophists and the public that if ignorance is the cause of wrongdoing, those who think the art of measurement cannot be taught are mistaken. They neither go to teachers themselves nor send their children, and "the result is that you are the worse off both in public and private life."

## Reading a poet: Simonides and Pittacus

- When Protagoras accuses Simonides of contradicting himself ("hardly can a man become truly good" versus blaming Pittacus for "hardly can a man be good"), Socrates admits he felt "giddy and faint, as if I had received a blow from the hand of an expert boxer."
- He defends the poem by distinguishing *becoming* good from *being* good. Becoming good is hard but possible. Staying good is impossible for man, because only God has it, and circumstances overpower even the skilled.
- He reads "truly" with "hard," not with "good." The poem is an attack on Pittacus's saying, not a muddle.
- He also claims Spartan and Cretan wisdom lies in terse sayings, like the Delphic "Know thyself" and "Nothing too much."
- Key claim inside his reading: "no wise man... will allow that any human being errs voluntarily"; all who do evil do it against their will.
- He treats the whole exercise partly as mischief and then drops it: quoting poets is like a vulgar party hiring flute-girls. Real conversation tests the speakers themselves.
