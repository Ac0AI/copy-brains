## Questioning the world versus knowing oneself

- Socrates declines to spend time rationalizing myths such as Boreas carrying off Orithyia. He says allegorical explanations take endless "labour and ingenuity" (Hippocentaurs, chimeras, Gorgons, winged steeds).
- His reason: "I must first know myself, as the Delphian inscription says; to be curious about that which is not my concern, while I am still in ignorance of my own self, would be ridiculous."
- He says the common opinion is enough for him on such tales. The question he wants answered is about himself: "am I a monster more complicated and swollen with passion than the serpent Typho, or a creature of a gentler and simpler sort, to whom Nature has given a diviner and lowlier destiny?"
- He calls himself a "lover of knowledge" and says the men in the city are his teachers, "and not the trees or the country." He adds that a book held out in front of him could lead him "all round Attica."
- He claims to "know nothing" of his own invention. He says his speeches come from having been "filled through the ears, like a pitcher, from the waters of another."

## Defining terms first, and the two kinds of madness

- Socrates' first speech, which he later retracts, opens with a rule for good counsel: "a man should know what he is advising about, or his counsel will all come to nought."
- He says people assume they know the nature of things, skip agreeing on definitions, and so end "contradicting one another and themselves." His remedy is to agree on a definition and keep it in view.
- That speech defines love as irrational desire that overpowers an opinion aiming at the best. He later calls this speech foolish and "to a certain extent, impious," and reproves it as lacking truth or honesty.
- He then gives his recantation, which echoes Stesichorus: "I told a lie." Madness is not simply an evil: "there is also a madness which is a divine gift, and the source of the chiefest blessings granted to men."
- He names four divine madnesses and their gods:
  - prophetic (Apollo)
  - initiatory (Dionysus)
  - poetic (the Muses)
  - erotic (Aphrodite and Eros)
- Examples he gives: the Delphic prophetess and the priestesses of Dodona "when out of their senses have conferred great benefits on Hellas, but when in their senses few or none." He also says the sane man who comes to the door of poetry "by the help of art" is not admitted.
- He argues that if love is a god, as Eros is, "he cannot be evil." He calls the madness of love "the greatest of heaven's blessings."

## The soul: immortal, self-moving, a charioteer with two horses

- Proof of immortality: what is moved by another ceases to live when the motion ceases. Only the self-moving never stops, and it is the source of motion in all else. Being a beginning, it is unbegotten and indestructible. He concludes that self-motion is "the very idea and essence of the soul."
- Image: the soul is a charioteer driving a pair of winged horses. The gods' horses are all noble. A human has one noble horse and one ignoble, so driving is "a great deal of trouble."
  - The good horse is white, upright, "a lover of honour and modesty and temperance." He needs no whip, "guided by word and admonition only."
  - The bad horse is crooked and dark, "the mate of insolence and pride," "hardly yielding to whip and spur."
- The wing is the part of the body most akin to the divine. It is nourished by "beauty, wisdom, goodness" and wastes away on "evil and foulness."
- Souls follow the gods up to the edge of heaven to see true being: justice, temperance, and knowledge absolute. These are "colourless, formless, intangible," visible only to mind. Souls that fail to see feed on "opinion."
- A soul that falls ranks into human lives by how much it saw. The philosopher, artist, or lover of the Muses ranks first; the tyrant ninth. Wings regrow after ten thousand years, except for the philosopher (and the lover who is not devoid of philosophy), who gets them in three thousand years.
- Learning is recollection. A man must have "intelligence of universals" and proceed "from the many particulars of sense to one conception of reason." Hence "the mind of the philosopher alone has wings," though the crowd thinks him mad.

## Love as the soul growing wings

- Beauty is the one Form we can see clearly. Sight is "the most piercing of our bodily senses," and beauty "is also the most palpable to sight." Wisdom itself cannot be seen.
- The corrupted or newly arrived lover rushes toward pleasure "like a brutish beast." The one who remembers true beauty feels a shudder and awe and reveres the beloved as an image of a god. Gazing warms the soul, and the wings begin to grow.
- The lover forgets family, property, and propriety. He cannot sleep by night or stay in place by day.
- Each lover follows a god (Zeus, Hera, Apollo, Ares) and seeks a beloved of that god's character. He then tries to shape the beloved into the likeness of the god. Followers of Zeus seek a philosophical nature and "do all they can to confirm such a nature in him."
- The beloved gives back "love for love" (Anteros) and mistakes it for friendship.
- Self-control decides the outcome:
  - If the better elements prevail, the pair live "masters of themselves and orderly," and win "one of the three heavenly or truly Olympian victories." He says "nor can human discipline or divine inspiration confer any greater blessing on man than this."
  - If they yield to the lower life of ambition, they still earn "no mean reward," but their bond is less dear and they lose their wings for the time.
- The non-lover's "worldly and niggardly" prudence, by contrast, sends the soul "bowling round the earth" for nine thousand years.

## Rhetoric must rest on knowledge of truth and of the soul

- Socrates tests the claim that an orator needs only opinion, not truth. He compares it to praising an ass as a horse to someone who does not know the difference. A speaker ignorant of good and evil, persuading an ignorant city, will reap "the reverse of good."
- Deception works through resemblances, and gradual shifts hide the lie best. He argues that "he who would deceive others, and not be deceived, must exactly know the real likenesses and differences of things."
- Rhetoric has most power where people disagree, as with justice and goodness, unlike iron and silver. So the rhetorician must first divide matters into agreed and disputed kinds.
- He rejects the technical manuals (exordium, statement of facts, witnesses, probabilities, refutation, and recapitulation). He names Theodorus, Evenus, Tisias, Gorgias, Prodicus, Polus, and Protagoras. These are only "the preliminaries" of the art. A man who knows drugs but not "to whom," "when," and "how much" is no physician, and a man who knows only how to make long or short speeches is no tragedian.
- The true art mirrors medicine. Medicine defines the nature of the body; rhetoric must define the nature of the soul. Pericles became the best rhetorician by learning from Anaxagoras: "All the great arts require discussion and high speculation about the truths of nature."
- The three requirements:
  - describe the soul and whether it is simple or multiform
  - explain how it acts and is acted upon
  - classify kinds of souls and kinds of speeches, and show why one soul is persuaded by one argument and another is not
- The learner also needs experience in real life. He must be able to say, "This is the man... who ought to have a certain argument applied to him," and know "when he should speak and when he should refrain."
- He answers Tisias' claim that probability beats truth: probability is "engendered in the minds of the many by the likeness of the truth," so only the knower finds the likenesses. The long and hard road is worth taking because the aim is to "say what is acceptable to God," not to please "fellow-servants."

## Dialectic: collection and division, and the shape of a good speech

- Socrates names two principles that his two speeches happened to use:
  - Gather scattered particulars into one idea, which gives a definition that is clear and consistent.
  - Divide by species "according to the natural formation, where the joint is, not breaking any part as a bad carver might."
- He says he is "a great lover of these processes of division and generalization; they help me to speak and to think." He follows anyone who can see "a One and Many in nature... as if he were a god," and calls such people dialecticians.
- Every discourse "ought to be a living creature, having a body of its own and a head and feet; there should be a middle, beginning, and end, adapted to one another and to the whole."
- He criticizes the speech of Lysias on these grounds. It never defines love, it begins at the end where the lover would finish, and its parts could be shuffled like the lines of the Midas epitaph.

## Writing versus living speech

- Socrates tells the story of the Egyptian king Thamus, who rebukes the god Theuth for inventing letters. Letters will "create forgetfulness in the learners' souls" because they will trust external marks. The discovery is "an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence," giving "not truth, but only the semblance of truth." Readers will be "hearers of many things" who "have learned nothing," with "the show of wisdom without the reality."
- He argues writing is like painting. It looks alive, "but if you ask them a question they preserve a solemn silence," gives "one unvarying answer," and cannot defend itself when it is abused or misread.
- The better kind of word is "an intelligent word graven in the soul of the learner, which can defend itself, and knows when to speak and when to be silent."
- The farmer comparison: a sensible man sows seeds in fitting soil and waits months, not in a quick "garden of Adonis" for eight-day show. In the same way, the one who knows the just, good, and honourable writes only "for the sake of recreation and amusement," as reminders for old age. The serious work is for the dialectician, who "finding a congenial soul... sows and plants therein words which are able to help themselves," leaving a seed others can make immortal.
- There is no disgrace in writing as such; "the disgrace begins when a man writes not well, but badly." Disgrace attaches to anyone who writes laws or speeches with false confidence in their clarity while ignorant of justice and injustice, good and evil.
- A writer whose work rests on knowledge of the truth, and who can defend it by spoken argument that leaves the writing "poor in comparison," deserves a higher name. Socrates will not call him wise ("a great name which belongs to God alone") but "lovers of wisdom or philosophers."

## Prayer and the measure of a good life

- Socrates ends with a prayer to Pan and the local gods: "give me beauty in the inward soul; and may the outward and inward man be at one."
- He adds: "May I reckon the wise to be the wealthy, and may I have such a quantity of gold as a temperate man and he only can bear and carry."
- He says "friends should have all things in common" when Phaedrus asks to share the prayer.
- He predicts that young Isocrates will outgrow Lysias and rhetoric itself: "there is in him a divine inspiration which will lead him to things higher still. For he has an element of philosophy in his nature."
- He says that "to fail in an honourable object is honourable."
- He says conversation beats idleness at midday. The grasshoppers report to the Muses who honours them, and Calliope and Urania favour the philosophers.
