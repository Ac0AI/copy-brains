## Self-discipline is the training ground for anyone who wants to rule, and for anyone who wants to be happy

- Socrates argues that whoever is educated to govern must be trained in self-control over food, drink, sleep and sexual desire, and in endurance of cold, heat, toil and pain. He asks Aristippus which of two boys, one raised to rule and one raised not to, should get this training. Aristippus answers that it must be the future ruler, "if we would not have affairs of state neglected."
- He compares people ruled by appetite to animals caught by their own greed. Fish and beasts are drawn to bait by hunger or thirst, and quails and partridges rush into the hunter's snare at the hen's cry. The adulterer, who knows the penalties and still plunges in, is "tormented by an evil spirit."
- He notes that war and agriculture are carried on in the open air, so a man who will lead must be able to bear cold and heat lightly. He ranks the self-disciplined as fit to rule and the rest as unfit.
- Voluntary hardship differs from forced hardship. The man who starves by choice can eat when he likes, and he faces the toil "gaily, buoyed on hope," like a hunter who finds the chase a pleasure. Those who toil for good friends, for victory over enemies, for a well-run household and for service to their country bear their sorrows lightly. They have their conscious virtue and the world's praise to support them.
- Indolence and fleeting pleasures build neither a healthy body nor a soul with worthwhile knowledge. Socrates cites Hesiod: wickedness is easy to take "wholesale," while "in front of virtue the immortal gods have placed toil and sweat." He also cites Epicharmus: "The gods sell us all good things in return for our labours."

## "Neither rule nor be ruled" is no safe path: freedom is not found by avoiding other people

- Aristippus says he wants a middle path between rule and slavery, "through freedom," and that he will roam the world as a foreigner under no single state. He also says ruling is folly because rulers serve everyone else's wants and get blamed when they fall short.
- Socrates replies that this works only if the path could avoid human beings. Among people, the stronger make the weaker "sit on the stool of repentance," in public and in private. He gives examples: stronger neighbours cut down the crops and fruit trees of those who refuse to pay them court, and the powerful enslave the helpless and cowardly.
- Free citizens protect themselves with laws, friends, walls, armaments and foreign allies, and they still sometimes suffer injustice. A solitary foreigner has none of these protections and is an obvious target. He cannot count on a proclamation of safe conduct, or on being too poor a slave for anyone to want.
- He turns Aristippus's own treatment of slaves against him. Masters curb a wanton, thieving, idle servant with hunger, bolts and bars, bonds and the lash, and Aristippus admits he does the same ("I correct them with all the plagues"). Socrates uses this to show that a man with no self-discipline invites such treatment himself.

## The Choice of Heracles: Virtue and Vice offer two roads

Socrates retells Prodicus's tale of Heracles at the crossroads of youth.

- **Vice** (who says her friends call her Happiness) promises the easiest and pleasantest road: no war or business, every pleasure of food, drink, sight, smell, love and sleep without pain, and the fruits of other people's labour. Her figure is made up to look taller and rosier than nature made it.
- **Virtue** says the gods give nothing lovely and good apart from toil. She lists what each good costs:
  - the favour of the gods requires serving them;
  - the love of friends requires benefiting them;
  - honour from the state requires aiding the state;
  - good harvests require tending the earth and flocks;
  - military power requires learning the arts of war and practising them;
  - bodily strength requires habituating body to mind "with toil and sweat."
- Virtue rebukes Vice's followers. They are satiated before they are hungry, and they need cooks, ice in summer, feather-beds and rockers to produce an appetite or sleep. They have never heard praise or seen one good deed of their own. Vice's followers are "impotent of body" when young and "witless in mind" when old, and they carry the shame of what they have done and the weariness of what is left to do.
- Virtue's followers enjoy food and drink that are sweet because they wait until desire ripens, and their sleep is sweeter than that of those who do not toil. Young and old honour one another, and they are dear to the gods, loved by friends and honoured by their country. At the end they "bloom afresh," their praise resounding for ever.
- Socrates urges Aristippus to lay this to heart and think about the future of his own life.

## Ingratitude to parents is the greatest wrongdoing, and a mother's sharp tongue is no excuse

- Socrates reasons with his son Lamprocles, who is angry at his mother. They agree that ingratitude is wrongdoing whether the benefactor is a friend or a foe, and that the greater the benefit, the greater the wrong of failing to repay it. No benefit is greater than a parent's.
- He lays out what parents give. They bring a child into being and let it see and share all that the gods give man. A father chooses a wife to bear the finest children and stores up provision for them. A mother carries the child "with travail and pain, and at the risk of life itself," feeds and tends it for many months and expects nothing back. Parents then teach the child themselves or pay others to do it.
- Lamprocles says his mother's temper is worse than a wild beast's. Socrates points out that she has never bitten or kicked him. He also reminds him how much trouble he himself caused her since childhood, and how she nursed him when he was ill.
- He compares her scolding to an actor hearing abuse on stage, which is harmless because it is only words. The difference is that she means him no harm: she wishes him every blessing, nurses him when sick and prays and makes vows for him. "If you cannot away with such a mother, you cannot away with such blessings either."
- He adds practical consequences. Lamprocles already wants to please neighbours, travelling companions and rulers, so refusing a mother who loves him most makes no sense. The state punishes neglect of parents: the offender is struck off the roll and barred from the archonship, and neglect of parents' tombs is examined in the magistrates' scrutiny. The gods and men will treat an ungrateful son the same way, and he will find himself "in a desert devoid of friends."

## Brothers: be the first to show kindness, because brothers are made to work together

- Socrates urges Chaerecrates, who is estranged from his brother Chaerephon, to value a brother above goods. Goods are senseless chattels needing protection, while a brother is a sensible being who can give it. People happily choose to live among many fellow citizens, yet they ignore the same logic about brothers. Brothers share a birth and a nurture, a natural start toward friendship, and a man with brothers commands more respect.
- Chaerecrates says his brother is pleasant to everyone else but hostile to him. Socrates answers that perhaps he is handling him ignorantly, as an unskilled rider is kicked by a horse. He observes that Chaerecrates already knows how to win people: to be invited to dinner, invite first; to get someone to mind his affairs, offer to mind theirs; to be sheltered abroad, offer shelter first. The same applies to his brother.
- He says the first move belongs to the younger brother, and "to you the final victory." Younger men already give way to their elders in the street and in seats, and "it is commonly held to redound to a man's praise to have outstripped an enemy in mischief or a friend in kindness."
- A proud and honour-loving nature "cannot be caught by a bribe"; it must be won by affection. At the worst Chaerecrates will have shown himself a good brother. More likely his brother will be "pricked on by emulous pride" to outdo him in kindness.
- He gives an image: brothers are like two hands or two feet made by God to help each other, which instead hinder one another. It is "insensate stupidity to use for injury what was meant for advantage." Hands, feet and eyes cannot reach more than a short distance, but "a pair of brothers, linked in bonds of amity, can work each for the other's good, though seas divide them."

## Friendship: the best possession, and the one people neglect most

- Socrates notes that people say a good friend is the best possession, yet few try to acquire or keep friends. They work to get houses, fields, slaves, cattle and furniture. A man will fetch the doctor for a sick servant but ignore a sick friend, and he mourns the servant's death as a loss. Most people can count their goods but cannot list their friends, and may retract names once they try.
- A good friend is "kindlier and more constant than the faithfullest of slaves." He supplements what his friends lack in private and public affairs, helps them with money, energy, argument or force, gladdens them in success, and steadies them when they have nearly slipped. He does for a friend what the friend could not do himself.
- Socrates asks Antisthenes whether friends have market values like slaves, using Nicias's talent for a mine overseer as an example. Antisthenes says one friend he would prefer to two minae, another he would not value at half a mina, a third he would keep for ten, and a fourth he would not want at any cost.
- Socrates turns this into self-examination: "What after all may I chance to be worth to my friends?" Each person should try to be as dear as possible to friends, so they do not care to give him up. If a base slave is sold for what he will fetch, a base friend is likewise dropped when someone can profit by the exchange; good slaves and good friends are not so easily parted with.

## Choosing and winning a friend: test character by deeds, and be good yourself

- Socrates tells Critobulus whom to avoid: the slave of belly, wine, lust, sleep or idleness; the spendthrift who begs from neighbours and hates them if refused; the money-grabber who is "glad enough to take in, but loath to pay out"; the man too absorbed in gain for anything else; the quarrelsome person who loads his friends with enemies; and the man who takes kindness and never returns it.
- The good friend is the opposite. He masters bodily pleasures, is kindly, upright in dealings and eager not to be outdone in kindness by his benefactors, and wants his friends to gain from knowing him.
- Test a man as you would a sculptor, by his earlier work and not by his talk. Kindness to older friends predicts kindness to new ones, as skill with horses predicts skill with more horses. Socrates adds that one should first ask whether Heaven approves the friendship.
- A friend cannot be captured by force, snares or fetters; those who are bound become foes. The spell is suited to the listener (the Sirens' call "Hither, come hither, thou famous man, Odysseus" was for souls athirst for fame and emulous of virtue). Flattery is hateful: telling a small, ugly, weak man he is tall and beautiful breeds repulsion.
- "To win the love of any good man we need to be good ourselves in speech and action." Service must be paid for with service, and a bad man cannot make good friends.
- He explains why even good men sometimes quarrel. "Seeds of love" are planted in us by nature, since men need, pity and help each other. There are also "seeds of war": rivalry over the same things thought beautiful or agreeable, more-having, avarice and envy. Good men still form bonds because they prefer scant means without pain to an empire won by war. They share food and drink and wealth, settle strife to the general advantage, check anger before it brings remorse, and treat each other's goods as common. Good men can also hold office together for the public good. A band of the best is stronger than rivals, and "one good man is better worth your benefiting than a dozen knaves," for with the base "the more you give them the more they ask for."
- On love of friends he says he demands to be loved in return and works to please whoever pleases him. He offers to tell the person Critobulus admires that Critobulus admires him, is kindly, is devoted to friends, takes joy in a good friend, takes pride in friends' deeds as in his own, and holds that virtue means outdoing friends in kindness and foes in hostility. But Aspasia taught him that matchmakers should report only what is true, since falsehood makes the deluded parties hate each other and the go-between.
- Praising a man falsely helps no one. If he is falsely called a good pilot, general or manager, he will be given a ship, a state or an estate to ruin. "In whatsoever you desire to be deemed good, endeavour to be good." Socrates adds that every virtue can be increased by learning and practice.

## Practical help for friends in need: put idle talents to work, find a defender, and buy friends cheaply

- **Aristarchus and his household of relatives.** Aristarchus is crushed by the burden of fourteen free-born female relatives and has no income. Socrates asks why Ceramon, with slaves to feed, prospers. He points out that Nausicydes (barley meal), Cyrebus (bread), Demeas (cloaks), Menon (mantles) and the Megarians (vests) all live well from useful trades. He tells Aristarchus that the women know how to make these goods, and that they should not live in idleness. Idle people grow to resent each other, while shared useful work brings affection. Honest work is nothing shameful for them, and what we know best we do with ease and pleasure. Aristarchus borrows capital, buys wool, and the women work and smile. When they complain that he is the only drone, Socrates tells him the fable of the dog and the sheep: the dog is rightly fed from the master's table because he guards the flock.
- **Eutherus.** Eutherus lives by bodily labour that will fail with age. Socrates advises him to manage a large estate. When Eutherus says he cannot bear to be held to account, Socrates replies that every work involves accountability and some mistakes. Eutherus should "avoid censorious persons," attach himself to the kind, accept what he can do and decline what he cannot, and do each task "heart and soul" as his finest work. That silences fault-finders and secures his old age.
- **Crito and Archedemus.** Crito is harassed by blackmailing lawsuits. Socrates asks why he does not keep a watchman, as he keeps dogs against wolves, since a man like Crito is more pleasant to gratify than to quarrel with. Crito finds Archedemus, honest, clever and poor, and gives him gifts of corn, oil, wine and wool and invitations to feasts. Archedemus finds crimes in the informers and prosecutes one, winning a payment from him. Crito's friends then ask for his guardianship too. Archedemus replies to critics that it is less shameful to repay honest folk with kindness than to wrong gentlemen for scoundrels' favour.
- **Diodorus and Hermogenes.** Socrates asks Diodorus, who pays to recover a runaway slave and calls doctors for a sick one, why he would not help Hermogenes, a friend in want. A willing, kindly and trusty assistant with good judgement "is worth dozens of slaves," and "nowadays when times are so bad it is possible to get good friends exceedingly cheap." Hermogenes will be ashamed not to repay. When Diodorus asks Socrates to send Hermogenes to him, Socrates refuses: Diodorus is no better entitled to summon Hermogenes than to go himself, and the advantage is not more on Hermogenes's side. Diodorus goes and wins a friend whose one concern is to help and gladden him.
