## Doing injustice is worse than suffering it, and escaping punishment is worse still

- Socrates holds that the doer of injustice is worse off than the victim. Being boxed on the ears, robbed or killed wrongfully is not the worst evil. Smiting and despoiling others is "far more disgraceful and evil to the doer of the wrong than to me who am the sufferer."
- There is a greater evil than doing injustice: the unjust man who is not punished.
- He says these conclusions are "fixed and riveted... in words which are like bonds of iron and adamant," unless someone refutes them. He claims he does not know how things are, but has never met anyone who could say otherwise without looking ridiculous.
- Nobody does wrong willingly. Avoiding injustice takes power and art (knowledge), not just good intentions.
- When an argument ends with only one man agreeing, he still wants it finished: "the discovery of the truth is a common good."

## Against Callicles' "natural justice" of the strong: who is "better"?

- Callicles says nature makes it just for the stronger and better to rule and take more, and that law is a trick of the weak majority. Socrates answers by pressing for a definition of "better."
- If the stronger are the better, then the many are stronger than the one. Their laws, which say justice is equality and doing injustice is more shameful than suffering it, would then be natural and good. Nature and convention would not be opposed, as Callicles claimed.
- Callicles shifts from "stronger" to "wiser" to "wise and courageous in running a state." Socrates charges him with "never saying the same about the same things."
- Socrates tests "the wiser should have more" with concrete cases: a physician with the food, a weaver with the coats, a shoemaker with the shoes, a farmer with the seed. In each, the expert's superiority gives authority over distribution, not a bigger personal share.
- He asks whether the ruler also rules himself. Self-rule means being "temperate and master of himself, and ruler of his own pleasures and passions."

## Temperance against unlimited appetite: the leaky jars

- Callicles says the happy man lets his desires grow to the utmost and satisfies them all. He calls temperance a virtue invented by the weak, and calls luxury, intemperance and licence "virtue and happiness."
- Socrates tells an image from a Sicilian or Italian thinker. The souls of the ignorant are leaky jars, or a colander, that are never filled. The unrestrained part of the soul is the leaky vessel.
- His two-casks figure compares two men:
  - One has sound casks filled with wine, honey and milk. Once full, he needs no more toil or worry.
  - The other has leaky casks and must fill them night and day, or suffer.
- Callicles replies that a filled cask means no pleasure, "the life of a stone." Socrates answers that the leaky life is that of a cormorant, always hungering and eating.
- He presses the consequences of Callicles' view. If all pleasure is happiness, the man who spends his life scratching an itch is happy, and so is the catamite, which Callicles will not accept.
- Socrates urges Callicles to choose the "orderly and sufficient" life, with due provision for daily needs, over the insatiate one.

## Pleasure is not the good

- Socrates gets Callicles to agree that good and evil fortune are opposites and cannot be had and lost at the same time, like health and disease of the eyes.
- Hunger and thirst are painful, and drinking while thirsty is pleasant. So pleasure and pain are present together. Thirst and the pleasure of drinking also cease at the same moment, but good and evil do not. Therefore good is not the same as pleasure, nor evil the same as pain.
- A second argument: the good are good by the presence of good. But fools and cowards rejoice as much as the wise and brave, and cowards more in some cases. If pleasure were the good, the bad would be as good as the good or better.
- Callicles concedes that some pleasures are good and some bad. Socrates then states the rule: all actions are done for the sake of the good, so pleasure is to be sought for the sake of the good and not the reverse. Choosing among pleasures takes art and knowledge.

## Flattery versus real care: cookery and medicine, for body and soul

- Socrates separates arts that aim at the good from mere "experience" that aims only at pleasure. Cookery is a knack that follows routine and never considers the nature of what it serves. Medicine is an art that knows the patient's nature and gives reasons.
- The same split exists for the soul. Whatever aims at pleasure with no thought of good or evil is "flattery." Examples:
  - flute and lyre playing;
  - dithyrambic poetry (Cinesias, Meles);
  - tragedy;
  - much public oratory.
- Poetry stripped of metre is speech to a crowd, so it is a kind of rhetoric. Rhetoric comes in two kinds: flattery and "disgraceful declamation," or the noble kind aimed at improving citizens by saying what is best "whether welcome or unwelcome."
- Callicles cannot name a living orator of the noble kind. Socrates adds that the dead statesmen he offers (Themistocles, Cimon, Miltiades, Pericles) did not distinguish good desires from bad either.

## Order in the soul: temperance, justice and the good life

- Every good thing, whether a house, ship, body or soul, is good through order and arrangement. Order in the body is health and strength. Order in the soul is called "lawful" and "law," which gives temperance and justice.
- A sick body is not given whatever it craves, and a soul in bad state should likewise have its desires restrained. Chastisement is better for such a soul than lack of control.
- The argument in his summary:
  - The soul with order is temperate and good.
  - The temperate man does what is proper toward the gods (holy) and toward men (just).
  - He is also courageous, since he pursues and avoids what he ought and endures when he ought.
  - So he is perfectly good, does well in all he does, and "must of necessity be happy and blessed."
  - The evil man is miserable.
- Practical conclusions:
  - Whoever wants to be happy must pursue temperance and flee intemperance "as fast as his legs will carry him."
  - Best is to live so as not to need punishment. If a person or city does need it, justice must be done and he must accept it.
  - The unrestrained "robber's life" leaves a man "incapable of communion," so he is incapable of friendship and is friend to neither god nor man.
- Socrates cites the philosophers' view that "communion and friendship and orderliness and temperance and justice bind together heaven and earth and gods and men," which is why the universe is a Cosmos. He charges Callicles with neglecting geometrical equality and chasing excess.

## Power, self-defence and tyranny

- Callicles says Socrates is defenceless: anyone can strike, rob or kill him. Socrates answers that the best defence is never to have said or done wrong toward gods or men. A man who is "his own defence" is well protected. He would be ashamed to fail at that kind of defence, but not to lack flattery or rhetoric.
- To avoid suffering injustice, one must be the ruler, or the equal and companion of the ruler. Like is friend to like, so the tyrant's only real friend is someone with the same likes and dislikes who is subservient to him.
- Imitating the tyrant keeps you safe from harm but leads you to do as much wrong as possible, unpunished. That corrupts the soul, "the greatest evil."
- Socrates also rejects survival as the supreme aim. The pilot, like the pleader, saves bodies and goods for two obols or at most two drachmae (Aegina to Athens, or longer voyages), and is modest about it. He cannot tell whether passengers are better for being saved. For a man with an incurable disease of the soul, life is no benefit.
- "No man who is not an utter fool and coward is afraid of death itself, but he is afraid of doing wrong." To arrive in the next world "having one's soul full of injustice is the last and worst of all evils."
- A man who is truly noble stops caring about living a set length of time. He leaves fate to God and asks how to spend his term well.

## Statesmanship as care for the citizens, and the myth of judgment

- Socrates argues that a statesman's task is to make citizens better. Before taking on public works, you should ask who taught you and what you have built privately. He does the same with medicine: ask whether anyone was ever cured by you. He asks Callicles whom he has ever made better, and gets no answer.
- Pericles, Cimon, Themistocles and Miltiades supplied ships, walls and docks. They fed appetites like bakers and cooks. Socrates says they left "no room for justice and temperance." He points out that Pericles was convicted of theft, Cimon ostracized, Themistocles exiled and Miltiades nearly put to death.
- Socrates says that, like a trainer who made horses fiercer, Pericles was a bad shepherd of men if the citizens turned savage. A true teacher of virtue could safely leave his pay to his pupils, since having removed injustice from them he has nothing to fear from them.
- He asks whether he should be the physician of the state or its flatterer, and calls himself "the only politician of my time" because he looks to what is best, not to what is pleasant. He expects to be tried like a physician accused by a cook before a jury of children.
- The myth of judgment:
  - Zeus reforms the old practice of judging the living, who came clothed in wealth, rank and witnesses. Now souls are judged naked and dead by the sons Minos, Rhadamanthus and Aeacus.
  - Death is only the separation of soul and body. Each keeps its marks, so a soul bears scars of perjuries and crimes.
  - Punishment has two purposes: to improve the curable, or to be an example to others when the offender is incurable. Most such examples are tyrants and kings, because power allows the worst crimes. The text cites Archelaus, Tantalus, Sisyphus and Tityus. Aristeides, son of Lysimachus, is named as a rare just man in power.
  - A philosopher who has "done his own work" is the likeliest to go to the Islands of the Blessed.
- Socrates' closing position: he renounces worldly honours, wants "to know the truth, and to live as well as I can, and, when I die, to die as well as I can." Of everything argued, what remains unshaken is that doing injustice is more to be avoided than suffering it, that the reality and not the appearance of virtue is to be followed, that the wrongdoer must be chastised, and that flattery of oneself and others must be avoided.
