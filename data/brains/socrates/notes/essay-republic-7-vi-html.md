## Why the philosopher's nature is the one fit to rule

- Socrates argues that guardians of a state must be able to see the truth of each thing. Those who lack knowledge of true being are "simply blind", because they have no clear pattern in their souls to look to and cannot "order the laws about beauty, goodness, justice" here by looking at the original.
- Where the philosopher equals others in experience and virtue and also knows "the very truth of each thing", he must have first place.
- Philosophers love the eternal nature that does not vary through generation and corruption. They love all true being and will renounce no part of it, large or small, honoured or not.

## Marks of the true philosophical nature

- **Truthfulness:** a lover of wisdom cannot also love falsehood, since nothing is more akin to wisdom than truth. He desires all truth from youth and detests intentional falsehood.
- **Temperance:** desire drawn toward knowledge is like a stream diverted into another channel. It weakens bodily pleasure, so the philosopher is temperate and not covetous.
- **No meanness:** a soul that longs for "the whole of things both divine and human" has no corner of illiberality. A mind that surveys all time and existence does not think much of human life and does not fear death.
- **Justice and gentleness:** a harmonious man who is not covetous, boastful or cowardly cannot be unjust or hard in dealings. Socrates says rude, unsociable behaviour in youth distinguishes the unphilosophical from the philosophical.
- **Pleasure in learning and a good memory:** no one loves what brings pain and little progress, and a forgetful soul is "an empty vessel" that ends hating itself and its fruitless labour.
- **Proportion:** truth is akin to proportion, so he needs a well-proportioned, gracious mind that moves of itself toward the true being of everything.
- Only men with these gifts, perfected by years and education, should be entrusted with the state.

## The ship of state: why philosophers look useless

- Adeimantus objects that philosophers become "strange monsters, not to say utter rogues", or useless. Socrates agrees that people say this and answers with a parable.
- A shipowner is strong but a little deaf, short-sighted and ignorant of navigation. The sailors quarrel over the helm, though none has learned the art and they claim it cannot be taught. They drug the captain and take over.
- They praise as "able seaman" whoever helps them seize command, and call the true pilot, who studies the seasons, sky, stars and winds, a "prater" and "star-gazer".
- Socrates concludes that philosophers' uselessness is the fault of those who will not use them. As with the physician, the sick man must go to the doctor: "he who wants to be governed" goes to the one able to govern. The true ruler should not beg subjects to be ruled by him.

## How great natures get corrupted

- Philosophical natures are rare, and they are destroyed by their own virtues (courage, temperance) and by the goods of life: beauty, wealth, strength, rank, connections.
- A seed that lacks proper soil is more damaged the more vigorous it is, so the best natures suffer most in a bad environment. Great crimes spring from "a fulness of nature ruined by education"; weak natures are capable of neither great good nor great evil.
- Socrates denies that private Sophists corrupt the young. The public are "the greatest of all Sophists": in assemblies, courts, theatres and camps the uproar of praise and blame sweeps a young man's sense of good and evil along. They back it with "attainder or confiscation or death".
- He says no one has been trained in virtue by public opinion alone. Whatever is saved in the present evil state of governments "is saved by the power of God".
- Hired Sophists teach only the opinions of the many. He compares them to a keeper who learns the moods of a great beast and calls its likes "good" and its dislikes "evil", with no notion of what the words mean.
- The crowd cannot believe in absolute beauty rather than the many beautiful, so it cannot be philosophical. Flattering friends who want the young man's future power also turn him away from philosophy.

## Pretenders, the rare few, and the right education

- Philosophy is dishonoured by unworthy pretenders: cleverest craftsmen who leap into it like prisoners "running out of prison into a sanctuary". Socrates likens them to a bald little tinker, newly rich, marrying his master's daughter. Their offspring are "sophisms captivating to the ear".
- The worthy disciples are a small remnant: the exiled, those born in mean cities, those who leave arts they despise, or those held back by illness, as with Theages. Socrates mentions his own "internal sign" as a rare monitor.
- Such a man, among wild beasts, shelters "under the shelter of a wall" and lives pure from injustice. This is great work but not the greatest, because only in a suitable state could he be its saviour.
- Socrates says no existing government is worthy of the philosophic nature.
- On education, he criticises studying philosophy only when young, in spare time from moneymaking, and dropping it when dialectic gets hard. Instead:
  - In childhood and youth, study suited to tender years and chief care for the body.
  - As the intellect matures, "increase the gymnastics of the soul".
  - When civic and military duties are past, let them range freely.
- He urges gentleness toward the multitude. The harsh feeling toward philosophy comes from pretenders who "make persons instead of things the theme of their conversation". True philosophers have no time for envy; their eyes are on fixed, orderly things, which they imitate.

## Philosopher-rulers as painters of constitutions

- Cities will not rest from evil until the few philosophers termed useless but not corrupt are compelled to take charge and the state is compelled to obey them. Alternatively, kings or their sons must be inspired with a true love of philosophy. Neither is impossible, only difficult.
- "One is enough": a single philosopher-ruler with an obedient city could bring the ideal polity into being.
- Such rulers would wipe the tablet clean, sketch the constitution, then look "upwards and downwards" between absolute justice, beauty and temperance and the human copy. They would erase some features and keep others, making human ways agreeable to the ways of God.
- Guardians must be tested by pleasures, pains, labours and dangers, and "like gold tried in the refiner's fire". Quick intelligence and steadfastness rarely grow together, so they must also be tested in many kinds of knowledge.

## The Form of the Good: the highest knowledge

- A merely fair measure of the virtues is not enough: "nothing imperfect is the measure of anything". Guardians must take the longer circuit to the highest knowledge, the idea of good, by which all else becomes useful.
- Without the good, no possession or knowledge profits us. Whoever does not know how the just and beautiful are good will be "but a sorry guardian" of them.
- He rejects both common answers:
  - Pleasure: its defenders must admit some pleasures are bad, so good and bad would be the same.
  - Knowledge: its defenders can only say it is knowledge of the good, which is circular.
- Many will accept the appearance of justice, but no one is satisfied with the appearance of good.
- On his own view, Socrates says "all mere opinions are bad, and the best of them blind", and that no one has a right to say positively what he does not know. He declines to state what the good is, offering only its "child", the sun.

## The sun analogy and the divided line

- The many things are seen but not known, and the ideas are known but not seen. Sight needs a third thing, light, and the sun is its lord. The sun is not sight but its author, and the eye is the most sun-like of the senses.
- As the sun is to sight and visible things, the good is to mind and intelligible things. The soul resting on truth and being is radiant with intelligence; turned to the twilight of becoming and perishing, it has opinion only.
- The good gives truth to the known and the power of knowing to the knower. It is more beautiful than knowledge and truth. As the sun gives generation and growth, the good gives being and essence to things known, though it "far exceeds essence in dignity and power".
- The divided line has four sections, each matched to a faculty, with clearness proportional to truth:
  1. Images (shadows, reflections): perception of shadows.
  2. Visible things (animals, plants, artefacts): faith or conviction. The copy is to the original as opinion is to knowledge.
  3. Mathematical objects: understanding. Geometers use hypotheses and diagrams but think of "the absolute square and the absolute diameter".
  4. The ideas, reached by dialectic: reason. Hypotheses serve as "steps and points of departure" to the first principle of the whole, then the soul descends through ideas alone.
