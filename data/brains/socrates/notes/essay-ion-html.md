## Socrates's craft test: judging a work requires knowing the whole art

- Socrates asks whether a person who can judge one speaker well on a subject can also judge the others. His examples:
  - In a discussion of arithmetic, the one who recognizes the best speaker is the arithmetician, and the same person recognizes the worst.
  - In a discussion of wholesome food, that person is the physician.
- His general rule: "he who knows the good" speaker on a topic "will know the bad speaker also." If you cannot judge the bad, you cannot truly judge the good.
- He applies it to Ion, who claims to speak brilliantly about Homer only. Socrates argues that poets such as Hesiod and Archilochus treat the same themes (war, human society, gods, the underworld). So real skill in judging Homer's treatment would carry over to the others.
- His diagnostic: when someone "wakes up" for one author and "goes to sleep" for the rest, he speaks "without any art or knowledge." Mastery of an art as a whole ("poetry is a whole") covers every instance of it.
- Parallel cases he gives from painting, sculpture and music: nobody expounds only Polygnotus, only Daedalus or only Orpheus while having nothing to say about other painters, sculptors or musicians.

## Each art has its own subject matter, so no one expert judges everything

- Socrates holds that different arts give knowledge of different things: "that which we know with one art we do not know with the other." If two arts gave the same knowledge, there would be no sense in calling them different.
- Test case: the five fingers. Two people know there are five, and they know it by the same art, arithmetic. So the same art has the same subject, and different arts have different subjects.
- Consequence: "he who has no knowledge of a particular art will have no right judgment of the sayings and doings of that art."
- He applies this to passages in Homer:
  - Nestor's advice on rounding the turn in a chariot race: the charioteer judges it better than the physician or rhapsode.
  - Hecamede's posset of Pramnian wine, goat's cheese and onion for Machaon: the art of medicine judges it.
  - The leaden plummet on an ox-horn line among the fishes: the fisherman judges it.
  - Theoclymenus' vision of ghosts and the eagle-and-serpent omen: the prophet judges these.
- Socrates says he can assign each passage to its proper art, and invites Ion to name the passages the rhapsode's own art would judge.

## Pressing the claim that a rhapsode knows a general's art

- Ion narrows his claim: the rhapsode knows what a man or woman, a free person or slave, a ruler or subject ought to say. Socrates tests each in turn:
  - A pilot knows better than the rhapsode what the ruler of a storm-tossed ship should say.
  - A physician knows better what the carer of a sick man should say.
  - A cowherd knows better how to soothe infuriated cows.
  - A spinning-woman knows better what to say about wool.
- Ion keeps only the general exhorting soldiers. Socrates points out that Ion may possess the general's art in addition to the rhapsode's, as one man may know both horsemanship and the lyre.
- Socrates separates which art a person judges with. A person who judges horses as a horseman and lyre-players as a musician uses a different art in each case.
- Ion then asserts that the rhapsode's and general's arts are "one and the same," so the best rhapsode must be the best general. Socrates draws out the absurdity:
  - If so, the best rhapsode in Hellas would also be the best general in Hellas.
  - Then Ion would not go about as a rhapsode when he could command armies.
- Ion's excuse is that Ephesus is subject to Athens. Socrates answers with foreigners whom the Athenians appointed general after they showed merit (Apollodorus of Cyzicus, Phanosthenes of Andros, Heraclides of Clazomenae).

## Inspiration, not art, explains Ion's gift: the magnet and the chain of rings

- Socrates's explanation: Ion's gift for speaking about Homer "is not an art, but... an inspiration; there is a divinity moving you."
- His image: Euripides' "magnet," the stone of Heraclea.
  - It attracts iron rings and also gives them the power to attract other rings.
  - A long chain of rings hangs from the original stone, and each ring takes its power from it.
- Applied to poetry:
  - The Muse inspires the poet first.
  - Rhapsodes and actors are the intermediate links.
  - The spectator is the last ring.
  - Through all of them "the God sways the souls of men in any direction which he pleases."
  - Each poet hangs from some Muse (Orpheus, Musaeus, or, for most, Homer). Ion is "possessed by Homer."
- Evidence from Ion's own behavior: he wakes up at Homer and sleeps at any other poet, just as Corybantian revellers respond only to the strain of the god who possesses them.

## Poets compose by divine power, not by rules of art

- Socrates states that good epic and lyric poets "compose their beautiful poems not by art, but because they are inspired and possessed."
  - He compares them to Corybantian dancers who are not in their right mind, and to Bacchic maidens who draw milk and honey from rivers only while possessed.
  - Poets say they bring songs from "honeyed fountains," like bees flying flower to flower.
  - His description: "the poet is a light and winged and holy thing, and there is no invention in him until he has been inspired and is out of his senses, and the mind is no longer in him."
- Why he says it is not art:
  - A poet good at one kind of verse (dithyramb, hymn, choral strain, epic or iambic) is not good at any other. Had he learned by rules of art, he would know how to treat all themes.
  - "God takes away the minds of poets, and uses them as his ministers, as he also uses diviners and holy prophets," so that listeners know the speaker is God himself, "conversing with us."
- Evidence: Tynnichus of Chalcis wrote nothing worth remembering except one famous paean, "simply an invention of the Muses." Socrates reads this as the god showing, "by the mouth of the worst of poets," that the best songs are divine.
- Ion is persuaded: "your words touch my soul."

## The rhapsode's trance and its effect on the audience

- Socrates asks Ion if he is in his right mind when recounting Odysseus leaping onto the floor, Achilles rushing at Hector, or the sorrows of Andromache, Hecuba and Priam. Does his soul not seem to be in Ithaca or Troy?
- Ion admits his eyes fill with tears at pity and his hair stands on end and heart throbs at horrors.
- Socrates then asks about a man at a festival, dressed in holiday attire with golden crowns, who weeps or panics before over twenty thousand friendly faces when nobody is robbing or wronging him. Ion concedes such a man "is not in his right mind," and that he produces similar effects on most spectators.
- Ion adds a practical detail he watches from the stage: the audience's pity, wonder or sternness. He says he attends to it closely because "if I make them cry I myself shall laugh, and if I make them laugh I myself shall cry when the time of payment arrives."

## The closing dilemma: dishonest or inspired

- Socrates says Ion has "as many forms as Proteus," twisting and turning and finally slipping away in the disguise of a general to avoid showing his Homeric art.
- Socrates sets out the choice:
  - If Ion has real art and knowledge about Homer, he is dealing unfairly, because he promised to show it and has not.
  - If he has no art and speaks beautifully under Homer's inspiring influence, Socrates "acquits" him of dishonesty and calls him inspired.
- Ion chooses inspiration as "by far the nobler." Socrates accepts: he will attribute Ion's praise of Homer "to inspiration, and not art."
- Pattern in Socrates's method here:
  - He starts with the person's own claim.
  - He gets agreement to general principles (the unity of an art, the distinct subject of each art).
  - He lets the contradiction between the claim and the principles show.
  - He leaves the person a face-saving alternative.
