## Wealth is what you know how to use

- Socrates defines wealth through use. A possession counts as wealth only if it benefits its owner, and "the same things... are wealth or not wealth, according as a man knows or does not know the use to make of them."
- His examples: a horse is no wealth to a man who is thrown each time he mounts. Land is no wealth if tilling it brings only loss. Sheep and cattle are no wealth to an owner who loses by them. A flute is "no better than the stones we tread under our feet" to someone who cannot play it, unless he sells it.
- Selling does not fix this. If the owner sells the flute for something he cannot use, the sale does not turn it into wealth.
- Money is included. Money spent on a mistress, to the harm of "body and soul and whole estate", is not wealth. Socrates would have such money "banished to the remote corners of the earth rather than be reckoned as wealth."
- Friends are wealth if a man knows how to use them, more truly than cattle are. Even enemies can be turned to profit by one who knows how, and many estates have grown through war.
- Economy is therefore a branch of knowledge. It lets a man enhance his house or estate, meaning all his useful possessions. A skilled man can manage another's estate as well as his own and earn a salary for it.

## Hidden masters: passions that enslave

- Socrates says some people with the knowledge and capital to prosper still do nothing, because they are ruled by unseen masters. The masters are "idleness and effeminacy and reckless negligence", plus false pleasures such as "dicings and profitless associations among men". These look innocent but turn out to be "pains tricked out and decked with pleasures."
- Others work hard yet wear out their substance. They are slaves to "luxury and lechery, intemperance and the wine-cup along with many a fond and ruinous ambition."
- These masters take a man's labour while he is strong and spend it on their own lusts. When he is old and cannot work they abandon him "to his gray hairs and misery" and seize new victims.
- Socrates says to "wage ceaseless war" on these passions "for very freedom's sake", as if they were armed enemies. Even a conquering enemy can improve the conquered by chastening them. These tyrants only torment body, soul and substance.

## Rich is relative to needs: Socrates and Critobulus compared

- Socrates says he has enough. His whole estate, house included, would sell for about five minae. Critobulus's would fetch over a hundred times that.
- Yet Socrates calls Critobulus "singularly poor". His property covers Socrates's wants, but Critobulus's wealth does not cover his obligations.
- He lists those obligations:
  - costly sacrifices to the gods;
  - entertaining foreign guests;
  - feasting fellow citizens to keep their support;
  - state liturgies such as horse-rearing, training choruses and gymnasia;
  - war levies such as trireme pay and war taxes.
- Neglecting any of these, he says, is punished by Athenians as strictly as theft. Critobulus would be only barely well off with three times what he has.
- The worse fault is that Critobulus fancies himself rich, takes no thought to increase his revenue, and amuses himself.
- Socrates's own security is his friends. If he needed anything, they would "deluge my humble living with a flood of plenty" with a trifling contribution. Critobulus's friends are richer than he is but look to him for help.

## Knowing teachers: what Socrates says he can and cannot teach

- Socrates says he cannot teach economy from experience. He never owned the "instrument of the science", which is wealth, so he never went through a pupil stage. He compares himself to a man who never owned a flute and cannot learn it unlent.
- He warns that a learner may spoil the instrument, so practising on Critobulus's estate could ruin it.
- His method is to direct a seeker to better teachers, as one would send a man to another house for fire or water, or to a more skilful music teacher.
- He had long studied who in Athens is most expert in each field. He was amazed that among people doing identical work, half are destitute and half rich.
- When he investigated, he found "it all happened very naturally". Those who ran their affairs haphazardly "were punished by their losses". Those who "kept their wits upon the stretch and paid attention" were rewarded with greater ease and profit.
- He tells Critobulus to learn from the second group and says that, "unless the will of God oppose", he can become a clever man of business.

## Order, discipline and the wife as partner

- Socrates names several contrasts that mark the essentials of economy:
  - Two men build houses, one spending heavily on useless ones, the other building cheaper homes with everything needed.
  - Some households own plenty of furniture but cannot find any when needed, which torments the family and servants. Others have everything ready at once, because each thing has "that to which it naturally belongs", not just the first place it lands.
  - Some slaves are fettered and keep running away. Others are loosed, yet choose to work and stay loyal.
  - Two farmers work alike farms: one says agriculture ruined him, the other has abundance from the same art. Socrates also means those who claim to be farmers but lack money for the real needs of the business.
  - Some men grow poor with horses and others rich.
- He tells Critobulus to come and look, and to test himself. Critobulus will rise at cock-crow and trek far to see a comedy, yet never invites Socrates to see such cases. Socrates says that is "far more ridiculous to yourself".
- He says Critobulus watches people as a theatre-goer watches actors, entertained but not aiming to be a poet. When circumstances force him to deal with horses, it is foolish not to avoid being "a mere amateur."
- On wives, Socrates says some husbands gain true helpmates in their wives, and others suffer disaster. He compares this to shepherds and riders: blame falls on the keeper.
- If a wife was taught and still does wrong, she may be to blame. If her husband "never tried to teach her the first principles of 'fair and noble' conduct", then the husband is to blame.
- Critobulus admits he entrusts important matters to his wife more than anyone, yet talks with her less than almost anyone. She married very young with little knowledge of the world. Socrates says it would be more astonishing if she knew how to speak and act well than if she went astray.
- A good wife, he says, is the husband's "counterpoise and counterpart for good". The husband brings goods into the house, but the wife's thrift checks most of the expenditure, and on that depends whether the estate grows or shrinks.
- He offers to introduce Critobulus to Aspasia, who can explain this "in a far more scientific way".

## Which occupations suit a free citizen

- Socrates says the "base mechanic arts" have a bad name and are rightly held in ill repute by civilised communities. They ruin the bodies of workers and overseers who must sit at the loom or crouch before a furnace. Weakened bodies bring weakened souls.
- Such work leaves no leisure for friendship and the state, so those engaged in it are "sorry friends and ill defenders of the fatherland". Some warlike states bar citizens from any mechanical craft.
- He recommends the two arts the Persian king honours most, husbandry and war, and says the king takes a close interest in both.
- Socrates's evidence:
  - Governors must keep set numbers of cavalry, archers, slingers and targeteers. The king reviews troops each year, in person or by trusted proxies.
  - He honours officers whose forces are complete and well equipped, and punishes and replaces those who neglect or profit from their posts.
  - On royal progresses he inspects land. He rewards governors whose districts are thickly populated and fully cultivated, and removes those whose land lies idle through harshness, insolence or neglect.
  - Civil governors, who oversee the population and the farmers, and military commandants, who guard the land, can accuse each other. A badly tilled land cannot support its garrisons or pay tribute. A satrap holds both roles.
  - The king keeps "paradises" (orchards and gardens) wherever he lives and spends his days there when the season allows.
- Gifts go first to brave warriors, "since all the ploughing in the world were but small gain in the absence of those who should protect the fields", and next to those who made their lands productive. Cyrus said he deserved gifts of both kinds, since he excelled at stocking the country and protecting it.
- Socrates takes it as proof of a ruler's worth that followers follow him freely. Not one man deserted Cyrus for his brother the king, while tens of thousands went the other way. When Cyrus died, his friends died fighting around his body.
- In the story told by Lysander, Cyrus showed him his paradise at Sardis, with trees planted in straight, regular rows. Cyrus said he had measured and arranged it himself and planted some trees with his own hands. He added: "when in ordinary health I never dream of sitting down to supper without first practising some exercise of war or husbandry in the sweat of my brow". Lysander replied: "Cyrus, you have indeed good right to be a happy man, since you are happy in being a good man."

## Why husbandry is the noblest livelihood

- Socrates calls agriculture "thrice blest". It gives a sense of luxury, the satisfaction of an improved estate, and the physical training of a free man.
- The earth freely provides necessities and many luxuries, including flowers and incense for altars, food, and animals for sacrifice. She does not let her gifts be received effeminately, but trains men to endure summer's heat and winter's cold.
- Those who labour with their hands gain strength, and those who oversee and think are made "more manly", rising at cock-crow and making long journeys.
- It supports public service. Farming lets a man keep a warhorse for cavalry, builds a foot-soldier's robustness, and supplies the hunt and hounds. Horses and dogs repay the farm: the horse carries the master to work, and the dog guards flocks.
- It trains for war. Men are fitted "to run, and hurl the spear, and leap with the best".
- It also builds justice and mutual help. Earth "gives lessons in justice and uprightness": "the nobler the service of devotion rendered, the ampler the riches of her recompense."
- If invaders seize the work of their hands, men raised on husbandry are strong enough to take supplies from the enemy, and in war it is often safer to seek food "with sword and buckler than with all the instruments of husbandry".
- The husbandman must create enthusiasm and ready obedience in his workers, as a general does with gifts for the brave and punishment for the disorderly. Both need seasons of exhortation, and slaves as well as free men need "the lure of hope and happy expectation".
- He endorses the saying that husbandry is "the mother and nurse of all the arts". When the land lies desert, "the spring that feeds the other arts is dried up."
- He sums it up with a rural image: a generous hostess who keeps open house, with a warm bath and fire in winter and cool streams and shade in summer. She is a favourite of the good wife and the children and welcomes the master's friends.

## Husbandry lies in the gods' hands: piety and the search for a "beautiful and good" man

- When Critobulus objects that most farming outcomes are beyond human control (hail, frost, drought, flood, mildew, murrain), Socrates answers that farming and war alike lie "in the hands of the gods".
- Men about to fight seek divine favour with sacrifices and omens. Farmers should do the same: "the wise and prudent will pay service to the gods" for crops, cattle, horses, sheep, goats, and all possessions, "great and small, without exception."
- In his summary, Socrates restates the agreed points: husbandry is the noblest employment for a gentleman, a "beautiful and good" man. It is the easiest to learn and the pleasantest to follow. It gives the body beauty and hardihood and leaves the soul leisure for friendship and civic duty.
- It also makes men brave, because the crops grow outside the city walls. It is held in highest repute by statesmen and states for producing the best citizens.
- To test the different attitudes, he suggests seating farmers and artisans apart and asking whether to defend the fields or guard the walls. Farmers would vote to defend the land. Artisans, trained to sit still, would vote not to fight.
- Socrates describes how he sought a model. He quickly examined carpenters, bronze-workers, painters and sculptors, but wanted to know what justified the title "beautiful and good".
- He first approached beautiful people, hoping to find "good" joined to beauty, but found some who were outwardly beautiful and inwardly "the veriest knaves". He then set aside outward beauty and sought out Ischomachus, whom everyone called "beautiful and good". Their conversation fills the later chapters.
