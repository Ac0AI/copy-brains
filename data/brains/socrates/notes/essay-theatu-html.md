## Socrates's midwifery: drawing out and testing other people's ideas

- Socrates says he is the son of a midwife, Phaenarete, and practises midwifery himself, but on men's souls instead of women's bodies. He attends them "when they are in labour" and examines whether what the mind brings forth is "a false idol or a noble and true birth."
- He compares his own situation to the midwives' own. Artemis lets only women past childbearing be midwives, and Socrates is "barren" of wisdom. He says: "the god compels me to be a midwife, but does not allow me to bring forth."
- He admits the common reproach that he asks questions and never answers them is "very just." He claims "I am not myself at all wise, nor have I anything to show which is the invention or birth of my own soul."
- The test is whether the offspring is real or a "wind-egg." After a birth he proposes to "run round the hearth" with the child and decide whether it is "worth rearing" or "only a wind-egg and a sham."
- Like a midwife who also knows the best matches, he can tell who is not pregnant with ideas. He sends such people on to other teachers, "many of them I have given away to Prodicus."
- The pain is part of the work. Those who consort with him suffer "perplexity and travail" day and night, which he says is worse than the pangs of childbirth.

## Why Socrates will refute a cherished idea, and how he wants it taken

- He warns Theaetetus not to "quarrel with me" if his first conception is exposed as "a vain shadow." Some people were "ready to bite me when I deprived them of a darling folly."
- He says he acts "from goodwill," because "no god is the enemy of man." It would be wrong for him "to admit falsehood, or to stifle the truth."
- He says that disciples who learn from him learn nothing from him. "It is quite clear that they never learned anything from me; the many fine discoveries to which they cling are of their own making." He and the god only deliver them.
- Some pupils left early out of self-conceit or bad company. They lost what he had delivered and "stifled whatever else they had in them." They ended "seeing themselves, as others see them, to be great fools." Aristeides, son of Lysimachus, is one. Truants often return and ask to start again, and he takes them back only "if my familiar allows."
- He denies that the theories he lays out are his own. "None of these theories come from me; they all come from him who talks with me. I only know just enough to extract them from the wisdom of another, and to receive them in a spirit of fairness."

## "What is knowledge?": ask for the one nature, not a list of examples

- Socrates opens with a puzzle he says he "can never solve to my satisfaction": what knowledge is. He first gets agreement that learning is growing wiser, that wisdom is what makes the wise wise, and so wisdom and knowledge are the same.
- Theaetetus first lists geometry, astronomy, harmony, calculation, and the crafts such as cobbling. Socrates says this is "Too much" and gives many things when he asked for "one simple thing."
- Listing the subject matter of each art does not answer the question. "We wanted to know not the subjects, nor yet the number of the arts or sciences... but... the nature of knowledge in the abstract."
- His illustration: asked "What is clay?", it is absurd to answer with "a clay of potters, a clay of oven-makers, a clay of brick-makers." The short answer is "clay is moistened earth."
- He argues that you cannot understand a name without knowing the nature of the thing. Someone who does not know what knowledge is has no real knowledge of shoemaking or any other craft.
- He wants Theaetetus to repeat the move he made with square roots. Theaetetus had grouped the endless roots under one scheme, splitting numbers into square (equal factors) and oblong (unequal factors) numbers, with roots of the second kind called "powers." Socrates says "try and bring the many sorts of knowledge under one definition."
- He encourages the failure to find an answer: the task "would task the powers of men perfect in every way." Missing it does not make the praise of Theaetetus false, just as a boy runner beaten by a man is still a fast boy.

## Wonder as the beginning of philosophy, and how to teach with questions

- When Theaetetus says he is amazed and dizzy at the puzzles about size and change, Socrates replies that this shows his nature: "wonder is the feeling of a philosopher, and philosophy begins in wonder."
- He cites the genealogy that Iris, the messenger of heaven, is the child of Thaumas, "wonder."
- He tells the young man to "take courage," "quit yourself like a man," and say what he thinks, and he praises a brave answer: "that is the way in which you should express your opinion." He does not let Theaetetus escape by saying "I cannot tell."
- He softens a refutation with encouragement. After a good answer he says there will be no dispute "because I want you to grow."
- He explains his reason for the long discussion: he and Theaetetus "have no professional aims" and only want to see "whether they are consistent with each or not." He prefers to "calmly and patiently review our own thoughts" over a "sparring-match."
- He asks that people who have never practised the question-and-answer method be coaxed in. Theodorus, who is "unused to your game of question and answer," is pushed to "strip" and wrestle with an argument, and Socrates says doing so "will do yourself good as well as me."

## Test of the claim "knowledge is perception" (Protagoras)

- Theaetetus claims that knowledge is perception. Socrates links it to Protagoras: "Man is the measure of all things." Things are to each person as they appear, as with a wind that feels cold to one and not to another. Appearing and perceiving are the same, so perception is always of what is and is "unerring."
- Socrates lays out the supporting "Heracleitean" view, which he does not endorse as his own. Everything is motion and becoming, nothing "is," and a colour belongs to neither eye nor object but to what passes between them for each perceiver.
- He then gives the objections that arise. Dreams, illnesses and madness produce false perceptions, and nobody can prove we are awake rather than dreaming. Truth cannot be settled by how long a state lasts.
- A key argument: knowing and seeing cannot be the same. A man who has seen a thing and now closes his eyes still remembers it, so he would know it while "not seeing" it, which the theory makes impossible. So "knowledge and perception are one" involves "a manifest impossibility," and they "must be distinguished."
- Letters and foreign speech: we know and perceive the shape and colour of unfamiliar letters, and the pitch of foreign sounds. We do not perceive by sight or hearing what grammarians and interpreters teach about them.
- He also points out that if each person is the sole judge of what is true for them, nobody needs Protagoras as a teacher, and "the whole art of dialectic" and midwifery would be a "tedious and enormous piece of folly."

## Where Protagoras undermines himself, and the line between disputing and dialectic

- Everyone thinks others are wiser or more ignorant than they are in some matters. In danger at sea, in war or in sickness, people "look up to their commanders as if they were gods" for their knowledge. This belief in wisdom and ignorance as true or false opinion contradicts the claim that all opinions are true.
- If everyone's opinion is true, then the many who think Protagoras is wrong are right. "His truth is more untrue than true" in proportion as the many outnumber the one, and his own thesis becomes "true neither to himself nor to any one else."
- Socrates will not pretend that every opinion is equally good. He grants that on immediate sensations such as hot, dry or sweet each person's appearance stands. But about health, and about what is expedient for a city, some people and some counsellors are wiser than others.
- He lets Protagoras speak in his own defence and says that the wise man replaces bad impressions with better ones, as a physician does with drugs or a farmer with plants. Socrates shows he wants the strongest version of the opposing view argued before rejecting it.
- He separates sophistic disputation from dialectic. He criticises himself and Theaetetus for "being satisfied with mere verbal consistency," "like a good-for-nothing cock" that crows without winning the victory.
- A paid fighter in argument can trap a young person by tripping him on words and make him pay "the sum to be paid for your release." By contrast, the dialectician "will be in earnest, and only correct his adversary when necessary," so that the person "will lay the blame of his own confusion and perplexity on himself," love the questioner, and "escape from himself into philosophy, in order that he may become different from what he was."
