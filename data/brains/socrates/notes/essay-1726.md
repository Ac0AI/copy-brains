## The philosopher and the lawyer: leisure, freedom and the soul

Socrates contrasts those trained in philosophy with those trained in the law courts, as a freeman differs from a slave.
- The philosopher has leisure. He "has his talk out in peace," wanders from subject to subject, and does not care whether his words are many or few. "His only aim is to attain the truth."
- The lawyer is always hurried by the water clock and bound by the indictment. He argues before a master who holds the verdict, and the trial usually concerns his own life.
- This makes him keen and shrewd, skilled at flattering his master in word and deed. But "his soul is small and unrighteous." Fear and deception in youth leave him "stunted and warped," and he thinks himself a master of wisdom.
- Socrates adds that the philosopher looks foolish in court because he is untrained in trivial matters. He knows no scandal and cannot answer insults.
- Socrates says the lawyer gets dizzy and stammers when pulled up to questions about justice, injustice and human happiness. Anyone not brought up a slave sees this.

## The philosopher's detachment and what he laughs at

Socrates describes the philosopher as present in the city only in body, while his mind measures earth and heaven.
- He does not know the way to the agora, the courts or the assemblies. He ignores clubs, banquets and the gossip about who has disgraced his family.
- The story of Thales is the model. He fell into a well while watching the stars, and a Thracian maid joked that he could not see what was at his feet. Socrates says the jest applies to every philosopher: he hardly knows whether his neighbor is a man or an animal, and he asks what the nature of man is.
- He laughs at praise of tyrants and kings. To him they are like herdsmen praised for the milk they squeeze from a harder animal to manage, and they are as uneducated as shepherds because they have no leisure.
- He sees ten thousand acres as a trifle, since he is used to thinking of the whole earth.
- He sees pride in a long pedigree as poverty of ideas. Every man has had thousands of ancestors, rich and poor, kings and slaves, Greeks and barbarians, and "a little arithmetic" would cure the vanity.

## Becoming like God: why virtue is pursued

Socrates says evils can never pass away, because something must always oppose good. So a man should "fly away from earth to heaven as quickly as we can."
- "To fly away is to become like God, as far as this is possible; and to become like him, is to become holy, just, and wise."
- He rejects the common reason for virtue, which is only seeming good, calling it "an old wives' fable." God is perfect righteousness, and the most righteous man is most like him.
- Knowing this is true wisdom and virtue, and ignorance of it is folly and vice. The cleverness of politicians and of the arts is "coarse and vulgar" by comparison.
- The unjust man does not know the real penalty of injustice. It is not blows or death, which wrongdoers often escape, but a penalty that cannot be escaped.
- Two patterns are always set before men, one blessed and divine and one godless and wretched. Evildoers grow like the second, and live in the likeness of their own evil selves with evil friends.
- Socrates notes that such clever men, if they stay in private argument, grow discontented with themselves. "Their rhetoric fades away, and they become helpless as children."

## Refuting Protagoras on the future and on expert judgment

Socrates argues against "man is the measure of all things" using the future and expertise.
- Laws aim at expediency, which concerns the future. States often fail in this aim, so a state's belief about what is good does not make it so.
- If each man is his own judge of what will be, a layman and a physician would both be right about whether a fever is coming. Socrates calls this ludicrous.
- Examples of experts who judge the future better: the vinegrower on the coming vintage, the musician on what will sound harmonious, the cook on the pleasure of a dinner in preparation.
- Protagoras sold his teaching as superior judgment of what will persuade a court. That admits one man is wiser than another, and so a wise man is the measure.
- Socrates adds that he, "who know nothing," has no obligation to accept the honor of being a measure of anything.

## Why knowledge is not perception

Against the flux theory, Socrates argues that if everything is always changing, no word such as "white" or "seeing" can be used rightly. Every answer would be equally right, and the theory would need a new language, perhaps the word "no how."
- Knowledge is not perception, because the senses are instruments, and perception is through the eyes and ears, not with them. Different senses meet in one mind.
- The mind grasps things the senses cannot: being and not-being, likeness and unlikeness, sameness and difference, unity and number, the noble and the base, good and evil.
- The soul reaches these "by a power of her own," comparing past, present and future. Their meaning is "slowly and hardly gained, if they are ever gained, by education and long experience."
- Without grasping being, a man cannot attain truth, and without truth there is no knowledge. So "knowledge does not consist in impressions of sense, but in reasoning about them."

## Thinking as the soul's silent conversation, and the puzzle of false opinion

Theaetetus then offers "true opinion" as knowledge. Socrates takes up how false opinion is possible.
- He defines thinking: "the conversation which the soul holds with herself," asking and answering, affirming and denying. Opinion is "a word spoken," silently, to oneself.
- Puzzle: a thing is known or unknown. A man cannot mistake one known thing for another known thing, nor think of what is not, since that is thinking of nothing.
- Treating false opinion as swapping one thing for another fails too. No one tells himself that an ox is a horse or that odd is even.
- His first model is a wax block in the soul, with memory as the seal. Error comes from fitting a present perception to the wrong impression, "like a bad archer" who misses the mark. Deep, clean, well-tempered wax makes men wise. Hard, soft or muddy wax makes them confused and ignorant.
- It fails on pure numbers: people do mistake five plus seven for eleven. That error would involve no perception.
- His second model is an aviary of knowledges. A man can possess a bird without having it in hand, and error is catching the wrong bird. This too collapses into a circle.
- Socrates concludes that it is shameless to explain the verb "to know" before knowing what knowledge is, and that "that must be first ascertained; then, the nature of false opinion."

## Knowledge as true opinion with an account, and why each version fails

Socrates tests the idea that knowledge is true opinion plus a rational account.
- True opinion alone is not knowledge. Orators and lawyers persuade judges of events no one witnessed. The judges may reach a true opinion "without knowledge," but cannot be taught the facts in the time of a water clock.
- The "dream" theory says the simple elements can only be named, and complexes are known by combining names. Socrates tests it with letters and syllables.
- A man who knows the syllable "SO" must know S and O. If the syllable is a single form with no parts, it is as unknowable as a letter. So either way the theory fails. Socrates says elements are more certainly known than compounds, as shown by learning to read and by the harp-player who knows each note.
- Three meanings of "account" fail in turn:
  - Giving verbal expression of one's thought. Everyone who has a right opinion can do this, so it does not separate knowledge from opinion.
  - Listing the elements, as a wagon is wheels, axle, body, rims and yoke. A learner who spells "Theaetetus" correctly once but misspells it another time has right opinion and an enumeration, yet not knowledge.
  - Naming the mark that distinguishes a thing from all others, as the sun is the brightest body in the heavens. This is circular, because it demands a right opinion of the differences we are supposed to be explaining, so we "go round and round."
- Socrates ends without a definition. Knowledge is neither sensation, nor true opinion, nor true opinion with an account.

## Socratic midwifery and what failed inquiry is for

Socrates closes by describing what his questioning does.
- His art is midwifery: "The office of a midwife I, like my mother, have received from God; she delivered women, I deliver men."
- He tests whether what is born is real or "wind." Here the offspring of Theaetetus's brain were "not worth bringing up."
- The benefit of a failed inquiry: if Theaetetus conceives again, he will be better for it. If not, "you will be soberer and humbler and gentler to other men, and will be too modest to fancy that you know what you do not know."
- Socrates states the limits of his art: he claims no knowledge of what great and famous men know.
- Earlier he praised boldness in answering, because "either we shall find what we seek, or we shall be less likely to think that we know what we do not know."
- He ends by leaving for the porch of the King Archon, where he is to meet Meletus and his indictment.
