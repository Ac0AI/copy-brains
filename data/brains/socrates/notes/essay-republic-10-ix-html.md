## The tyrannical soul is formed when one master passion enslaves the rest

- Socrates holds that everyone, even good people, has a lawless "wild-beast nature" that "peers out in sleep." It is the set of unnecessary, unlawful appetites that wake when reason is asleep.
- In the self-controlled person these appetites are banished or left few and weak. In the unruly person they are strong and numerous.
- The tyrannical man is built in stages. A son grows up under a democratic father, is pulled into what seducers call "perfect liberty," and then has a single master passion planted in him as lord over his idle lusts.
- Socrates images this passion as "a sort of monstrous winged drone." Madness becomes captain of its guard.
- The passion drives out any good opinions, appetites and shame in the soul until temperance is purged and madness fills it.
- He links this to old sayings: love is called a tyrant, and a drunk or deranged man fancies he can rule even the gods.
- The tyrannical man appears when nature, habit or both make him "drunken, lustful, passionate."

## How the tyrannical man lives, and why he ends up a criminal

- Desire is the lord of his house. Feasts and revelry spend his money, then comes debt, and then his desires cry out "like young ravens" for more.
- He needs money "no matter how" to escape pain, so he takes from his parents. He cheats them first, then uses force, and feels no compunction about it.
- Socrates notes the irony of him preferring a new, dispensable lover over his mother and father, his "ancient friends" and most necessary ones. He says "a tyrannical son is a blessing to his father and mother."
- He moves on to burglary, stealing cloaks and robbing temples. His childhood opinions about good and evil are overthrown by the freed passions, which now guard love's empire.
- What he once did only in dreams (murder, forbidden acts) he now does awake.
- When such men are many and feel their strength, they pick the one with the most tyrant in his own soul and make him tyrant of the city. If the city resists, he beats his fatherland as he beat his parents.
- As private men they flatter and fawn when they need something and drop people once satisfied.
- Socrates' verdict: "They are always either the masters or servants and never the friends of anybody; the tyrant never tastes of true freedom or friendship." They are treacherous and utterly unjust.
- He calls the tyrant "the waking reality of what we dreamed," and says the longer he lives the more tyrant he becomes.

## The tyrant is a slave, poor and afraid, and the real tyrant is the most miserable of all

- Socrates sets the city and the soul side by side. A city under a tyrant is enslaved, poor, full of fear, and rich in lamentation. The tyrannical soul is the same.
- That soul is a slave's, because its best parts are enslaved and a small, maddest part rules.
- It is least able to do what it wants, goaded by a gadfly and full of remorse.
- It is poor and insatiable, "full of fear."
- He then argues that the man who is tyrannical by nature and also rules as a public tyrant is more miserable than a private tyrant.
- Analogy: a rich man with some fifty slaves lives without fear because the whole city protects every owner. Carry him into a wilderness with no freemen to help him and he must flatter and cajole his own slaves. Surround him with neighbours who would kill a slave-master and his plight is worse still.
- The tyrant lives in exactly that prison. He cannot travel or see what free citizens see. He "lives in his hole like a woman hidden in the house" and envies anyone who goes abroad.
- He must "be master of others when he is not master of himself," like a paralytic forced to fight other men.
- Socrates concludes: "He who is the real tyrant, whatever men may think, is the real slave," forced into servility and flattery of the vilest people.
- Power makes him worse: more jealous, faithless, unjust, friendless and impious, "the purveyor and cherisher of every sort of vice."
- Judge by someone with insight who has seen the tyrant at home "stripped of his tragedy attire" and in public danger, not by the pomp that dazzles outsiders like children.

## Proclaiming the verdict: the five regimes ranked, and the first proof

- Socrates has Glaucon rank the five regimes and the matching souls by virtue and vice, happiness and misery: royal, timocratic, oligarchic, democratic, tyrannical.
- The announcement: "the son of Ariston (the best) has decided that the best and justest is also the happiest," the man "most royal" and "king over himself." The worst and most unjust is the most miserable, "he who being the greatest tyrant of himself is also the greatest tyrant of his State."
- He adds that this holds "whether seen or unseen by gods and men."
- This is his first proof, resting on the parallel between city and soul.
- He calls the question of good and evil "the greatest" of all questions and insists the answer should be reasoned out, not conjectured.

## Second proof: three parts of the soul, three kinds of people, and who is qualified to judge pleasure

- The soul has three principles, each with its own pleasure, desire and rule:
  - the part that learns, which loves wisdom and knowledge
  - the spirited part, which loves ruling, conquering and honour
  - the appetitive part, which loves money and gain because money satisfies its desires
- So there are three kinds of people: lovers of wisdom, honour and gain. Each praises his own life as pleasantest and dismisses the others.
- Socrates asks how to decide, since the question is which life is more pleasant, not more honourable. The criterion he picks is "experience and wisdom and reason."
- On experience, the philosopher has tasted the other pleasures from childhood. The lover of gain has hardly tasted the sweetness of knowing truth.
- Honour comes to the rich, brave and wise alike, so all have tasted it. Only the philosopher knows the delight of knowing true being.
- On the instrument of judgment, reason is the philosopher's own tool, not the covetous or ambitious man's.
- Result: the pleasure of the intelligent part is pleasantest, the life of the honour-lover comes second and the gain-lover's last. In his words, "the wise man speaks with authority when he approves of his own life."
- This is the second time the just man has overthrown the unjust.

## Third proof: true pleasure versus shadow pleasure

- Socrates notes there is a neutral state of rest between pleasure and pain. The sick say nothing is pleasanter than health, and people in pain say nothing is pleasanter than being rid of it.
- Rest cannot be both pleasure and pain, since pleasure and pain are motions of the soul and the neutral state is not motion. So relief from pain only appears pleasant by contrast. It is "an appearance only and not a reality."
- Many pleasures have no antecedent pain, such as smells. They come and go without leaving pain, which shows pure pleasure is not mere cessation of pain.
- The strong bodily pleasures are mostly reliefs of pain, as are anticipations of pleasures and pains.
- Illustration: a man carried from the lower to the middle region thinks he has gone up. Someone who has never seen the true upper region mistakes the middle for it. Likewise, people inexperienced in truth mistake freedom from pain for pleasure, "like contrasting black with grey instead of white."
- Hunger and thirst are emptiness of the body. Ignorance and folly are emptiness of the soul. Food fills the one and wisdom the other.
- What is filled with things of more real being (true opinion, knowledge, mind, virtue) is more truly filled and enjoys truer pleasure than what is filled with perishable things.
- Those who know no wisdom or virtue and live for gluttony and sensuality "go down and up again as far as the mean" and never reach the true upper world.
- He compares them to cattle with eyes always on the ground and heads at the dining-table. They fatten, breed, and "kick and butt at one another with horns and hoofs which are made of iron," killing one another from insatiable lust.
- Their pleasures are shadows mixed with pain, fought over as the Greeks fought over the shadow of Helen at Troy.
- The spirited part too goes wrong when it pursues honour or anger "without reason or sense."
- Lovers of money and honour who follow reason get the truest pleasures available to them. When the whole soul follows the philosophical principle, each part does its own work, is just, and enjoys its own best pleasures.
- The more a desire departs from law and order, the further it is from reason, so the tyrant lives farthest from true pleasure and the king nearest.

## The 729 calculation: how much more pleasantly the king lives

- Socrates counts the steps from true pleasure. The tyrant is third from the oligarch, who is third from the royal man, so the tyrant is removed from true pleasure by a number three times three.
- His "shadow pleasure" is a plane figure (length by width), and raised to a solid it is cubed.
- Result: "the king is parted from the tyrant in truth of pleasure... living 729 times more pleasantly, and the tyrant more painfully by this same interval."
- He remarks that the number "nearly concerns human life," since it can be set against days, nights, months and years.
- He adds that the just man's superiority in propriety of life, beauty and virtue is "immeasurably greater" still.

## The image of the soul, and why injustice never profits

- To answer the claim that injustice pays for a man reputed to be just, Socrates builds an image of the soul. It has a many-headed beast that can grow heads of tame and wild animals, a lion, and a man, all joined and wrapped in a single human shape.
- The defender of injustice is in effect saying it pays to feed the monster and strengthen the lion while starving the man and letting the three bite and devour each other.
- The defender of justice says to give the man within complete mastery. Like a good husbandman, he should cultivate the gentle heads, stop the wild ones growing, make the lion his ally, and unite all the parts with one another and with himself.
- Socrates tests the unjust man with questions. The noble is what subjects the beast to the man, "or rather to the god in man." The ignoble subjects the man to the beast.
- Taking gold to enslave the best part of oneself to the worst is worse than selling a son or daughter into slavery to evil men. It is selling "his own divine being to that which is most godless." He compares it to Eriphyle taking the necklace for her husband's life.
- Common blame maps onto the image:
  - Intemperance means the monster is let loose.
  - Pride and bad temper mean the lion and serpent grow too strong.
  - Luxury and softness weaken the lion.
  - Flattery and meanness mean the lion is trained for money until he becomes a monkey.
- Mean trades and manual arts are a reproach because the higher principle is so weak that the person must flatter the creatures within.
- On rule, it is better for everyone to be ruled by divine wisdom within, or failing that by an outside authority, so all are "friends and equals." This, he says, is not for the servant's injury, answering Thrasymachus.
- He sees the same purpose in law, and in how children are not freed until a ruling principle has been set up in them.
- Injustice never profits: "He who is undetected only gets worse, whereas he who is detected and punished has the brutal part of his nature silenced and humanized." The soul is perfected by justice, temperance and wisdom more than the body is by beauty, strength or health.

## How the man of understanding lives

- He honours studies that shape his soul and ignores others.
- He trains his body to preserve the soul's harmony, so he treats even health as secondary. His aim is not to be fair or strong or well unless that brings temperance.
- He gets wealth by a principle of order and will not be dazzled by the world's applause or heap up riches to his own harm.
- He looks at "the city which is within him," guards against disorder from excess or want, and gains or spends accordingly.
- He accepts honours that will make him better and avoids those, public or private, that would disorder his life.
- On politics, he will not be a statesman in his native land "unless he have a divine call," but he will rule in the city of the founders' own making.
- That city exists only in idea: "In heaven... there is laid up a pattern of it, methinks, which he who desires may behold, and beholding, may set his own house in order." Whether it ever exists matters little, since he lives by its manner and no other's.
