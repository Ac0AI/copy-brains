## Socrates tests "paying debts and telling the truth" as a definition of justice

- Cephalus offers justice as "to speak the truth and to pay your debts."
- Socrates tests it with a counter-example. If a friend left weapons with you while sane and asks for them back when mad, returning them is wrong, and so is telling him the truth.
- His conclusion: truth-telling and debt-paying "is not a correct definition of justice."
- He treats a definition as something to be tested against exceptions, not accepted because it sounds right.
- He says he likes talking with the old because they are travellers on a road he may also have to take. He asks Cephalus whether the way is "smooth and easy, or rugged and difficult."
- Cephalus says old age is bearable for a calm, good-natured man whatever his wealth. Socrates draws out that the chief value of money is peace of mind: no need to deceive or defraud anyone, and no debts to men or offerings owed to the gods.

## Socrates refutes "justice is doing good to friends and harm to enemies"

Polemarchus, defending Simonides, reworks the definition into "justice is the art which gives good to friends and evil to enemies." Socrates takes it apart in four steps.

- **Art analogy.** Medicine gives drugs and food to bodies, and cookery gives seasoning. Socrates asks what justice gives. The physician helps friends in sickness and the pilot at sea. Justice would help friends and harm enemies in war and alliances.
- **Uselessness in peace.** If justice is useful only in war, it is useless in peace. Polemarchus says it serves in contracts and partnerships. Socrates shows that in every real partnership an expert is better: the draughts-player, the builder, the horse expert, the shipwright.
- **Safekeeping.** The just man is preferred only when money is "to be kept safely" and not used. Socrates concludes justice is "useful when money is useless," and so "not good for much."
- **The thief.** The best guard of anything is also the best thief, as the best boxer is best at warding off blows. So the just man who keeps money well would be good at stealing it. Socrates jokes that justice becomes "an art of theft," which Homer's Autolycus "excellent above all men in theft and perjury" would approve.

## Why harming anyone, even an enemy, can never be just

- **Appearance versus reality.** People often misjudge who is good or bad. If justice means helping friends and harming enemies as they seem, the just would end up harming the good. Polemarchus admits "the doctrine is immoral."
- **Repair.** Polemarchus fixes it: help friends only if they are good, and harm enemies only if they are wicked.
- **Harm degrades.** Socrates asks whether injured horses and dogs get better or worse at their own excellence. They get worse. Injured men likewise get worse in "the proper virtue of man," which is justice, so harming them makes them more unjust.
- **No art does the opposite of its aim.** The musician cannot make men unmusical, the horseman cannot make bad riders, and heat cannot produce cold. So the just man cannot, by justice, make men unjust: "the good cannot harm any one."
- **Conclusion.** Harming anyone, friend or enemy, is "the act of the opposite, who is the unjust."
- **Origin of the saying.** Socrates suggests it belongs to a rich and mighty man with a high opinion of his own power, such as Periander, Perdiccas, Xerxes or Ismenias, not to a wise man like Simonides, Bias or Pittacus.

## Answering and questioning: Socrates on Thrasymachus' demand

- Thrasymachus bursts in, accusing Socrates of only asking and refusing to answer. He also bans the answers "duty, advantage, profit, gain, interest."
- Socrates replies that a seeker of justice is like a seeker of gold. Two men looking for it together do not "knock under" to each other, since justice is "more precious than many pieces of gold."
- He gives the twelve example: if you ask what makes twelve and forbid "twice six, three times four, six times two, four times three," no one can answer. Even a forbidden answer, if it seems true, should be given.
- He says of himself that he "knows just nothing," and cannot be expected to speak when told by a man of authority not to utter his half-formed notions.
- He learns from others and pays for it "in praise, which is all I have."
- Socrates asks Thrasymachus to accept the hearers' pity, not anger, since they "cannot" find the answer.
- He says he does not ask in order to injure or trick Thrasymachus, and would "as well shave a lion" as try to cheat him.
- He prefers answering one another by admissions, so the inquirers "unite the offices of judge and advocate in our own persons." The alternative is rival set speeches that need outside judges.
- He asks only for consistency: "if you change, change openly and let there be no deception."

## Refuting "justice is the interest of the stronger"

Thrasymachus holds that every government makes laws in its own interest, and that obeying those laws is justice. Socrates answers in two stages.

- **Rulers can err.** Thrasymachus admits rulers sometimes mistake their own interest, and that subjects must obey. Then subjects are sometimes commanded to do what harms the stronger, so justice is "the reverse" of the stronger's interest.
- **The strict-sense ruler.** Thrasymachus retreats: a ruler "in the strictest sense" never errs, as a true physician or arithmetician does not err while practising the skill.
- **Arts serve their subject.** Socrates accepts that standard. Every art has an interest, which is the perfection of its subject. Medicine considers the body, horsemanship the horse. No art serves itself, since arts are complete and have no needs.
- **Rulers as artists.** The true physician is a healer, not a money-maker. The true pilot is a captain, not a mere sailor. So no ruler "in so far as he is a ruler" considers his own interest, only that of his subject. The arts rule over the weaker and serve them.
- **Shepherd.** Thrasymachus says shepherds fatten sheep for their own table. Socrates replies that this treats the shepherd as a diner or trader, not as a shepherd. The shepherd's art concerns only the flock.

## Why rulers need payment, and why good men rule reluctantly

- **Pay is a separate art.** Each art has its own function and good. Medicine gives health, building gives houses. Pay comes from the additional art of payment. A man who works for nothing still confers benefit, so the benefit comes from the art and not from the pay.
- **Why rulers are paid.** No one wants to reform others' evils without remuneration, so rulers must be induced in one of three ways: money, honour, or a penalty for refusing.
- **Good men.** Good men do not want to be called hirelings or thieves, and they care nothing for honour. So they rule from fear of punishment: "the worst part of the punishment is that he who refuses to rule is liable to be ruled by one who is worse than himself."
- **A city of good men.** In such a city avoiding office would be fought over as office is now. Everyone would "rather receive a benefit from another than have the trouble of conferring one." This shows the true ruler is not meant to seek his own interest.

## Justice as wisdom and virtue, injustice as ignorance

Thrasymachus now claims the unjust life is more profitable, and calls injustice "discretion" and justice "sublime simplicity." Socrates argues from the arts that the just man is wise and good.

- **Reaching beyond.** The musician and the physician do not try to exceed those with the same skill, only the unskilled. Whoever has knowledge tries to do the same as his like. The ignorant try to outdo both the knowing and the ignorant.
- **Mapping to justice.** The just man tries to outdo only the unjust. The unjust tries to outdo both just and unjust. So the just man resembles the wise and good, and the unjust resembles the ignorant and evil.
- **Reluctance.** Thrasymachus gives these admissions with "extreme reluctance," sweating on a hot day, and Socrates sees "what I had never seen before, Thrasymachus blushing."

## Injustice divides; the work of the soul; justice is happiness

- **Injustice cannot unify.** A state, army, band of robbers or any gang could not act together if they injured one another. Injustice "creates divisions and hatreds," while justice "imparts harmony and friendship." Within one person, injustice makes him "not at unity with himself" and an enemy to himself, to the just and to the gods.
- **Even thieves keep some justice.** Evildoers who act together must have "some remnant of justice" in them. Utterly unjust men "would have been utterly incapable of action."
- **Function and excellence.** Everything has an end that nothing else can fulfil as well: the eye sees, the ear hears, the pruning-hook prunes. It fulfils that end by its proper excellence and fails through its defect. A blind eye cannot see.
- **The soul.** The soul's functions are "to superintend and command and deliberate," and life itself is among them. Its excellence is justice, and its defect is injustice. So the just soul lives well and the unjust lives ill.
- **Conclusion.** Living well is being blessed and happy, so "the just is happy, and the unjust miserable." Happiness is profitable, so "injustice can never be more profitable than justice."

## Socrates admits he has not found what justice is

- He closes by comparing himself to an epicure who snatches a taste of every dish without enjoying the first.
- He moved from asking what justice is to whether it is virtue and wisdom, and then to whether it is more advantageous. He never finished the first question.
- His verdict: "the result of the whole discussion has been that I know nothing at all."
- Without knowing what justice is, he says, he cannot know whether it is a virtue, nor whether the just man is happy or unhappy.
- He still says "no light matter is at stake, nothing less than the rule of human life."
