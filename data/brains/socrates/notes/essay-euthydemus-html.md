## The unexamined account of wisdom as the only good

Socrates argues that all people want to be happy, and that happiness requires having many good things. He first lists the commonly accepted goods: wealth, health, beauty, good birth, power, honours, temperance, justice, courage, wisdom and good fortune.

- Having good things is not enough. They must be used, and "there is no advantage in merely having them."
- Using them wrongly is worse than not using them at all. Non-use is neither good nor evil, while wrong use is an evil.
- A carpenter with plenty of tools and wood who never works gains nothing from them. A man with food and drink who never eats or drinks is not helped by them.
- Only knowledge directs the right use of wealth, health and beauty. In every possession and use of a thing, knowledge gives a man both good fortune and success.
- Socrates concludes that wealth, health, beauty and the rest "are not to be regarded as goods in themselves." Under ignorance they are greater evils than their opposites, because they better serve the evil principle that rules them. Under wisdom and prudence they are greater goods. "In themselves they are nothing."
- So other things are indifferent. Wisdom is the only good and ignorance the only evil.

## Why wisdom counts as good fortune

Socrates adds good fortune to the list of goods, then argues it is already covered by wisdom: "Surely wisdom is good-fortune; even a child may know that."

- He gives examples: flute-players succeed at playing, scribes at writing and reading, and wise pilots in the dangers of the sea.
- He asks Cleinias whom he would rather go to war with (a wise or a foolish general) and whom he would rather have in a dangerous illness (a wise or an ignorant physician). The answer is always the wise one.
- Reasoning: a wise man would never err, so he acts rightly and succeeds, "or his wisdom would be wisdom no longer." He who has wisdom has no need of fortune.

## Doing less with wisdom beats doing more without it

Socrates argues that possessions and activity profit a man nothing without good sense.

- If a man does fewer things, he makes fewer mistakes. Fewer mistakes bring fewer misfortunes, and fewer misfortunes make him less miserable.
- It follows that, without wisdom, a poor man does less than a rich man, a weak man less than a strong man, a coward less than a courageous man, and an indolent man less than an active one. In each case the lesser capacity does less harm.
- This is why the goods of fortune only become good "under the guidance of knowledge."

## The exhortation to philosophy

From these premises Socrates draws a practical conclusion: everyone should try to make himself as wise as he can.

- Since all men desire happiness, and happiness comes from the right use of the things of life, and knowledge gives that right use, "everybody ought by all means to try and make himself as wise as he can."
- Wisdom is a treasure worth far more than money. Asking a father, guardian, friend or suitor to impart it is "not at all dishonourable."
- No service done to anyone, whether a lover or not, is to be blamed if its aim is to get wisdom.
- He notes a question still open between himself and Cleinias: whether wisdom can be taught or comes spontaneously. Cleinias says it can be taught, and Socrates is glad to be spared "a long and tiresome investigation."
- He sets this up as the model of a sincere exhortation: persuade the young person to love wisdom and virtue, then ask which single kind of knowledge makes a person good and happy.

## The knowledge we need must both make and use

Socrates tests which art could be the knowledge of happiness, and finds that it must be one that uses what it produces.

- Knowing where gold lies hidden, or how to turn stones into gold, would be useless without knowing how to use the gold. The same goes for money-making, medicine, or a knowledge that made men immortal without knowing how to use immortality.
- The makers of lyres and flutes have an art that makes. The player has a different art that uses. The wanted knowledge is "one that uses as well as makes."
- Speech-writing fails the test. Some composers cannot use the speeches they write, and some users cannot compose them. Socrates calls this art a branch of enchantment, charming juries and assemblies as the snake-charmer charms snakes.
- The general's art fails too. Per Cleinias's argument, which Socrates endorses, hunters of every kind (generals, geometers, astronomers, calculators) only catch their prey and hand it to another art to use. A general who takes a city hands it to the statesman.

## The kingly art and the dead end

Socrates next considers the kingly, or political, art, which seems to command all other arts and so to be the knowledge he seeks.

- It fails when asked what it produces. Medicine produces health and husbandry produces food, but the kingly art cannot name its product.
- Its supposed results (wealth, freedom, tranquillity) are neither good nor evil in themselves. The only good is knowledge, so the kingly art would have to make others wise or good.
- But it cannot say in what it makes them good. It makes others good, who make others good, "without ever determining in what they are to be good." Socrates calls it "the old, old song over again."
- He confesses the inquiry ended in perplexity: "we were like children after larks, always on the point of catching the art, which was always getting away from us." He describes a labyrinth where they "came out again at the beginning."

## Eristic trickery versus real philosophy

Socrates contrasts his own questioning with the verbal tricks of Euthydemus and Dionysodorus.

- Their puzzles ("do the wise or the ignorant learn?", "you want him to be what he is not, so you want him to perish") trade on ambiguous words. Socrates explains that "to learn" has two senses and that such quibbles are "not serious."
- He says that a man who knew every such trick "would not be at all the wiser; he would only be able to play with men," like someone who pulls a stool away as a man sits down.
- He treats this as a mere preliminary game, "initiation into the correct use of terms," and keeps asking the brothers to turn to serious exhortation.
- He ironically praises their tricks, noting that they can be taught quickly and that an art anyone can pick up cheaply will be undervalued.
- He argues that their doctrines (no falsehood, no contradiction, no ignorance) are self-defeating. If error is impossible, there is nothing for them to teach.

## Judge philosophy itself, not its teachers

When Crito is put off by a critic who dismissed the sophists' discussion as "making much ado about nothing," Socrates defends philosophy.

- He tells Crito not to mind whether the teachers of philosophy are good or bad, "but think only of philosophy herself."
- In every trade (gymnastics, rhetoric, money-making, generalship) the inferior practitioners are many and worthless, and the good are few and "beyond all price." Crito would not shun those arts or forbid them to his son for that reason.
- Test philosophy honestly. If she is evil, turn all men from her. If she is what Socrates believes, "then follow her and serve her, you and your house... and be of good cheer."
- He also rebuts the orator-politician who claims to stand between philosophy and politics. Something in a mean between two goods that tend to different ends falls short of both. Such people are "third, although they would like to stand first," though he adds that anyone who manfully pursues something like wisdom deserves some affection.
