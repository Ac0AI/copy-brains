## Why cities and souls decline: Socrates' five constitutions and their matching characters

- Socrates holds that "governments vary as the dispositions of men vary" and that there are as many constitutions as there are types of soul.
- Cities are not made "of 'oak and rock'" but out of the human natures in them, which "turn the scale and draw other things after them." In his words: "the States are as the men are."
- He lists five constitutions, each with a matching soul:
  - Aristocracy, rule of the best, matching the just and good man.
  - Timocracy (Crete and Sparta), rule of honour, matching the contentious and ambitious man.
  - Oligarchy, a state that "teems with evils," matching the money-lover.
  - Democracy, matching the man of unordered freedom.
  - Tyranny, "the fourth and worst disorder of a State," matching the tyrant.
- His method is to take the city first, then the individual, then compare the most just life with the most unjust. This tests whether to pursue injustice, as Thrasymachus advises, or justice, and whether the best man is also the happiest.
- Political change begins in divisions among those who hold power: "a government which is united, however small, cannot be moved."

## From aristocracy to timocracy: discord, neglect of music, and the metals

- Socrates says everything that has a beginning has an end, so even the ideal city will dissolve.
- Decline starts when rulers, using calculation mixed with sense-perception, misjudge the timing of births and breed children who are unfortunate.
- These unworthy children take over as guardians, and they neglect the Muses: "first by under-valuing music," then gymnastic, so the young become less cultivated.
- The new rulers lose the power to test the metals of the gold, silver, brass and iron races. Iron mixes with silver and brass with gold, producing "dissimilarity and inequality and irregularity, which always and in all places are causes of hatred and war."
- Discord splits the classes:
  - The iron and brass types turn to acquiring money, land, houses, gold and silver.
  - The gold and silver types incline toward virtue and the old order.
- The compromise is private ownership of land and houses. The rulers also enslave the people they once protected as free citizens and make them subjects.
- The resulting timocracy is a mean between aristocracy and oligarchy:
  - It keeps honour for rulers, common meals, a warrior class that avoids farming, crafts and trade, and a focus on gymnastics and military training.
  - It fears putting philosophers in power, since they are no longer simple and earnest, and prefers spirited characters suited to war.
  - Its rulers secretly hoard gold in private treasuries and are "stealing their pleasures," like children running from their father, the law.
- They were "schooled not by gentle influences but by force," having neglected "the true Muse, the companion of reason and philosophy."
- One element dominates: "the spirit of contention and ambition," the spirited part of the soul.

## The timocratic man: how a quiet father produces an ambitious son

- This man is self-assertive, less cultivated yet a friend of culture, a good listener but no speaker, rough with slaves, courteous to free men, and obedient to authority.
  - He loves power and honour and claims rule on the strength of military feats, not eloquence.
  - He loves gymnastics and hunting.
- He despises riches when young but grows more avaricious with age, "having lost his best guardian": "Philosophy, tempered with music, who comes and takes her abode in a man, and is the only saviour of his virtue throughout life."
- His origin is a grave father in an ill-governed city who declines office, avoids lawsuits and waives his rights to escape trouble.
- Outside voices then pull the son the other way:
  - His mother complains that her husband has no place in government and is "only half a man."
  - Old servants urge the son to retaliate against his father's debtors and be "more of a man."
  - In the city, those who mind their own business are called simpletons, while busybodies are honoured.
- The father waters the rational principle in the boy, while the others feed the spirited and appetitive parts. The boy, not originally bad but in bad company, settles in the middle and hands "the kingdom which is within him" to contentiousness and passion.

## Oligarchy: how love of wealth destroys virtue and splits the city in two

- Oligarchy is "a government resting on a valuation of property, in which the rich have power and the poor man is deprived of it."
- Timocracy turns into it as private hoards of gold build up and people invent illegal ways to spend:
  - One man sees another grow rich and rivals him, until most citizens become money-lovers.
  - "When riches and virtue are placed together in the scales of the balance, the one always rises as the other falls."
  - What is honoured is cultivated and what is dishonoured is neglected, so virtue and the virtuous lose standing.
  - A property qualification for citizenship is then set by law, enforced by arms or intimidation.
- He lists its defects:
  - Choosing rulers by wealth is like choosing pilots by property: a poor but better pilot would be refused the helm, and the ship would be wrecked. Ruling a city is the hardest case of all.
  - The state is two cities, rich and poor, on one spot and always conspiring against each other.
  - It cannot wage war: arming the multitude makes the rich fear them more than the enemy, and not arming them leaves "few to fight as they are few to rule." Its rich also dislike paying taxes.
  - The same people are farmer, tradesman and soldier at once.
  - A man can sell everything and remain in the city as "only a poor, helpless creature," belonging to no class.
- Socrates calls the spendthrift who is neither ruler nor subject a drone, "the plague of the city as the other is of the hive."
  - Stingless drones end as paupers.
  - Stinging drones become the criminal class.
  - Where there are paupers, there are hidden thieves, cutpurses and temple robbers. He traces this to "want of education, ill-training, and an evil constitution of the State."

## The oligarchic man: saving, fear and a divided soul

- This man's origin is a timocratic father who is ruined by the state, perhaps tried by informers and stripped of office and property.
  - The son, taught by fear, throws ambition and passion off their throne.
  - Humbled by poverty, he saves and works until he has a fortune.
  - He seats the appetitive, covetous element as great king and makes reason and spirit sit on the ground beside it.
  - Reason may only calculate how small sums become larger, and spirit may only admire wealth and the wealthy.
- He satisfies only necessary appetites, subdues his other desires as unprofitable, and is the "shabby fellow" who saves from everything and whom the crowd applauds.
- His honesty is "enforced virtue." He restrains his bad desires "by necessity and fear," not by reason, because he trembles for his possessions.
  - To see his real nature, watch him where he can cheat safely, such as guardianship of an orphan, or when he spends money that is not his.
  - He is "two men, and not one," at war with himself. He is more respectable than most, but "the true virtue of a unanimous and harmonious soul will flee far away and never come near him."
- He is a poor competitor for honours because he will not spend on glory, fighting "with a small part only of his resources."
- Lack of education shows: had he been educated he would not have made "a blind god director of his chorus."

## Oligarchy into democracy: ruinous lending, the "stings", and a remedy

- Oligarchic rulers refuse to limit the extravagance of spendthrift youths because they profit by lending to them and buying up their estates.
- Socrates says "the love of wealth and the spirit of moderation cannot exist together in citizens of the same State to any considerable extent."
- Ruined men of good family stay in the city, armed with grievances and ready to sting. They are in debt or stripped of citizenship, hate those who took their property, and want revolution.
- Money-men, who pretend not to see their victims, keep lending to the unwary and multiply the original sum many times over, so drones and paupers abound.
- He proposes a milder remedy than banning extravagance: let every voluntary contract be entered "at his own risk." This would reduce scandalous money-making and push citizens to look to their characters.
- The rulers' young men live in luxury and idleness of body and mind. On campaign, a tough, sunburnt poor soldier beside a puffing rich one concludes that the rich are only rich because nobody has the courage to despoil them. They say privately, "Our warriors are not good for much."
- A weak state, like a diseased body, falls ill at the slightest touch and goes to war with itself.
- Democracy arises when the poor win, killing or banishing some opponents and sharing "an equal share of freedom and power" with the rest. Magistrates are commonly chosen by lot.

## Democracy and the democratic man: freedom as the only good, and desires without order

- Democracy is free and frank, and "a man may say and do what he likes." It holds the greatest variety of human natures, an "embroidered robe" spangled with flowers. Socrates says it is like a bazaar of constitutions where a founder could pick one.
- Nobody must rule, obey, fight or hold office unless he fancies it. This is "supremely delightful," but he adds "for the moment."
- Democracy tramples the principles of the ideal city, including that a good man must be raised from childhood among beautiful things. It promotes anyone who professes to be "the people's friend," and gives "a sort of equality to equals and unequals alike."
- The democratic man is the oligarch's son, raised miserly and then drawn into the unnecessary pleasures. Socrates distinguishes:
  - Necessary desires cannot be got rid of and benefit us. Eating simple food for health and strength is one.
  - Unnecessary desires can be removed by training from youth and do no good or harm. Delicate food and luxuries are examples.
  - The oligarch serves only the necessary ones, while the drone-like spendthrift serves the unnecessary ones.
- The change begins when the young man tastes "drones' honey" among crafty companions who supply endless pleasures.
  - Desires from outside ally with desires inside, as like helps like in a city.
  - Reverence and a father's advice may briefly restore order.
  - Fresh desires then multiply, because he does not know how to educate them, and seize his soul's citadel, which is "void of all accomplishments and fair pursuits and true words."
  - False and boastful conceits take over, and he lives among the lotus-eaters.
  - Modesty is renamed silliness and temperance unmanliness. Insolence is called breeding, anarchy liberty, waste magnificence and impudence courage.
- He gives each desire its turn. He rejects the advice that some pleasures are noble and should be honoured while others are evil and should be mastered, and says they are all alike. His life is "motley and manifold," with no law or order, and he calls it "joy and bliss and freedom."
  - He drinks and plays the flute, then drinks only water.
  - He trains in gymnastics, then idles, then plays at philosophy.
  - He dabbles in politics and copies warriors or businessmen.

## From democracy to tyranny: excess of freedom, drones and the protector who becomes a wolf

- Oligarchy fell by its insatiable desire for wealth, and democracy falls by its insatiable desire for freedom. Socrates says "the excess of liberty, whether in States or individuals, seems only to pass into excess of slavery."
- A democracy thirsty for freedom with "evil cupbearers" punishes rulers as "cursed oligarchs" unless they pour freely. It sneers at loyal citizens as slaves who "hug their chains."
- Anarchy spreads everywhere:
  - Fathers fear their sons, and sons have no reverence for parents.
  - Resident foreigners, citizens and visitors are equal.
  - Teachers flatter pupils, and the old imitate the young.
  - Slaves are as free as their buyers, and even animals "march along with all the rights and dignities of freemen."
  - Citizens "chafe impatiently at the least touch of authority" and stop caring even for written or unwritten laws.
- The doctor-lawgiver should treat idle spendthrifts as the plague of every city, "what phlegm and bile are to the body," and keep them out like a wise bee-master.
- Democracy has three classes:
  - Drones, now nearly the entire ruling power, with the keener speaking and acting while the rest buzz about the platform and allow no opposition.
  - The orderly, wealthy class, which the drones squeeze for honey.
  - The working people, the largest and most powerful class, who gather only for a little honey.
- Leaders strip the rich and give the people a share while keeping the larger part. The rich are accused of plotting against the people. Driven by deceived majorities and the drones' stings, they become oligarchs in fact.
- The people then set a champion over them. This is "the root from which a tyrant springs" and he first appears as a protector.
- He turns into a tyrant by shedding kinsmen's blood through false accusation, hinting at cancelling debts and redistributing land. Socrates compares him to the Arcadian tale of the man who tasted human entrails and became a wolf.
- He then asks for a bodyguard "lest the people's friend be lost to them," is exiled, returns, and finally becomes tyrant.

## The tyrant in power: war, purges and a ruler's dependence on bad company

- At first he smiles, greets everyone, and promises debt relief and land.
- Once foreign enemies are dealt with, he stirs up wars so the people need a leader. War also impoverishes them with taxes, which keeps them busy with daily needs, and lets him expose anyone suspected of favouring freedom.
- Growing unpopular, he must remove the brave, high-minded, wise and wealthy among his own backers. This is the reverse of a physician's purge, which removes the worse and leaves the better. Socrates calls his position "a blessed alternative": to dwell with the many bad who hate him, or not to live.
- The more hated he is, the more guards he needs. He hires drones from many lands and frees citizens' slaves to enrol them in his bodyguard. They admire him, while the good avoid him.
- Socrates quotes Euripides, "Tyrants are wise by living with the wise," and says poets who praise tyranny as godlike would not be admitted to his city. They attract mobs, hire persuasive voices and draw cities toward tyranny and democracy.
- To pay his followers he confiscates sacred treasures and attainted persons' fortunes, then lives off the people and finally off his father's estate, meaning the people. If they try to expel him, he beats them with force. The tyrant is a parricide, and the people escaping "the smoke which is the slavery of freemen" fall "into the fire which is the tyranny of slaves."
