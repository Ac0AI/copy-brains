## Fixing the budget: wealth and poverty corrupt crafts

- Adeimantus objects that the guardians are made miserable: they own nothing, are fed but not paid, and cannot travel or spend on luxuries. Socrates answers that the guardians "may very likely be the happiest of men", but that the city was founded for "the greatest happiness of the whole", not the disproportionate happiness of one class.
- He compares this to painting a statue. You do not paint the eyes purple because purple is the most beautiful color; you give each feature its due proportion so the whole is beautiful.
- If every class were given easy luxury, the farmer would stop farming and the potter would stop potting, and the city would lose its distinct classes. Pretense is harmless among cobblers, but when guardians are only "seemingly and not real guardians" they "turn the State upside down".
- He names two causes that make the arts deteriorate: wealth and poverty. A rich potter grows "indolent and careless" and becomes a worse potter. A poor one cannot afford tools and teaches his sons and apprentices worse.
- Wealth is "the parent of luxury and indolence" and poverty "of meanness and viciousness, and both of discontent". The guardians must watch for both, because they creep into a city unobserved.

## Why a poor city can still win wars and how large it should be

- Asked how a city without money can fight a rich enemy, Socrates says trained warriors fighting an army of rich men have the advantage. He compares them to a single expert boxer who can retreat, turn and strike, and overturn more than one stout opponent.
- He suggests sending an embassy to one of two rival cities: "Silver and gold we neither have nor are permitted to have, but you may; do you therefore come and help us in war, and take the spoils of the other city." No one would choose to fight "lean wiry dogs" rather than side with them against "fat and tender sheep".
- Other cities are not single states. Any city, however small, is two cities, the poor and the rich, at war with each other. Treat them as many and you will "always have a great many friends and not many enemies".
- His limit on size: let the city grow only "so far as is consistent with unity". It should be "neither large nor small, but one and self-sufficing". He says such a city could be the greatest in deed and truth with no more than a thousand defenders.

## Education as the one great thing, and the danger of musical innovation

- Socrates says the rules he sets are "trifles" if care is taken of "the one great thing", which he would call sufficient rather than great: education and nurture. Well-educated, sensible citizens will work out questions like marriage and child-rearing for themselves, following the proverb that friends have all things in common.
- A well-started city "moves with accumulating force like a wheel". Good nurture implants good constitutions, which improve further when rooted in good education.
- Rulers must preserve music and gymnastic in their original form and allow no innovation. He cites Damon: "when modes of music change, the fundamental laws of the State always change with them."
- Adeimantus describes how lawlessness enters through play. It looks harmless, then seeps into manners and customs, then contracts, then laws and constitutions, ending in "an overthrow of all rights, private as well as public".
- Children who gain habits of order through well-regulated play carry them into all their actions. Socrates holds that the direction in which education starts a man "will determine his future life", because like attracts like.

## Why detailed legislation is pointless: the sick man and the political quack

- Educated good men will find out for themselves what rules they need for the market, contracts, juries, harbour dues and manners. Written rules about dress, hair and deference to elders are unlikely to last.
- Socrates compares cities that endlessly mend their laws to invalids with no self-restraint. They are "always doctoring and increasing and complicating their disorders", trying every nostrum and treating as an enemy the man who tells them the truth: unless they give up "eating and drinking and wenching and idling", no drug, cautery, spell or amulet will help.
- He mocks ill-ordered cities that forbid changing the constitution on pain of death, yet honor whoever flatters the citizens and anticipates their humors as "a great and good statesman".
- He is kinder to politicians deluded by popular applause: when a man cannot measure and many others who cannot measure say he is four cubits high, he will believe them.
- Reformers who try to end fraud in contracts by legislation are "cutting off the heads of a hydra". The true legislator does not bother with such enactments. They are useless in a bad city and easy to devise in a good one.
- The greatest matters, temples, sacrifices, the service of gods and heroes, and burial rites, he leaves to Apollo at Delphi, since "these are matters of which we are ignorant ourselves".

## Method for finding justice: the four virtues, wisdom and courage

- Socrates proposes to find justice by assuming the rightly ordered city is perfect, so it is wise, valiant, temperate and just. Find three of the virtues and justice is the residue.
- **Wisdom** is good counsel, a kind of knowledge. Knowing carpentry, bronze-working or farming does not make a city wise. The knowledge that does is the knowledge of the whole: how the city deals with itself and other cities. It belongs to the perfect guardians, the smallest class. The whole city is wise through its smallest part.
- **Courage** resides in the fighting class. It is "a kind of salvation", the preservation under all circumstances of the law-implanted opinion about what is and is not to be feared, in pleasure or pain, desire or fear.
- His dyeing illustration: dyers prepare the white wool carefully so the sea-purple becomes a fast color that no lye can wash out. Education in music and gymnastic prepares soldiers to take the dye of the law. Pleasure is a "mightier agent far in washing the soul than any soda or lye", and sorrow, fear and desire are the strongest solvents.
- He excludes the uninstructed courage of a wild beast or a slave. The courage he means is the courage "of a citizen".

## Temperance as harmony, and justice as doing one's own business

- **Temperance** is the ordering and control of certain pleasures and desires. "Master of himself" is a paradoxical phrase, since the master is also the servant. It means the better principle in the soul rules the worse; when bad education leaves the better principle overwhelmed, the man is "slave of self".
- Complex, manifold desires are found mostly in children, women, servants and the lowest, most numerous class. Simple, moderate desires guided by reason belong to the few, best born and best educated. The temperate city lets the few hold the many down.
- Unlike wisdom and courage, which sit in one part, temperance runs through the whole like a scale. It is agreement between the naturally superior and inferior about who should rule, in cities and individuals alike.
- With three virtues found, Socrates hunts justice like a huntsman, then realizes it was "tumbling out at our feet" from the start, like people looking for what they hold in their hands. It is the founding principle: each person does the one thing their nature suits best, "doing one's own business, and not being a busybody".
- Justice is the condition and preserver of the other three virtues. Courts also reflect it: a man may "neither take what is another's, nor be deprived of what is his own".
- A carpenter doing a cobbler's work does little harm. The ruin comes when a trader, "lifted up by wealth or strength or the number of his followers", forces his way into the warrior class, or a warrior into the guardians. Meddling between the three classes is "the greatest harm to the State" and is injustice; each class doing its own work is justice.

## The three parts of the soul

- Socrates tests whether the individual's justice matches the city's. He admits the method is not fully adequate and that "the true method is another and a longer one".
- His principle: the same thing cannot act or be acted upon in the same part, in relation to the same thing, at the same time, in contrary ways. Contradiction shows the things are different. A spinning top is at rest at its axis and in motion at its circumference.
- A thirsty man may refuse to drink. So something bids him drink and something else forbids him. The forbidding principle is **reason**; the bidding one is **appetite**, the irrational part that loves, hungers and thirsts. They must be two.
- **Spirit** (anger) is a third. The story of Leontius, who saw corpses by the Piraeus wall, wanted to look, and was disgusted. He forced his eyes open and said "Look, ye wretches, take your fill of the fair sight", angry at himself. Spirit fights desire and sides with reason.
- A noble man who thinks he has wronged another does not resent the punishment, but when he is the one wronged he "boils and chafes", on the side of justice, until reason, the shepherd, calls his dog off.
- Spirit is distinct from reason too: children are full of spirit at birth, some never reach reason, and animals have spirit. Socrates cites Homer's line of Odysseus striking his breast and rebuking his heart.

## Justice and injustice as health and disease of the soul

- The just individual is wise through the ruling reason, which knows what is good for each part and the whole. He is courageous when spirit holds to reason's commands about what to fear, in pleasure and pain. He is temperate when all three parts agree that reason should rule.
- Music and gymnastic bring reason and spirit into accord: noble words and lessons strengthen reason, harmony and rhythm soothe the wildness of spirit. Together they guard the appetitive part, "the largest part of the soul and by nature most insatiable of gain", lest it enslave the others and overturn the whole life.
- Justice concerns "not the outward man, but the inward, which is the true self". The just man does not let his parts interfere with each other. He "sets in order his own inner life, and is his own master and his own law, and at peace with himself", binding the three parts like the high, low and middle notes of a scale until he is "no longer many, but has become one".
- Just actions preserve this harmony and are called just and good; the knowledge presiding over them is wisdom. Actions that impair it are unjust, and the opinion presiding over them is ignorance.
- Injustice is "a rebellious subject against a true prince": strife, meddling and the rising of a part of the soul against the whole. It is the same as intemperance, cowardice, ignorance "and every form of vice".
- Justice and injustice are to the soul what health and disease are to the body. Virtue is "the health and beauty and well-being of the soul, and vice the disease and weakness and deformity of the same". Good practices lead to virtue and evil practices to vice.
- Glaucon says it would be ridiculous to argue that life is worth living with all wealth and power if the soul itself is corrupted, answering the old question of whether injustice pays if unpunished. Socrates agrees but proposes to look at the forms of vice. Virtue is one while vice has innumerable forms, and there are five forms of state and five of soul. The first is what they have described, called monarchy or aristocracy, one form either way if the rulers are trained as supposed.
