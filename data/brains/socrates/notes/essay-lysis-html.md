## Friendship cannot be bought with praise: how a lover should speak to his beloved

- Socrates notes that Hippothales, who is in love with the boy Lysis, only sings of Lysis's wealth, ancestors, horses and Olympic-style victories. He calls this "twaddle" a child could produce.
- He argues such songs are really hymns in honour of the lover himself. If the lover wins, he glories in them. If he loses the beloved, "the more you have praised him, the more ridiculous you will look".
- The wise lover "does not praise his beloved until he has won him, because he is afraid of accidents."
- Praise also fills the beloved with pride and vainglory, which makes him harder to win. Socrates compares this to a hunter who frightens off his prey, which is the mark of a bad hunter.
- His counter-method is to talk with the youth and humble him, not puff him up. He offers to demonstrate this by conversing with Lysis, and he almost tells Hippothales so directly.

## Freedom and trust follow knowledge, not age

- Socrates asks Lysis whether his parents, who love him and want him happy, let him do what he likes. Lysis admits they do not. A hired charioteer drives the family chariot, a slave muleteer holds the whip, and a slave tutor governs Lysis himself.
- Lysis blames his age. Socrates doubts this. Lysis is already trusted to read, write and play the lyre as he pleases. The difference is not "a deficiency of years, but a deficiency of knowledge".
- He generalises: "whenever your father thinks that you are wiser than he is, he will instantly commit himself and his possessions to you." The same holds for neighbours and the Athenian people.
- His example is the Great King, who would let two better cooks season the soup, or two better physicians treat his own son's eyes, before his son.
- Where we have knowledge, everyone trusts us and we are "free, and masters of others". Where we lack it, even parents and dearest friends will hinder us, and those things are not truly ours because we do not benefit from them.

## Usefulness is the root of being loved, and conceit has no ground without knowledge

- Socrates concludes that nobody loves anybody "in so far as they are useless to them." If Lysis is wise, "all men will be your friends and kindred, for you will be useful and good". If he is not, not even his father or mother will be his friend.
- A person who still needs a teacher has not attained wisdom, so he has nothing to be conceited about. Lysis agrees.
- This is Socrates's practice of humbling the beloved, in contrast to Hippothales's flattery.

## A passion for friends, and the first failed definitions

- Socrates says that from childhood his passion has been for friends. He would rather have "a good friend than the best cock or quail in the world", or the best horse or dog, or "all the gold of Darius". He admits he does not know how a friend is acquired.
- **Either party in a one-sided love?** If only one loves, are both friends? Menexenus first says either may be the friend. Socrates presses: if the lover is not loved back, or is hated, who is the friend? Without return love there seems to be no friendship, which contradicts the first answer.
- **The beloved as the friend?** Using the poet's line about dear children, horses and dogs, and children who are dearest to parents even while hating them for punishing, Socrates tries making the beloved the friend. That makes many men "friends of their enemies, and enemies of their friends", which he calls absurd, even impossible.
- With the lover not a friend, the beloved not a friend, and both together not friends, Menexenus cannot find any friends left.

## Like loves like? Opposites attract? Both fail

- **Like to like.** Poets and natural philosophers say god draws like to like. Socrates argues bad men grow to hate each other the more they deal with each other, since an injurer and the injured cannot be friends. Bad men are also restless and at odds with themselves, so they cannot be at unity with anything. So only the good could be friends of the good.
- He then undermines this. Like can do nothing for like that it cannot do for itself. The good, in so far as good, is self-sufficient, wants nothing, desires nothing and therefore loves nothing. "How can such persons ever be induced to value one another?"
- **Opposites.** He recalls a claim attributed to Hesiod that likes are full of envy and strife ("Potter quarrels with potter, hard with bard, beggar with beggar"). On this view the poor need the rich, the sick the physician, the ignorant the knower, and opposites feed opposites (dry and moist, hot and cold, empty and full).
- He rejects this too: it would make the just friends of the unjust, the temperate of the intemperate, the good of the bad, and love the friend of hate. "Neither like and like nor unlike and unlike are friends."

## The neither-good-nor-evil loves the good because of evil

- Socrates dizzily ventures a guess: "the beautiful is the friend", and the beautiful is the good. He assumes three kinds: good, bad, and what is neither.
- Since good cannot befriend good, bad or like, what is neither good nor evil must be the friend of the good.
- His illustration is medicine. A healthy body does not court the physician. A sick body does. The body is neither good nor evil in itself, but disease (evil) drives it to befriend medicine (a good).
- This only works while the thing is not yet corrupted: a presence of evil that has not yet made it evil. His analogy is white lead on hair, which makes it only appear white, unlike the real whiteness of old age.
- This explains who loves wisdom. Gods and the already wise do not, and the ignorant who are hardened and "fancy that they know what they do not know" do not either. Only those who are ignorant but aware of it, neither good nor bad, are lovers of wisdom.
- His first formula: friendship is "the love which by reason of the presence of evil the neither good nor evil has of the good".

## Doubting his own conclusion: friends for the sake of a first friend

- Socrates immediately distrusts his result: "arguments, like men, are often pretenders." He says they may have been "grasping at a shadow only".
- He notes that the sick man loves the physician because of disease and for the sake of health. If health is dear, it is dear for the sake of something further, and so on. The chain of "dear for the sake of" must end in a first principle of friendship, "which is not capable of being referred to any other". Everything else called dear is only an illusion of friendship.
- His example is a father whose son has drunk hemlock. The father values the wine that might save him, and the cup holding it, only for the son's sake. Likewise we say we value gold and silver, but we really value the further end for which we acquire them.
- A deeper difficulty follows. If the good is loved only as a remedy for evil, then with evil gone the good would be useless and love would vanish. Yet bodily desires like hunger and thirst would remain and would be neither good nor evil, so some friendship would still exist. So evil cannot be the cause of friendship.

## Desire, the congenial, and the unresolved ending

- Socrates's next proposal is that desire causes friendship. What desires is dear to what it desires, and it desires what it lacks. So love and friendship belong to "the natural or congenial".
- No one would love or desire another unless that person were somehow congenial to him, "either in his soul, or in his character, or in his manners, or in his form". So a true lover must be loved in return. Lysis gives only a faint assent.
- He cannot separate the congenial from the like. If they are the same, the old objection returns that like is useless to like. If the congenial means the good, only the good befriends the good, which was already refuted. If the congenial includes evil to evil, then the bad become friends of the bad.
- He sums up that if the beloved, lover, like, unlike, good and congenial are all ruled out, "I know not what remains to be said."
- The dialogue ends without a definition. The boys' tutors drag them home. Socrates says: "how ridiculous that you two boys, and I, an old boy... should imagine ourselves to be friends... and as yet we have not been able to discover what is a friend!"
