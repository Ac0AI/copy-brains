## Temperance cannot be reduced to quietness

- Socrates asks Charmides to say what temperance is, reasoning that if it is present in him it must give him some notion of its nature.
- Charmides first answers that temperance is "quietness", doing things orderly and quietly.
- Socrates first gets agreement that temperance is noble and good. He then shows that in writing, reading, playing the lyre, wrestling, boxing, running, learning, teaching and remembering, the quick and agile is better than the slow and quiet.
- The same goes for the soul: shrewdness is "a quickness or cleverness of the soul". He who deliberates and discovers most easily and quickly is the one worthy of praise.
- Conclusion: quick actions are at least as good as quiet ones, so temperance cannot be quietness, and the quiet life is not more temperate than the unquiet.

## Temperance as modesty fails because modesty is not always good

- Charmides next defines temperance as modesty, being ashamed.
- Socrates secures two admissions: temperance is noble and good, and whatever is good makes men good.
- He quotes Homer, "Modesty is not good for a needy man", to show that modesty is sometimes bad.
- Temperance makes men only good and is always good, while modesty is "as much an evil as a good". So the two cannot be the same.
- The method is to test a proposed definition against things already agreed, and drop it when it contradicts them.

## "Doing one's own business" is a riddle, and truth matters more than the speaker

- Charmides offers a third definition he heard from someone else: temperance is "doing our own business".
- Socrates says it does not matter who said it: "the point is not who said the words, but whether they are true or not."
- He tests it literally. A scribe, a physician, a builder or a weaver does things for others as well as for himself. A state that made everyone weave his own coat and make his own shoes would not be well ordered. Yet a temperate state is a well-ordered state.
- He concludes that the author must have meant something hidden, since he could not have been a fool.
- Critias then rescues the definition by splitting "making" from "doing" (citing Hesiod) and equating temperance with doing good actions.
- Socrates answers by asking whether a physician who cures well but does not know whether his treatment will help is acting temperately. He may do good and not know it. Critias will not accept a man being temperate or wise without knowing himself, and so he drops the definition.

## Why Socrates argues: examining himself, not winning

- Critias complains that Socrates is only trying to refute him. Socrates replies that his motive is the same as when he examines himself: "a fear of my unconsciously fancying that I knew something of which I was ignorant."
- He says he pursues the argument "chiefly for my own sake, and perhaps in some degree also for the sake of my other friends."
- His reason: "the discovery of things as they truly are, a good common to all mankind."
- He tells Critias to ignore "whether Critias or Socrates is the person refuted" and attend only to the argument.
- He disclaims knowledge: "I enquire with you into the truth of that which is advanced from time to time, just because I do not know," and says he will decide whether he agrees only after reflecting.
- He counts it a duty to examine a thought that comes to mind: "if a man has any feeling of what is due to himself, he cannot let the thought ... pass away unheeded and unexamined."

## Temperance as self-knowledge, and the "science of science"

- Critias proposes that temperance is "Know thyself". He reads the Delphic inscription as the god's greeting to worshippers, equivalent to "Be temperate!".
- Critias then says temperance is a science of itself and of other sciences, which also knows the absence of science. The wise man would know what he knows and does not know, and could test what others know or only think they know.
- Socrates notes that every other science has a subject distinct from itself and a product, such as health from medicine and houses from building. He asks what the subject or product of this science would be.
- He argues that a self-directed science with no object is monstrous. There is no vision that sees no colour but only itself, no hearing that hears no sound, and no desire, wish, love, fear or opinion that is only about itself.
- For relative things, he reasons, a thing related to itself takes on the nature of its object. Something greater than itself would also be less than itself, and sight that sees itself would have to see a colour.
- He says he is not sure such a science exists, and that someone wiser is needed to decide whether anything can be related to itself. Even if it exists, he will not call it wisdom until he sees whether it does any good.

## Even granted, knowing that you know is not knowing what you know

- Granting for argument's sake that the science of science exists, Socrates asks how it would show a man what he knows.
- It would only tell him that something is or is not knowledge. It would not teach health, building or justice. Those come from medicine, building and politics.
- So the wise man would know that he has knowledge, not what the knowledge is about.
- Such a man could not tell a true physician from a pretender. To test a doctor he would have to ask about health and disease, and for that he would have to be a physician himself. He could at most recognize someone with some knowledge, as a fellow expert.
- Socrates describes the real benefit that was hoped for. If the wise man could discern what he and others know, we would never err. We would hand each task to the one who knows it, and houses and states would be well ordered, and men "would have done well, and would have been happy."
- He concludes that no such science has been found, and that the knowledge of knowledge brings only the minor gain of learning more easily.

## Knowledge of good and evil is what makes life go well

- In his "dream", Socrates imagines wisdom ruling absolutely: no false pilot, physician, general or prophet could deceive us, and every craft would be done well.
- He still doubts that living by knowledge makes us act well and be happy.
- Pressed by Critias on which knowledge makes a man happy, Socrates eliminates draughts, computation, health, shoemaking and metalwork. What remains is "the knowledge with which he discerns good and evil."
- His conclusion: without the science of good and evil, medicine would still give health, shoemaking shoes, piloting would save lives at sea and generalship would save them in war. Yet "none of these things will be well or beneficially done, if the science of the good be wanting."
- That science is "a science of human advantage" and "not a science of other sciences, or of ignorance, but of good and evil."
- Since wisdom as defined produces neither health nor advantage, it seems useless. Socrates holds that this must reflect a failure in the inquiry, not in wisdom: "wisdom or temperance I believe to be really a great good."
- The dialogue ends without a definition. Socrates calls himself "a bad enquirer" and tells Charmides to examine himself to see whether he has the gift and can do without the charm. He adds that "the more wise and temperate you are, the happier you will be."

## The charm: curing the soul before the body

- Socrates tells Charmides, who complains of headaches, that doctors say the eyes cannot be cured without the head, nor the head without the whole body. He reports that a Thracian physician added that the body cannot be cured without the soul.
- On this view, "all good and evil, whether in the body or in human nature, originates ... in the soul, and overflows from thence." Hence "the part can never be well unless the whole is well."
- The cure for the soul is "fair words", and by these "temperance is implanted in the soul". Where temperance is, health follows for the whole body.
- He swears not to cure the head until the person has first "given you his soul to be cured by the charm."
- He asks Charmides to examine whether temperance is in him, instead of accepting praise from others. He will not press him: "I will not press you if you would rather not."
- Charmides ends by saying he does not know if he has temperance, and agrees to be "charmed" by Socrates daily until Socrates says he has had enough.
