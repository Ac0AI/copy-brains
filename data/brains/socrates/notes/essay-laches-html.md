## Courage cannot be defined by listing what brave people do

- Laches first says a courageous man "remains at his post and fights against the enemy." Socrates says this answers a different question from the one he asked.
- He counters with Scythian cavalry who fight while flying, and with the Spartans at Plataea, who fled the light Persian shields, then turned and won once the Persian ranks broke.
- He widens the target beyond hoplites to courage "in perils by sea," in disease, in poverty, in politics, and against desires and pleasures as well as against pain and fear.
- His method is to ask for the one quality common to all the cases, not for examples of it.

## Defining by the common quality: the "quickness" analogy

- To show what he wants, Socrates asks what quickness is. It shows up in running, playing the lyre, speaking and learning.
- His answer is "the quality which accomplishes much in a little time."
- He asks Laches to give courage the same kind of answer, one that covers every use of the word, in pleasure and in pain.

## Testing "courage is endurance of the soul"

- Laches proposes that courage is "a sort of endurance of the soul."
- Socrates objects that not all endurance is good. Courage is noble, foolish endurance is "evil and hurtful," and nothing evil and hurtful is noble. So only wise endurance can count as courage.
- He then asks "wise in what?" Wise spending of money, or a physician refusing a patient's request for food and drink, is not courage.
- He sets two soldiers side by side. One endures with good odds, support and position. The other endures with none of those advantages. Laches says the second is braver.
- Socrates follows this through cavalry, slingers, archers and divers. Laches ends up saying that people who endure without skill are braver than people who endure with it.
- This contradicts the earlier agreement that foolish endurance is base. Laches admits "we are not right."
- Socrates notes that their deeds look courageous but their words about courage do not. They are "not attuned to the Dorian mode, which is a harmony of words and deeds."
- His remedy is to persevere in the inquiry itself: "we too must endure and persevere in the enquiry." He compares this to a good sportsman who follows the track and does not give up.

## Testing "courage is knowledge of what is fearful and hopeful"

- Nicias recalls a saying he heard from Socrates: "Every man is good in that in which he is wise, and bad in that in which he is unwise." He concludes that courage is a kind of wisdom.
- Nicias defines courage as "the knowledge of that which inspires fear or confidence in war, or in anything."
- Laches objects that physicians and farmers know the dangers of their own crafts and are not courageous for it. Nicias replies that a physician knows health and disease but not whether living is better than dying for a given person.
- Socrates raises the problem that animals would then lack courage. A lion, stag, bull or monkey would be no braver than a pig unless it had rare wisdom.
- Nicias bites the bullet. Fearlessness without forethought is "rashness," found in "many men, many women, many children, many animals." Courage is thoughtful and rare, and courageous actions are wise actions.

## Why the knowledge definition collapses

- Socrates says the terrible things are "the evils which are future," and the hopeful are the good or not-evil things which are future.
- He holds that one science covers past, present and future of the same subject. Medicine looks at health at all times, farming at the earth's produce at all times, and the general knows the future of war better than the soothsayer, which is why law puts the soothsayer under the general.
- So the knowledge of future goods and evils is only one-third of a larger knowledge of good and evil at every time.
- A person who knew all good and evil, and how they are, were and will be produced, would be perfect, with justice, temperance and holiness as well. Courage would then be "all virtue," not a part of it.
- That contradicts the starting premise that courage is one part of virtue alongside justice and temperance. The dialogue ends with "we have not discovered what courage is."

## Settle questions by knowledge, not by vote

- When Lysimachus wants a majority ruling between Nicias and Laches, Socrates asks whether he would take the majority's advice on his son's gymnastic training. Melesias says no; he would follow the one trained under a skilful master.
- Socrates's reason is that "a good decision is based on knowledge and not on numbers."
- So the first question is whether anyone present has knowledge of the matter under discussion.
- The stakes are high. Children are "your riches," and how they turn out decides the whole order of a father's house.

## Ask about the end before the means

- Socrates distinguishes the means from the end being served. Someone applying medicine to the eyes is thinking about the eyes. Someone bridling a horse is thinking about the horse.
- The question about fighting in armour is therefore really about the soul of the youth, and a good adviser must be skilled in treating that.
- Advisers should show proof, either teachers who were themselves proven or works of their own. He asks whom they have improved, whether Athenians or strangers, bond or free.
- Otherwise they risk experimenting on friends' children, and he quotes the proverb about "break the large vessel in learning to make pots."
- Socrates also says that to advise how a thing is attained, one must first know what it is. We cannot advise on giving sight without knowing what sight is, or on imparting virtue without knowing the nature of virtue.
- He proposes starting with one part of virtue, courage, since the whole may be more than they can manage.

## Admitting ignorance and learning together

- Socrates says he has never had a teacher of the art of virtue, though he wished for one from youth, and he was too poor to pay the Sophists.
- He admits he has not discovered it himself either, and says he cannot decide which of the two speaks truly.
- Nicias warns that anyone who talks with Socrates "is liable to be drawn into an argument" until he has to give an account of his present and past life. Nicias welcomes this, quoting Solon: a man should wish to be learning as long as he lives, and old age does not itself bring wisdom.
- Laches says he admires the man whose life is "a harmony of words and deeds" and dislikes the man whose actions contradict his words. He adds that Solon's learning should be "of the good only."
- Socrates declines to take the boys as pupils, since all three men are equally perplexed and none should be preferred.
- His advice is to seek out the best teacher available, "regardless of expense," first for themselves and then for the youth. If others laugh at them for going to school at their age, he cites Homer: "Modesty is not good for a needy man."
- He concludes that "the education of the youths" should become "our own education."
