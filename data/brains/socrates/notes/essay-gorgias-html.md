## Rhetoric is flattery, not an art

- Socrates asks Gorgias to name his art the way a cobbler names his: by its nature, not its praise. He says Polus "has attended more to the art which is called rhetoric than to dialectic" because Polus praised the art without defining it.
- He gets Gorgias to admit that rhetoric works by persuasion about the just and unjust. It produces belief without knowledge, since "no one can be supposed to instruct such a vast multitude about such high matters in a short time".
- The rhetorician only has to persuade the ignorant that he knows more than those who know. Socrates says that when the rhetorician beats the physician before a crowd, "the ignorant is more persuasive with the ignorant than he who has knowledge".
- Socrates denies that rhetoric is an art. It is "a sort of experience" in producing delight and gratification, and he calls it "the habit of a bold and ready wit, which knows how to manage mankind". He sums it up as "flattery".
- An art can "explain or give a reason of the nature of its own applications". Cookery and rhetoric cannot, and Socrates will not "call any irrational thing an art".

## The four arts and their four counterfeits

- Socrates sets out two arts for the body and two for the soul, each paired with a flattering imitation:
  - Body: gymnastic (to keep it fit) and medicine (to cure it). Their imitations are tiring (the art of attiring and cosmetics) and cookery.
  - Soul: legislation and justice. Their imitations are sophistry and rhetoric.
- He gives the proportions "after the manner of the geometricians": tiring is to gymnastic as cookery is to medicine; sophistry is to legislation as rhetoric is to justice.
- Flattery "aims at pleasure without any thought of the best". It makes pleasure "the bait of the unwary".
- His test case: if a physician and a cook competed before children, "the physician would be starved to death".
- Rhetoric is "the ghost or counterfeit of a part of politics". He calls it ignoble because it seeks the pleasing, not the good.

## Why the rhetorician must be just, and the contradiction in Gorgias

- Socrates shows that whoever has learned a thing is what the knowledge makes him: the carpenter, the musician, the physician. So whoever has learned what is just is just.
- The just man always wants to do what is just and will never consent to injustice. A rhetorician who knows justice therefore could not misuse his art.
- This contradicts Gorgias's earlier claim that a teacher should not be blamed if a pupil misuses rhetoric, as with a trainer and a boxer who strikes his father.
- Socrates notes the inconsistency and expects "a great deal of discussion, before we get at the truth of all this".

## Willing the good: why tyrants have no real power

- Polus says rhetoricians are like tyrants who "kill and despoil and exile any one whom they please". Socrates replies that they have "the least possible power in states".
- Men do not will what they do. They will the end for the sake of which they do it. Examples:
  - Taking painful medicine: they will health.
  - Sailing and trading: they will wealth.
  - Killing or exiling: they will their own good.
- So someone who does what only seems best to him, but is actually bad for him, does not do what he wills. A fool doing what he thinks best has no power, because power is a good only if it benefits the possessor.
- He mocks the idea with a dagger in the Agora: carrying a dagger and being able to kill anyone is not "great power". Such acts bring punishment, and punishment is an evil.
- Killing, exile and confiscation are good "when they are just, and evil when they are unjust".
- He tells Polus not to envy the unjust ruler. Such men are "wretches who are not to be envied, but only to pity".

## Truth over numbers: the single witness

- Polus points to Archelaus of Macedonia, who murdered his relatives to take the throne. Polus says Archelaus is wicked and yet happy.
- Socrates says he cannot judge whether a man is happy without knowing "how he stands in the matter of education and justice". He claims that "the men and women who are gentle and good are also happy... and the unjust and evil are miserable".
- He rejects courtroom-style refutation by many reputable witnesses: "a man may often be sworn down by a multitude of false witnesses who have a great air of respectability".
- His method needs only the agreement of the person he is arguing with: "I shall produce one witness only of the truth of my words, and he is the person with whom I am arguing."
- He names Nicias, the family of Pericles and others who would side with Polus. He still stands alone, because "who can refute the truth?"
- He says he is "not a public man" and cannot take votes. He will not count heads.
- Whether a man is happy or miserable is, for him, the greatest question, because "what ignorance more disgraceful than this?"

## Doing wrong is worse than suffering it

- Socrates says it is worse to do injustice than to suffer it. "I should not like either, but if I must choose... I would rather suffer than do." He thinks everyone shares this view, including Polus.
- The argument:
  - Polus admits that doing wrong is more disgraceful than suffering it, though he thinks suffering is the greater evil.
  - Beautiful things are beautiful by reason of pleasure, utility or both. Disgraceful things are disgraceful by pain, evil or both.
  - Doing injustice does not cause more pain than suffering it, so its extra disgrace must lie in greater evil.
  - So doing injustice is the greater evil. No one would choose a greater evil over a lesser one.
- Gorgias's pupil is led to agree that "to do injustice is the greater evil of the two".

## Punishment as medicine, and what rhetoric is for

- Socrates asks Polus whether being punished is doing or suffering. Polus says suffering. The agent is the punisher, who acts justly, and the one punished suffers justly. What is just is honourable and so good, so the punished man is benefited: his soul is improved.
- There are three parallel evils. Poverty belongs to the estate, disease to the body and injustice to the soul. The soul's evils (injustice, ignorance, cowardice, intemperance) are the most disgraceful and the most hurtful.
- Three arts remove them: money-making cures poverty, medicine cures disease and justice (the judge) cures vice. Justice is the best of the three.
- Being healed is useful but not pleasant. The man who was never ill is happier than the man cured. Order of happiness:
  - First: never having had vice in the soul.
  - Second: being delivered from vice through admonition, rebuke and punishment.
  - Worst: the unjust man who escapes punishment.
- Escaping punishment is like a child so afraid of the knife or cautery that he lets a disease become "the incurable cancer of the soul". Such people gather "money and friends" and "cultivate to the utmost their powers of persuasion" to evade justice.
- Consequences for how to live:
  - Everyone should guard against doing wrong.
  - If he or someone he cares about does wrong, he should go to the judge "as he would to the physician".
  - Rhetoric is useful only to accuse oneself and one's family and friends, so that "the wrong-doer may suffer and be made whole". He should not shrink from "stripes, bonds, fines, exile or death".
  - Rhetoric is useless for excusing injustice. It could serve only someone who wanted an enemy to escape punishment, which Socrates treats as a reductio.
