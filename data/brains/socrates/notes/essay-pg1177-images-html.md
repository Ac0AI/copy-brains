## Generals must know the whole job, not only tactics

- Socrates argues that anyone seeking the office of Strategos who neglects to learn its duties deserves a fine, more than a charlatan who takes a contract to carve a statue without having learnt sculpture. The reason is that "the whole fortunes of the state are entrusted to the general during a war."
- He holds that the man without the science is no general or doctor "even if the whole world appointed him."
- Tactics is "only an infinitesimal part of generalship." A general must also supply the army and its provisions, be quick in devices and full of resource, and miss nothing. He must combine clemency and fierceness, be a watchman and a robber, be as open-handed as a spendthrift and as tight as a miser, and be impregnable in defence and daring in attack.
- Tactics is still worth having. He compares it to a heap of stones, bricks, wood and tiles, which is useless until it is arranged by principle into a dwelling.
- Arranging troops presupposes knowing good from bad. Piling coins "best at top and bottom" is no help unless you can first tell real from counterfeit.
- Arrangements must also suit the occasion. The same order of march or battle is out of place in countless circumstances.
- If a professor takes the pupil's money and sends him away without this, Socrates says he ought to blush.

## The leader's purpose is the happiness of those he leads

- Socrates reads Homer's "shepherd of the peoples" to mean that a general must see his soldiers safe, supplied, and able to reach the aim of their soldiering. That aim is to beat the enemy and add to their own good fortune and happiness.
- "Warrior bold" and "good king" mean more than personal courage. The leader must inspire the whole host with prowess and be the source of happiness to those he rules.
- A king is not chosen "to take heed to himself, albeit nobly, but that those who chose him may attain to happiness through him."
- Men go soldiering to improve their lives and choose generals as guides to that goal. So he who takes the office must procure for them what they seek.
- Stripped of everything superficial, he says, the function of every leader is to make happy those he leads.

## A cavalry commander must improve horses and men, and win obedience through knowledge

- Socrates asks a new hipparch what he is after. Riding at the head of the knights is no goal, and notoriety is "a fatal distinction." The real aim is to hand the cavalry to the state in better condition and be the cause of some good to Athens.
- He presses practical duties:
  - Check that horses are sound in feet and legs, well fed, well broken and steady in the ranks.
  - Make the men expert at mounting.
  - Drill on ground resembling a real battlefield, not the sandy practice ground.
  - Train them to shoot on horseback.
  - Whet their courage and kindle their rage to meet the enemy.
- Obedience is the crux: without it, "not one particle of good will you get, for all your horses and troopers so brave and so stout."
- People follow those they regard as adepts, such as the cleverest physician, the most skilful pilot or the best farmer. A commander must therefore prove himself the better man and show his troopers that obeying him brings greater glory and surer safety.
- Teaching that is far easier than teaching that bad things are better than good.
- The commander must be able to speak and argue. He says the noblest things we learn, and our knowledge of life itself, come through speech and reason, and the best teachers are those with the freest command of thought and language.
- Athens beats the world in choruses not through voice, stature or strength but through "ambition and that love of honour." Appeal to that, and the cavalry can also lead the world in arms, order and eager encounter.

## Management of a house and of an army are the same art

- Socrates meets Nicomachides, bitter that Athens elected Antisthenes, a wealth-gatherer with no military record, over him. Socrates notes in Antisthenes' favour that he can supply troops, is pertinacious about winning, and chose the best performers whenever he was choragos.
- His principle: "given a man knows what he needs to provide, and has the skill to do so, no matter what the department of things may be--house or city or army--you will find him a good chief and director of the same."
- He lists what a steward and a general share:
  - securing ready obedience;
  - assigning the right tasks to the best qualified;
  - punishing the bad and rewarding the good;
  - winning the goodwill of subordinates;
  - winning allies and supporters;
  - guarding what is in their charge;
  - working painstakingly;
  - facing enemies and getting the better of them.
- On the battlefield the good economist knows "nothing is so advantageous or so lucrative as victory in battle" and nothing so disastrous and expensive as defeat. He seeks out everything conducive to victory, guards against all that leads to defeat, and is cautious about giving battle until preparation is complete, then fights energetically.
- "The difference between the devotion requisite to private affairs and to affairs of state is merely one of quantity." Both deal with human instruments, and success in either goes to those who know how to deal with people.

## Reviving Athens: fear breeds order, inheritance breeds virtue

- Talking with Pericles the younger, Socrates weighs Athens against Boeotia. Athens matches Boeotia in numbers and fine men and is more united and more jealous of honour, with a richer heritage of ancestors' deeds.
- Pericles points to the decline since the defeats at Lebadeia and Delium. Socrates replies that the state was never "so ripe for a really good leader." Boldness breeds carelessness and insubordination, while fear breeds application, obedience and order. He compares calm weather aboard ship, when disorder reigns, to the approach of a storm or an enemy, when crews hush and obey.
- To rekindle virtue, appeal to inheritance. Just as one stirs men to claim ancestral property, show them that headship combined with virtue is an old, honoured heritage that belongs to them above all. He cites Cecrops, Erechtheus, the Heraclidae, Theseus and the wars against Asia.
- Athens declined because "we are victims of our own success." Like an athlete grown lax through easy superiority, they neglected themselves. The cure is to rediscover the forefathers' institutions or to imitate and outdo today's leaders.
- Pericles lists Athenian failings: contempt for age, neglect of the body, defiance of magistrates, envy, litigiousness and quarrelling over common interests. Socrates urges him not to think the people incurably depraved. They obey well in naval matters, at gymnastic contests and in training choruses, and the Areopagus is a model of honour and justice.
- Where discipline fails, he suggests it is because the officers are the least knowledgeable. No one leads harpists, dancers or wrestlers without the requisite knowledge, yet "most generals are amateurs and improvisers." A leader should be able to say where he learned his craft, and should seek out those who know when he finds ignorance in himself, "sparing neither gifts nor gratitude."
- His practical proposal: hold the mountain barrier along Boeotia's border with a corps of young, lightly armed Athenians. They would be a thorn to the enemy and a bulwark for the country, as the Mysians and Pisidians do for the Persian king.

## Know what you propose to manage before seeking office

- Socrates flatters the under-twenty Glaucon's ambition, then asks how he will benefit the state. Wealth is the obvious answer, and Glaucon cannot name the state's revenues or its expenditures.
- Socrates presses on further matters:
  - Enriching Athens from its enemies requires knowing both sides' land and naval forces. A statesman who knows his city is stronger can advise war, and one who knows it is weaker can advise caution.
  - Whether the outposts should be swept away rests on conjecture unless he has inspected them himself.
  - He has never visited the mines.
  - He does not know how long the corn supply lasts.
- "A man could not even manage his own house or his estate well, without, in the first place, knowing what he requires, and, in the second place, taking pains, item by item, to supply his wants." The city has more than ten thousand houses. If Glaucon cannot improve his own uncle's household, how could he persuade all Athens?
- "Do you not see how dangerous it is for a man to speak or act beyond the range of his knowledge?" People who exceed their knowledge gain blame, and those who enjoy admiration are the ones with the highest knowledge. The advice is to make sure of "the knowledge as far as in you lies of what you wish to do," and then the goal is reachable.
- Charmides, who gives sound private advice but shrinks from the Assembly, gets the opposite correction. A person who could win a crown and refuses to compete, or who could exalt his city and hangs back, is a coward.
- The Assembly is made of fullers, cobblers, carpenters, farmers and traders, not experts. Socrates compares fearing them to a man who beats trained athletes yet quails before amateurs. He closes: "do not be ignorant of yourself!" and urges Charmides not to rush to examine others while neglecting to examine himself.

## Good, beautiful and useful are measured by fitness for the end

- When Aristippus asks if he knows anything good, Socrates asks whether he means good for fever, for sore eyes or for hunger. He says he neither knows nor wants to know of "any good thing which is good for nothing."
- Beautiful things can be unlike each other, like a runner versus a boxer, or a shield versus a javelin.
- "Relatively to the same standard all things are at once beautiful and good." The standard is what the thing is useful for, so a dung basket is beautiful for its use and a golden spear ugly for its.
- "Everything is good and beautiful when well adapted for the end in view, bad and ugly when ill adapted."
- Applying this to housebuilding, a house should be both pleasant and useful. A southern aspect with a higher south side catches winter sun, and the roof shades it in summer. A lower north side keeps out cold winds, and the whole should be a secure place to keep goods. Paintings and ornamental mouldings "deprive one of more joy than they confer."
- He advises placing temples and altars on a site visible from afar and untrodden by foot.
- He tests the corselet maker Pistias on "proportion." It means fit to the wearer, not an absolute measure. A fitting corselet distributes weight over collar-bone, shoulders, chest, back and belly and feels "like another natural integument." An ill-fitting one, however gilded or inlaid, is "a curiously-wrought and gilded nuisance." Since the body moves, the true fit is the corselet that does not gall the wearer in use.

## Courage, wisdom, virtue and rule rest on knowledge and practice

- Courage: Souls differ by nature as bodies do, yet "by learning and practice the natural aptitude may always be strengthened towards courage." Scythians, Thracians and Lacedaemonians would each refuse to fight with the others' weapons. Natural differences are compensated by care and attention, so everyone should "learn and practise those things in which we would fain achieve distinction."
- Wisdom and temperance are not distinguished: one who recognises the beautiful and good well enough to live in them, and knows the base well enough to avoid it, is wise and sound of soul.
- Knowing right action but not doing it is not wisdom or self-control: "Every one... deliberately chooses what, within the limits open to him, he considers most conducive to his interest." Those who act crookedly are neither wise nor self-controlled.
- "Justice, and all other virtue is wisdom." Those who know what is beautiful and good will choose nothing else, and those who lack the knowledge cannot do it, even if they try.
- Madness is not simple ignorance but ignorance of oneself, "to imagine and suppose that he knows what he knows not." On a large scale, he says, this is called madness.
- Envy is pain felt only at the successes of friends, not at an enemy's fortune or a friend's misfortune. He calls it the condition of a silly mind.
- Leisure is the freedom to turn from something worse to something better. No one has leisure to turn from the better to the worse.
- Rulers: not those who merely hold the sceptre, are chosen by the crowd or by lot, or seize power by force or fraud, but "those who have the special knowledge how to rule." Ship, farm, sickness and athletics all show people obeying the expert, and women rule men in wool-spinning because they know the art. A tyrant who rejects wise counsel errs and is punished. Killing his best ally brings swift ruin.
- Success: the best pursuit is "successful conduct," doing a thing well through learning and practice. Fortune, success without seeking it, is the opposite. Those who do well in their calling, whether farming, healing or statecraft, are best and dearest to God.

## Learning from craftsmen, caring for the body, and modest living

- With painters and sculptors: Socrates asks Parrhasius whether the soul's moods can be depicted. They are invisible, yet loving or hostile looks, relief or anxiety, nobility or servility show through the face, posture and movement, so they can be imitated. He tells Cleiton that statues seem alive through faithful copying of muscle tension, gesture and weight, and that the sculptor must also capture the soul's workings.
- Theodote: He treats the courtesan as a person with something to learn about friendship. She lives on friends, which he calls better property than flocks. Friends are won by kindness and pleasure, not force, and by acts of love rather than lip phrases. She should ask only what a well-wisher can grant without repenting, and requite in kind. Favours should come when appetite exists: "create hunger, and even coarser stuff seems honey-sweet." He tells her, "Seek and you will find means, if you truly need me," and declines to be drawn to her ("I wish you to come to me").
- Body: He tells the feeble Epigenes that a bad bodily habit leads to death in war, capture and slavery, disgrace, and impaired thought, including forgetfulness, despondency and "madness". A healthy body brings safety, honour and a better legacy. Because Athens does not train publicly, one should train privately. "It is a base thing for a man to wax old in careless self-neglect before he has lifted up his eyes and seen what manner of man he was made to be."
- Everyday corrections:
  - Do not rage at a man's rudeness when you would not rage at his poor health.
  - For a lost appetite, "stop eating."
  - Hot household water means a ready warm bath, and servants use it contentedly.
  - A master flogging a lazy slave should ask which deserves it more.
  - A journey to Olympia is just several ordinary walks strung together, so set off a day early.
  - A man too tired from a trip carrying only his cloak, while his slave carried the baggage, is a poor athlete.
- Banquets: Socrates has small and large food contributions pooled so the lavish stop catering for delicacies. He mocks eating many dishes at once as extravagant and destructive of the cook's art, and notes it makes a man feel starved when few dishes are served. "Making good cheer" means eating what troubles neither soul nor body, is easy to find, and fits a modest, orderly life.
