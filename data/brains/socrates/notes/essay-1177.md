## Socrates on piety, divination and the limits of human knowledge

- Socrates sacrificed often, at home and at the state's common altars, and relied on divination. His saying "A divinity gives me a sign" was known to everyone. Xenophon notes it was no stranger than other people's trust in omens (birds' flight or cries, chance meetings, a victim's entrails), since the gods, not the bird, signify what is profitable.
- He advised friends to consult the oracle on matters whose outcome cannot be calculated. Examples: who will harvest a field one sows, who will live in the house one builds, whether a campaign will profit a general, whether a fine wife brings joy or sorrow, whether political connections will get a man cast out of his city.
- He held that "to suppose that all these matters lay within the scope of human judgment... was preternatural folly."
- The reverse was equally foolish. Anything learnable by "numbering, weighing, and measuring" (whether to hire an expert coachman or a landsman as skipper) should not be put to the gods, and asking was "a sort of profanity." His rule: "where we are permitted to work through our natural faculties, there let us by all means apply them. But in things which are hidden, let us seek to gain knowledge from above."
- He believed the gods know everything: words, deeds and the counsels "in the silent chambers of the heart." He thought they are present everywhere.
- He would no more disregard a divine sign than follow a blind man who didn't know the road. He scorned people who defied warnings to avoid disrepute among men.

## Why Socrates rejected speculation about the cosmos

- He never discussed the nature of the Universe, how the "kosmos" came to be, or what forces cause celestial phenomena. He called it folly to trouble one's brain with these things.
- His questions to such thinkers:
  - Do they know human affairs well enough to move on to divine ones?
  - Do they hope to create winds, waters and seasons at will once they understand the causes, or only to know how things come about?
- He pointed to their disagreement as proof of futility: one says Being is one, another that it is infinite in number; one that all things flow, another that nothing can move; one that all is birth and death, another that nothing is born or dies. He compared them to madmen, who differ from one another in the same way.
- He discussed human topics constantly: piety and impiety, the beautiful and the ugly, the noble and the base, just and unjust, sobriety and madness, courage and cowardice, what a state, a statesman and a ruler are.
- He said knowing these answers "conferred a patent of nobility on the possessor," while those who lacked it "might deservedly be stigmatised as slaves."

## Piety in practice: law, prayer and modest sacrifice

- He followed the Pythia's rule: "Act according to the law and custom of your state, and you will act piously." He called people who acted otherwise "busybodies and vain fellows."
- His prayer was simply "Give me that which is best for me," because the gods know best what is good. Praying for gold, silver or power is like praying for a particular dice throw or battle stake, with uncertain results.
- Small sacrifices from small means were no worse than large ones from ample means. If gods preferred large offerings, bad men's gifts would often outrank good men's, and life would not be worth living. The gods' joy "is greater in proportion to the holiness of the giver."
- He liked Hesiod's line "According to thine ability do sacrifice to the immortal gods," and applied "do according to your ability" to dealings with friends, strangers and life in general.
- As Council member under oath, he presided over the Assembly when it wanted to condemn the nine generals (Thrasyllus, Erasinides and others) by one inclusive vote. Despite the people's anger and threats from powerful citizens, he refused to put the question, valuing his oath over pleasing the crowd or protecting himself.

## The argument from design: Socrates and Aristodemus

Socrates challenged Aristodemus "the little," who neither sacrificed nor consulted divination and mocked those who did.

- **Design vs. chance:** Aristodemus admired Homer, Melanippides, Sophocles, Polycleitus and Zeuxis. Socrates asked whether a maker of lifeless images or of living, intelligent creatures deserves more admiration. Aristodemus replied that it would be the latter, provided they came from design and not chance. Things serving an evident purpose are the work of design.
- **Evidence of foresight in the body:**
  - eyes, ears, nose and tongue, each matched to a use
  - eyelids as doors, lashes as screens, brows to stop sweat
  - front teeth to cut, molars to grind
  - the mouth placed near the nostrils and eyes, and waste passages kept far from the senses
  - the desire to beget, the mother's urge to rear her child, and the creature's love of life and fear of death
- **Mind in the universe:** Aristodemus has a small portion of earth and water in his body, yet supposes that only mind was snatched up by lucky chance. Not seeing the gods is no objection, since he cannot see his own soul, "the master agent of your body," either.
- **Gifts to humans:**
  - erect posture, which gives a wider view
  - hands
  - a tongue shaped for articulate speech
  - an appetite not limited to seasons
  - the best soul, able to recognise the gods and learn
  - his remark that a human mind in an ox's body, or hands without human wit, would be useless
- **Why care is plausible:** Aristodemus said he would believe if the gods sent him counsellors. Socrates answered that oracles, portents and warnings to Hellas are meant for him too. He added that:
  - the longest-lasting cities and peoples are the most god-fearing, and individuals grow more religious with age and judgment
  - as the mind rules the body and can think of Egypt or Sicily at once, divine wisdom can embrace all things at one instant
  - he should test the gods by acts of service, as he tests which friends will repay kindness and whose counsel is wise
- **Effect:** Xenophon says such talk made listeners avoid unholiness, baseness and injustice even in solitude, since no conduct escapes heaven.

## Self-control as the foundation of virtue

- Socrates called self-restraint "the very corner-stone of virtue," to be laid as "the basis and foundation of his soul." Without it, nobody can absorb or practise a good lesson.
- He argued with analogies. In war we wouldn't choose a leader enslaved to belly, wine, lust or sleep. A dying man wouldn't entrust his sons, daughters and property to a libertine, and nobody would take an intemperate butler or errand boy even as a free gift.
- The intemperate man is worse off than the covetous one. The covetous enrich themselves by taking from others, but the intemperate wrecks his own body and soul, which is the greatest mischief. A guest who loves the wine more than his friends is unwelcome.
- His practice matched his words. He took only enough food that appetite served as sauce, drank only when thirsty, and advised avoiding foods that tempt you to eat when not hungry. On Circe's pigs: Odysseus "through his continency" did not turn into a swine. He also favoured moderate exercise and rejected both excess and neglect of the body.
- On beauty, he said to keep clear of attractive people: "once lay finger on these and it is not easy to keep a sound head and a sober mind." He teased Critobulus for kissing Alcibiades' son, warning Xenophon that it would cost his freedom, force him into hurtful spending, and leave no leisure for noble study. Beauty "wounds so far off," like Eros the archer, and is worse than the tarantula because no contact is needed. His advice was to "run helter-skelter for bare life" from fair forms, and Critobulus should "go abroad for a year" to heal.
- He declared himself immune: he found it easier to abstain from beauty's fullest bloom than others find it to avoid "weeds and garbage."
- Taking money from donors "sets up over himself a master" and binds one to "an abominable slavery."

## Defending a simple life and refusing fees: Socrates and Antiphon

- **Antiphon's attack:** the sophist said Socrates lived wretchedly, with cheap food and drink, one cloak for both seasons, no shoes or shirt, no money-making. Socrates should call himself "professor of the art of wretchedness."
- **Reply on needs:**
  - Not charging means he need not talk to anyone but those he chooses.
  - Sharp hunger needs no sauce and thirst needs no exotic drink.
  - Clothes and shoes serve only against cold, heat and sore feet, and he doesn't stay indoors or fight for shade.
  - Training makes even a weak body outdo a neglected strong one.
- **Reply on happiness:** the best pleasure is the consciousness of "becoming better oneself, of acquiring better and better friends," not luxury. A man with few needs has more leisure for friends and country, serves as a soldier more easily, and holds out better in a siege. His creed: "To have no wants at all is... an attribute of Godhead; to have as few wants as possible the nearest approach to Godhead."
- **Fees:** Antiphon said Socrates must not value his wisdom, since he'd never give his cloak or house away free. Socrates answered that beauty and wisdom both have a fair way and a foul way to be disposed of.
  - Selling beauty to anyone is bad, but making a worthy lover a friend is sensible.
  - Selling wisdom to the first bidder makes a sophist, "a man who prostitutes his wisdom." Teaching a person of noble nature everything good and making him a friend is a good citizen's duty.
  - He compared taking pleasure in good friends to other men's pleasure in horses, hounds or fighting cocks. He teaches them whatever good he has, recommends them to others who can help, and reads the old wise men's books with them.
- **Politics:** Antiphon asked why he didn't enter politics himself. Socrates asked whether it was more statesmanlike to practise politics alone or to make many others fit for it.

## Honest virtue over false appearance

- Socrates said the best route to renown is to become good at what you want to be thought good at.
- A pretender to flute-playing needs the equipment, the attendants and a bought claque, and can never perform without being exposed. He gets expense, bad reputation and mockery.
- A pretended general or pilot either fails to convince anyone, or succeeds and ruins the people who trusted him. Pretending to be rich, brave or strong fails the same way: you'll receive commands beyond your power "and the world will give you no commiseration."
- He called one who defrauds people of money a great cheat. "Of all imposters" the biggest is the one who persuades people he is fit to lead the state while worthless.

## Defence against the charges: corruption of youth, Critias and Alcibiades, and the laws

- **Teaching by example:** Xenophon says Socrates never claimed to teach virtue, yet by being virtuous himself made companions hope to resemble him by imitation. He took no fees, which he thought protected his freedom, and called fee-takers "vendors of their own persons." He expected those who agreed with him to be true friends to him and each other for life.
- **Persuasion over violence:** to the charge that criticising election of officers by lot made the young violent, Xenophon replies that those who value wisdom prefer persuasion. Force breeds enmity and needs many allies, whereas persuasion wins "single-handed."
- **Critias and Alcibiades:**
  - They sought him for his independent, disciplined life, not to learn civic virtue, and Xenophon thinks they would have chosen death over living as he did.
  - While with him they were temperate. They fell away once they thought themselves masters, and once he was no longer there to check them, as flattery, wealth and power corrupted them.
  - Virtue needs practice. Without it the soul forgets what it learned, as a long poem is forgotten without repetition. Hence the saying that the good are formed by good company and ruined by base company.
  - A teacher should not be blamed when a pupil fails under another, and praise would be due for earlier good conduct.
  - Socrates rebuked Critias for pursuing Euthydemus carnally, comparing him to a pig rubbing against stones. Critias resented this.
  - His real companions were Crito, Chaerephon, Chaerecrates, Hermogenes, Simmias, Cebes and Phaedondes, who wanted goodness of soul to do their duties to family, friends, fellow-citizens and the state.
- **Confronting the Thirty:** Socrates said a herdsman who thins his cattle is a poor herdsman, and a ruler who thins the citizens is worse. Critias and Charicles then banned him from talking to anyone under thirty. He asked whether he could buy goods or ask where Charicles lived from someone younger. They told him to drop his shoemakers, carpenters and coppersmiths, and the just and holy, "especially from cowherds."
- **Pericles and law:** Alcibiades, before twenty, questioned Pericles on what a law is. They agreed that anything forced on citizens without persuasion, whether by a tyrant, a minority or even a majority over the wealthy, is "violence rather than law."
- **Disputed teachings:**
  - He did say the sick depend on a doctor and the litigant on a lawyer, not on relatives. Likewise, friends are worth honouring only if they can be useful, because "lack of intelligence means lack of worth." His examples were burying a body once the soul has gone and trimming nails or hair, not disrespect to kin. His aim was that people become useful to those whose esteem they want.
  - On madness and ignorance: someone who may be justly confined for folly can't object to being kept in check by those who know more, and the ignorant should sit at the feet of those who know.
  - Hesiod's "No work is a disgrace" meant that only good work counts. He called dicers and gamblers "idle drones."
  - On Homer's Odysseus beating commoners, he said only that useless, insolent people who can't help the army, state or people should be restrained.
- Xenophon concludes that Socrates, a lover of the people who took no pay, harmed no one, and spent his life making others better, deserved honour rather than death.
