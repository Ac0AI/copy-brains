## The Socratic critique of imitative poetry: three removes from truth

Socrates opens Book X by saying the rejection of imitative poetry is the part of the city's order that pleases him most, now that the parts of the soul have been distinguished. He claims "all poetical imitations are ruinous to the understanding of the hearers, and that the knowledge of their true nature is the only antidote to them."

- He admits a lifelong awe and love of Homer, "the great captain and teacher of the whole of that charming tragic company", but says "a man is not to be reverenced more than the truth."
- He starts his usual way: where many things share a name, assume there is one idea or form. There are many beds and tables but only one form of each.
- A craftsman makes a particular bed "in accordance with the idea", but no artisan makes the idea itself.
- That gives three beds and three makers: the one bed in nature, made by God; the carpenter's bed; and the painter's bed.
- God made only one bed, because a second would imply a third form behind both, so He is the natural maker of the essential bed.
- The painter is an imitator, "third in the descent from nature". The tragic poet, like every imitator, is "thrice removed from the king and from the truth."

## Imitation yields appearances, not knowledge

Socrates argues the imitator knows only appearances, and so can "do all things" only in a shallow sense.

- A mirror turned round and round "makes" the sun, earth and yourself, but only as appearances. The painter is "a creator of appearances".
- Painting reproduces a bed as it appears from one angle, not as it is. It aims at appearance, not reality.
- A painter can paint a cobbler or carpenter without knowing their arts, and fool children or simple people from a distance.
- Anyone who claims someone knows all the arts has probably met a wizard or actor and could not "analyse the nature of knowledge and ignorance and imitation."
- He applies this to the claim that Homer and the tragedians know every art and all virtue and vice. Either they were fooled by imitators, or they truly know their subjects. His test follows below.
- Three arts relate to any object: the one that uses it, the one that makes it, and the one that imitates it.
- The user has knowledge. The maker gets only correct belief, learned from the user. A flute player tells the flute maker what makes a good flute.
- The imitator has neither knowledge nor right opinion about whether his images are good, so he copies "that which appears to be good to the ignorant multitude." Imitation is "a kind of play or sport".

## Testing Homer: what did he ever actually do?

Socrates asks what claim Homer has to be an educator, if he had real knowledge rather than imitation. He focuses on the "chiefest and noblest subjects": military tactics, politics and education.

- No city was ever better governed through Homer. Lacedaemon owes its order to Lycurgus, and Italy and Sicily boast of Charondas. Solon is renowned at Athens. Even the Homerids do not call Homer a legislator.
- No war was won by his counsel, and no useful invention is credited to him, unlike Thales or Anacharsis.
- He left no private following or "Homeric way of life", unlike Pythagoras, whose order endures. Tradition says even his companion Creophylus neglected him.
- Sophists like Protagoras and Prodicus tell people they cannot manage their house or state without them as educators, and their companions all but carry them on their shoulders. If Homer or Hesiod could have made people virtuous, nobody would have let them wander as rhapsodists. People would have kept them "as unwilling to part with them as with gold."
- Conclusion: the poets copy "images of virtue and the like" but never reach the truth.
- Poetry persuades through melody, metre and rhythm. Stripped of music and recited in prose, the tales look like faces that "were never really beautiful, but only blooming", with the bloom of youth gone.

## Why imitation harms the soul: reason versus the passions

Socrates ties poetry to a conflict within the soul.

- Optical illusions (a stick bent in water, big and small by distance) show that perception can contradict itself. Measuring, numbering and weighing "come to the rescue", and these belong to the calculating, rational part of the soul.
- One faculty cannot hold contrary opinions about the same thing at once, so the part that opposes measure is a lower part. The better part trusts measure and calculation.
- Imitation is "an inferior who marries an inferior, and has inferior offspring": it consorts with the part of the soul far from reason.
- Grief shows the same split. A good man who loses a son will moderate his sorrow, and more so when seen by others than when alone. Law and reason bid him resist, while his feeling pushes him to indulge.
- Reason's counsel is to be patient, since we cannot know whether such things are good or evil. Grief stands in the way of what is needed, namely to "take counsel about what has happened, and when the dice have been thrown order our affairs in the way which reason deems best." We should not behave like children who hold the part struck and howl.
- The fretful, complaining part is easy to imitate, while the calm, wise temperament is hard to imitate and hard for a festival crowd to appreciate.
- So the popular poet is drawn to the passionate and fitful character. Like the painter, he deals in inferior truth and appeals to an inferior part of the soul.
- This is why the poet is refused admission to a well-ordered state: "he awakens and nourishes and strengthens the feelings and impairs the reason." It is like letting the evil rule a city while pushing the good aside.

## The "heaviest count": poetry corrupts even good people

Socrates says poetry's gravest charge is its power to harm even the good.

- The best of us delight in a hero's long lament and praise the poet who stirs us most. Yet in our own sorrow we pride ourselves on being quiet and patient, and call the other behavior womanly. It is inconsistent to praise in a stage hero what we would be ashamed to do ourselves.
- The explanation: the natural hunger to weep, held in check in our own troubles, is satisfied by the poets. The better part, not yet trained by reason or habit, relaxes its guard because the sorrow is someone else's.
- The spectator thinks he loses nothing. He does not see that "from the evil of other men something of evil is communicated to themselves." Pity fed at the theatre is hard to restrain in our own misfortunes.
- Comedy works the same way. Jokes you would be ashamed to make yourself, you enjoy on stage, and you end up "playing the comic poet at home."
- The same goes for lust, anger and every desire, pleasure and pain: "poetry feeds and waters the passions instead of drying them up; she lets them rule, although they ought to be controlled, if mankind are ever to increase in happiness and virtue."
- His verdict on Homer's admirers: they may be excellent people, and Homer is the greatest poet and first of the tragedians. But only "hymns to the gods and praises of famous men" should be admitted. Otherwise pleasure and pain will rule instead of law and reason.

## The ancient quarrel and the terms for poetry's return

Socrates frames the ban as a regretful, reasoned judgment, not hostility.

- He cites the "ancient quarrel between philosophy and poetry", with phrases like "the yelping hound howling at her lord" and "the mob of sages circumventing Zeus."
- He is charmed by poetry himself, especially Homer. He proposes that she may return from exile if she can defend herself in verse, and that her lovers who are not poets may defend her in prose. They must show she is useful to states and human life, not merely pleasant.
- If the defence fails, we must give her up "after the manner of lovers", who restrain a desire that opposes their interest, though "not without a struggle."
- Meanwhile he prescribes a charm to repeat while listening: poetry is not to be taken seriously as attaining truth, and the listener, "fearing for the safety of the city which is within him," should guard against her seductions.
- The stakes: "great is the issue at stake, greater than appears, whether a man is to be good or bad." Nothing, including honour, money, power or the excitement of poetry, should lead a person to neglect justice and virtue.

## The argument for the soul's immortality

Socrates says the greatest rewards of virtue come over eternity, since seventy years is "nothing" beside it, and an immortal being should think of the whole. Glaucon is astonished when told the soul is immortal; Socrates says the proof is easy.

- Every thing has its own evil that destroys it: ophthalmia for the eyes, mildew for corn, rot for timber, rust for iron. Good cannot destroy a thing, and neither can what is neither good nor evil.
- The soul's evils are unrighteousness, intemperance, cowardice and ignorance. None of them wastes the soul away and separates it from the body. The unjust man does not die of his injustice.
- If the soul's own inherent evil cannot destroy it, an external evil cannot either. Bad food harms the body only by producing bodily disease, which is the body's own corruption.
- So fever, the knife at the throat, or cutting the body into pieces cannot destroy the soul, unless the soul is shown to become more unjust as a result. Nobody will show that death makes souls more unjust.
- Glaucon adds that injustice often keeps the murderer "alive, aye, and well awake too", so it is far from a "house of death".
- Conclusion: a soul that cannot be destroyed by internal or external evil must exist forever. The number of souls stays constant, since none are destroyed and increase would have to come from something mortal.
- The soul in its true nature cannot be a "variety" of many elements. We see her like the sea-god Glaucus, whose original form is crusted over with seaweed, shells and stones. To see her truly, look at her love of wisdom, her kinship with "the immortal and eternal and divine", and how she would be if freed from the sea of earthly things.

## Justice's rewards, and the Myth of Er: choosing a life

Having shown justice is best for the soul in itself "whether he have the ring of Gyges or not", Socrates asks that justice be given back her good reputation. The just are loved by the gods, and everything works out for good for them in life and death. Their aim is "to be like God, as far as man can attain the divine likeness, by the pursuit of virtue."

- Clever unjust people are like runners who start fast but do not finish, while the true runner is crowned. Even if the unjust escape in youth, most are found out and end old, miserable and mocked.
- He then tells the tale of Er of Pamphylia, who was killed in battle and revived on the funeral pyre on the twelfth day. Souls are judged and sent up or down. For every wrong they suffer tenfold, over a thousand-year journey (a hundred years counted as a lifetime, paid ten times). Rewards for justice follow the same proportion. The tyrant Ardiaeus, who murdered his father and elder brother, is refused re-entry and dragged off to be punished.
- Souls travel to the spindle of Necessity, where Lachesis, Clotho and Atropos sing the past, present and future. A prophet announces: "Your genius will not be allotted to you, but you choose your genius... Virtue is free, and as a man honours or dishonours her he will have more or less of her; the responsibility is with the chooser. God is justified."
- Lots decide the order of choosing, and the available lives include tyrannies, famous and obscure lives, and animal lives. The prophet adds, "Even for the last comer, if he chooses wisely and will live diligently, there is appointed a happy and not undesirable existence. Let not him who chooses first be careless, and let not the last despair."
- The first chooser grabs the greatest tyranny without thinking it through and finds he is fated to devour his own children. Then he blames chance and the gods instead of himself. He came from heaven and had lived in a well-ordered state, but his virtue was "a matter of habit only, and he had no philosophy". Souls returning from suffering on earth chose more carefully.
- Choices follow past experience: Orpheus picks a swan, Ajax a lion, Agamemnon an eagle, and Thersites a monkey. Odysseus, cured of ambition, takes the last lot and finds the quiet life of a private man with no cares, which everyone else neglected, and says he would have chosen it even if first.
- Socrates calls this "the supreme peril of our human state". Each person should leave every other kind of knowledge and seek only to "discern between good and evil, and so to choose always and everywhere the better life." The standard: call a life evil if it makes the soul more unjust, and good if it makes the soul more just; "all else he will disregard."
- Take into the next world "an adamantine faith in truth and right", and "choose the mean and avoid the extremes on either side."
- The souls then drink from the river of Unmindfulness (forgetfulness), and those not saved by wisdom drink more. Er alone was kept from drinking.
- His closing counsel to Glaucon: "hold fast ever to the heavenly way and follow after justice and virtue always, considering that the soul is immortal and able to endure every sort of good and every sort of evil." Then we shall "live dear to one another and to the gods" and fare well in this life and the thousand-year pilgrimage.
