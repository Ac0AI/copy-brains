## The pious must be defined by one form, not by examples

- Socrates asks Euthyphro, who is prosecuting his own father for murder and claims exact knowledge of piety, "what is piety, and what is impiety?"
- Euthyphro answers that piety is "doing as I am doing," meaning prosecuting wrongdoers, even a father.
- Socrates rejects this: "I did not ask you to give me two or three examples of piety, but to explain the general idea which makes all pious things to be pious."
- He wants one idea that makes the pious pious and the impious impious, in every action.
- Reason: with that standard "I may look, and by which I may measure actions, whether yours or those of any one else."
- Rule drawn from this: a single example, even a admired one, does not define a virtue.

## Gods who disagree make "dear to the gods" an unusable definition

- Euthyphro's second answer: piety is "that which is dear to the gods," impiety is what is not.
- Socrates tests it against Euthyphro's own admission that the gods quarrel.
- He argues that disputes over number, size or weight are settled by counting, measuring or weighing, so they do not breed enmity.
- Quarrels arise over "the just and unjust, good and evil, honourable and dishonourable." Gods quarrel about the same things, so each loves what it deems just and hates the opposite.
- Result: the same act is loved by some gods and hated by others, so it would be both pious and impious. Chastising the father may please Zeus but displease Cronos or Uranus.
- Socrates notes that people and gods alike do not argue that the guilty should go unpunished. They dispute the particulars: who did it, what, and when.
- He does not ask for proof that all the gods approve Euthyphro's act, saying it would not settle what piety is.
- He then amends the definition to "what all the gods love is pious, and what all hate, impious," and tests that too.

## The Euthyphro question: loved because holy, or holy because loved?

- Socrates asks: "whether the pious or holy is beloved by the gods because it is holy, or holy because it is beloved of the gods."
- His method: a thing is carried because someone carries it, led because someone leads it, seen because someone sees it. The state follows the act, never the reverse.
- Applied to love, the state of being loved follows the act of loving.
- Euthyphro agrees the holy is loved because it is holy, not holy because it is loved.
- But what is "dear to the gods" is dear because it is loved by them.
- So the two differ. One kind of thing is loved because of what it is; the other is loved only because it is loved.
- Conclusion: Euthyphro has offered "an attribute only, and not the essence," namely being loved by all the gods. He still has not said what holiness is.
- Socrates says to leave aside whether it is dear to the gods ("a matter about which we will not quarrel") and say what holiness really is.

## Piety as a part of justice: genus and species

- Euthyphro admits his arguments "turn round and walk away." Socrates jokes that he is more than Daedalus: "I move those of other people as well," and he would rather not.
- Socrates asks whether all that is just is pious, or all that is pious is just.
- He illustrates with the poet Stasinus ("where there is fear there is also reverence"), which he disputes. Many fear poverty and disease without reverencing them.
- Where reverence exists, fear exists, so reverence is a part of fear, "just as the odd is a part of number."
- Likewise justice is the wider notion and piety only a part. The next question is what part, as one could say an even number is "a number which represents a figure having two equal sides."
- Euthyphro answers that piety is the part of justice that attends to the gods, while the other part attends to men.

## Testing "attention to the gods" by analogy with the crafts

- Socrates asks what "attention" means. Horsemanship attends to horses, the huntsman's art to dogs, the herdsman's to oxen.
- In each craft, attention aims at the good or benefit of what is tended, and never at its hurt.
- Does piety then improve the gods? Euthyphro says no.
- He retreats to "such as servants show to their masters," a ministration. Socrates asks what end it serves, as medicine serves health, shipbuilding a ship, housebuilding a house.
- What is the "fair work" the gods accomplish with human help? Euthyphro dodges, saying it would be tiresome to learn.
- Socrates notes the evasion, observing "the asker of a question is necessarily dependent on the answerer, whither he leads I must follow."

## Piety as trade with the gods circles back to the start

- Euthyphro says piety is knowing how to please the gods by prayers and sacrifices, the "salvation of families and states."
- Socrates restates it as a science of asking and giving: sacrificing is giving, prayer is asking.
- The right way to ask is to ask what we want, and the right way to give is to give what the gods want. Piety becomes "an art which gods and men have of doing business with one another."
- Socrates objects: "I have no particular liking for anything but the truth."
- The gods give every good thing, so humans seem to get the better of the deal. What benefit do the gods receive?
- Euthyphro says gifts are "tributes of honour" and what pleases them. Socrates points out that this returns to piety as "dear to the gods," the definition already refuted.
- He says the argument comes "round to the same point" and that Euthyphro is the greater artist than Daedalus.

## Method and attitude: feigned ignorance and persistence in inquiry

- Socrates states he is charged with impiety by Meletus. He ironically calls Meletus a good "husbandman" who starts by clearing the young shoots.
- He says he wants to learn piety from Euthyphro so he can clear himself, and offers to become his disciple.
- He doubts the myths: that Zeus bound Cronos, and that gods fight. "What else can I say, confessing as I do, that I know nothing about them?"
- He says he is "a benevolent" talker who would "even pay for a listener," and that Athenians resent a man only once he imparts his wisdom.
- He insists inquiry should test claims: "Ought we to enquire into the truth of this... or simply to accept the mere statement on our own authority and that of others?"
- He argues that someone who truly knew piety would not risk prosecuting his father, so Euthyphro must know it. He urges him to speak, "like Proteus," saying he will detain him until he tells.
- The dialogue ends without a definition. Euthyphro leaves in a hurry, and Socrates laments that he was left "in despair."
