## Love as lack: Socrates' claim that he knows only "the art of love"

- Socrates says he is a master only of "matters of love" and agrees to praise Love only if he may "speak the truth" rather than flatter.
- He objects to the earlier speeches. He had thought praise meant choosing "out of the true" the best things and setting them forth well. He now sees the others attribute "every species of greatness" to Love, "without regard to truth or falsehood", so that Love only appears praised.
- He says such praise fools "those who know him not", but "you cannot impose upon those who know him".
- He will not rival them: "I do not praise in that way; no, indeed, I cannot."
- He starts from a few questions to Agathon, using his admissions as premises, and then retells what the priestess Diotima of Mantineia taught him.

## The elenchus on Agathon: desire implies want

Socrates' questioning of Agathon runs step by step.

- Love is always love *of* something, as a father is father of a son and a brother is brother of someone.
- Whoever desires something lacks it. "He who desires something is in want of something... absolutely and necessarily true."
- A man cannot desire what he already is. The strong do not desire strength, and the healthy do not desire health.
- When a rich or healthy man says "I desire what I have", he really wants those goods *preserved in the future*, which he does not yet possess.
- Agathon had said Love sets the gods in order through love of the beautiful. So Love lacks beauty. What lacks beauty is not beautiful, and the beautiful is also the good, so Love lacks the good too.
- Agathon concedes: "I cannot refute you, Socrates." Socrates replies: "Say rather, beloved Agathon, that you cannot refute the truth; for Socrates is easily refuted."

## Diotima's correction: Love is neither fair nor foul

Socrates reports that Diotima corrected his own earlier belief that Love was "a mighty god, and likewise fair".

- Not fair does not mean foul, and not good does not mean evil. There is a mean.
- The parallel she gives is *right opinion*. It lies between knowledge and ignorance, because it cannot give a reason (so it is not knowledge) yet it does reach the truth (so it is not ignorance).
- Gods are happy and possess the good and fair. Love lacks them, so Love is not a god.
- Love is a *daimon*, "a great spirit... intermediate between the divine and the mortal". He interprets between gods and men, carrying prayers up and commands down, and so binds the whole together.
- Socrates' error was a confusion of *love and the beloved*. The beloved is the beautiful and perfect. The lover has "another nature".

## The myth of Love's parentage: Poros and Penia

- Love was conceived on Aphrodite's birthday. Poros (Plenty), son of Metis (Discretion), fell asleep drunk on nectar in Zeus's garden, and Penia (Poverty) lay with him.
- From his mother Love is always poor: "rough and squalid, and has no shoes, nor a house to dwell in", sleeping on bare ground at doors.
- From his father he is "bold, enterprising, strong, a mighty hunter", "always weaving some intrigue", "fertile in resources", "a philosopher at all times".
- He is neither mortal nor immortal, flourishing in plenty and dying in want. "That which is always flowing in is always flowing out, and so he is never in want and never in wealth."

## Why the philosopher is a lover: wisdom, ignorance and the mean

- "No god is a philosopher or seeker after wisdom, for he is wise already; nor does any man who is wise seek after wisdom."
- The ignorant do not seek wisdom either. "Herein is the evil of ignorance, that he who is neither good nor wise is nevertheless satisfied with himself: he has no desire for that of which he feels no want."
- Philosophers are therefore those in between, and Love is one of them. Wisdom is among the most beautiful things and Love is of the beautiful, so Love is a philosopher.
- Diotima's point is that desire for wisdom begins with felt lack. This links to Socrates' own profession of ignorance: "my ignorance is the reason why I come to you; for I am conscious that I want a teacher."

## What lovers actually want: the good, forever

Diotima's questions run in sequence.

- A man who loves the beautiful wants it to be *his*. Putting "good" in place of "beautiful": he wants the *possession of the good*, and the result of that is happiness. No further reason for wanting happiness is needed. "The answer is already final."
- All men desire their own good. Only some are called lovers because the name of the whole is given to one part, as "poet" is given to makers of music and metre though all art is making.
- Against Aristophanes' story that lovers seek their "other half", she says they seek neither half nor whole "unless the half or the whole be also a good". Men would cut off their own diseased hands and feet. "There is nothing which men love but the good."
- Love is therefore "the love of the everlasting possession of the good". Since mortals can only approach this through generation, love is also love of immortality.

## Birth in beauty: why love aims at immortality

- All people are "pregnant" in body and soul and want to give birth, but only in beauty. Beauty is like the goddess of childbirth: near it the conceiving power is "propitious, and diffusive, and benign"; near ugliness it "frowns and contracts".
- Love is not of the beautiful itself but "of generation and of birth in beauty", because generation is the mortal creature's share of eternity.
- Even one person is never the same: hair, flesh, bones and blood change, and so do habits, opinions, desires, pleasures, pains and fears. Knowledge too is lost and renewed by "recollection". Mortals are preserved by *succession*, leaving a new thing in place of the old.
- This explains why animals will starve and fight to the death for their young.
- It also explains ambition. Men risk more for fame than for children. Alcestis, Achilles and Codrus would not have died without hope of "immortal" memory of virtue, and "the better they are the more they do them".
- Souls "pregnant" more than bodies conceive wisdom and virtue. The greatest, fairest wisdom "is that which is concerned with the ordering of states and families, and which is called temperance and justice".
- Such a person seeks a beautiful, noble, well-nurtured soul, talks to him of virtue and tries to educate him. Their shared "children" are fairer and more immortal than bodily ones, which is why Homer, Hesiod, Lycurgus and Solon are honoured for their offspring.

## The ladder of love (the "greater mysteries")

Diotima describes the order of going aright, guided by an instructor:

1. Begin in youth by loving one beautiful body and "create fair thoughts" out of it.
2. See that the beauty of one body is akin to that of all, and so abate the "violent love of the one".
3. Value beauty of soul above beauty of body, so that a virtuous soul with little comeliness is still worth loving and tending, and bring forth thoughts that "improve the young".
4. Contemplate the beauty of institutions and laws.
5. Move on to the sciences, no longer a slave "narrow-minded" to one youth or man or institution, but looking on "the vast sea of beauty".
6. Suddenly see the beautiful itself.

This beauty is:
- everlasting, neither growing nor decaying
- not fair in one respect and foul in another, nor fair to some and foul to others
- not found in a face, hands, speech, knowledge or any particular being
- "absolute, separate, simple, and everlasting", imparted to all changing beautiful things without diminishing or changing

The rungs are "steps only": from one to two, to all fair forms, to fair practices, to fair notions, to the notion of absolute beauty, until one "knows what the essence of beauty is".

## The best life and why Socrates endorses love

- Diotima: the best life is "the contemplation of beauty absolute". Gold, fine clothes and beautiful youths, who now "entrance" Socrates, are nothing beside it.
- Seeing true beauty "with the eye of the mind", one brings forth "not images of beauty, but realities", and so "true virtue". That makes one "the friend of God and... immortal, if mortal man may". She asks: "Would that be an ignoble life?"
- Socrates says he is persuaded and tries to persuade others that "human nature will not easily find a helper better than love". Every man should honour Love "as I myself honour him, and walk in his ways, and exhort others to do the same".

## Socrates as seen by Alcibiades: his temperance, endurance and effect on others

These points concern Socrates' conduct, as shown by Alcibiades' account, which Socrates invites with "speak the truth" (he allows it to be corrected if false).

- On Alcibiades' offer to give himself in exchange for Socrates' help toward virtue, Socrates answers ironically that Alcibiades would be trading "appearance" for "true beauty", like Diomede swapping gold for brass. He says, "The mind begins to grow critical when the bodily eye fails". They shared a bed and nothing happened; Alcibiades rose "as from the couch of a father or an elder brother."
- Alcibiades reports that Socrates' words alone stir the soul more than the best orators. They make him feel shame and admit that he should not neglect his soul "busying myself with the concerns of the Athenians". He adds that Socrates is "the only person who ever made me ashamed".
- Alcibiades reports that Socrates scorns wealth, beauty and honour, drinks without ever being drunk, and marches barefoot on ice. At Potidaea he stood in thought from dawn through the next morning, and he saved Alcibiades' life and arms in battle.
- Alcibiades also says Socrates' plain talk of "pack-asses and smiths and cobblers and curriers" is like a Silenus statue that opens to reveal "divine and golden images"; the words contain "the whole duty of a good and honourable man". He says Socrates, who began as a lover, ends by being courted by the young.
- At dawn Socrates is shown arguing that the genius of comedy and tragedy is one, and that the true tragic artist is also a comic artist.
