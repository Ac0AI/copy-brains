## The allegory of the cave: what the "unenlightened" nature looks like

Socrates offers an image of "how far our nature is enlightened or unenlightened."
- Prisoners sit chained in an underground den from childhood. They can only face forward and cannot turn their heads.
- A fire burns behind them. Between the fire and the prisoners runs a raised way with a low wall, "like the screen which marionette players have," over which men carry statues and figures of animals made of wood and stone.
- The prisoners see only the shadows these objects cast on the wall. If they could talk, they would take the names they use to refer to the real things. An echo would make them think the passing shadows were the ones speaking.
- Socrates concludes: "to them, the truth would be literally nothing but the shadows of the images." He says plainly that they are "like ourselves."
- Prisoners honour whoever is quickest at spotting which shadow comes before or after another and best at predicting what follows. Socrates treats this "wisdom of the den" as cleverness about appearances.

## The painful ascent: why truth is hard to see and hard to accept

Socrates describes what happens when a prisoner is freed and turned toward the light.
- At first he suffers sharp pains and glare. He cannot make out the realities whose shadows he knew, and he would think the shadows "far truer" than the objects now shown to him.
- If forced to look straight at the light, he turns back to what he can see. If dragged up a steep, rugged ascent into the sun, he is "pained and irritated" and dazzled.
- Sight must be acquired by habit, in stages: shadows first, then reflections in water, then the objects themselves, then the moon and stars by night, and last the sun "in his own proper place," as it is.
- The freed man would reason that the sun gives the seasons and years and is guardian of the visible world. He would pity his former companions and refuse their honours. He would say with Homer that it is better "to be the poor servant of a poor master, and to endure anything, rather than think as they do and live after their manner."
- Returning to the den, his eyes are full of darkness. He looks ridiculous in contests of measuring shadows. The prisoners say it is better not to ascend, and that anyone who tries to free and lead them up should be put to death.

## The idea of the good, and how to read the allegory

Socrates gives his interpretation, "whether rightly or wrongly God knows."
- The prison is the world of sight, the fire is the sun, and the ascent is "the ascent of the soul into the intellectual world."
- In the world of knowledge the idea of the good "appears last of all, and is seen only with an effort." Once seen, it is inferred to be "the universal author of all things beautiful and right," source of light in the visible world and of reason and truth in the intellectual.
- Anyone who would act rationally, "either in public or private life," must keep his eye fixed on it.
- Those who reach this vision are unwilling to descend to human affairs, which is natural. Someone coming from contemplation into a courtroom, arguing about "the images or the shadows of images of justice" before people who have never seen absolute justice, will blink and look foolish.
- Bewilderment of the eyes has two causes: coming out of the light and going into it. A sensible person asks which has happened before laughing. If he must laugh, there is more reason to laugh at the soul rising into the light than at the one returning from it.

## Education as turning the soul, not filling it

Socrates rejects the claim of "certain professors of education" that they can "put a knowledge into the soul which was not there before, like sight into blind eyes."
- The power and capacity to learn already exist in the soul. Just as the eye cannot turn from dark to light without the whole body, the instrument of knowledge turns toward being only by "the movement of the whole soul."
- Education is therefore an art of conversion: it does not implant sight but turns a faculty that is "looking away from the truth" in the right direction, and it must do so in the easiest and quickest way.
- Other virtues resemble bodily qualities and can be implanted later by habit and exercise. The virtue of wisdom contains "a divine element which always remains," made useful or harmful by which way it faces.
- The "clever rogue" shows this. His keen intelligence is "the reverse of blind," but it is forced into the service of evil, so he is "mischievous in proportion to his cleverness."
- If such natures were released in youth from the pleasures of eating and drinking, "like leaden weights" dragging the soul down, the same faculty would see truth as keenly.
- Turning the soul is "not the turning over of an oyster-shell." It is the passage from a day "little better than night" to the true day of being.

## Why philosophers must rule, and must be made to descend

Socrates argues that neither the uneducated nor the endlessly educated make able ministers of state. The first have "no single aim of duty" guiding their actions. The second will not act unless compelled, thinking they already dwell "in the islands of the blest."
- The founders' task is to compel the best minds to ascend to the good, and then to make them go back down among the prisoners "and partake of their labours and honours, whether they are worth having or not."
- To Glaucon's objection that this is unjust, Socrates replies that the lawgiver aimed at the happiness of the whole city, not of one class. He binds citizens by persuasion and necessity, making them benefactors of one another.
- He tells the philosophers they have been educated better than others "to be rulers of the hive." Each must go down in turn and get "the habit of seeing in the dark," after which they will see "ten thousand times better" because they know the beautiful, just and good in their truth.
- Key political claim: "the State in which the rulers are most reluctant to govern is always the best and most quietly governed, and the State in which they are most eager, the worst."
- Rulers must have a better life than ruling to look forward to. Only those "rich, not in silver and gold, but in virtue and wisdom" will rule well. Men who come to office "poor and hungering after their own private advantage" fight over it and ruin themselves and the state.
- "The only life which looks down upon the life of political ambition is that of true philosophy." Those who govern should not be lovers of the task, or "there will be rival lovers, and they will fight."

## The curriculum: arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, harmony

Socrates looks for studies that draw the soul "from becoming to being." Gymnastic concerns generation and decay, and music built habit through harmony and rhythm but gave no science. The useful trades are "mean." What remains is what all arts share: number and calculation.
- **What provokes thought:** Sense objects that give a clear answer, like a finger being a finger, do not invite thought. Those where the senses report opposites at once (large and small, hard and soft, light and heavy) perplex the soul, which then summons calculation and intelligence and asks "What is great?" and "What is small?" This is where the visible and the intelligible separate.
- **Arithmetic:** Unity appears as both one and a multitude, which stirs the question "What is absolute unity?" Arithmetic has a double use: the soldier needs it to array troops, and the philosopher needs it to "rise out of the sea of change and lay hold of true being." It must be studied "not like merchants or retail-traders" but "in the spirit of a philosopher, and not of a shopkeeper," until the nature of numbers is seen "with the mind only." Mathematicians refuse to divide absolute unity, because they speak of numbers "realised in thought." Even dull students become quicker for it, and it is hard to find a more difficult study.
- **Geometry:** Beyond the little needed for war, its value is as knowledge "of the eternal, and not of aught perishing and transient." Practitioners wrongly talk of "squaring and extending and applying." Socrates notes that solid geometry is neglected because no government patronises it and students lack a director; with state backing, disciples and discoveries would follow.
- **Astronomy:** Socrates denies that gazing at the sky makes the soul look up. Whoever studies "the fretted ceiling" or seeks particulars of sense is looking downward, "whether he floats, or only lies on his back." The visible heavens should serve as a pattern, like skilled work by Daedalus: a geometer would admire it but never look there for "the true equal or the true double." So "employ problems, and let the heavens alone."
- **Harmony:** Musicians who put their ears to strings and compare audible notes labour in vain. Even the Pythagoreans fall short, because they "never attain to problems," never asking why some numbers are harmonious and others not. This is useful only "if sought after with a view to the beautiful and good."
- All these studies have value only when brought into "inter-communion" and seen in their mutual affinities. They are "but the prelude" to dialectic.

## Dialectic as the coping-stone, and who may study it, and when

Dialectic is the "hymn" for which the sciences are the prelude. Socrates says "I have hardly ever known a mathematician who was capable of reasoning," and that men unable "to give and take a reason" cannot have the needed knowledge.
- **What dialectic does:** It proceeds by reason alone, without the senses, until pure intelligence perceives the absolute good, ending the intellectual journey as seeing the sun ends the visible one. It alone goes "directly to the first principle" and "does away with hypotheses." Geometry and similar sciences only "dream about being," since they leave their hypotheses unexamined. If a man "knows not his own first principle," his conclusions are built "out of he knows not what."
- **Four divisions of knowing:** science, understanding, belief, and perception of shadows. The first two are intellect, concerned with being; the last two are opinion, concerned with becoming. "As being is to becoming, so is pure intellect to opinion."
- **The test of knowing the good:** Someone who cannot define the idea of good and "run the gauntlet of all objections," disproving them "not by appeals to opinion, but to absolute truth, never faltering at any step," knows neither the good nor any good. He apprehends a shadow and is "dreaming and slumbering" through life. Rulers must not be "like posts, having no reason in them," so they need training in "asking and answering questions."
- **Who qualifies:** Natures that are steady, brave, quick, with good memory, and "an unwearied" lover of labour. Industry must not be "lame": a man who loves exercise and hunting but hates learning, or one who hates deliberate lies yet tolerates ignorance "like a swinish beast in the mire," is half-sound. Philosophy fell into disrepute because such "bastards" take it up instead of true sons.
- **Method for the young:** Start in childhood, but "a freeman ought not to be a slave in the acquisition of knowledge." Knowledge gained under compulsion "obtains no hold on the mind," so early education should be "a sort of amusement" that reveals each child's natural bent. Solon was wrong that the old can learn much; "youth is the time for any extraordinary toil."
- **The staged path:**
  - Around age 20: those selected see the sciences brought together and their kinship with true being. "The comprehensive mind is always the dialectical."
  - Age 30: a further selection, tested by dialectic.
  - Five years of philosophy, then sent back down into the den to hold military or other offices and be tested by temptation.
  - Fifteen years of that, until age 50.
  - At 50, the survivors who excelled in every action and branch of knowledge finally behold the absolute good and use it as the pattern for ordering the state and their lives. They rule when their turn comes "simply as a matter of duty," and when they have trained successors they depart to the Islands of the Blest.
  - Women with the same natures share this life.

## The danger of dialectic taken up too early

Socrates warns that dialectic has introduced "lawlessness" among its students.
- His image is a supposititious son raised in wealth among flatterers. He honours his supposed parents until he learns they are not his real ones; once he cannot find his true parents, he turns to the flatterers.
- Likewise, someone raised on inherited maxims of justice and honour is questioned until arguments leave him believing "nothing is honourable any more than dishonourable, or just and good any more than the reverse." Having lost the old beliefs and not yet found true ones, he drifts to the life that flatters his desires and becomes a lawbreaker. Socrates calls this natural, "most excusable," and "pitiable."
- Youngsters who first taste argument "argue for amusement," contradict everyone, and "like puppy-dogs, they rejoice in pulling and tearing at all who come near them." They end up believing nothing and give philosophy a bad name.
- Older men do not fall into this. They imitate "the dialectician who is seeking for truth, and not the eristic, who is contradicting for the sake of amusement."
- Hence the care: only orderly, steadfast people, not "any chance aspirant or intruder," and only at age 30 or later after careful selection.
