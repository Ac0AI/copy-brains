## Garry's framing: the Semmelweis reflex and the cost of changing the world

- Garry opens with Ignaz Semmelweis, the first doctor to find that handwashing saved mothers' lives, as a warning about what happens to people who make something new.
- He names the "Semmelweis reflex": people reject anything that challenges them out of hand. His position is that anyone building something that "will push forward humanity and break the existing status quo will face incredible pressure."
- "Great change comes at a great cost... it doesn't come for free." Breakthroughs mishandled "have the potential to break us."
- His answer is that true change takes perseverance, fundamental focus, and then communicating the breakthrough.
- He lists five mistakes Semmelweis made so founders can avoid them (and the asylum). Garry made the video in about 3 hours, aiming for one video a week to help future founders and makers reach the next stage.

## The Semmelweis case: a breakthrough that was not adopted

- Semmelweis was born in 1818 in the Kingdom of Hungary and worked at a Viennese hospital where maternal mortality ran as high as 18%. Women begged not to be admitted.
- At 29 he required handwashing with chlorinated lime between autopsy work and patient examinations. The death rate went from 18.3% in April 1847 to 2.2% in June and to zero in August of that year.
- The theory was almost completely rejected. Doctors believed in miasma theory (bad air), and many felt that being gentlemen meant their hands could not be unclean. Many also misread him as only saying the fever was contagious, missing the point that organic dead tissue transmitted disease.
- He died in an asylum at 47 (1865), two weeks after being subdued by guards when he tried to escape, from a wound on his hand. Germ theory took about 20 more years to be widely accepted.

## Lesson 1: Speak for yourself

- Semmelweis never published on his own or directly addressed the confusion in the journals. His students wrote the lectures and papers for him.
- Garry's rule: "Don't expect others to speak for you." Data cannot speak for itself; "without you, the data is mute."
- In the 1800s you had letters and journals. Today things move overnight.
- Tactic: practice writing and social media now, and get in the habit of communicating with strangers on the internet. At critical moments later in your career, that skill will matter.

## Lesson 2: Persevere and don't ragequit

- Semmelweis's boss, Chief Resident Johann Klein, did not want to keep him on for political reasons. He fought a little, then quit one day and never came back.
- Garry admits this has happened to him in his own career. Things get heated, plans go wrong, promises are not honored.
- His guidance: take a breath, think about the people around you and what you actually want from the situation. He has "yet to meet a person who has ever told me they wish they were more rash when making a hard decision, like where to work."
- You still have to decide, but "feel the emotions, but be sure it's the rational part of you doing it."

## Lesson 3: Speak truth when you know you're right

- At 29, unestablished, Semmelweis was not taken seriously by heads of maternity hospitals and professors. They were also structurally wrong: no scientific understanding, and a prevailing belief in miasma.
- Garry's lesson: "the establishment is often absolutely wrong." Continuing to say what you know to be right is "an act of courage."
- The condition: you must have the data and the conviction to back it up. "Just because people say things are so does not make it so."

## Lesson 4: When they go low, we go high

- By 1861 Semmelweis was writing letters full of bitterness and desperation, and contemporaries concluded he was losing his grip on reality.
- Garry's rule: no matter how wrong your haters are, don't stoop to their level. Quoting Michelle Obama: "When they go low, we go high."
- How you address disagreement matters. The road is long and hard, but if you resort to the bad or worse arguments of your adversary, "nobody wins, especially not you."

## Lesson 5: The breakthrough is only half the work

- Garry contrasts Semmelweis with Louis Pasteur, who overcame the establishment with data and engaged the debate at the right level so he could change minds.
- His bottom line: "The breakthrough is only half of the work. The other half is spreading it to the rest of the world."
- Innovations must be spread and communicated effectively to have any impact on the world.
