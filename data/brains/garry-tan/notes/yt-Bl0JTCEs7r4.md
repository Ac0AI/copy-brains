## Garry's Cultural Politics: Why He Fights for San Francisco

- Garry says Bay Area tech and local government are tied together. He grew up in Fremont, and SF is "a little bit of a one industry town."
- When critics say "get out techies," he calls it "actually a call to, you know, let's destroy the city."
- He points to Oakland as the warning: it lost its sports teams and is "one of the most dangerous places in the country now."
- His stance: "I'm not going to Austin. I'm not like leaving the city. There's no place to run to. So we just said we're not going to leave. We're going to stand and fight."

## How He Got Into Local SF Politics

- The trigger was a COVID-era Clubhouse room where then-DA Chesa Boudin said crime was down while Garry saw Asian-American elders being attacked. Asian-Americans are about 35-40% of SF.
- He cites Hanako Abe's death (New Year's Eve 2020, a repeat offender arrested about 30 times) as the moment accountability "seemed like there was going to be no accountability."
- Nancy Tung, who ran against Boudin, offered Clubhouse listeners a walk through SF politics. Garry took it, and she became "a really big mentor."
- He learned the city by following people deep in the weeds on Twitter/X: nonprofit operators like Stop Crime SF and people who read city budgets. He argues that if you search actively rather than trust the feed, "it's all available to see." Then he met them in person, "like an internet meetup."
- The second issue was education. He says SF eighth graders found it hard to take algebra. His own path (food-insecure at times, public school, calculus, Stanford, computer engineering) came from a good public education, so he refuses to bring thousands of families to SF to start companies while their kids can't get algebra.
- He frames the failures as over-equity driven by "pathological empathy" that destroys safety and education.

## Why Small Local Money Works

- Garry says people pour millions into national races while "very small amounts of money" in local politics move things much more.
- He gave "a couple hundred grand" total and says that is "a relatively small in the global sense amount of money" for the effect.
- He credits a shift in the Overton window: he started saying things people feared would get them fired (e.g. that violence against elders needs accountability), nothing happened to him, and others privately agreed.
- Results he cites:
  - Boudin recalled; Brooke Jenkins is now DA.
  - Three school board members recalled; algebra "coming back a little bit too slowly."
  - A moderate, pro-business, pro-public-safety Board of Supervisors, "for the moment."
  - Nancy Tung now heads the SF Democratic Party.
- His 10-year goal: "San Francisco Democrat" should mean something different. "You can be in tech, you can be prosperity, you can create abundance, but then also you can make it work for everyone in your community."
- He backs paying taxes if the money buys safety and schools, and wants to build housing ("rent should not be $10,000 a month for a one-bedroom").
- He does not want office: being an elected official "seems terrible"; he is "pretty focused on money and media," with politics maybe 10-20 years out.

## Building Community as a Strategy

- Garry bought a converted 1902 church (former Church of Christ, Scientist) in the Mission as an "event house." He found it through Redfin, got roughly $1.5M off after it sat about 2 years with no offers, and uses it as a salon.
- It seats about 50 for dinner and holds about 150. Guests have included Pat Gelsinger, Peter Thiel, London Breed and Scott Wiener.
- His reasoning: after COVID, "a lot of my job is just bringing people together."
- Uses: political events, YC events, founder dinners. He personally funds about 50 companies a year, and YC partners also invest.
- On the internet vs. in person: "when you sit down for a drink with someone and you get to know someone, it just hits different." He wants tech to get involved in local community: neighbors, churches, Chinatown businesses and activists.
- He belongs to the Lion's Den karaoke bar in Chinatown (membership placard reads "Twitter Menace") and says politicians, local business people and activists mix there.

## Handling Backlash and Public-Figure Noise

- Sauna incident: founders at a dinner asked to jump in his sauna and took a selfie; it spun into rumors. He suspects many accusers were rejected from YC repeatedly: "something breaks in people's brains." His rule now: "no haters in the sauna and no selfies," and no deal talk there.
- Tupac karaoke incident: he says he sang "Hit 'Em Up" at a dads' karaoke night, politics had him fired up, he changed a lyric, and people "didn't get the joke." He apologized the next morning, said "that's just not me," and noted the climate of political violence.
- Rule he now follows: delete Twitter from his phone after a couple of drinks. He expects phones to eventually block this for us ("Do not rage tweet").

## Handling Rejection at YC

- Garry says YC rejects 99.3% of applicants. Rejection emails go out and he gets angry tweets and emails.
- Average accepted founder in the latest batch applied at least twice, and more of them were rejected multiple times than accepted on the first try.
- He treats it as a resilience test: "the first step to getting into YC is getting rejected from YC." Founders will face rejection from users, investors, hires, Hacker News and reviewers: "everyone's going to hate you."
- Some founders have "never failed in their entire lives" and then "get punched in the face with users."
- Joke he floated: make accepted founders hang from a fixed bar for 60 seconds, then a mental-toughness seminar if they can't. He says startups are "almost no better exercise or test of endurance."
- On errors: YC is human and misses both ways, rejecting winners and admitting weaker teams. Unicorn rate is "only 6 to 12%."

## YC Strategy: One Team, No Growth Fund

- Garry's path: designer in residence and partner at YC, left to found Initialized with Harj Taggar and Alexis Ohanian (he credits Harj as hiring him at YC), then returned as YC CEO after Paul Graham and Jessica Livingston tracked him down. Brett Gibson now runs Initialized.
- He says about 90% of Initialized's wins came from YC and credits the YC ecosystem for its successes (e.g. Rippling, Flock Safety).
- The old YC Continuity growth fund is gone. His reasoning: with about 7-9% ownership in each company, taking pro rata means YC never misses the decacorns, and the power law means most return is in decacorns (Stripe, DoorDash, Coinbase, Airbnb), not just unicorns.
- Airbnb example: YC invested about $12-15K for 7%, returned $2B at IPO, and would have returned about $7B with pro rata for a couple hundred million more.
- A good YC return is "8x." The old structure couldn't do pro rata because it hadn't raised the money.
- Now: one team of 15 equal general partners, one partner per company for life ("people should think about YC as the defining equal partnership in all of venture"). Eliminating the separate team removes the extra relationships and structural friction.
- Message to founders: YC owns about 7% of you, so "make us earn it. Come back. Do office hours with us." He gives founders his cell number.
- YC also wants to be a friend to other investors: "We're out of competing with you. We just want to take our pro rata."

## The Future of YC and Venture

- Garry's model for YC is Airbnb-style: a two-sided marketplace where demand (the batch) is huge and supply (great early-stage investors, the "superhosts") is the limiting reagent.
- He wants YC to grow because "venture can and should be 10 times bigger." Batch size grew mostly because acceptance went from about 2-2.5% to about 7%, and AI made starting easier.
- Outcome scale: when he started Initialized fund one, Dropbox at about $4B (2011) looked like the ceiling; he now says $50B and $100B are on the table. He wants YC to create many future Elon Musks (and "$1.5 trillion" outcomes).
- On capital: "it's not clear to me capital is going to be a bludgeon anymore." More value will go to "discernment, taste, ability to help."
- Advice to VCs: do early stage and "show up," fund people "before it's obvious," and club together less. He criticizes the 2018-style playbook of one firm leading and friends following as a lock-in.
- He doesn't want seven trillion-dollar companies: "we should have thousands of companies worth billions to tens of billions of dollars," and says AI makes that moment possible.
- He told Michael he likes that Michael is a consumer founder turned investor and builds an audience, calling that the smartest use of his background.

## Faith, Meaning and the AI Moment

- Garry is a converted Christian who grew up atheist (dad Buddhist, mom Christian) and had a rough childhood. The turning point in his mid-20s was a Rumi poem about the wise man and the snake: God lets us struggle with evil and difficulty so we grow.
- He church-hopped in his 20s (Korean church faith healing, Pentecostal tongues). His route to faith was worship music more than intellect.
- He sits on the board of Acts 17, the nonprofit that brought Peter Thiel and Pat Gelsinger on stage; founders include Michelle Stevens (wife of Anduril's Trae Stevens). Aim: give people a chance to be "seeking" without being told this is the only way.
- He attends Epic Church in SF with his family; his kids like Sunday school. He says Epic roughly doubled attendance in a year.
- His explanation for tech's religious turn: people look for meaning at "the dawn of AGI," with "existential angst" after COVID. Bay Area faith is mostly non-denominational and Jesus-focused rather than "hellfire and brimstone."
- He likes Trae Stevens' idea of "good quests": work on things that "link us to our fellow man" and solve real problems rather than "help people click on ads."
