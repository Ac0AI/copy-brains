## What YC is, and what it's for

Garry describes YC as "Disneyland for transformation": a process that takes earnest, technical people and turns them into formidable founders. He frames it as a social process, since reality is "socially constructed." When he first showed up at Startup School, it felt like "a fish out of water jumping into water," surrounded by builders. The recent YC site redesign tried to write this down, using old photos of founders like the Collisons and the Airbnb founders when they were young. He says that in broad strokes YC has changed far less than it has stayed the same, and that this is by design: when you have a great product, you don't mess with it.

## Evaluating founders in the age of Claude Code

Garry says something shifted at the end of November 2025, when Opus 4.5 came out. He rebuilt his 2008 startup in roughly 90 hours over two weeks, about 70,000 lines of code, while holding a full-time job and raising kids. It was better than what five engineers and he had built over about two years, and he says he couldn't have done it with $5 million and five engineers back then.

- For the spring batch, YC applications let founders upload a Claude Code or Codex transcript of building a feature. YC runs these in a security sandbox, expecting prompt injection.
- He says you can tell whether someone can build from how they prompt: whether they use plan mode, think about systems, over-engineer or prematurely optimize, consider edge cases, and know what counts as a complete release.
- His principle: "How you do anything is how you do everything." He cites Steve Jobs on carpenters who look at the back of the cabinet, since only other craftspeople will inspect it.
- On Instacart's Apoorva Mehta: he built an iPhone demo himself, and the tell was that it scrolled very smoothly, which wasn't true of apps in that early App Store era.
- The question he asks of any founder: "would I go work for this person if I weren't doing the thing I'm doing now?"

## Agency, taste, and the quality bar

Garry argues the most important traits for any product, vertical or customer set are agency and taste.

- Agency: seeing a problem and believing you can solve it with technology.
- Taste: building the first version, getting it in people's hands, asking whether it worked and how it broke, then going deep into the weeds.
- He describes the "tireless, ceaseless gardener" needed to fix every bug across hundreds of thousands of lines of code. His model is the Bahá'í gardens in Haifa, where "not a single weed, not a leaf out of place" reflects people who care almost religiously.
- He says the bar keeps rising: every six months for the past three years, what founders demo at the product showcase a few weeks into the batch has gone up.
- YC shouldn't fund someone for going to Harvard. It should fund people who "actually understand the user and are unbelievably tenacious."
- AI widens the net more than it changes the criteria. He still wants to fund genius engineers, and expects more founders in the mold of Parker Conrad (non-traditional background, strong product and commercial thinker) to get through.

## Competition, pivoting, and speed of decisions

- Garry says YC spends most of its time telling founders not to worry about competition. He imagines the Lora founders not launching because they saw Harvey. The advice is to make something people want and out-execute, not build a market map first.
- Companies can now try many ideas within a batch. You can do two years of work, find nobody wants it, throw it out and start again, and the learning isn't wasted.
- On large companies, he describes the old cycle of meetings, docs and opinions over architecture taking weeks or months. Now you can go into plan mode, try both options, and have something in an hour. He says incumbents that need two years to make a decision "or never" are the ones at risk.
- His view on "is SaaS dead": he thinks it's dead, but any SaaS company can survive by embracing Claude Code and a top-to-bottom agentic view of how everything works.

## What's defensible, and where capital still matters

- He sees marketplaces that aggregate people (Airbnb, DoorDash) as safe from AI. Hard tech is protected because it is hard to source and make work.
- Systems of record that touch money or regulation, such as payroll, are sticky. Moats built on integrations or data connectors are brittle.
- Coding agents' recommendations are becoming a real moat. He jokes about writing API docs to prompt-inject Claude Code into using your product.
- He argues against "we won't need capital." If a competitor has 50 engineers, you wouldn't choose five, because human desire is effectively unlimited and AI is a reason to attempt more. He says "let's boil a few lakes."
- He notes capital works as a moat in markets like prediction markets, where regulatory change opens a green light, but it matters less for AI companies. He also notes sales, unlike support, is not automated yet.
- Capital can mislead: a company can have it and "move fast in the wrong direction," as with fine-tuning models that frontier models then beat.
- He says tiny teams can win, citing Giga (10 to under 20 people) beating incumbents.

## Working with investors and growing the founder base

- He says most investors are B or B+. Having one who simply doesn't mess with you is "really good," and you should take a great investor's money when you can get it. For everyone else, B+ capital is fine.
- YC needs more A and A+ investors. He expects most future top VCs to be YC alumni (naming Ilya Sukhar, Dan Levine, Liz of First Round).
- He says the bottleneck for YC now is great founders wanting to do startups, not follow-on capital.
- YC can't just exist as "something in the sky on the internet." The best founders know someone who did YC or met a partner in person. Recent actions:
  - Over 30 college campuses visited last year (led by Jared).
  - A trip to Europe, with India planned for the spring.
  - A push to reach grad students and founders in their mid to late 20s.
- YC Fellows gives grants and community to exceptional college students who aren't ready for a startup yet.
- Founders are trending younger. He thinks AI favors younger people, and that big tech stopped hiring. He rejects the "last chance to participate in capitalism" mindset as shortsighted.

## YC's structure and the mission

- Garry says YC now has about 30 partners including 15 visiting partners.
- Under his tenure each partner picks their own companies and runs their own autonomous "pod" of around 30 companies, roughly the size of YC when he went through it. That is why going from 2 to 4 batches a year, or doubling partners, needs no structural change.
- The mission he echoes from PG: the conventional VC view says only about 10 companies a year will matter, and YC's answer was "let's make more of them." Everything is driven by wanting that number to go up.
- He says YC is a "managed marketplace" that wants to back every type of company. He cites Coinbase, where Brian Armstrong was an anti-fraud engineer at Airbnb, and notes early on it matters more who the person is and what they believe than the idea.
- His stated directive from the board: make sure the partners are having fun.

## Little tech, building, and politics

- He argues capital and management must take responsibility for workers' fears about AI, and that the answer is not shaving 1 to 2% off margins each year but imagining radically better products.
- He says it's become more important than ever to be a founder ("management that becomes capital").
- He backs YC's "little tech" policy push (Luther Lowe in DC) so startups can train models and enter markets, arguing this is consistent with respecting big tech while keeping markets open.
- He cites *Breakneck* on the US having stopped building since about the 1960s and 70s and becoming a litigious culture, and supports Matt Mahan for California governor as someone who builds (more than 1,400 homes in San Jose, homelessness down 20%).
