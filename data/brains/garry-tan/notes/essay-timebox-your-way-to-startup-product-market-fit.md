## Why founders get stuck: too few tries

Garry's core idea is that a founder has a finite number of tries, and timeboxing each try lets you make more of them. He says founders mostly fail because they don't try enough things, and they don't try enough things because they keep repeating the same thing and won't drop a hypothesis that time has proven false.

- He defines a product-market hypothesis as two parts: a set of **people** you can reach, and a **problem** to solve for those specific people. One hypothesis is one possible try.
- He quotes Alcoholics Anonymous: "Insanity is doing the same thing over and over again, and expecting different results."
- He pushes back on the hustle idea of never giving up, quoting W. C. Fields: "If at first you don't succeed, try, try again. Then quit. It's no use being a damn fool about it."
- He says new insights are the hardest thing to come by. Ideas multiply execution, and good ideas are rare. A bad idea is a zero that cancels perfect execution.
- He sees very talented founders get stuck here. They have great engineering, product and marketing, yet it can't be sold, the numbers don't go up and there's no growth.

## The two failure cases, in order

Garry names two common failure cases and says the order matters:

- **A:** It's a real problem, but you can't get people to know about it and use it.
- **B:** It's not a real problem.

This ordering implies a specific system for startup need-finding. Test acquisition first, because failing to reach people looks the same as having no demand until you've tried enough channels.

## Stage one: getting users with many channel experiments

In office hours with very early-stage startups, most of the focus is on how to get the product in front of people. Garry cites Ben Horowitz: "there are no silver bullets, only lead ones."

- Channels to try:
  - content marketing
  - SEM
  - SEO
  - events
  - webinars
  - partnerships
  - street teams
  - Craigslist
  - stealing users from other platforms
- Other obvious ones he lists: enterprise sales, cold emails, searching for warm introductions, LinkedIn spelunking, and selling through existing platforms.
- His advice is to "get creative."
- The most common failure is getting stuck on one or two tactics and doing more of what doesn't work.
- What success looks like:
  1. Try one thing and it works.
  2. Double down and it works again.
  3. Keep going until you establish a new normal, a steady state much bigger than where you started.
  4. That is scalable growth, and it is product-market fit.

## Stage two: keeping users (retention and the pile of pilots)

Once you have an initial go-to-market, Garry says the real struggle begins: keeping the users.

- He has funded companies with incredible early traction followed by massive churn, and that still doesn't work.
- Another failure mode is pilots that don't expand. He calls this the **pile of pilots** problem, a well-known issue in digital health and biotech. You can get a pile of pilots that never expand.
- The consequence he gives: "you have a seed company that cannot raise at Series A."
- What's required is expansion and net retention. If you can't get them, he says this isn't something people want.

## What to do when users don't stick

Garry lists four moves, each of which counts as a whole new try:

1. **Improve the product.** Go directly to the people using it and ask, "Why aren't you using it?" Solve those problems and you get another shot.
2. **Change who you're solving the problem for.** Go back to the product-market hypothesis and change the people.
3. **Change the price point.**
4. **Pick a different problem to solve.** A different set of people has different needs, which you may be able to fill with better software.

## How to timebox a hypothesis

Garry's tool is to explicitly set how much time you'll spend on any given hypothesis, and then quit when it's up.

- You need enough time to know either way, and he describes the risk on both sides:
  - **Too short:** you may give up on something that could have worked.
  - **Too long:** you waste time you'll never get back, and burn critical runway in ways that hurt you fundamentally.
- When you timebox, you set aside a specific amount of time for this market and problem, then move on to the next lead bullet.
- His promise: if you're a great team doing the right things and you timebox, keep trying new things and keep "firing new lead bullets," "you can and will find product market fit."
- If it's not something people want, you have to do something else.
- Timeboxing is a way to be very conscious of how long you spend on any given problem.
