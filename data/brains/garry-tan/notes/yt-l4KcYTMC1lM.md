## Zero-based accounting: running YC like a founder

- Garry describes running YC with "zero-based accounting": if YC were started from scratch today, he'd keep only what he would add again. Brian Chesky (Airbnb) told him this, and Brian brought "founder mode" energy to YC's board meetings before the term existed.
- He treats YC as a "tree of prosperity" that needs pruning. It is hard to say no to things and people, because "culture is everything", and it comes down to who you fire, hire and promote.
- Brian's Airbnb experience was the warning. After product-market fit, boards push you to hire experts from other companies, until you wonder whether it is your company, the board's or the shareholders'. Covid was Brian's moment to reset Airbnb's culture.
- YC's 2010s expansion into side bets was followed by a return to the core. He says "never say never" about expanding again, and hopes to prune as the tree grows rather than cut back wild growth later.
- Early batches had 8 to 15 companies twice a year. YC now runs about 150 companies four times a year, and the market is still not captured.

## Who YC is for: the zero-sum and abundance mindsets

- Garry holds both: a zero-sum fight for founders "from central casting" (cracked, with the right resume), where YC competes with top VC and seed names and that's fine, and an abundance side that creates prosperity where there was none.
- The abundance side is Paul Graham's original insight: highly technical young founders are the most mispriced asset.
- Focus is hard, since "if you focus on all of them, are you really focusing on any of them?" YC is clearly focused on technical founders.
- He wants to dispel the idea that YC is only for people from certain companies or Harvard, Stanford and MIT. It is also for people from Cornell, UIUC and elsewhere.
- Brilliant, hard-working engineers get rejected from elite schools, which he calls unfair. YC is most valuable to people without a built-in brand and network, because they get "shot out of a rocket into Silicon Valley culture".
- The principle: the world needs "shelling points" for builders and talented people, where capital, customers, knowledge and the best friends of your life come together. He wants diversity and merit in the same place.
- His own example: at Stanford, being a fraternity brother of Joe Lonsdale and Stephan Cohen brought him in touch with Peter Thiel, which he says helped a child of immigrants from a poor family.

## Why YC's pick rate is so good, and how partners advise

- His explanation is "game recognizes game". Partners who are builders themselves get far more reps than anyone else.
- He tells 22-year-olds desperate to become VC associates not to do it: "just go start a company." He quotes Jay-Z: everybody wants to tell you how to do it, they never did it.
- Founders want to sit one-on-one with someone who has been there. Partners should not validate bad decisions. They give a view, as a "soft advisor", and say what they think you should do.
- Partners are not the boss. He recalls Paul Graham telling a batch founder to do whatever they wanted, since YC funds 100 companies, even if they wanted to drive off a cliff.
- Partners "don't know the path, we just know how not to die". There are a billion ways to die: losing a co-founder over dumb arguments, picking the wrong investor, bad first hires, and picking too many things to focus on. YC has seen 50 people die each way and can connect you with one of them.
- Median startup advice is useless because it's on five podcasts already. Valuable advice comes from people who have seen enough to give "spiky" advice, sometimes the worst advice. Good founders discard bad advice, and YC's meta-message was "don't take anybody's advice carte blanche, including ours".
- Of the bad advice Paul gave, the example he recalls is on naming.

## Lean vs fat startups, and compound startups

- On the lean vs fat debate: "if you can get Keith Rabois to invest in your startup, you should do a fat startup." The same goes for Vinod Khosla or Elad Gil. If they want you to go fat, you've been blessed.
- For almost everyone else, you can't get to a fat startup without doing it lean first.
- On compound startups (going multi-product very early): it's better to be as multi-product as early as you can if you can hire and build consistently. Ramp is a good example.
- The common thread is "no dogma". A systems-thinking founder shouldn't take rules from a podcast.
- In a blitz-scaling race, the constraint is how many companies can draw 150-IQ talent at 50, 100, 500 or 1,000 people. Parker Conrad (Rippling) can, with Matt MacInnis, acqui-hiring such people to take new product lines from zero to $10 million ARR. He thinks the compound startup is possible if you can do that, but "very few people are really capable of doing that".

## What a special founder looks like

- First archetype: commerciality. These founders sit with people who have a need and pull the requirements "right out of the customers". After a few meetings with product in market, the yanking turns into customers pushing, and "that's product-market fit."
- This is counterintuitive. Great founders are perceptive rather than forceful, like a great seller who is the best listener. The cargo cult says listen less, while he says listen 10 times more.
- Second: unusual people do unusual things early. Example: Serena Ge of Datacurve dropped out of Waterloo at about 19 or 20 and, at about 16, had built one of the top rock-climbing apps in the App Store. He describes such people as "an inch wide and a mile deep".
- Third: hacks. He looks for people who are "a little bit naughty" and have done unusual hacks.
- Fourth: systems thinking. Can you sit down with anyone and understand their motivations, how they get promoted, their ideology? To "program the world" you first reverse-engineer it. This predicts recruiting, selling and fundraising.

## AI and vertical software: moats, scale and team size

- Buyers in old industries (education, healthcare, legal) that struggled to adopt cloud are now hungry for AI. Competition is higher, since a good idea draws 19 teams.
- Language models let founders make claims that were not possible before. Example: a medical billing company claims that a 10 to 15 person clinic (dermatology, even veterinary) needs only clinicians plus one back-office person. People believe the demo, especially with five customers already running it.
- He expects "10,000 flowers" in vertical AI software, narrowing from about a thousand to a hundred over time, and the usual one-two-three market structure to hold.
- Unless AGI or ASI ends software, the old rules apply, including the moats: switching costs, proprietary data, brand, network effects.
- Whether to blitz-scale is each founder's call. There is emerging evidence you don't have to: you might reach $50 to $100 million ARR with under 20 people, as Linear is an example of. Founders now increasingly automate internally with their own agents instead of hiring large teams.
- When a nine-way race exists, he says to scramble up the wall as fast as possible.

## Hard tech and YC Demo Day as a shelling point

- His advice to any founder is to build a shelling point. YC's is Demo Day, which draws well over $1 billion of investor capital a year ("going on $1.2"), a number he wants to grow to $2 billion or $2.5 billion in 5 to 10 years. It isn't contractually captive, but it rises every year.
- Median YC startups raise about $1 to $1.5 million at Demo Day. Hard tech companies with real commercial validation or a technological breakthrough in the three or four months can raise $3, 5, 10 or even 20 million, enough to build a real hard tech company from YC alone.
- Examples of success: Solen, Astranis.
- Every YC partner does hard tech. YC is adding specialised programming, such as Andy Lapsa of Stoke Space running a hard tech mini-conference for the last batch.
- YC's value is less specific services than mindset and a cross-section of other builders, who can help in ways partners can't, since a general partner's last real engineering was years ago.
- Example of batch trust: when Garry did YC, Heroku's James Lindenbaum helped him fundraise. Nine months after his seed round, Heroku worried about hosting some of the biggest Rails sites, and asked Garry for his source code. Garry's one of those sites. He says you'd do that for a batchmate and no one else.
