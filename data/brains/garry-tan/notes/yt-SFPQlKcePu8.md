## Garry's 360 review: the "relies on others to say no" feedback

- Garry did a 360 degree review in April 2020. His exec coach interviewed the people he worked with most, one on one, then anonymized the feedback.
- A 360 is "an absolutely powerful tool for you to know how you're doing and what you could be doing better."
- The feedback that landed: "Gary relies on other people to say no for him. He feels things but does not articulate them until later. People can't immediately understand what he thinks or feels about a situation."
- He calls this a manager's red flag. He wasn't doing his job and wasn't being clear when he needed to be.
- Root cause: he was conflict avoidant and could not say no or even express a preference in the moment.
- A year-later 360 showed he had solved it: he no longer asks others to fight his battles, and the team is happy.

## Why Garry ends up debating both sides: modeling other people

- Garry often argues both sides of a decision. One side is his own viewpoint, shaped by experience (a go/no-go on a product, how much to pay for something).
- The other side is detailed mental models of what others want: customers, users, investors, co-workers. Their motivations and needs differ from his.
- He sees this as a strength. Building models of other people's behavior from their experience, not just his own, is a big part of what makes him good as a designer, VC and manager.
- The failure mode is when the internal argument isn't resolved. He then makes a poor leadership decision or leans on others to "interpret the tea leaves and say yes or no for me."
- His diagnosis: "I am not integrated enough." Other people's wants were so vivid to him that he sometimes lost his own perspective.

## The "many minds" idea from neuroscience

- Garry draws on Michael Gazzaniga's split-brain research, shown in Adam Curtis's documentary "Can't Get You Out of My Head."
- In a patient whose brain hemispheres were surgically disconnected to stop epilepsy, a second force kept taking control (for example, one hand picking clothes she didn't want).
- The argument: the human brain holds multiple selves, each taking control at different moments. The conscious verbal self makes up stories to rationalize what the others did.
- Garry's summary: "many minds decide and then our verbal centers rationalize." The unified self may be an illusion.
- His point for leaders: if you can't integrate the minds inside yourself, you can't integrate a team. Start at the individual level, then do it for the team.

## Kegan's five stages of adult development

- Garry uses Harvard researcher Robert Kegan's ladder as a map for how to grow:
  - Stage 1: impulse and reflex (infancy, early childhood).
  - Stage 2: sense of self ruled by one's own needs and wishes; others matter only if they support those.
  - Stage 3: self is socially determined, based on real or imagined expectations of others. Garry places this around our twenties.
  - Stage 4: self is determined by values you have authored yourself. Rarely achieved, and only in adulthood.
  - Stage 5: self is no longer bound to any one aspect of yourself or your history; you focus on the flow of life. Garry says it "sounds like nirvana."
- Moving past stage 3 requires asking whether you can transcend your own bodily needs and your desire for money, sex, fame and material goods.
- Garry's self-diagnosis: he was stuck in stage 3, a socially constructed self, which is why he couldn't state a preference.

## How hiring covered the gap

- Knowing deep down he'd struggle with saying no, Garry hired well and set up values the team could work toward even when he couldn't voice a preference himself.
- He credits the strength of that team for getting him through a very hard time.
- His caveat: "relying on the team to say things that I needed to say myself, that's always going to be worse than doing it yourself."

## Garry's method: notice the conflict, then inflect into it

- Through coaching and therapy, he learned to notice when internal conflicts are happening.
- His signal is physiological: faster breath and heart rate, a hot face.
- At that moment he pauses and takes stock of the many minds. Giving a voice to each part lets him see what he actually wants, so he can express a preference when before he couldn't.
- He cites Ray Dalio on meditation as a key to success: slowing things down so you can act calmly in chaos, "just like a ninja in a street fight."
- Chaos and conflict are a given. Separating what you truly want from your modeling of others is how you reach Kegan stage 4, "a life that is truly authored by you."
- Payoff for teams: leaders who are integrated and aware at the individual level can integrate their team into "a powerful resolute super organism" that builds products bigger than any one person. "Knowing is half the battle."
