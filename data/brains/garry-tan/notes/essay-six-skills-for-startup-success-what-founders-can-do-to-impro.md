## Skills are leverage, and leverage attracts people

Garry lists six skills a future founder can build before starting: engineering, product, design, sales and marketing, finance, and management and leadership. He says the more of them a founder has, the likelier the startup is to succeed. He stresses that none of them is required: "Starting a start-up isn't deterministic like that." Some people with few of these skills succeed, and some with all of them fail. The skills make the risk "a more calculated risk."

## Why breadth attracts cofounders and employees

- People want to join a rocket ship taking off and avoid anything that will fail without them. Founders therefore "repel people when we need them the most" and attract them when they have other options.
- The more of the six skills a founder has, the more high-quality cofounders and employees they attract.
- His rule: be deep in one or two areas and "broad enough to be dangerous" in the rest. Breadth lets a founder tell who is actually good in each function and work with the best people in it.
- Early in a career, find which skills you are particularly good at and enjoy, but still learn the others. Strength in your own area makes you complementary to the rest of the team.

## Engineering: technology as the ultimate moat

- Garry says technology "remains the key leverage in this world. It's the ultimate moat." His example is PageRank, which protects something worth billions because nobody can walk in and take it without building it first.
- A computer science degree isn't required but helps. Top CS schools remain the gold standard.
- For a non-technical founder looking for a technical cofounder, he advises learning enough to be dangerous. Most people searching for technical cofounders don't try to understand the craft. A founder who builds a good but not perfect first version stands out from that sea of non-technical applicants and gets the ball rolling.
- Ways to learn online: Codecademy, Insight Fellows (for scientists moving into data science and data engineering), and Career Karma, a YC startup that helps people find the right bootcamp or decide whether the bootcamp path fits.

## Product: a continuous process, learned by shipping

- Product is knowing what to build and for whom. Garry cites Suhail Doshi: it is running the process "on a machine to continually find and refine product market fit. This isn't a one time thing."
- There is no obvious route to becoming a good product person except practicing. His top recommendation is side projects.
- The practice loop: build something, release it to users, and see whether they stick. Then talk to users about why they did or didn't take to it: did it solve their problem, and what were their hangups?

## Design: interaction design, visual design and taste

- Design has two parts: interaction design (UX, how it works) and visual design (how it looks).
- Visual design can be cultivated like drawing or painting. Doing it yourself isn't required but gives faster iteration, and it helps most for consumer products.
- Taste matters more than execution. It means knowing whether a design fits the aesthetic and mood, speaks to the customer, and conveys the right level of quality. Garry says it is important particularly for consumer, "but these days, for all businesses."
- He calls interaction design "the meat of design." It is closest to product and moves from who it's for and what problem it solves to how. Questions it answers:
  - Where do the buttons go and what do they say?
  - What copy around the pages gets people through something complicated?
  - How is a complex multi-step process broken into manageable pieces?
  - How does the user know where they are and where they want to go?
- Recommended learning: Steve Krug's *Don't Make Me Think*, and his own two-hour Y Combinator Startup School talk on YouTube.

## Sales, marketing and brand

- Garry quotes Ryan Peterson of Flexport: "Sales is the most underrated profession."
- He cites cofounder Alexis Ohanian: everybody is a brand, and a brand's ability to capture attention has never mattered more.
- Brand is important because "every business today is a meme," and it has never been easier for a startup to reach exactly the people who have the problem.
- Being able to sell and get in front of people is "super key."
- Engineering, product, sales and marketing are the key to getting product market fit.

## Finance: essential once you go from zero to one

- Finance isn't very important at the outset, but financial literacy becomes "absolutely essential" after zero to one.
- He cites Matt MacInnis of Rippling: finance helps you figure out how to grow repeatedly and how well you're doing. "Every business in the world is either growing or dying," and without metrics you can't tell which.
- Finance and budgeting make sense of decisions such as:
  - Should you hire that executive and let them build a team?
  - Should you invest in that new office?
  - When do you get paid back, and does it make economic sense?

## Management and leadership

- Leadership is getting people to do things, sometimes without authority, first "by example." Garry rejects the idea that leadership is being stern. He quotes Harvard professor Barbara Kellerman: "Followers are more important to leaders than leaders are to followers."
- Leading from empathy builds a more connected team that can weather more storms and make better decisions together.
- Beyond empathy, leaders must hold people accountable. That means crucial, hard conversations about what went wrong, as constructive as possible, while making clear that the bad thing can't continue.
- How to practice: in school, take leadership roles that run programs and coordinate people (a sport, a club). At work, take on projects that require coordination with many people and drive toward a numerical outcome, such as increasing sales by X percent or creating a set amount of value.
- Recommended book: *How to Win Friends and Influence People*. Despite the cheesy title, he says it applies skills needed across the whole gamut, so reading it improves leadership and the other skills too.

## Teams cover what one person cannot

- Getting good at even one or two of these skills takes a lifetime or two. One person can realistically be great at only one or two.
- A startup that matters needs a team that is great at all of them. Putting lifetimes of experience in one room is the process of building a startup team.
- Founders are "the speck of dust that forms the crystalline structure that actually solves a real problem," which is why teams are crucial.
- Having all the right skills is still no guarantee of finding the right idea or building the right team.
