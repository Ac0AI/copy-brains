## Landing page first impression: say who it is for and what it does

- Garry says a startup's first job on its homepage is to answer two things fast: "what is this" and "is it for me".
- He calls this a hierarchy, like Maslow's: if a visitor can't tell who the product is for, nothing lower on the page helps.
- He warns against clever taglines: "You don't want to be clever if clever equals confusing."
- On Rosebud ("AI generated game assets", "game development", "GPT to game"), he is distracted by three concepts at once. He notes founders often like taglines they find clever because they know the product, which makes it harder for new visitors.
- On Reality Defender, he is halfway down the page and still can't tell what the product does. He says most visitors won't try that hard.
- He wants a visitor to look at the page and think "that's for me", which means writing the page for one specific customer (the ICP), not "anyone who might need our technology". In his words, being too general is "a little bit problematic".

## Use your customers' own words for the headline

- Garry's tactic for a vague homepage: talk to existing users and ask "how would you describe my product?"
- Then take the exact phrase they use and put it at the top of the site.
- His reasoning: the phrase that resonates with existing users is likely to resonate with similar potential customers.

## One opinionated call to action, with contrast

- Garry recommends deciding the main KPI and the outcome you want from people who hit the site. He guesses it is not "explore all of these products and pick the one you like".
- He uses Rosebud as the example. It shows four or more products (asset generator, game dev platform, skyboxes, talking heads), and he suggests focusing on the primary one (likely game assets) and moving the others to secondary pages.
- Be opinionated with the buttons: make "try beta" the main button and turn "learn more" into a plain link instead of an equal-weight button.
- "One of the most important things to use is contrast" to tell the user what to do.
- Rosebud's "try beta" matters because it signals the product isn't vaporware.

## Show the product and a feat of strength

- For AI products, Garry wants an animation or video showing the product, ideally "a feat of strength": type a few things and see magic output you couldn't produce before.
- He says these demos are why people are so excited about the technology right now.
- Get visitors to the aha moment as quickly as possible, directly inside the product.
- Don't make people click to try the product. Rosebud could put a few clickable example ideas or a fly-out on the home page. Garry's logic: "every time you make people do extra clicks you have risk of drop off".
- He likes a "how it works" section early on because it raises understanding quickly, comparing it to doing a demo in a YC interview.
- Specific, concrete claims work well (for example, "30 percent cheaper and 25 percent faster"), though he notes visitors will wonder how you know. A footnote isn't required.

## Generic products: lead with specific use cases

- On MagicFlow (AI workflow builder), Garry draws on YC's experience with generic workflow companies.
- His point: nobody wakes up thinking "I really need workflow software". People have a specific problem to solve.
- What works is focusing on the most common specific use cases to get people into the product. Then they discover they can do more.
- He likes naming familiar tools (Hugging Face, Stability AI, ChatGPT, Replicate) because visitors recognize their own stack and feel the product is for them.
- He likes claims that look like they came from many user conversations, such as faster and cheaper, because they answer "why not just build it myself".

## Don't put a wall in front of the demo

- On MagicFlow, the "drag and drop" demo shows a hand cursor but clicking drops you into a login wall. Garry says it doesn't build trust.
- His fix: "do your first one free", with no account required up front. Prompt for signup only when the visitor wants to save or use the result, because by then they're invested.
- If you must gate, give context about why signing up helps, and don't pretend a mock is a live try-it.
- His framing: "you're always fighting the urge for the back button or to go look at Hacker News."
- His main feedback on MagicFlow: the good material is small and buried on the side, and "you want to hit people over the head with it" or visitors scroll past.

## Spacing, motion and polish as trust signals

- On Reality Defender, Garry says uneven spacing (too much between header lines, too little between logos and body) and wrong header line height break the "enterprise trust" feeling for him.
- Most people won't consciously notice, but some will. He says good spacing contributes to a feeling of quality, and small fit-and-finish flaws "ruin the illusion".
- On Pump (cloud cost savings), the bright colors and constant movement overwhelm. Garry's advice: "less is more".
- Use animation sparingly, to draw the eye to the one thing you want people to notice next.
- He contrasts this with Voiceflow, which has motion that intrigues rather than overwhelms.

## Differentiation, social proof and what a good page looks like

- As a YC partner, Garry asks of Pump: dozens of products cut cloud costs, so why this one? He says visitors will be asking the same.
- Pump's real differentiator seems to be group buying (companies pooling for better AWS pricing), but it is buried. He says to hit people over the head with whatever is different.
- Make the savings concrete up front. For Pump, the aha is showing how much a visitor would save, making it a "no-brainer".
- Put FAQs within easy reach, with the most important question first, so a skeptical visitor gets answers in about two clicks.
- Social proof helps: a recognizable customer logo, ideally with a case study from the number one customer. He wants a known customer who saved X percent.
- Reality Defender earns trust through government and enterprise logos and a broadcaster case study, but it still lacks data to make him enter his info.
- Voiceflow is his positive example (very good as a design review): a clear call to action, a hero interaction that explains the product before reading, calm scroll, many recognizable customer logos, and real quotes from named people at those companies. He says it feels trustworthy and he "would totally use this".
- He suggests studying older, iterated products like Voiceflow. They have spent years refining, so "you get to take their learnings for free".
- Why-now framing: for many AI startups, "why now" is the key question, and a new category sometimes has to be described from scratch.
