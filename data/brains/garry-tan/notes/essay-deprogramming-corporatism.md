## Why big-company experience misleads founders

Garry argues that corporate culture is learned, and that its habits cause experienced founders and corporate-trained hires to fail at startups. Founder culture is learned too.

## Corporate work can look productive without producing results

- Garry says big-company employees can "just do things that look like work that aren't," and nobody can tell.
- Customers are easy to get (sometimes trivial) with a large installed base, a sales force or a huge brand name.
- Large protected revenue streams hide failure on the quarterly financials.
- Corporate founders aren't stupid. They're used to doing things and having something happen, with the wind at their backs.
- It is bewildering to work "really freaking hard" at something and get no results back, but it happens all the time.

## Startups have no air cover

- "There's no air cover for startups. You don't have backup troops. It's just you versus the world."
- If the product or service sucks, the startup dies.
- You can't just look like you're doing stuff. You have to make the thing, almost entirely on your own.

## Cover-your-ass culture and credit-taking

- In big organizations, working hard to no effect doesn't get you fired. Looking like a bozo or making your boss look bad does.
- Garry says the worst version is taking credit for what other people do, which he sees as correlated with people who climb high in organizations.
- This fails in a small startup because there's nowhere to hide. Someone has to do the work.
- Startups can develop cover-your-ass culture too. It is the founders' and CEO's job to keep people focused on what actually matters.
- Smaller teams blunt the toxic group dynamics.

## Buzzwords and trend-following fail in startups

- In a corporation, if everyone is saying it, nobody can fault you. Latching onto the buzzword of the day (big data, Internet of Things, NoSQL) lets you blend in. If the big data initiative fails, "everyone else was doing it," so you won't get fired.
- Founders confuse this with a recipe: "We said all the right secret words! Why am I failing?" He says it was never about the words.
- Startups have to know what's coming and be right. A wrong market or a wrong read on what people want means the startup is toast.
- He isn't saying everything with a buzzword label fails. A big data startup, for example, must make life better for specific customers so they're willing to pay. "It has to make sense."

## Garry's Palantir mistake

- At 23, he turned down being first engineer at Palantir (which he says is "now rumored to be worth $20 billion!"), even though Peter Thiel took him out to dinner to recruit him.
- He treated buzzwords as signal. In 2004 none of the mainstream press or tech blogs were abuzz about a hot government enterprise software startup.
- His lesson: work on things that (a) you know are right and (b) most people don't know yet.
- This is why Peter likes to ask: "What super valuable company is nobody building yet?"

## Deprogramming: focus on results and change your surroundings

- Experienced founders from corporate backgrounds aren't doomed. Those who succeed avoided, overcame or escaped these habits.
- The recurring theme is results. Garry says they push founders toward action: "build product, talk to customers, that's it."
- Think in concrete numbers: user growth, savings to customer, or revenue.
- Many places let you survive without results of your own, "but startups are not one of them."
- Why YC works: it takes a village of people who all believe a thing and practice it daily. It means fundamentally changing your surroundings and the people around you.
- He says to avoid the coworking space and work harder than ever in your own space.
- YC is a concentrated form of Silicon Valley, which for decades has been where people escape corporate culture to create something new. YC lets you do it with many more like-minded people beside you.
