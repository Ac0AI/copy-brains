## Mental health and the founder's inner state

- Garry says unexamined childhood trauma shows up in how founders manage conflict. His parents were immigrants, his father struggled with alcoholism and holding a job, and money was tight.
- At his first company he avoided conflict until he blew up at people. A direct report told his co-founder Garry was "the worst manager he'd ever had" and had never been so disrespected.
- He took the online Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) test and scored very high. He recommends it, and says a high score is okay and you don't have to pretend to be normal.
- He credits years of therapy and coaching with letting him approach conflict in a healthy way, and calls himself a work in progress.
- His failure mode: Posterous didn't work out partly because he hadn't dealt with this early enough, and by the time he was making hard calls on teams it was "a little bit too late."
- Startups take on the personality of their founders, so unexamined demons end up in the company.
- His advice is to get therapy early. It sounds expensive, but people spend 10x to 1000x more on things that won't fill the hole when all they needed was to talk to someone.

## The $200M+ mistake: turning down Palantir

- In 2004 Garry was a level 59 PM at Microsoft making $74,000 a year. Peter Thiel took him to dinner and wrote a check for $74,000 in cash so he could quit at zero risk and join friends starting Palantir.
- Garry said no because "I might get promoted to level 60 next year." Palantir had offered about 1% of the company. He later joined as employee number 10 and designed the logo. He estimates the missed stake at roughly $350M to $500M, and calls it his "$200 million mistake."
- Lesson 1, media is stale: anything you read in the press is a third-hand account of what happened six months ago. He judged Palantir by what he could read (selling enterprise software to three-letter agencies wasn't in the news) and got it exactly wrong.
- Lesson 2, great ideas sound dumb: the iPod was dismissed as "all that hype for an MP3 player." In 2003 he skipped Google because nobody on campus thought it would make a dollar.
- Two Venn diagrams matter. One is people who can build technology and people who understand hairy problems nobody else can solve. The other is ideas that are actually good but look like bad ideas. At YC this shows up as founder-market fit. He points to Thiel's *Zero to One* for the second.
- Lesson 3, he spent his money: he took on a nice apartment and a new car on top of student debt, trying to fill "a hole in my heart." He says he should have husbanded every dollar as months of freedom to create equity.

## How to think about big companies and jobs

- Work is a transaction. Microsoft made about $650,000 in profit per employee while paying him $74,000. Unless you own the place, you get a fraction of the value you create.
- It isn't valueless: you get money and stability, and above all you can learn something there. He says to leave big tech before it makes you soft.
- At Microsoft he owned a tiny checkbox in a dialog box (Windows Mobile ActiveSync, which users called "Microsoft ActiveStink"), and he wasn't having fun.
- He rage-quit a job roughly every couple of years once he was six or seven years in. In hindsight each time he had learned what he wanted to learn. He doesn't recommend burning bridges, but says "learning or earning" is what matters.
- He would have done web software in 2003 rather than Windows Mobile.
- Don't sell your time. He spent four years as a consultant and started at 14 by cold-calling the Yellow Pages for an internet design agency job, using the money to help his parents buy their first home.
- His advice to his younger self at 18 or 22 is to make products instead. Time is the most precious finite asset, while software or hardware can "keep making money" while you sleep.

## How Posterous started and the Katamari model

- Garry had just rage-quit Palantir and was working at a friend's startup as a designer while taking $200 cash photography jobs off Craigslist. He attended one of the first YC Startup Schools at Dinkelspiel Auditorium, sitting on the floor with a big Canon lens.
- Event photography was missing, so he put his photos on the blog platform he was building on the side with his friend Sachin Agarwal. He posted it to Hacker News, and Paul Graham and Jessica Livingston then interviewed them for YC.
- TechCrunch's Michael Arrington left them on top of the homepage all weekend, which brought 10,000 signups, then 20,000. They grew it into a top-200 website and sold it to Twitter.
- He says it started by figuring out what skills and equipment he had and how to turn them into something useful for others, not by asking anyone to be a mentor.
- Katamari Damacy is his metaphor for a startup. You roll a ball that sticks to things smaller than it, and if you try to grab something too big you bounce off and shrink.
- You are picking up three things: co-founders and people, a product that solves a problem and brings customers, and capital that lets you hire and buy more customers. This becomes a virtuous cycle.
- It works as a loop. Push on whichever of the three you can move, switch focus when you stall, and come back. A great co-founder helps you win customers, which helps you raise money, which helps you hire.

## Be a Swiss Army knife and act small

- Don't let the market turn you into an interchangeable part, such as a "junior back-end Redis engineer." Be a "super knife" who does many things.
- He bought a Wenger Giant Knife on eBay for $4,700. A commodity Wenger pocket knife costs about $3, so versatility is worth over 1,000x.
- Non-technical people who ask how to start should learn to code. This isn't to become the coder but because it improves hiring, customer conversations and speed.
- Don't try to look big. Don't mimic Microsoft's homepage language. The advantage of a new startup is being a real human at a time when people crave authentic connection.
- Treat startups less like building a car and more like throwing a great party: greet people, take their coat, introduce them to friends. Great design and breadth matter for that.
- Most companies treat their support inbox as "a suggestion box that goes straight to the shredder."

## Fanatical customer support and being a real person

- At Posterous, once the team was seven or eight people, they split the days of the week and each person owned the inbox all day. The aim was to reply to every email within 10 minutes and fix bugs on the spot.
- Being hands-on and authentic ("I'm the CEO, what do you need?") is an unscalable thing he considers very powerful. Customers will tell you what they want if you listen.
- He cites an early study of Usenet. When a post got no reply, only 16% of posters came back to the forum. With even one reply, 26% came back.
- That 10-point retention difference compounds into the difference between a flatlining startup and a working one.
- It applies to B2B and dev tools too. There are only about 30 million developers, so developer tools are effectively "a consumer go-to-market" with 30 million consumers instead of a billion.

## Choosing problems, timing, and mission

- To find good problems, do something weird. Ignore Time, TechCrunch and what's hot on Twitter, and focus on "what do I uniquely know and what do I believe in."
- His examples are Kyle Vogt at Cruise, who loved self-driving cars at MIT when nobody thought it was a commercial business, and Brian Armstrong reading the Satoshi Nakamoto white paper.
- On school versus startups, school is fun and one of the best places to meet co-founders. His bar for quitting is to know who you are, be good at things, have found co-founders, and have an idea you think you uniquely can solve.
- He was a late bloomer and didn't start a company straight out of college. He needed Microsoft, a startup stint and learning to manage people first. He says some of the best YC founders aren't fresh out of college, and you should follow your own path.
- He rejects the "speedrun society" path of making as much money as possible and then saving the world (he mocks "save the shrimp" and virtue signaling). He recommends Randy Komisar's *The Monk and the Riddle* and says you don't have to live on the deferred life plan.
- He disagrees with the finance adage of "too much money chasing too few good ideas." He says there are far too many unsolved problems, and builders should be accelerationists working on human abundance.
