## Why you, why now: stress-testing a startup idea

Garry Tan argues that pointing at a good idea is not enough. In his words, "You can't just point at the moon, you've got to build the goddamn spaceship." Before committing to an idea, a founder has to show an advantage that other people don't have. He sums that up as two questions: why me, and why now.

## The hard questions Garry asks before building

He splits the exercise into two steps: define the problem, then define how you'll solve it.

**Define the problem:**
- Who am I building for?
- Who is my audience?
- What problem do they have?

**Define the solution:**
- Is my solution possible and feasible?
- How much will it cost?
- What technology exists today, and how much of it can I use? Is it open source or licensable?
- Is there published research describing a new method?
- What are the alternatives? How do people solve this today?
- Who else has tried this, and what went wrong with their solution?

**Then the advantage:**
- Why now?
- What do I have that nobody else has?
- Is it technology, or something that stops others from just copying me?

## The kinds of moat Garry looks for

- **Pure technology.** He calls it "often actually the best moat of all."
- **Customer relationships.** Landing an early lighthouse customer who unlocks a whole industry. Example: Envoy, the visitor sign-in and check-in product, started by selling Airbnb as one of its first 10 clients.
- **A better go-to-market.**
- **Content or some other relationship.**
- **Something you do that nobody else does.** He asks founders to name it explicitly.

A moat that holds up is what makes the company protectable.

## What weak answers mean, and what to do with them

Honest answers sometimes expose too many "no"s. Garry's examples of weak spots:
- The audience is too small.
- The problem isn't felt severely enough for anyone to fix it.
- It can't be built with today's technology.
- It can be built, but too expensively for buyers to pay.
- Obvious alternatives are just as good or cheaper, or the current way of solving it is "basically fine."
- You have no advantage over others trying to build it.

He calls this "the moment of maximum disillusionment," when people start thinking they shouldn't work on the idea. His reframe: the questions also show exactly what you need to work on as a founder. The weak answers become your to-do list.

## Technology as the moat for technical founders

For a technologist, using technology as the moat is "the bread and butter." Garry says great engineers can find a way to be much better, cheaper or faster than all the alternatives.

His example is Standard Cognition, which has a pure technology moat:
- It put into practice a new method that had never been commercialized.
- As a result it has something "even Amazon cannot duplicate."
- It can offer cashierless checkout at possibly 1/10 to 1/100 the cost of the state of the art.

## Posterous (2008): a simple technical insight as the moat

Garry's own startup, Posterous, was a dead-simple blogging-by-email platform. Users emailed one address, post@posterous.com, and it published.

- On day one it did something people thought wasn't possible. The common belief was that this couldn't be done securely, which is why other services used addresses like "secretpassword123@flickr.com."
- The solution was not machine learning. "Frankly, it was IF statements." If Posterous had received email from you before, it asked you to confirm it. Once confirmed, that sender's path was treated as safe. This anti-spoofing approach came from his co-founder, Sachin Agarwal.
- Because competitors couldn't work out what Posterous had, nobody copied them right away. They became a top 200 site without "fending off a thousand other copycats."
- The anti-spoofing limitation was enough of a moat to reach their first hundreds of thousands of users.

His lesson: "Without that moat, without that reason to be, without that thing that other people did not have, we didn't have a good reason to exist."

## The takeaway

Garry says there isn't just one way to get a moat, but it's prudent to ask: "Why me? What do I have that nobody else has?" His closing rule: "if that answers nothing, you've got to keep working."
