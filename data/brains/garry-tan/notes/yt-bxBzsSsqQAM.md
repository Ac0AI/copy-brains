## Find a painful problem first; moats come later

- Garry's core advice is to find a specific person with a real, severe pain point and solve it. The advice is deliberately "anti-business school": "make something people want."
- He says you can go almost anywhere and find problems that software, and especially AI, isn't solving. They are so numerous and severe that solving one can mint a billion-dollar, $10 billion or even hundreds-of-billions market cap business, "lying in plain sight."
- He says moats show up along the way. You stumble into the seven powers as you work with customers, build the product and work out what data you need.
- The pain should be existential for the customer: "I am not going to get promoted this year. Maybe I will get fired", or "the business is going to go out of business." It should not be a "nice if I could do this."
- His closing advice is to focus mainly on speed, which he treats as a moat in its own right (it is not one of Hamilton Helmer's seven powers). Go zero to one first.
- Most of the powers below are problems of going from one to a billion, ten billion or a trillion. Using this framework to avoid a startup, or to pick between two ideas by forecasting five-year moats, is "the real stupid thing."

## Why moats matter at all

- Garry notes the irony that "The 7 Powers" (Hamilton Helmer) is business school material, while YC's own maxims are simple and anti-business-school.
- He cites Peter Thiel's "competition is for losers." With infinite competition, margins go to zero and the business dies, so a moat is "relatively existential eventually."
- A moat is a defensive tool. You need something worth defending, and before that "it's just like a puddle in a field."

## Process power and cornered resources

- **Process power:** Garry's best example is Plaid, which has to support thousands to tens of thousands of financial institutions, each with different sites and crawlers, plus a huge CI/CD structure.
  - Better engineering is "the most profound form of process power."
  - He speculates that Plaid's founder Zach would want the latest code-gen tools to add every new financial institution faster than anyone else.
- **Cornered resource, government:** He points to Scale AI and Palantir with the DOD.
  - Getting there takes hiring the right people, long stretches in DC and Langley, and building skiffs (special secure data centers).
  - The cornered resource can be "the diamond mine in your customer's heads", meaning mindshare inside government.
- **Cornered resource, startups:** Closer to what YC sees is the forward deployed engineer (FDE) model, where founders sit with customers who normally never get good software.
  - They map the real time-and-motion of the process. For example: a request arrives by email, gets enriched in a certain way, and sometimes a call center phones the person.
  - That becomes the startup's own prompts, evals and eventually data sets for tuning models. All of it is "incredibly valuable."
- **Models are not required early:** Context engineering gets you 80 to 90% of the way, which is "plenty" for the first two years of a startup.
  - Cursor did not begin with full-parameter fine-tunes. It "started just by making something people want."
  - He says don't use the frameworks to "count yourself out prematurely."

## Switching costs in the AI era

- AI can lower switching costs and also create new ones.
- **Lowering them:** Garry says that if you can use code gen to extract data from old, ossified systems or from competitors, you can take a switching cost that protected an incumbent "potentially down to zero." Startups can use this as a lever.
- **Consumer memory:** He says memory is already a switching cost for him. His ChatGPT relationship has evolved over the last year and it now seems to know what he cares about. He thinks that will grow, and personalization for consumers is a huge piece of it.

## Counter-positioning against SaaS incumbents

- **Per-seat pricing:** He sees per-seat pricing as a structural trap for incumbents. If their AI works, customers need fewer seats, so success cuts their own revenue. Startups price on work delivered or tasks completed instead.
- **Cultural lag:** The top thing he hears from late-stage founders is how hard it is to reset engineering culture to use AI tools and to do context and prompt engineering. Teams that can't become AI-native can't ship products that work.
  - His closing advice to the last YC batch was to spend a month at a late-stage company to see this.
- **Bigger wallet share (Avoca, HVAC):**
  - He thinks ServiceTitan gets about 1% of an HVAC company's gross transaction value.
  - Avoca sells customer support software and can capture 4 to 10% of their spend, because it takes over the customer-support work itself.
  - His conclusion: "we may well be all undervaluing how powerful and how big the vertical SaaS AI companies will actually be."
- **Labor displacement:** He argues customer support jobs have 50 to 80% annual attrition, so people are quitting anyway.
  - Per Avoca, former support staff now manage AI agents, handle the interesting edge cases and can adjust prompts. He calls this "way more interesting" than following a script.

## Brand and ChatGPT vs Google as counter-positioning

- Garry calls Google losing the consumer AI lead to ChatGPT a perfect example of counter-positioning. Google had the greatest cash cow in history, so disrupting its own ad business was unattractive, even at the cost of slowing access to knowledge by a few years.
- He gives the ChatGPT origin story as a speed moat. It shipped in months with a very small team of a couple of engineers.
  - Getting there took Sam Altman, YC Research and Greg Brockman hiring Ilya Sutskever out of DeepMind.
  - Many of OpenAI's founding people came from DeepMind, which was in the right place but "didn't nurture exactly the thing that society really needed."

## Schlep work is what keeps the last 10% yours

- On reliability, Garry notes that a hackathon version of an AI tool is faster than ever to build, but the work after it is different. It means getting it to run reliably across tens of thousands of KYC requests a day.
- That last stretch is "painstaking drudgery work" that many engineers don't want to do. He expects it to be hard for people at big labs inventing AGI to get excited about the final 5% of consistency on one vertical tool.
- Vertical knowledge matters here too, because you need it to know what the edge cases are.
