## Garry's learn / earn / quit framework

- Garry's test: "are you learning or are you earning?" Both means a great place. One of the two is still fine. Neither means "it's time to move on, you're wasting your life."
- Learn first, then earn. He calls it (via his friend Steven Sinofsky) "the protein before the carbs."
- Earning first usually still teaches you something, but slowly, and it can cost a lot and trap you on a spending treadmill.
- If you are in neither, plot your exit.

## How to start learning: pick maker or hustler

- Most people early in their careers should focus on learning first.
- Two paths: maker (design, product, engineering) or hustler (management, sales, marketing).
- Step one is learning about yourself: what you enjoy and what puts you in a state of flow.
- Step two is acquiring the skill. If you love it, the 10,000 hours won't feel like work.
- Garry cites Bill Gates: the people who reach 10,000 hours are those who chose, and were chosen, many times. About 90% drop out at each early stage (50 hours, then another 50) because they dislike it or aren't good.

## Breaking in as a hustler

- Marketing: "helping other people communicate their problem and how their solution is the best."
- Find a product, website or service you like, suggest improvements, then build, design or write the improvement yourself.
- Cold email the founder with it, or just send it.
- Sales: go get customers without being asked and send them to a founder. "They'll always take your email, they'll always remember you."

## Breaking in as a builder

- Build it yourself instead of relying on others. Find a real problem your software can solve and ship a first version.
- Don't wait for the perfect problem; it only has to be real and solvable.
- Example: some of the best founders he has met started with gradebook software because they had teachers nearby who were frustrated with their gradebooks. Many later built billion-dollar companies in other areas, but they learned to create there.
- "Creating you can only learn by doing." Make something, then iterate until users love it.
- A product users love beats a credential. A beautiful app, website or elegant GitHub repo puts you in the top 10% with no credentials, even without Stanford.
- His own example: at 14 he got a job cold calling the Yellow Pages internet section, pointing to an award-winning website he made for his junior high underground newspaper. He then cold emailed his way into his first coding job.

## Earning: equity is the highest form

- The best way to earn is equity: own stock in a company that becomes valuable. A founder starts with 100% of something worth nothing, then makes it worth something.
- Cash can be large too. He notes Google's revenue of over $166B across about 135,000 employees is over $1.2M per employee.
- Founder math: owning 10-30% of an exit worth tens or hundreds of millions to billions can mean becoming a centimillionaire to billionaire.
- Early employees might hold 25 basis points (0.25%) up to 2% or more. Joining early means less equity but a company not yet at its peak value, and it can be an amazing deal if growth is fast.
- Founders and early employees can learn while they earn.

## Warning: the hedonic treadmill and meaningless jobs

- Many people trade happiness for financial stability. He says that is not necessarily bad, but warns of the cycle: earn, spend on a bigger house, car or nicer things, spend more, repeat. Buying things you don't really want doesn't deliver the satisfaction you're after.
- Advice for would-be founders: if you are young and thrifty, don't inflate your lifestyle. Save that money so you can invest your time in building your own startup or "wealth engine."
- Some high-paying jobs feel meaningless, and the more you're paid, the more meaningless the job can become. He argues "you only get one life, it's too short to waste" on unfulfilling work, even if comfortable.
- If you don't learn and don't love the job, leave once you've earned enough to take the leap.

## Garry's own learn-and-earn history, and when to quit a bad startup

- Palantir: learned to run teams and build product from scratch while earning equity in what became a $40B company.
- Y Combinator: learned to invest and work with startups at the earliest stage, while earning valuable carry.
- Now: he learns daily from founders as founder and managing partner of his own VC firm. He runs YouTube for fun and donates the proceeds to nonprofits like Code2040, but learns from viewers' comments and DMs.
- Bad startup example: a junior-year enterprise software internship from the Stanford career fair. The company raised money but never closed customers. He made marketing material, mock-ups and prototypes for sales pitches, they never closed a sale all summer, and the startup died. He neither earned much nor learned much.
- His rule: if you aren't learning, can't earn equity or a good salary, and it isn't fun or meaningful, "you gotta quit."
