## Garry's path from Winnipeg to YC, and what YC actually is

- Garry was born in Winnipeg, grew up in Fremont, and took BART to UC Berkeley for his first CS classes. His first coding job was at Adjacency, the design firm that built the first Apple e-commerce store for Steve Jobs.
- He describes YC as an accelerator: you apply online, about 1% get in, and you get half a million dollars for roughly 7% of the company. He says the money matters less than getting a YC partner. There are 15 partners, and each picks companies out of the applicant pool.
- Partners decide yes or no on the half million within a 10-minute interview.
- He cites YC's 20-year record as more than 100 billion-dollar companies. When he did YC in 2008 the batch got about $13,000, and the first batch included Reddit (Steve Huffman and Alex Ohanian) and Sam Altman's Loopt.
- He found YC through Palantir, where he was employee number 10 and designed the logo. The team was trying to hire away YC alums, which showed him where the smartest engineers went.
- YC's mantra, printed on the t-shirt: "Make something people want." He tells founders to dwell on what people actually want and to build for real customers.

## YC's eras, and why Garry came back in 2022

- **Era one:** Boston, with Paul Graham, Jessica Livingston, Trevor Blackwell and Robert Morris.
- **Era two (from 2011):** Airbnb followed Dropbox as a billion-dollar company, and the valley realized YC was repeatable. Check sizes went from roughly $12-15K to $120K-$175K. Investors took about 3-4 years to catch up with what engineers already knew.
- Garry joined as a partner around 2011 after Harj Taggar suggested it. He had rage-quit his own startup and had no plan to become an investor. He started by helping YC companies design their homepages.
- He then raised Initialized Fund I, a $7M fund that returned more than 55x DPI. He says the power law was real: across 98 companies the combined return was about 1x until Coinbase was counted. Instacart was about 1x and Coinbase carried the fund.
- He says 80-90% of Initialized's wins were YC or YC alumni (Ripling and Flock Safety among them).
- Brian Chesky and Paul Graham pushed him to return in 2022. He calls it a dream job and says "YC gave me my start as a founder... as an investor," plus most of his friends.
- He calls YC a "managed marketplace" and a network-effects business. About 20% of companies worth $5B+ that started from 2012 on came through YC, and that share has been flat for years. He wants it at 30-50% by 2036.

## What Garry changed at YC: founder mode applied to the institution

- From Brian Chesky and Paul Graham's "founder mode" idea, Garry took that leadership is presence, not absence. He says the standard advice, "hire the smartest people... give them the keys to the kingdom," did not work for Chesky.
- Changes he made:
  - Brought back in-person interviews.
  - Moved the program to San Francisco and got an office there. Founders are told to move to the Dogpatch.
  - Ended the remote batch: "you cannot do YC remotely."
  - Extended the program to 13 weeks. Batches had shrunk to about 11-12 weeks.
- Weeks 1-10 are for finding product-market fit: get into market, nail messaging, decide who you build for, and land the first 3-5 real customers, which "sets the tone for the whole company."
- Fundraising gets about two weeks. The investor push starts the Saturday after the send-off.
- Defense tech is up more than 2x in the last batch. Icarus booked over $1.5M from the Department of War during the batch.
- The first thing a founder can do to make fundraising easy is build something valuable and get paying, retained customers.
- Fundraising "is the starting gun," not the finish line. He treats SAFEs like debt: "you sort of signed up to say... we're going to return a big multiple of that."
- Moving fast during YC creates momentum ("objects in motion stay in motion"). He says billion-dollar companies later try to recapture the energy of those first eight weeks. Airbnb's example: post-it graph targets of 10% weekly growth.
- YC runs on its own advice: when choosing between spending heavily and hiring, or doing it "quick and dirty" and iterating, they pick the startup way "10 times out of 10."
- New ideas he is trying:
  - Rebatching: a second batch after Series A, starting with companies he funded.
  - A Cambridge office, where Garry says YC just added its first partner.
- He says more founders get rejected than he likes. YC gets about 80,000 applications a year and funds roughly 700-800 companies. It keeps him up at night. He notes many get in on a second to fourth attempt, and "if you can survive the mortal ego wound of being rejected... you're made to be a founder."

## How Garry evaluates founders and why YC partners are good at it

- He loves builders. Even with AI, he says the default output is bad: "boilerplate... kind of sucks," so there is "no excuse" for not making beautiful, well-made products.
- He uses the Steve Jobs cabinet-maker image: great cabinet makers finish the back of the cabinet too.
- Partner skill comes from reps. A partner reads 1,000-2,000 applications a year and writes 50-75 seed checks. Garry says two or three years as a YC partner equals about 20 years in venture.
- He says partners are strong at pulling out the billion-dollar idea, but their main value is stopping founders from hitting the "10,000 landmines." Examples: co-founder conflict, taking money from the wrong investor, single-channel concentration.
- He says partners never freak out: "soft advisors who never freak out."
- Selection is subjective. Each partner picks their own cohort, passes along companies they cannot fit, and the process is "random winning the lottery" when done wrong and a long-term commitment when done right.
- He admits mistakes. He and a colleague should have accepted one company in the fall batch and took them the next winter. He says he will feel bad about missing the next Airbnb and will "turn it into productive energy."

## Co-founders, conflict and fixing your own patterns

- Garry says the most common YC failures are running out of money and co-founder conflict. He adds that YC companies usually quit because they get tired and demoralized, not because they run out of cash.
- Great co-founders "fight absolutely all the time and about everything" because they care. The key is doing it in a way that preserves the relationship: "There is never a conflict or a fight that is so much more important than the relationship itself."
- His own pattern, which he calls self-abandonment: he would swallow disagreements to protect the relationship, then blow up. He says he was a terrible co-founder at Posterous. One time he blew up in a 1-on-1 with an early designer.
- His model: a horse and a rider, where his rider (prefrontal cortex, language) can override the horse (body) until it "says F this." He lost the ability to eat and sleep. He got through it with therapy and coaching, citing Cameron Yarrow's coaching (Torch).
- Tool he uses: "freeze all motor function" (a Westworld debug-mode analogy), then add an "if statement." For him the trigger is his face getting hot.
- Why it matters: startups take on the founder's personality. "How you do anything is how you do everything." Founders' unresolved patterns show up in who they hire, who they fire and how the company handles conflict.
- He warns "maybe psychedelics are not for everyone."

## Garry's AI workflow: metaprompting and multi-model review

- He credits Jared Friedman and Pete Koomen for "metaprompting."
- Steps:
  1. Take any repeated knowledge-work task and drop a set of real inputs and outputs into a context window (for him, notes and the finished YouTube script).
  2. Ask the model to write a prompt for an agent that turns that input into that output, and to say what patterns it notices.
  3. Use it, then correct it in conversation ("I would never say that," "use the short word").
  4. Ask for the next version of the prompt incorporating everything discussed.
- He keeps a folder of prompts. His YouTube prompt is on about V27.
- For hard cases he runs GPT 5.2 Pro, Grok and others at max thinking, pastes all outputs into Claude, and asks Claude to rate each response with pros and cons in numbered form. He agrees or disagrees by number, then asks it to synthesize.
- He uses Perplexity for anything needing the web because it synthesizes more sources.
- That weekend he used Claude Code to build a new website and redo interaction design.

## Why Garry fights for San Francisco, and his case for tech optimism

- His stat: staying in the Bay Area makes a startup 2.5x more likely to reach a $1B outcome. New York is about 2x. Everywhere else is the baseline. He cites capital access, ambition rising with your peers, and access to people who have done what you need to do.
- He opposes California's proposed one-time 5% "billionaire tax," which he calls an "asset seizure tax." He says it counts voting control as ownership, so Larry and Sergey would be taxed on 60% of Alphabet's value. He estimates a mid double-digit chance (40-50%) it passes, and says about $1T in net worth has already left the state.
- He also opposes a proposal to 10x San Francisco's gross receipts tax and a Mission District supervisor's effort to ban AI labs and R&D, which he says was stopped after people organized on the internet in December.
- His tactic: do research and go direct on X when mainstream outlets won't publish it. His covenant as a creator is to try to be truthful while admitting he won't get everything right, and to keep opposing views in the mix.
- His tech optimism: software touches a tiny share of GDP, only 10-20 million people can code well, and AI will let billions create. He argues for more markets, citing Uber, Airbnb and Waymo. He quotes Ryan Petersen of Flexport that "humans just want stuff." He also points out that firewood was once about 25% of US GDP.
- He says the Matrix is "surprisingly relevant to startup founders" and that YC is how he took the red pill. A company that stays plugged into customers is "in the real."
- He names Brian Chesky and Aaron Levie as the founders he learns from most. He says a fund that shorted incumbents with non-hacker CEOs would "outperform the S&P."
