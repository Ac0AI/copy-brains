## How a VC fund works: returns, limited partners and why the fund's results matter

- Garry says a VC firm exists to generate returns for investors. The money he invests is not his; it comes from "limited partners": endowments, nonprofits, retirement plans and sovereign wealth funds that professionally manage most of the world's money.
- He invests in the best possible founders because the goal is to return far more to those limited partners than they put in.
- He cites Sequoia (started in 1972 with an $8 million fund, now funded companies representing more than $3.3 trillion in value) and Y Combinator (about $400 billion of wealth created, founded 2005, where he worked and got his start).
- Initialized, which he started in 2012 with a first fund of $7 million, is now involved in over $100 billion of market cap in under nine years. It returned hundreds of millions of dollars to its first investors on Coinbase's IPO day.
- Initialized was the first investor in almost a dozen startups now worth over $1 billion, including Instacart, Flexport, Patreon, Rows, TruePill, Standard Cognition, Cruise Automation, Opendoor and Blend.
- Coinbase: he paid 15 cents a share about nine years before the IPO; shares topped out at $420 on IPO day. The point he draws: "we were first, way before it was obvious."

## Why VC is winner-take-all, and why the brand is not the point

- Garry says that who funds a startup matters a lot, much like a degree from Harvard, Stanford or Oxford. A startup backed by a top VC firm can hire smarter people, raise more money from better next-stage investors, and build a better product that wins.
- He is explicit that this is how it works, not that it is good: VC is winner-take-all, so founders (and people choosing a VC career) should pick wisely. Getting the right investor early can be pivotal.
- His caution: being the best-known brand is "the sizzle", but "the steak is in the returns."
- Cynicism does not work in VC. Firms fund ideas while they are still not obvious and often seem crazy to the public, so you have to believe in building the future.

## Favor technical, product and design-oriented builders

- Initialized has a strong preference for technical, product and design-oriented founders, because "the ones who can build it usually do." YC likewise prides itself on partners with deep product or technical backgrounds.
- He contrasts today with the late-1990s bubble (hit-and-run, sales-led companies with weak products, "forget details, just do deals") and calls the swing toward realness a good thing: it is less about money or bluster and more about founders who deliver.
- Instacart story: the YC application deadline had passed two months earlier, and Apoorva Mehta emailed YC alumni for introductions. Garry replied that a late application was "nearly impossible," which Apoorva read as "possible." Apoorva then ordered a six-pack of beer through Instacart to Garry at YC headquarters; the driver texted when delivered, and Garry called half an hour later and invited him to meet the partners the next day.
- Garry says that as an engineer and designer, once he used the app he knew YC had to fund it. It did not matter that dozens of startups (like Webvan) had tried and failed; what mattered was that this founder had built something truly great. Instacart is now worth tens of billions.
- The lesson he takes: drive and never giving up are recognizable, and "it all boils down to belief." A builder who can make you believe can make everyone believe.

## Be contrarian and right: avoid paint-by-numbers ideas

- Garry says many giant startup outcomes are both contrarian and right. In a cynical world, the person with deeper real belief is who he looks for.
- He draws on Peter Thiel: to be contrarian "is to actually think about things," to have well-defined beliefs and think for yourself.
- Paint-by-numbers ideas are like bad Hollywood pitches (a college football star joins elite hackers to catch the shark that killed his friend). The startup version strings together buzzwords such as sharing, mobile and social. Thiel's line he repeats: "the something of somewhere is really mostly just the nothing of nowhere."
- Hollywood-style ideas sometimes work, but founders pursue them at a far higher rate and they produce much more failure.
- Garry cites René Girard's mimetic theory (our desires come from other people) as the basis for why people crowd into the same ideas.
- His test for secret knowledge: ask "What do you believe that nobody else believes?" He looks for independent, first-principles thinkers who can see paint-by-numbers immediately; the crowd (get-rich-quick people, scammers, do-nothings) does not see value where he does.

## Be a benevolent investor: fair, direct and honest

- Garry says what matters most is being fair, direct and honest. He admits the firm is not perfect, but intends to build a venture firm that wins "without playing the games that are commonplace" in venture and business culture.
- His argument: greed is not good. The best founders can choose any investor and will choose the most trustworthy, so being a bad actor is adverse selection. In an iterated, infinite game (venture structures last 10 to 14 years, and increasingly longer, to get from idea to IPO) you may win a deal but lose in the long run.
- He quotes Bill Walsh ("the score takes care of itself"): focus on the process and quality of execution, and winning follows. His version: "do right and you'll win."
- Funds can afford to behave well when returns are strong. He cites Jason Lemkin: with a 1x net fund a write-off is high drama; in a 2x net fund every write-off hurts; in a 4x-plus net fund write-offs don't matter. Initialized is that third kind, with five core funds and $770 million in committed assets.
- The failure pattern he describes when investors squeeze founders in trouble: (1) the team misses revenue targets for a quarter, (2) the investor starts micromanaging, (3) the founder spends more time on board reports than fixing the problem, and the startup never recovers. "Squeezing harder on a handful of sand will force more grains out."
- His alternative: help find the root cause (executive roles and responsibilities, business model, go-to-market, or product). "You can only help if you first do no harm." He believes this is why Initialized will win.

## Hiring at Initialized: roles and who fits

- Garry announced Initialized was hiring through an open search, which most VC firms don't do. He says diversity of experience and background improves decisions.
- Principal: no investing experience required. Could be a founder or executive who can help startups go from zero to one. The role mainly helps founders and also teaches how to make investments.
- Partner: better for people with investing experience and a successful track record, such as an angel investor or a partner or principal at another firm.
- No required school, and no need to have worked at Google or Facebook; he cares about what you have done and can do.
- These are fairly senior roles because founders need experienced help: "everybody wants to tell you how to do it but they never did it" (Jay-Z).
- His view on capital: people say too much money chases too few good ideas and teams, but he thinks there is an infinite number of problems to solve and of smart, good people to solve them.
