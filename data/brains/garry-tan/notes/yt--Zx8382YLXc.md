## Founder-market fit: build for yourself, then pick a lane

- Garry's startup Posterous began as a weekend project: a way to email any photo and have it become a gallery. The team didn't know it could be a business.
- He says 2008 was an unusually good moment for it. The iPhone was brand new and the App Store had no great photo-posting apps yet. Posterous grew 10x year on year and reached a top-200 website on the internet. Twitter later bought it.
- Launch tactic: Y Combinator got them onto TechCrunch. Michael Arrington's launch post ("posterous beats Tumblr in Simplicity") stayed up all weekend and brought in roughly 10-20k users.
- He notes the framing that helped at launch is not the one that gets you to the next stage: "what got you here won't get you there."

## Posterous's mistakes: ignoring Instagram and answering "both"

- When Instagram launched, it could cross-post to Posterous. Investor Chris Sacca asked what the team thought of Instagram. Garry's co-founder replied "it sucks." Garry says that was when Posterous stopped growing.
- His lesson is that the "don't worry about competition" maxim can be taken too far. A founder is always betting on what the future looks like, and being able to predict it matters.
- Posterous's real competitor was a dominant, photo-based social network built on the iPhone, which was a one-time opportunity.
- At the Series A pitch, Peter Fenton of Benchmark asked one question: "are you a platform or are you a network?" Instagram was a network and Squarespace was a platform. Garry and his co-founder answered "both."
- He calls that the wrong answer. They could have owned photo/visual social, or charged money like WordPress or Squarespace. By doing neither, they did both poorly and let competitors win each space.
- Rule: be deliberate about the path, because saying yes to one thing means saying no to another.

## Profitability vs. raising the next round

- Garry frames this as a choice founders must actually make, and the common mistake is choosing both. Posterous was afraid to charge because it feared hurting growth, yet it also wasn't investing fully in network features.
- With the current funding environment, he says unless you are in AI, LLMs, computer vision or another very hot space, the next round is not guaranteed.
- His advice is to get to profitability or cash-flow breakeven, and then grow out of cash flow. The macro picture is uncertain (relations with China, inflation versus deflation), so profitability is the safest path.

## Finding product-market fit: the thin edge of the wedge

- Garry sees two kinds of founders. Some arrive with a real insight and are simply right. He names Brian Armstrong at Coinbase and Parker Conrad at Rippling, whom he says "just knew it" and who often produce the biggest outcomes. Others are drawn to the lore and community of startups and have only the desire to build one.
- Weak pitches from the second group sound like "a book report," recited and boring. He calls this trying to "MBA your way" into an insight.
- Startups have no tournament, unlike school or the professional world: "a B plus is an F." You have either found product-market fit or you haven't.
- The example is Clipboard Health. Its founder was running an undifferentiated nurse job-posting marketplace, much like Indeed, for years. She found product-market fit by spending time with skilled nursing facilities and convalescent homes. These spent heavily on one-off staffing agencies that worked by phoning around to fill shifts, and software had never touched that.
- She built a sticky product for that segment, and the company is now making nine figures in revenue.
- His takeaway: huge businesses sit where builders have never reached parts of the economy that have never seen software. Find a narrow, desperate segment that is 10x or 100x better served, and treat it as the thin edge of the wedge.
- On user research: people are nice and tell you what you want to hear. Until they pay or change a behavior out of self-interest, feedback is worthless. Be skeptical of what you want to hear and listen hard to what you don't.

## Founder traits: plain communication and the "75 IQ" test

- Garry uses the midwit meme to make a point: things that sound like "75 IQ" ideas are often "150 IQ" ones.
- Great founders explain complex breakthroughs in very few words, because at the core of a breakthrough is something obvious.
- This matters because the founder has to close customers, candidates and money, and nobody else can do those three early on. You need to make the case in a minute or less. He says to "breathe" the thing you believe that nobody else believes into other people.
- In office hours he looks for the "gem" rather than the "ritual," and doesn't want a 20-minute explanation of why something failed. Startups fail for many reasons. His analysis of hundreds of startups he has worked with since 2012 found the number one cause was not making something people wanted.
- Michael Seibel's line he repeats: don't try to be the median startup at YC. The median startup fails, and the value is in the power-law top end, so you must be superlative.
- In office hours he operates in one of two modes: people need to believe in their idea more, or they need to see the thing about to kill them. He cites the Stockdale Paradox: relentless optimism combined with a cynical view of the facts.

## Leadership and culture after product-market fit

- Past zero to one, Garry says the CEO must hold people to account. Culture is who gets hired, promoted and fired. Big tech avoids these choices by doing both. Inside a startup, everyone is watching which of two people the leader backs.
- Culture radiates from the founder. He points to Brian Chesky's speeches: the leader has to be unapologetic about who they are and what they stand for, and not pull punches.
- Founders' rough edges are what make them special, and they resonate with a small group of the right people. Smoothing them off to appeal to everyone was long the common wisdom, and he says it's a mistake.
- Early-stage venture is heavily resume-based, but being an employee in an organization is a very different skill from being founder and CEO.
- He thinks Airbnb could still grow 10x, because only a small share of the world's available space is on it.

## Co-founder relationships

- Garry and his Posterous co-founder didn't speak for about eight to ten years. Growth flatlined in 2010 after two and a half years of non-stop growth, and he says he hadn't hired enough engineers, so technical debt was severe.
- He took "have a good relationship with your co-founder" to mean being collaborative: if they wanted X and he wanted Y, he'd water down Y by 50%. That works in a marriage, he says, but in a co-founding relationship you are better off simply having the fight.
- The best partnerships are with people you enjoy disagreeing and arguing with, because you'll do it again and again and the relationship must survive the heat.
- Early on, strong growth masked the flaws in the relationship. He burned out badly and later saw his body reacting to it (insomnia, not eating, fevers).

## YC, valuations and the future of venture

- Garry learned to invest by reading applications at Y Combinator. The most he read in one cycle was about a thousand. He says nowhere else exposes you to that many types of people and ideas, and a VC firm sees only dozens of deals a year.
- He values YC's generalist breadth: the best founders share ways of looking at problems and values that YC has a process to spot.
- On fundraising today: it's close to a roll of the die and was far easier in 2021. Stack the odds with trusted advisors, hone "what do we believe that nobody else believes," bring evidence, and have a truly huge hypothesis.
- Valuations and dilution: the average YC company takes roughly 10-12% dilution, versus 20-40% at seed and another 20-40% at Series A in the old norm. He cites YC's $750k deal on a $3M post as an example. Founders keeping more control means bigger outcomes.
- Fund returns: his seven million dollar initialized fund modeled a best case of about 5x if it caught a Dropbox-size exit. It returned more than 55x DPI, driven by Coinbase and Instacart. He expects decacorns and hectocorns to become much more numerous.
- AI shift: with large language models, two or three people in a garage can land a $10-100k/year contract that expands to millions within three to six months. He says he had never seen that happen before.
- He argues the limiting factor is not capital or ideas but community: people with the right values working on the right problems. He calls himself an effective accelerationist.
- On location: he thinks San Francisco is still the best place to find product-market fit, and it makes sense to keep at least one foot there for zero to one. Many things stop mattering once you have it.
