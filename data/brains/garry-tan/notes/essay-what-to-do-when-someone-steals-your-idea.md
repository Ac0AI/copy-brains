## Why ideas get stolen, and why it matters less than founders fear

Garry says it does happen: good ideas are rare enough that it is often easier to look at what already works and copy it. His mock example is the "brain rape" scene from Silicon Valley (VCs pumping a founder for details), which he calls extreme and not what to expect in the Valley. The real reasons are structural, not malicious.

- Ideas that actually work are "very, very rare."
- They appear when one of three things changes: technology, regulation, or a macroeconomic force.
- When that change hits, many people see the opening at once, so fast followers will often find the same idea independently.

## The three forces that make an idea possible

Garry's checklist for why a startup can work now:

- **Technology:** The first iPhone needed battery tech, display tech, miniaturization, and software efficient enough to use far less memory. "Almost a perfect storm." Platforms stack: before Instagram could happen, you needed the iPhone.
- **Technology example from his portfolio:** Standard Cognition does cashierless checkout like Amazon Go but with no shelf sensors, cameras only. He says that can cut deployment cost by 10 to 100 times.
- **Regulation:** Telemedicine took the FDA putting out a letter saying a doctor in the loop, driven by software through a smartphone or browser, is "okay to do now." When regulation shifts, a wave of activity follows because something you could not do suddenly becomes allowed.
- **Macroeconomic forces:** Remote work is the example. San Francisco housing pushed cost of living so high that retaining software engineers became nearly impossible. These forces are bigger than any one person or company but enable whole new markets.

## Answer "why now" before anything else

Garry says answering the "why now" question is fundamental for anyone starting something new. If you can name which of the three forces unlocked your idea, you understand why the opening exists, and why others will see it too.

## The idea is only a multiplier on execution

- Because workable startups are bounded by these specific forces, fast followers are inevitable.
- His conclusion: "the idea is only a multiplier on execution." Ideas are out there and everyone can pursue them.
- What distinguishes you at the end of the day is execution. He frames it as three questions: What is your product like? Is it actually what people need? Do people choose you over all possible alternatives?
- A great idea alone is not enough. To take advantage of it you have to "jam it down their throats," which means a lot of manual work and pure execution.

## Don't clam up: lean in when you have competition

Garry calls this one of the most counterintuitive facts about bringing ideas to life in startup land.

- Many founders facing copycats or heavy competition freeze, get scared, and stop sharing the idea. He says that is "almost the exact opposite of what you need to do."
- With lots of competition, lean in harder. Become so deeply associated with the problem space, and the best solution for it, that you win.
- That association is how you crowd out competitors. "If you clam up, you're doing exactly the opposite of what you need to do to beat your competition."

## How to beat copycats: outrun them

- Execute faster, go to market faster, and innovate ahead of everyone else.
- Then even people trying to copy you can only copy the version of you from six or nine months ago.
- His summary: "You can beat the copycats, but you've got to outrun them."
- He closes with Jeff Bezos on competition: the number one thing behind Amazon's success "by far" is obsessive focus on the customer rather than obsession over the competitor. Bezos says he can tell when other founders talk about customers but are really focused on competitors.
