## How YC companies reach $10M+ ARR with tiny teams: vertical AI, compressed sales, and a 10%-a-week growth baseline

Garry argues that AI has changed how fast software revenue compounds.

- He says it is now routine to see YC companies with "10 or 20 people get to 10 or 20 million a year in revenue in 10 or 20 months", which has "literally never happened before in software".
- During the 12-week YC batch, average revenue growth is about 10% per week, and some batches have run 15-20% per week. That has held for more than a year.
- Before, 10% a week was the aspirational outlier for one or two companies. He estimates the old average was closer to 2-4% per week.
- The reason: buying software used to be a "nice to have" for replacing spreadsheets or SAP. Now a prospect sees a demo, can see it hit their bottom line immediately, and says "I need it right now. When can you start?"
- His read is that "there's something in the water", and that this is the best time in the history of startups to start one.

## Case Text: how a niche legal data company became a huge AI business

Garry uses Jake Heller's Casetext (YC, 2012-13) as the example of timing and moats.

- Casetext started as a "Web 2.0 for law" product. It indexed case law, legislation, judgments and other legal documents, grew mostly through SEO, and ran for close to 10 years.
- Garry says some products can only ever total up to about 10, 20 or 50 million in value. Founders worry about that ceiling early, but he thinks the worry may be premature.
- The users, understanding and corpus of data Casetext had built became a moat "right at the correct moment as technology itself shifted". Something that could make tens of millions a year could suddenly make hundreds of millions to billions.
- Jake had early access to GPT-3 through the YC community (OpenAI spun out of YC Research). It was a toy then: it hallucinated, and in law one wrong answer gets you fired.
- With GPT-4, Jake found that shrinking the question to a small enough step made output reliable. Garry says this is what we now call context engineering. Asking "is the defendant guilty" fails; chunked tasks work.
- Jake's method:
  - Ask what a human would do, e.g. skim each sentence or paragraph and score whether it belongs in a timeline.
  - Write evals and tests across many inputs and outputs for each specific task.
  - Do a tailored time-and-motion study of how a lawyer actually works.
- Garry compares Jake to "the first man on the moon" for inventing early prompt engineering.

## Selling AI to buyers: demo the money saved and the "feat of strength"

Garry describes why Casetext's sales cycle collapsed.

- The first useful product combed through thousands of pages of documents and produced an accurate timeline of events. A legal analyst or associate would cost thousands to tens of thousands of dollars for that work.
- So the pitch becomes "I will save you this much money, direct to the bottom line. Do you want to buy?", which "becomes an easy answer".
- The demo used the corpus of Enron emails. Jake's example was asking about nuanced things like ironic jokes the CEO made, and the model found the joke about their fraud and the reference. Garry says lawyers saw it and said they needed to buy it right now.
- Garry's takeaway is that the "feat of strength" demo matters. It was astonishing enough to end the evaluation.
- He says he remains astonished as new models ship.

## Competing against OpenAI, Google and big incumbents: scope to a specific job

Asked how startups compete with ChatGPT and Gemini, Garry gives two answers.

- Small teams use frontier models but adapt them to the "very specific things that real people in the economy actually need". Those are often unglamorous, such as customer support for HVAC.
- The AOA example (YC company): customer support for HVAC firms. Service Titan is the incumbent software.
  - HVAC firms spend about 1% of revenue on software like Service Titan, i.e. about $1 per $100 of revenue.
  - They spend $5-6 per $100 on people answering phones and scheduling.
  - A product aimed at that labor spend can take over the 1% and expand it "5x or 6x bigger".
- You "can't just take ChatGPT and have it do this type of work yet". He says it is conceivable eventually, but while it is not true, people are building the next Service Titans.
- On horizontal vs vertical (the ReadAI view that one tool should do everything and just adjust for sales): he says it may be too early to tell and does not claim certainty. His own YC evidence points to narrow verticals.
- He lists "infinity" of off-the-beaten-path, underserved verticals: HVAC, accounting, compliance, audit. "Where there's brass, there's gold."
- He also names consumer as a still non-consensus bet. ChatGPT is "the best and most astonishing consumer launch of any product in the history of products".

## Staying lean and focused as a founder

Garry pushes back on the idea that the classic YC advice has changed.

- He says Sam Altman wondered whether "make something people want" and staying lean was still right, then changed his tune once he saw that startups using OpenAI's APIs are effectively its commercialization arm.
- Garry's view: "a lot of people who start really huge companies had to start companies that were much more specific." Elon Musk started with Zip2.
- His reframe: YC wants founders to be "directly in control of their own destiny" to the extent they can.
- Vibe coding and AI code generation lower the cost of building. He says the Claude Code team reportedly writes about 95% of its code with Claude, so each engineer does the work of about 20.
- He expects verticalized software for niches (his example: a CRM for podcasters or HVAC, rather than only HubSpot or Salesforce) because quality software has always been inaccessible. Even Apple has basic Calendar bugs.
- If codegen keeps improving, software quality itself can become the way HVAC firms compete with each other.
- The ideal is a founder who is a strong engineer and 20x's themselves with AI tools. A 10x engineer plus 20x becomes "200x".
- Even founders who miss a huge outcome gain: he cites his Posterous exit to Twitter and a batchmate (BackType) team that ended up building Twitter ads. Facing real users and shipping product teaches you faster than slow bureaucratic jobs.
- He notes YC applicants aged 18-22 are up over 100% year over year, 22-25 up about 20%, and 25-30 down 10-20%. He says many in the older group started during ZIRP and are hanging on at FANG or startups, and some are the biggest AI deniers.

## Consumer AI products and speed as the real advantage

Garry sees consumer as the next opportunity and discusses what separates good AI wrappers.

- He points to Rosebud AI (his YC batchmate Chris Bader's journaling and therapy-style app). It is a small team of two or three, with very high retention and paying users.
- Counselor and therapist-style use is a major ChatGPT behavior change. The product works because it replaces a more expensive, less accessible thing (a therapist) and remembers prior sessions.
- He compares its slow organic growth to Evernote.
- Andrew's feedback was that the product lacked design polish. Garry's response: that kind of feedback is fixable "tomorrow".
- On whether thin wrappers can become real companies: ideally the founders are strong engineers who use AI tools to move much faster. MVPs built on top of other tools are a starting point.
- For second-time and repeat founders, he says the most important thing is time. With resources already in hand, the only thing that matters up front is speeding up, and "it takes a village to actually properly speed up".

## How YC changed under Garry, and how partners work with founders

Garry describes what he changed when he returned as president and CEO.

- He frames YC 1.0 as Paul Graham, Jessica Livingston, Trevor Blackwell and Robert Morris setting the vibe. The second decade under Sam Altman and Jeff Ralston turned YC into an Alphabet-like set of competing efforts (nonprofit, research, Continuity growth fund).
- The board, including Brian Chesky, decided to go back to being "Google" instead of Alphabet. "It's like a thousand times easier to be Google than to make Alphabet work."
- Hardest cut: Continuity. The people involved now run their own funds and are doing well.
- YC's focus is the initial batch and the partnership:
  - Group partners are no longer "camp counselors". The title is now general partner, 15 equal partners, similar to Benchmark's structure.
  - Each company has at least one primary partner who stays in touch years later, like "your best angel investor who is there for you all the time".
  - "Let's stop competing with all the other VCs. Let's be their partners."
- The old model's problems: if you did not get a Continuity check, it could be a negative signal, and handing founders off after demo day made it a compartmentalized job that was easier for the firm but "less fun" and weaker for founders.
- What a partner does in practice:
  - Understand the business, the founders and their customers, then ask questions: could this be bigger, what is the most interesting thing you have learned about your customer, what gets people promoted, what resonates.
  - Be frank about what has not worked: if you are spending money or time on it, set it to zero, and double down on what works.
  - Act as a sounding board outside the day-to-day.
- He says the YC batch provides dozens of peers who came out of a top-1% process. In his own batch, the Heroku founders helped Posterous raise its seed and give scaling advice, then later asked for Posterous's codebase to debug their own scaling issue, and Garry's team shared it.
- He says having one person who has "taken an oath to look out for you" for the life of the company is better than 90% of startup founders get.

## Using AI to write YouTube scripts: Garry's prompt-iteration workflow

Garry shares a concrete process for building a repeatable content prompt.

1. Fed the scripts of his top-performing YouTube videos into Gemini (2.5, chosen for its long context). Asked it to extract the salient features common across them.
2. It surfaced patterns such as: hook hard and fast, an inner-game lens, founder and creator psychology, and a sentence rhythm of claim, brisk explainer, vivid example, takeaway.
3. Turned that into a prompt: given a set of notes or ideas, write a script in this format. Feeds in raw material such as notes, a set-piece idea, a tweet or a video clip.
4. Had the model produce three-act narratives. A 10-minute script that would normally take "two, three hours" or several hours of "breaking my brain" can be ready on his phone during a commute.
5. Iterated: label and number each script, use ChatGPT's canvas, and edit as if directing a junior writer.
6. At the end of each session, asked: "given what we did in this session to improve the prompt, output the next version of the prompt." He has done this about 20-30 times.

- The prompt grew into a "grab bag of tricks" the AI and he co-discovered over 10-15 scripts, including a pop-culture cold open (a 15-45 second film or TV news clip that mirrors the thesis before the hook) and an authority pillar quoting Paul Graham, Alan Watts or Naval Ravikant.
- First versions were bad; the output improved with each pass.
- His rule: if you give no direction and just say "write me a script", it is bad. The creator has to inject their own voice or it is "slop". He treats the model as a co-writer.
- He plans to publish the prompt session publicly.
