## Pre-A is the right window, and YC multiplies progress
Garry says the most common question he gets is whether a team is too early or too late for YC. His answer is that YC can help any company before its Series A.

- Teams range from fresh college grads with an early prototype to companies that already raised a seed round and have revenue and employees.
- He cites Crowdtilt and Thalmic Labs as some of the best-performing YC companies, and both came in having already raised seed rounds. They did so well during YC that they raised competitive Series A and later rounds right out of demo day.
- His framing: "It turns out to be a multiplier on success."
- For first-time money seekers, he says this is not a summer program or an internship. You are committing to build a serious business and telling investors you will return their money plus a healthy return. "It's not a grant."
- His reason for doing startups: to create massive value and reward yourself and the people who help create it.

## Hair on fire problem, or do it right
Garry argues technology creates no value by itself. It only matters as a way to solve people's problems. Great hackers get caught up in the technology and forget this.

He repeats Paul Buchheit's two categories of successful startup ideas:
- They solve a "hair on fire" problem that has never been solved before.
- Or they solve a problem that already has solutions, but do it so much better that they are "done right."

How to pitch each:
- **Hair on fire:** state how bad the problem is. How do you know it exists, who has it, and how specifically have you solved it? A new problem is an uphill battle just to let people know you exist, so show you can get people to notice you.
- **Crowded space:** be very clear about how your solution is unique. "It's not OK to do exactly the same thing." Explain what you have figured out that nobody else has.

His rule of thumb: "If you're new, focus on explaining what you are. If you're not new, focus on explaining how you're different. If you're neither of these types, you're doing it wrong."

## Founding team: builder and hustler
Garry used to say a startup needed three roles (designer, engineer, hustler). He changed his mind after seeing evidence to the contrary, and now says the ideal team needs two: **builder** and **hustler**. Designer and engineer collapse into "builder." Some enterprise software companies don't need much design, but all tech startups need tech cofounders.

- He has seen teams of hustlers fail because they could not create any product on their own.
- He has seen teams of pure builders (amazing designer/engineers) fail because they could not get the product into users' hands.
- The roles don't have to map one person to one role. What matters is that the team is capable of both.
- If you lack a skill, find your most talented and trusted friends who have it and get them to join you.
- Be realistic about what you and your teammates are good at.
- On cofounders: "You are getting married to your cofounders." You are on a life raft in the ocean together and must work well together to survive.
- Starting without a strong team is "going to war without guns, ammo, training, or all of the above."

## Write clearly and concisely
Garry says that over five years this became more obvious as examples piled up: the best startups are incredibly good at writing and communicating in as few words as possible.

- Paul Graham's lesson: if a founder can't express an idea clearly to others, the likeliest explanation is that the idea isn't clear to them either.
- He points applicants to PG's essays as the standard to aim for. Everything should be crisp, articulate and to the point.

His writing process:
- Write down every idea copiously and brainstorm to get it all out.
- Then edit. Group similar concepts next to each other.
- Edit mercilessly until no word can be removed without losing significant meaning.

The common pitfall is trying to sound professional. He says to use the plain English you would use to explain things to normal people with no special background. Sounding corporate or like a press release is "among the worst ways to communicate in the history of language." Writing should communicate, not get in the way.

His rule of thumb: does it sound like something you would say to a friend? If not, rewrite it.

## Get feedback from as many people as possible
Garry advises getting as many trustworthy, intelligent eyes as possible on a written application. He says this holds for any application, because more reviewers help you flesh out concepts, spot weak links and strengthen your case.

- Ask your mentors.
- Email founders of YC companies, or founders of any company, for feedback.
- His reasoning: "We're here to help -- someone helped us."

## Keep building whether or not you get in
Garry's closing advice is to keep working on the startup no matter what the outcome is. He says you don't need anyone to give you a "permission slip" to create your own startup.
