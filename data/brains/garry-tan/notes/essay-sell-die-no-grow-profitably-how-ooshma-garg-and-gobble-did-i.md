## Default alive: Garry's lesson from Gobble's pivot to profit

Garry (a Gobble board member) describes how Ooshma Garg took Gobble from burning over $1M a month to earning nearly that much in profit, with a global pandemic going on, after a failed Series C.

- Gobble's overfunded competitor Blue Apron had a badly underperforming IPO. Garry says it damaged the whole meal kit market.
- Gobble could not close a Series C. Garry lists the four options Ooshma faced: shut down, recap, sell (asset sale, customer list sale or acquihire), or turn the company around and operate profitably.
- Garry notes that most founders end up with one of the first three. Ooshma chose the fourth, profitability, after reading her retention cohorts and cutting marketing spend.
- Garry says the result was that "we basically like raised a series C of millions of dollars in free cash ourselves" without diluting the company.
- His takeaway: "you can go profitable, or at least you can try", and "it's a very important tool for the tool belt".

## Markets are voting machines early and weighing machines later

Garry argues that early-stage startups live and die by popularity, and later by fundamentals.

- "The game is a popularity contest early. It's basically a voting machine early, and then later, it becomes a weighing machine."
- The weighing questions he names are: what kind of business have you built, what are the metrics, and does it throw off money?
- With Gobble, the market "voted against" the meal kit category, even though insiders could see from the competitive numbers that Blue Apron was not executing as well as it could.
- Capital raising is "somewhat divorced" from having the best product or service.
- Because the Series C partners could not bring the rest of the firms over the hump in an out-of-favor category, being able to generate your own cash gives a company leverage that a sale or recap does not.

## Cohort retention is the best health check, and it takes time to read

Garry calls cohort retention "one of the most important tools for every founder" because it shows the health of the business as a brand and a going concern.

- Plot customers who bought in month zero. The curve starts at 100% and drops each month.
- The best, enduring brands have an asymptote that "stretches into infinity".
- He ties this to valuation: the price of equity is the discounted future cash flows of the business, so knowing your retention cohorts tells you whether the company is actually valuable.
- Founders often cannot tell early. It can take six months, nine months or years to see what kind of business you have ("10-year overnight success", 2011 to 2020).
- Ooshma modeled scenarios before cutting marketing: if marketing stopped, would 20%, 40%, 50% or 60% of users remain in six months, and what would they do in each case? A vast majority stayed, which she called the most exciting validation in 10 years.

## Digital ad spend: marginal customers get worse, and "turn off marketing" is often a lie

Garry explains the demand generation dynamics behind Gobble's turnaround.

- Digital marketing (Facebook and Google ads, direct mail, email marketing and other tools) has depth. The first customers are well targeted, sticky, high LTV and cheap to acquire. As you chase more marginal customers, they get worse, especially with a huge amount of capital funneled in.
- If you need to show 3x or 5x growth a year, "you could probably do it by burning a mountain of cash."
- Founders have a growth knob: grow faster and burn more, or grow slower and more sustainably. Even 50% a year growth with real cash generation can be a great business.
- Ooshma's warning, which Garry endorses in discussion: "one of the biggest lies that startups tell themselves and their boards is that they can turn off marketing and be profitable." Near-cancerous marketing growth can obscure cohorts and long-term retention while you just refill a bucket.
- The test: if you turned the marketing knob off, are you already profitable underneath the extra spend? Be honest about it, especially in an environment like a pandemic.

## Garry's Posterous regret: never trying to charge

Garry uses his own startup, Posterous, as the counterexample.

- Posterous had tens of millions of uniques a month and close to a million regular posters.
- If even a small percentage had paid $5 a month, he says the company would have been "wildly profitable" on a base of five or 10 engineers.
- Nearly all the market value created came from the initial seed round. Seed investors, common shareholders, co-founders and the team did fine, but he doesn't think the Series A or B investors made much money.
- He attributes this to not being cash efficient and never charging money, and calls it "one of the biggest regrets I have as a founder."
- Lesson: you can try going profitable, and most founders never even try.

## Why Gobble's product is defensible: proprietary food IP

Garry backs the product thesis that made Gobble's retention work.

- Typical meal kits gave you a tomato or an onion plus an instruction card, which Garry calls the "onion in a box" model, and took about an hour to make.
- Gobble's approach is better, faster and cheaper: proprietary sauces and recipes that cut prep time by 30 to 50 minutes. Garry says customers will happily pay an extra dollar or $2 a meal for that.
- Ooshma's version: "the secret sauce is the sauce". Gobble has 2,000 proprietary SKUs and sauces, and you can't recreate a Gobble recipe at the grocery store. Convenience makes it sticky and proprietary flavor makes it stickier.
- Garry's framing is that this contrasts with competitors using commodity ingredients, where deal hunters switch between boxes weekly and the cost of maintaining the customer base is far higher.
- Shipping boxes via USPS, FedEx or UPS also helped contribution margin per box, since multiple nights of food go in one box instead of one delivery per night.

## Finding other founders for real talk

In closing, Garry reinforces Ooshma's advice for her 22-year-old self.

- The hardest startup problems are the unknown unknowns, so have closed-door conversations with founders a step or two ahead of you or in your industry.
- Startup culture pushes a "killing it" posture at conferences and VC dinners.
- Garry's advice: find the tribe of people you can be real with. "If you don't find that, then this whole thing becomes 10 times harder."
