## Why good startup ideas look bad at first

- Garry says most founders copy the same ideas: "photo-sharing, podcasting, travel plans, local event apps." He says "desire is mimetic. We want what other people want."
- Founders copy whatever has already succeeded, because that is the obvious thing to follow.
- Garry says that is the wrong approach. The most famous startups were not famous when they started.
- He quotes Chris Dixon: great disruptive ideas are hard to spot because "when they start they're toys." Examples he gives:
  - The personal computer ("why would anyone want a computer on every desk?").
  - Bitcoin ("your magical internet beans are a joke").
  - Social networking (people asked how Facebook could ever make money).
- Garry's warning: hundreds of thousands of people now chase the same ideas, and as Peter Thiel says, "infinite competition is for losers."

## Your unique experience is the source of the idea

- Garry endorses Steve Jobs's point about innovation. You need to zoom out and connect experiences, and you can't have "the same bag of experiences as everyone else" or you will make the same connections.
- His advice: get different experiences, embrace being weird, and know that "what makes you new, different and unique in the world will be the exact thing that makes you succeed." He says this is why diversity matters.
- Find a problem that needs solving from inside your own experience, rather than copying the founders below.

## Flexport: a founder who knew a hidden market intimately

- Garry was an early investor in Flexport (through Y Combinator and his fund Initialized). It is now worth more than $3.2 billion.
- His take on meeting Ryan Petersen: he knew immediately that Ryan had "stumbled onto a huge market."
- Garry's favorite pitch meetings are "with founders who teach you something about the world you didn't know before." Global logistics was one of those, and Ryan knew it intimately.
- Ryan's path:
  - One of the first Amazon third-party sellers, roughly 10 to 15 years before the video, paying $40 a month.
  - Imported motorbikes and electric scooters from China.
  - Lived in China for two years running the supply chain.
  - Never treated it as a startup. In Ryan's words, it was "a couple of guys trying to earn a living."
- The idea came from personal experience of the supply chain.

## Patreon: seeing a broken problem early as a creator

- Garry met Jack Conte and Sam Yam very early, when they were just starting Patreon. Patreon is now worth more than $1.2 billion.
- Jack started the band Pomplamoose with his wife and posted music videos on YouTube from 2008, in the first wave of creators using the platform.
- He saw that monetization for creators was "totally broken" and that there had to be a better way for people reaching millions online to make a living.
- Garry's takeaway: they saw the problem very early because they lived it, and that funded "a new generation of creator."
- His caveat: "These are just breadcrumbs. History doesn't repeat, but it rhymes." Don't do what they did; find what is unique to you.

## Pitfalls: novelty and imperfect first versions

- You don't have to be novel. Flexport and Patreon were not the first attempts at their ideas.
- If there is competition, you must be better, faster and cheaper to stand a chance. "Competition is not your friend. It's fierce and it will make your startup fail."
- The first version doesn't need to be perfect. Garry cites Chris Dixon on disruptive tech being dismissed as a toy because it undershoots user needs while still developing.
- Example: the first telephone carried voice only a mile or two. Western Union passed on acquiring it because it couldn't see the use for businesses and railroads. They failed to anticipate how fast the technology and infrastructure would improve.
- The same mistake, he says, was made by mainframe makers about the PC and by telecoms about Skype.

## How to find a good idea: Paul Graham's process

- Garry points to Paul Graham's 2012 essay on startup ideas. The advice he highlights:
  - Work on hard problems, driven mainly by curiosity.
  - Keep a "second self watching over your shoulder, taking notes of gaps and anomalies."
  - Give yourself time. You can control how fast you become a prepared mind, but not when the stimuli that spark ideas arrive.
- The cautionary example: if Bill Gates and Paul Allen had forced themselves to come up with an idea in one month, they might have picked a month before the Altair even appeared.
- They saw the Altair in a magazine in Harvard Square, wrote to the company, and offered to do a BASIC for it. Altair BASIC became a hit and the company became Microsoft.

## Garry's closing advice

- Don't build another "photo-sharing-travel-plans local events" startup.
- Find something new, follow your interests, and note gaps, anomalies and things you genuinely believe the world needs.
- Keep improving your builder skills: engineering, product management, leadership, consistency, conscientiousness and communication.
