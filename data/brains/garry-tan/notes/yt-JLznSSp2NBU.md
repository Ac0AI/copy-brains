## How Garry builds on distribution, AI leverage and YC's simple selection process

**Note on source:** The transcript attributes the speaker only as the YC CEO, and the speaker is addressed as Garry throughout.

## YC's simple, fast selection process and the numbers behind it

Garry describes YC as "kind of a miracle" that is earnest and simple: make something people want, apply, and YC tries to find the things that could be really big.

- The terms are the same for everyone: about 7% equity plus a bunch of money, and a room full of the top 1% of people building.
- The application is a one-page online form. The interview is 10 minutes, then a thumbs up or down.
- He argues this simple process picks winners at a higher rate than investors with elaborate processes. Banks pitch "differentiated process" and "smartest people in the room," but YC runs the simpler process and wins.
- YC has 14 equal group partners (himself included). They hand-read applications, and the 10-minute interview is with a human, not an AI.
- Garry says roughly 5-10% of companies in a batch become $1B+ companies. The numbers he cites on that:
  - Airbnb: under $20,000 invested became about $2B at IPO.
  - He estimates a few billion dollars of value created per batch.
  - He puts it at roughly $20M of carry per employee per year, and close to $100M per employee per year in value created overall.
- He says a YC white paper is coming. It looked at investors who put money into at least 3 YC companies per batch from 2018 to 2020. The top decile returned 16x, the top quartile 8x, the median 5x and the bottom quartile 3.3x. For comparison, he says a top-quartile VC fund in a given year is typically about 3x.

## What early winners look like and the "capital as a service" idea

- Garry says extreme winners look very similar to average founders early on. Brian Armstrong of Coinbase was a solo software engineer working on anti-fraud at Airbnb who had never started a company, never done a product launch and never raised money.
- His profile of who YC funds: "highly technical people who are just like sort of smart and earnest."
- He says the point of YC's early work with founders is the basics, such as launching and raising.
- Capital as a service, which he credits to Chamath's idea, is plausible to him. A founder connects Stripe, a bank account and QuickBooks, an LLM ranks whether the business is real and does the due diligence, and anyone who clears the bar gets money.
- He says the hard part is getting really smart people to work on a thing like that.

## Why founders must build their own audience and distribution

- Garry argues that if you run a business today you kind of have to be a creator. Otherwise you must buy eyeballs, and Google and Meta hold the monopoly on them.
- Their second-price auction mechanism extracts every incremental dollar of gross margin. If you and a competitor sell the same widget with the same supplier, the ad auction takes the difference.
- He calls saying interesting, valuable things on YouTube, X, Instagram and TikTok "the purest form of alpha that exists still." He thinks TikTok is giving away distribution to new creators, and that Meta is dropping the ball on creators by only serving those who are already big.
- This applies to consumer businesses, and sometimes to dev tools and B2B too. He says consumer has lacked breakout businesses for about 10 years, and distribution is why.
- AI has not broken the big tech distribution advantage. He says big tech has "a total stranglehold on the incremental user."

## B2B sales mindset

- About 70-80% of YC companies are B2B. The sales mini conference is the best attended each batch.
- YC gives every founder a copy of Pete Kazanjy's book *Founder Sales*.
- Most of the shift is mindset: most people have never been rejected 95 times out of 100.
- His framing: a website converting at 5% is phenomenal. Knocking on 100 doors and getting 95 no's feels like failure, but five enterprise contracts at $10,000-$100,000 a year makes you a big winner.

## Building AI businesses that replace rote knowledge work

- He argues LLMs have opened up almost anything a knowledge worker with roughly 130 IQ or below can do, and software can now do it without management headaches. He calls it a profound moment for all entrepreneurs.
- He expects 20-person companies to reach $100M to $1B a year. Large corporates are "completely unprepared" and will be run over by agile startups.
- The playbook he gives YC founders:
  1. Find real businesses spending hundreds of thousands of dollars a year on teams doing rote, repetitive knowledge work.
  2. Get access to their data and the flow of their work, and watch how they do it.
  3. Write test cases (evals) against that work.
  4. If the LLM hallucinates or is inconsistent, you are asking too much of it. Chop the prompts into smaller steps.
- He says current models handle roughly "120 IQ level work."
- Pivot advice: if a company selling to accounting firms struggles with enterprise sales, target people who already pay for a call center in the Philippines or Eastern Europe and replace it with an LLM plus good workflow and evals.
- He warns against "demo ware" and against "raw dogging your prompts" with no tests. Write tests and work test-driven.
- Moat strategy: land grab now. Companies that build evals, data access, switching costs and sales force today get a roughly 10x cost-structure drop when the next generation of models arrives. They would already hold revenue and market position against software incumbents. Models may also absorb some of what founders build, which is the risk.
- He thinks the value of coding yourself drops. What matters is instructing "virtual developers" precisely about what you want and the use cases.

## Lessons from Posterous and Palantir

- Posterous (2008) was a blog-by-email platform that grew 10x year on year for two years until Instagram arrived. Garry says they did not fully understand why they were growing: the iPhone was new and there was no easy photo-upload app.
- Framing mistake: TechCrunch framed Posterous as easier than Tumblr, and the team made that competition their identity. His lesson is "you sort of allow media or other people to sort of frame your reality and then you just play the wrong game." They were really competing with how people got photos off their phones.
- What he would do differently: with about a million dedicated users and a team of 12, charging $5-10 a month would have made them profitable. He points to Weebly, which raised very little, was profitable and compounding, and sold to Square for roughly $300-400M.
- Benchmark's Peter Fenton passed on their Series A after asking "are you a platform or a network?" and getting the answer "both." Garry says that is what founders without strong opinions say.
- His advice: "make something people want and that's the ending itself." Play your own game rather than the one chosen for you by investors, social media or tech press.
- He turned down Peter Thiel's offer (including a $70,000 check) to co-found Palantir in 2004. He then joined as employee number 10 and designed the logo in 2006. His first real million came from selling Twitter stock at IPO, after about 18 years in tech.
- Lesson he draws: "the world doesn't happen to you." Palantir's founders learned the CIA and NSA lacked good software by going to talk to them directly. Talk to users and gather first-source information rather than taking your worldview from media or social media.
- On Palantir's slow start, he says founders should not copy it unless they are Peter Thiel and willing to put tens of millions of their own money in and wait years.

## Keeping YC unconventional, and the lessons from big organizations

- From Paul Graham he learned to be wary of formality and corporateness. He recalls PG scraping the maintenance sticker off a corporate copy machine because it reminded him of Yahoo. YC's ethos mixed "very earnest and very formidable" with a family-like welcome. He says he stays up at night thinking about how YC never feels corporate.
- He says that wherever there is prestige or convention you should be wary, because soul-sucking baggage usually comes with it.
- On starting businesses: many people play business (business cards before a customer, then OKRs and quarterly measures) and have to pull themselves out of it.
- On big companies: he argues that bureaucracy impeded AI progress. At Google, compute was scarce and political, so papers ended up with 30 authors, while OpenAI could commit resources. Big tech is "big daycares for the most technical smart people."
- He notes it took Herculean effort by product people inside OpenAI to even ship ChatGPT, since the lab planned to keep GPT behind an API.
- He says Sam Altman was right to jump on AI when the smartest people were already talking about it, and that he himself was skeptical at first but is now a believer.

## Early career hustle and the spoon-bending lesson

- At 14, Garry cold-called businesses from the Yellow Pages offering to make web pages. He got a job at a local web design firm (Infolane) that built city sites, paid about $7-10 an hour, where he learned graphic design and HTML. His parents were food insecure and his dad lost jobs. He used his earnings to help his family make the down payment on a house in Fremont.
- He started an online zine with junior high friends that readers found through Usenet. Nobody knew they were kids.
- He joined YC at 27 and says he wishes he could tell his younger self not to sell his time but to make products.
- The spoon-bending story (Burning Man): the workshop pitches bending a spoon with your mind, but people really bend it with their hands. About half the room did, and the rest could not figure out how. His moral: believe you can change the world with ideas, but "you actually have to go with your hands and go talk to users and sit and build a thing." He calls it an allegory for bias to action.
