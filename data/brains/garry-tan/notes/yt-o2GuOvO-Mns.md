## Why Garry left Posterous: the cost of avoiding conflict

- Posterous grew 10x yearly and became a top-200 site in about two years. By the end of 2010 growth had flatlined, and the co-founders (friends since college) could not get along.
- Garry resigned from his last startup at the end of 2010. In the weeks before, he couldn't sleep or eat and his heart rate felt pegged at 120. He says the physical symptoms were his non-verbal brain "going on strike" against a story he told himself.
- He calls co-founder disputes "the number one early startup killer." In hindsight he says the rift was "entirely preventable."
- His warning: "You can't count on good times forever... winter is coming." Good growth hides problems underneath.

## What went wrong: too little conflict

- Garry and his co-founder stopped spending time together because they were avoiding conflict. When the business needed them most, they hadn't resolved issues for so long that the relationship failed too.
- He mistook the appearance of a good co-founder relationship for a real one. His advice: "Don't make it look like a good startup or a good co-founder relationship. Spend time actually making it a good startup."
- He blames himself. On product, personnel and strategy he "buckled" and "self-abandoned," sacrificing what he knew was right to the idea that good co-founders don't fight. Resentment grew.
- Tells that you're in this state:
  - You haven't spent time with your co-founder outside work.
  - You keep interactions to a minimum.
- His first executive coach, Cameron Yarbrough of Torch, taught him to watch for the "four horsemen" from psychologist John Gottman: defensiveness, criticism, contempt, stonewalling. Gottman uses them to predict marital failure with high accuracy, and Garry says the same holds for co-founders.

## Fixing too little conflict

- If you disagree, don't leave the room until you have a resolution. If an hour isn't enough, cancel the weekend, go on a hike or camping trip, and hold a retreat on the spot. "Those other things can wait."
- He says some of the best billion-dollar company founders he knows did exactly this.
- Give more feedback. "Err on the side of giving more feedback," and communicate exactly what you feel, in the right way.
- He has always valued harmony, but learned (sometimes the hard way) that you can't sacrifice what you know is right to get it. "Don't swallow your words. If you have a point, make sure you are heard."

## Symptoms of too much conflict

- Decisions are hard to make, and every proposal gets viewed through "how will the different factions view this."
- Horse trading ("I can have this if you can have that"), long acrimonious meetings, and paralysis over small decisions.
- Uncertainty about which venue should even decide an issue.
- Settled decisions get re-litigated, with old arguments renewed and past outcomes used as justification for new conflicts.
- Being able to identify factions at all is a clear warning sign. Worst is blending unrelated decisions because different groups want different things.
- Usual root cause: roles aren't well defined, so people don't know who is in charge of what.

## Fix: one owner per area (the Heroku approach)

- Make a list of every area the business needs, decide who is best at each, and assign exactly one person.
- Garry recalls the Heroku founders doing this at Y Combinator: they listed every task the company did and found exactly one owner, a founder or executive "whose ass was on the line to deliver that thing."
- Why it works:
  - It's clear who to talk to, and because it's written down everyone can see it. The owner collects feedback before deciding.
  - You move fast. The owner makes the call, and everyone agrees to "disagree and commit."
  - You stop re-fighting the same battles and stop waiting weeks, months or years for decisions.

## Listening: steel man, don't straw man

- If conflicts are fundamental, start listening. The loudest person shouldn't automatically win; that's conflict avoidance of a different stripe, giving no space to competing ideas.
- Listen first and let the other side know you hear them. Repeat back the strongest version of their argument (a steel man).
- He cites a Stripe PM Shreyas Doshi thread on why listening is hard: fear of being wrong, desire for validation, lack of curiosity, urge to impress, feeling of superiority.
- Garry admits he is "terrible at this." He is often so busy composing what to say that he fails to be present, especially when the content cuts against his convictions. He says those are the most important moments to listen.

## Get outside help early

- Talk it through with other founders, friends, coaches, or whoever you trust. Getting out of your own head helps.
- Hire an exec coach: "I can't recommend it enough for founders," especially when company-killing conflict is on the line. He suggests bringing them in early, individually or as a group, like a marriage counselor.
- He recommends Torch (torch.io), the coach marketplace founded by his first exec coach Cameron Yarbrough, which he helped fund and start.
- Cost: don't cheap out. "Making the wrong decisions will cost you your company and your vested equity." Pick coaches appropriate to your company's stage.
- Involve your board, investors, advisors and closest founder friends. "You can't do this alone." Don't broadcast the problem, but keep trusted people close despite the embarrassment; they offer new perspective.
- He notes that pretty much every founder he has talked to has some form of this problem, and he offers DMs on Instagram to help people find support.
