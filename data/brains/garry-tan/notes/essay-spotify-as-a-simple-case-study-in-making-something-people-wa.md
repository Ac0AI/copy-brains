## Garry's starting point: a precise problem statement

- Garry says the Spotify story shows what YC tries to help founders create: "something people want."
- He notes that "all of the great era-defining startups seem to start" with a simple problem statement: what was broken?
- For Spotify, the problem was piracy. Daniel Ek's framing (quoted by Garry): Napster was a "great consumer product, not a great business model," and "It's not like people want to be pirates. They just want a great experience."
- Garry's takeaway: Ek knew exactly what was broken, which is why he could sketch a better product than the thing people were stealing.

## Turn the product vision into one measurable standard

- Spotify's product vision was that streaming should feel like the music is already on your hard drive. Ek's question was "What would it feel like?"
- He turned that feeling into a hard number: music starts in 200 milliseconds or less, about half an average blink, so people perceive no delay.
- Ek told his lead engineer Ludvig Strigeus: "I don't accept anything that isn't below two hundred milliseconds."
- Garry's point: because they knew how to measure success, the target was unambiguous. If streaming acts like local storage, you win.
- Pattern he draws out: pick the specific dimension on which your product must be better than the incumbent behavior (here, piracy), and make it testable.

## Hard technology that gets you off the ground

- Strigeus first said "It can't be done. The Internet isn't built like that." Ek replied, "You have to figure it out."
- The solution was a faster streaming protocol plus a custom peer-to-peer network. They had a working prototype in four months.
- Garry splits the question in two:
  - Can it be done? Until those early prototypes, it hadn't been.
  - Can it be cloned? "As with almost anything software related, yes."
- He argues novel tech is "not an infinite defense, but it was enough of one for Spotify to get off the ground."
- The advantage doesn't need to last forever. It only needs to last long enough that competitors are copying your innovation from 6 months ago. Fast following can take that long, which keeps unoriginal competitors at bay while you "blaze ahead."

## The schlep is the moat

- Ek assumed he could "pick up a global license" like buying from a corner store, and planned to launch in the U.S. and Europe together. In reality he had to negotiate with every copyright holder, a "herculean task."
- The labels initially weren't interested: Ek was an outsider, a Swedish techie, and the pitch was to co-opt the people who had been pirating their music.
- Ek's quote: "If anyone had told me going into this that it would be three years of crashing my head against the wall, I wouldn't have done it."
- Garry's lesson: if founders knew how much work these things would be, often they wouldn't do them (he links Paul Graham's essay "Schlep"). But those who do build "protectable long term enterprise value."
- Implication: the grind that deters others, like licensing, is itself the durable defense.

## Caveat: hindsight and building anyway

- Garry stresses the analysis is done with 20/20 hindsight. In the moment, Ek, his cofounders and investors had no idea they'd be right.
- They figured they might be, so they built it.
- The framework in sum: name what is broken, define a measurable better experience, build the hard tech to hit it, and push through the schlep others avoid.
