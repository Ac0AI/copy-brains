## Selling a startup: the three types of acquirer

Garry and his partner Andrew Lee split acquisitions into three types, ordered from highest to lowest purchase price. Garry's own exit: Posterous sold to Twitter for $20M.

- **Strategic threat:** the company threatens an incumbent's business. Example: YouTube bought by Google for about a billion dollars, with Viacom also bidding because it saw YouTube as a real threat.
- **Revenue generating:** the buyer expects to earn more than it pays. Example: Guitar Hero bought by Activision for about $100 million, because the CEO knew marketing spend would return more than the price.
- **Team and talent:** the buyer wants the tech or people. Example: Salesforce buying Quip for Brett's tech and talent.
- Garry's framing: "companies are bought, they are not sold." A bigger company has something to protect, such as a moat or a new business line, and goes out to buy.
- Most of the companies they back are meant to be strategic threats, so they either get bought or IPO and "crush their competition."

## Why Garry actually sold Posterous (and why founders really sell)

- Garry is blunt that most founders sell because the company is in trouble: "We were out of money... You made something good and then you're not growing. You can't raise more money. The company's going to die."
- The conundrum: buyers approach when you are growing and don't need anyone, which is the best time to sell.
- Posterous grew very fast for two years. Growth stopped when Instagram launched. Early iOS photo apps were primitive, so email was the easiest way to get photos off a phone, and Posterous grew on that new behavior.
- Garry's read on the flatline: "that's how we knew that we weren't reaching, we weren't continuing to scale product market fit."
- The exit: employees and investors made money and the team learned the craft, though Garry says he wanted to build for a billion people and didn't get there.
- Without another network to grow off, there was no virality.

## When to consider M&A: stage and risk

- Garry's plan A is always to build toward product-market fit, because the upside of a great company is "so obvious and so tantalizing."
- Pre-Series A, without product-market fit, there is nothing to sell except the team. That is acqui-hire territory, and he notes founders could just take jobs at big tech companies instead. Focus on product and market.
- Start thinking about M&A when managing risk. Every time you need to raise and you are not default alive, "the future of your company is not in your hands." He links Paul Graham's essay on default alive vs. default dead.
- Selling is trading growth for safety. At each stage you have built a team, product, customers, recurring revenue, sometimes negative churn. Any of these is valuable to someone else.
- His test: if you think you will run out of gas before the next gas station, or the station may be closed to you, consider M&A.
- Take care of employees, investors and yourselves: "It's okay to sell your business along the way, actually."
- Andrew adds that it is a personal decision that hurts, and founders feel like frauds or lose confidence.

## Four questions to ask before deciding (from Atul Gawande's "Being Mortal")

Initialized uses these end-of-life-care questions with founders:

1. **What is your understanding of the situation and its impact?** Runway, market landscape and similar factors.
2. **What are your hopes and fears?** You may still want your vision, and teaming up with someone else may be how you get there.
3. **What tradeoffs will you make, and not make?** Salary, the work itself, the people around you, location.
4. **What is the course of action?** The most important one; it turns the answers into a plan.

## Level-setting before you go to market

- Know your metrics: ARR, runway, burn, and debt. Debt "turns out to actually be a really big issue."
- Talk through where the co-founders and leadership stand.
- Watch for brilliant jerks. Garry agrees "that happens," and Andrew says it can kill companies and sometimes holds up a deal.
- Take the employee temperature, especially if you are aiming for a talent and tech acquisition.

## How to run a sale

- **Start with strategic partnerships**, especially in enterprise. It is hard to sell to a larger company that can't tell you apart from a competitor. Make them a customer or partner to build trust.
- **Go through product, not corp dev.** Find whoever runs the line-item budget or the relevant team. That person is your champion against decision-makers who don't want to buy.
- **Extend runway** by pulling forward sales and cutting burn. If you have to lay people off: "cut quickly, cut deeply."
- **Interview leadership first.** Talent acquirers will want to interview your employees. Do that only once real offers are on the table, because it is disruptive and scary to non-founders, who aren't built for this risk.
- **Check references and build a BATNA.** Ask the buyer about their previous acquisitions and speak to those founders or CEOs. In parallel, keep alternatives alive: a fundraise, higher revenue and lower costs, and competing offers from other acquirers.

## The #1 deal killer: treating M&A like a VC fundraise

- Garry says M&A deals most often die because founders run them like another fundraising process.
- Corp dev and VCs have different motivations, a principal-agent problem. What gets a junior VC promoted differs from what gets a corp dev person promoted.
- Corp dev wants to get the deal done but not at any price. Their incentive is not overpaying.
- VCs often have groupthink: more bidders makes them more rabid to win, especially with a 100x on the other end. That dynamic doesn't exist in corp dev.
- Without a BATNA, corp dev can be "extremely unscrupulous." Garry: "Deals fall through. Always maintain some sort of BATNA." Their entire job is to bring the price down.
- Andrew adds that corp dev often gets a bonus for coming in under their approved amount. Small deals go smoothly because they are all upside, and very large deals also close because they are existential strategic threats.

## Andrew's JamLegend sale and how they chose a buyer

- JamLegend was wedded to music games. It grew virally (C-Net Webware 100, Alexa 100) then flatlined.
- Zynga was doing "fast follows," copying rising games within a month or two and hiring talent. Production costs were rising, so staying independent meant raising a significant round.
- There was no offer on the table at first. The choice was whether to talk to people who had asked to acquire them.
- They met several companies, got multiple offers, and chose Zynga because it fit what they cared about: a place for everyone on the team and a return for investors. The Zynga IPO was expected in a year or two.
- Garry's close: as long as you build something valuable, even if it doesn't go to plan, there is a way forward that lets you take care of everyone around you.
