## Daily routine and protected deep work block

- Marc works every day of the year and says "I love my day so much that I want to repeat it every single day." He dislikes Sundays because making things is his fun, so he sees no reason to take a day off.
- The day starts with coffee and breakfast with his wife, then the gym together (currently Hyrox practice: sled push, squats, running).
- Back home, he goes into work mode and stays completely offline: no phone, no email, no social media.
- He then spends a 4 to 6 hour block of deep work at his desk, in his code editor, only creating things.
- He stays offline because of fear of missing out, especially with AI: checking social media makes him feel he is not fast enough, and an hour is gone and his motivation to work drops.
- He calls the deep work block "a moment of time that I protect" because it is what got him where he is.
- He sums up the daily rhythm as: create first, handle everything else after.

## Keeping email and support out of the creative window

- Marc does not check email or customer support during the morning block.
- His reasoning: if he reads that something is broken in his app first thing, he ends up fixing that bug instead of working on what matters most to him, which is creating more stuff.
- Around 4:00 p.m. he goes online, checks Twitter and email, and does the more boring, less creative work.
- Dinner is around 5:30 p.m., followed by a walk. After that he switches everything off: phone off, computer off, no talk about work.

## Shipping volume and the numbers game

- Marc says he makes $77,000 a month as a solo entrepreneur and has shipped 35 startups.
- Of those 35, about 30 are "nothing": barely any money, barely any users. He puts the hit rate at about 5%.
- His takeaway: roll the dice once and stick with that roll, and nothing crazy happens. Keep rolling and at some point one will work, bringing happy users, money, and the ability to leave a job and roll more often.
- He says he has met hundreds of people who talk about a brilliant idea that is only in their head or on their computer, unshipped. He cannot recall anyone saying "I have tried 10 times and it never worked."
- His closing advice is to keep shipping and not give up.

## Don't get stuck on one pet project

- A common mistake he sees is spending 6 months building one idea, getting a few users and a little money, and then becoming emotionally attached to it.
- That attachment keeps people on something that might take three years to pay enough to quit their job.
- He argues it is easier to keep rolling the dice with a new idea, which might take off "100x faster" than the previous one.
- Each new launch also teaches him more and lets him share the work, so he grows a small audience of people who discover it.
- His conclusion: playing the game of launching stuff is the recipe for something that eventually works.

## Validating ideas and overcoming fear of shipping

- "The only way to validate an idea is to ship it with a buy button."
- He says fear of showing a new creation, the voice saying "it's too early or it's not good enough", is the biggest barrier to success.
- He compares it to the first gym visit: intimidating before you step in, but after one session it is no longer new. Doing it once kills about 80% of the fear, and further iterations kill the remaining 20%.
- He says AI makes shipping even easier, so this is even more true.

## Choosing what to build next

- He thinks less about expected output and more about "what is the thing I want to exist."
- Example 1, a macOS app that uses the webcam to detect slouching. He built it because he spends so much time at the computer and thought "this app must exist." It was a new type of app for him (a macOS app, not a web app).
  - Pre-launch checks: does the licensing system work, and do people get the email after checkout.
  - Launch: went live around 3:00 p.m. with a tweet, also shared on LinkedIn, Threads and Reddit.
  - Result: about $1,000 in revenue the first day, good feedback, and people posting about it on Twitter. Seeing the app in users' hands is "the ultimate happy moment" for him.
- Example 2, a project driven by curiosity: after seeing a tweet from Peter Levels that many people fake their revenue, he built a leaderboard of verified revenues so people can see who is telling the truth. It turned into a marketplace that makes "35 plus K per month" (the transcript names it "Trust the Mual", likely TrustMRR).
- His to-do list always grows: shipping and getting user feedback reveals new problems and ideas, so he ends each day with more items than he started with.
- After finishing a cycle (such as the macOS app), he reviews the list and picks what would have the most impact on users or make him happiest, and does whatever comes next.

## Boring AI setup: the KPI is things shipped

- Marc holds a "slightly controversial" view: many people are obsessed with productivity and run teams of agents in the background, but the single KPI that matters is still the number of things you ship.
- His setup is deliberately boring: the same code editor with a chat on the right, single-threaded. He asks the AI for a feature, releases it, and moves on to the next.
- Output with this setup: about 300 features on his marketplace in the last 3 months, and six new apps in 2026.
- He argues people fall for the productivity trick, when you could do a lot with one of the first versions of ChatGPT and a basic code editor.

## Sleep and wind-down as a performance lever

- He goes to bed at 9:00 p.m. every day ("a bit religious"), with a 30 to 60 minute wind-down: dimming the lights at home, reading or sometimes a movie.
- He never sets an alarm and wakes around 6:00 to 7:00.
- He says that with good sleep he is more emotionally stable and has "super locked-in focus", able to spend 4 hours on the same thing.
- He says that in his 18 to 28 years he would get sleepy and fall asleep whenever he had to focus, and calls sleep "really underrated."
- He says the routine only felt worth it once he saw people liking his work and his apps getting happy users: sticking to the routine meant more of that kept happening.
