## Why free plans slow a solo founder down

- Marc built a habit tracker with a free and a paid tier over about a year, then sold it in October of the previous year.
- It drew 140,000 website visitors with no existing audience. About 10,000 signed up, and it made $6,000 by year end.
- His rule of thumb: only about 3% of free users upgrade.
- Example: a $10/month plan means the average user is worth about 30 cents. Covering a $3,000/month lifestyle would take about 10,000 new users and roughly 75,000 visitors every month.
- He says free plans suit VC-funded startups that can spend heavily on marketing and feedback. For a solo founder with little or no budget, "a free plan literally slows you down on the road to profitability."

## Alternatives to a free plan

Marc offers three ways to let people sample a product without a free tier:

- **Free credits:** users try the product for a very limited amount, enough to understand it without getting real benefit.
- **Short trial with a credit card required:** cap it at 7 days at most. Marc treats an email address and a credit card as forms of commitment, and the card makes users commit.
- **A standalone feature as a free tool:** this is his favorite as a developer. Examples are Ahrefs' free keyword research tool, which leads to a paid membership, and his own Logo product, where people generate logos for free and are pointed to ShipFast if they want to code a startup.

Free tools do three things:
- They satisfy a developer's urge to create.
- They let people try the product without signing up.
- They work as marketing and audience-building, since each small tool can be launched on Product Hunt, Reddit and Hacker News.

## Shiny technology syndrome: pick one stack and stay

- Marc used to spend lots of time trying every new tool, framework and design trend he saw in tweets and videos.
- Once he started earning money, he stuck to the same technology and spent the time on users instead.
- The result was fewer bugs because he knew his tools well, plus higher productivity.
- His reasoning: "users don't care about the invisible." They care about a solution to their problem, not the shape or color of your buttons.
- He calls new tools and libraries "an endless treadmill" and a distraction for developers who want to earn from their code.
- His advice is to choose one tech stack and stick with it. He uses the same one across all 24 of his products.

## Subscriptions versus one-time payments

- In May 2023 he built an AI landing page generator that sold at a one-time $39 and made about $100 to $200 per day. He thought it was probably his most successful product at the time.
- He switched to a $5/month subscription. Sales "literally dropped to zero" for days. He went back to the one-time payment and sales picked up again.
- Why users resist subscriptions:
  - They have to think about the future: what if I don't use it for two weeks, or forget to cancel?
  - There is a mental process involved in committing to a subscription that a one-time payment doesn't need.
- His comparison: selling a $49 one-time product is about as hard as selling a $9/month subscription.
- Subscribers also churn constantly. Signing up today doesn't mean paying in six months.
- One-time payments are pleasant for customers and help generate first online sales. Operating costs can be negligible: his app gets half a million page views a month and he pays about $20/month for Vercel. Even a user costing one cent a month stays profitable for years.
- If the service grows a lot, he suggests grandfathering existing users on their one-time license and introducing subscriptions for new users.
- With recurring costs, such as an AI wrapper around the OpenAI API, he suggests pay-per-use. He finds it fair to users, because if they don't use the product they don't pay, and he believes it will replace most subscriptions within a few years.
- His advice: start as simply as possible with a one-time payment or pay-per-use. Make your first €1,000, then think about subscriptions later.

## The headline is the first impression

- Average website conversion is about 2%. For a new site it is likely closer to 1% or less, so out of 100 visitors you get about one user.
- Most visitors never see the product, but 100% of them see the headline. Marc calls it the most decisive part of whether people buy.
- Spend enough time writing a really good headline before launching.
- A good headline is short, evokes an emotional reaction, and answers why a random stranger on the internet should give you one minute of their time.

## Overengineering and the 24-hour startup framework

- His first entrepreneurial project took a full year. He wanted it perfect, with many features, a long onboarding and dozens of screens. He ended up burnt out with zero users and zero euros earned.
- Because only about 1% of landing page visitors convert, most people will never use the product, so building features nobody needs is waste.
- The 24-hour startup framework: ask what the startup would look like if you had to set it up in 24 hours. Airbnb could be a simple spreadsheet, and Instagram could be a public Google Drive.
- He is not saying to keep a sketchy product. He says to start with the 24-hour version as the base and build from there, instead of a grand masterplan.
- He removes three categories from the first version:
  - **Invisible to the customer:** no tests, no Git branches, shipping straight to production, no TypeScript. A big support button sits on the site in case something breaks, and it is rarely used.
  - **No direct customer value:** no dark mode, no professional logo, no password reset form.
  - **Secondary features:** customers come with one problem, usually solved by one feature. Anything unrelated to it is cut.
- The goal is to launch fast, get feedback from real users early, and build the features that earn money.

## Results and the pattern behind them

- Marc built 24 website projects in about 2.5 years. Early on only 25% made money. In the most recent year, 100% of his side projects made money.
- He says it required "firing the developer in my head" to become an entrepreneur.
- He now launches only products people use, value and pay for.
- He says these five mistakes are what mostly separate solo developers who earn from side projects from those who don't.
