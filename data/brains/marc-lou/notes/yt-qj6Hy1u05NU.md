## Marc cold-emailed his way to a $5,000 customer before writing a line of product code

Marc's first paying customer came from a cold email and a 42-minute call in 2018, before he had a working product. The idea was an escape-room Messenger bot, a game that gave discount coupons to players who finished it. He sold it first, then built it.

## Sell before you build: validating with a cold email

- Marc was a weak programmer in 2018 and knew the build would take a long time, so a friend and marketer, Andre, told him: "why don't you just sell it before you build it."
- He sent short cold emails to escape room businesses, about three lines each, with the owner's name at the top and a brief explanation of the product.
- Replies came while he was in Japan on a visa run. One owner asked for a call.
- The call lasted 42 minutes. He calls himself "a terrible marketer and a terrible salesperson", and she still said yes and paid through a payment link sent right after.
- That customer ended up spending almost $5,000 on the bot by 2023. He calls it his first dollar online.

## Building the product after the sale

- With a paying customer, he assembled the product from pieces of code found on YouTube and GitHub, building even on buses and between acting gigs.
- The first version had no user authentication. Customers paid through a Stripe payment page, then filled out a form with their Facebook page and email, and Marc set them up manually.
- Marc says he did not need a polished product to start getting customers.

## Cold email workflow for finding leads

- He found leads by searching "escape rooms in [city]" on TripAdvisor, then visited each business's website for a contact email.
- To personalize, he went back to TripAdvisor reviews, where a team member or the founder often replied to customers, and took their name and sometimes their business email.
- He used cold email templates from Alex, who writes about cold emails that sell, and personalized them with the owner name and business name.
- He tracked everything in a four-stage sales pipeline: new contact, then up to four emails in total, with a follow-up sent 3 days after the previous one if there was no reply.
- Volume was about 10 new leads and 10 emails per day. That produced one to two replies and one to two calls per week.
- Once a lead was on Zoom, he estimates a 60 to 70% chance they subscribed. Zoom was not yet popular in 2018, so getting prospects on a call was hard.
- About 90% of his customers came from cold emails, with a little Facebook ads on top.

## Pricing and revenue growth

- He charged $199 per month for the bot, but churn kept revenue around $1,000 to $1,500 per month at first.
- He then tried yearly memberships at a discounted $2,000 price. To his surprise, some customers paid it, and a $2,000 Stripe notification was "massive" for him.
- He had 10 to 15 customers across the US, Europe and Australia. Revenue went from about $1,000 per month to a peak of roughly $6,000 per month.
- He lived in Bali on less than $1,000 per month at the time, so this was enough to be free.

## Covid, abandonment and what remained

- In 2020, escape rooms had to close. He chose to stop collecting payments until they reopened, so revenue was zero for 4 months.
- After reopening, he made about $1,300 to $1,500 per month with no marketing, from customers left over from 2019.
- Later, struggling personally, he abandoned the business completely. The product was an API and needed little upkeep, so existing customers kept it.
- Four years on, revenue was slowly shrinking and he expected it to reach zero around 2024. One large customer with two locations, which had spent almost $13,000 in total, churned, bringing it down to about $200 per month.
- He says this was not a "Silicon Valley unicorn", just a random guy making a living with an app in his bedroom.

## Takeaway

- Marc's message: start with what you have, because "you might be one cold email away from closing a $5,000 client."
- The business shaped his preference for working alone, with no employees or investors, after a failed AI startup in South Korea where he and a friend, with $11,000 in angel funding and two hires, never delivered anything valuable.
