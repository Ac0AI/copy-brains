## From $10/hr to $1.5M/year: Marc's path and the failures that preceded it

- Marc was born into an engineering family with no entrepreneurs around him. After graduating he saw *The Social Network* and decided he would build "a billion dollar startup idea," a Tinder for sports lovers.
- He worked part-time as a waiter at night, earning about $10 per hour, and spent a year on the app with no business plan and no way to monetize it. He says he was "so arrogant" and kept the idea secret.
- After 365 days he realized the app would never exist or make money, threw away the code in 24 hours, and moved to South Korea.
- Failure 2 (2017): an AI startup with a friend, both engineers, neither good at marketing or product. It took 6 months to build, then got zero customers.
- Failure 3: selling gloves advertised on Facebook. He was bad at Facebook ads, sold about 30 gloves, and made zero profit.
- He lived on no salary and no savings in a roughly 12 m² flat with cockroaches. That frustration made him crave "tiny project[s] that have quick instant results."
- In total he says he failed around 30 startups over about 5 years before anything lasted.

## Selling before building: how Marc got his first dollar

- After the failures he decided to start with a problem instead of an idea. His reasoning: every business wants more customers, so he picked a universal problem.
- The idea was a tool to help escape room businesses get more customers.
- A marketer friend told him: "why don't you just try to sell it before you make it." Marc did exactly that.
- Tactic: send cold emails, then get the lead on a call. His first call was with a business in Australia and lasted 42 minutes. At the end she said "send me the invoice I'll pay." He made her confirm twice because he couldn't believe it.
- Lesson he draws: you don't need a fancy website or a perfect product, just a solid idea and someone willing to pay.
- That project grew to about $1,000 per month, then a peak of $4,000 MRR. His routine was emailing in the morning and trying to get someone on a call, "exactly like I did for the first dollar."

## Surviving the wipe-out, and why he restarted with a new identity

- The business served physical businesses. When Covid hit, revenue went from $4,000/month to zero in about 24 hours, and two years of work "just suddenly disappeared."
- At 28, married and with no income, he hit an emotional low and took a software engineering job at $9,000 per month, after five years of making about $1,000 per month.
- The job gave him a sense of worthiness and time for sleep, reading and workouts. Within about two months, though, he missed freedom and started seeing indie developers who "don't raise money, don't work with the team, build tiny project, make it fast."
- He was fired a few months later while living in Bali and treated it as a trigger to restart.
- His new rules: never spend months on a product, never raise money, never have employees.
- His reframe: "each failure is not a failure if you don't give up." It is a matter of small bets, and unless he quits in the middle, the failures bring success later.

## Small bets, building in public, and what made products start earning

- He decided to share his work publicly so that, even if an app failed, he would have a small following to use for the next project instead of "starting again from zero."
- He shipped 6 projects in 7 months: Mood to Movie (movie recommendations by mood), Habits Garden (habit tracker, about 10,000 users), a productivity tips ranking tool, an AI landing page generator, and a link-in-bio tool for entrepreneurs. Income stayed around $1,000/month.
- Shift 1, painkillers over vitamins: he built apps that solve a real problem and put them behind a paywall. People know their pain, so a painkiller is "much easier to sell" than a vitamin like a habit tracker, which is fun but not needed.
- Shift 2, launch for eyeballs: he focused on the launch itself and made fun skits, such as being on a podcast with Joe Rogan or in a movie with Leonardo DiCaprio. People felt he was having fun, and revenue went from about $1,000 to about $4,000 per month.

## ShipFast: turning a reusable codebase into a product

- By mid-2023 he had built roughly 10 to 15 products and noticed he repeated the same steps: landing page, deploy, payment button, email, account creation.
- He built a reusable codebase in under a week, then decided to put a price on it and sell it to other developers (this became ShipFast).
- He removed expectations: he told his wife "maybe we'll make 100 bucks." After launching he went skating, and came back to $500 within 2 hours, which was about a week of previous income. The next day was about $3,000 to $4,000.
- First month: about $40,000. Black Friday day: about $8,000. Steady state around $50,000/month, with a $65,000 November.
- His product-market-fit signal: people messaged asking to pay by crypto and thanked him for changing their lives. "Never usually I'm trying to convince people to give me money."

## Growing ShipFast: free tools and a YouTube boost

- Beyond the launch, he did free tools marketing: he built a logo generator with a banner on the side saying, in effect, "are you building a startup? Ship it fast with ShipFast."
- A YouTube episode (the one that came later) added a lot of revenue.
- Revenue went from about $50,000/month to about $85,000 in the first month after, then about $135,000 in each of the next two months, and now has slowed to around $80,000/month.
- He frames the result as a $1.5M/year solo business with no meetings and no employees.

## Advice for starting

- There's a strong inner voice that overthinks and finds a reason the idea isn't perfect enough to launch. His answer: "start anything," and make the smallest version of the app as fast as you can.
- Learn where you're comfortable sharing: text on Twitter, video on TikTok, images and storytelling on Instagram. Then blend these and watch how things react.
- "I failed 30 startups... but if you don't quit then at some point you'll win." His formula: start now and ship fast.
