## Validation by payment, not by problem-hunting

- Marc says he does not search for ideas or problems: "I just make anything and then the problems will come to me."
- He built "pretty much useless" apps (e.g. one that ranks Naval Ravikant quotes). These create problems, and those problems become startup ideas.
- Each new product he makes answers a problem from a previous product, so the products chain into one another.
- His bottleneck now is too many ideas, so the work is filtering which to build.
- He uses money as the validation metric: "when you get paid you it means you create value somewhere." If people pay, he has made something they use and need.
- He spent years building in a void that nobody wanted, so he wants to be heard through paying customers.

## Filtering ideas and scaling down to ship fast

His rules for deciding what to build:

- Only apps he can monetize.
- Apps he would use himself.
- Markets he already knows a little.
- Paid from day one, no premium tier.
- Can he market it? Does he care about the market? Is he competent to code it?

His rules for shipping fast:

- Build only the one feature customers need, the one without which the app is useless.
- Skip everything else, for example password reset, to get the first version out as soon as possible.
- Having an audience helps ideas: "the more I see those things the more I want to build that stuff."

## Constraints, Bali and the low-budget approach

- He still endorses living on a small budget in a cheap country (the "10K in Bali for a year" idea). He says "100%" he would do it again.
- Moving to a new country resets your life and lets you discover new things.
- Constraints force focus on the essentials: accommodation, food, and then making apps. There is no time for fancy extras.
- Once the apps make money, you can invest in a better lifestyle.
- He and his wife moved from Korea to Bali, a place she suggested. He discovered surfing there and stayed.

## Reframing failure and sharing it publicly

- Failed projects, such as some programmatic SEO projects that made no money, taught him lessons that fed later products.
- The frustration of repeated failure builds motivation: "right, I have to hit the next one."
- Merely building apps and learning from the internet puts you "ahead of 99% of people," so he says it is already a success.
- He advises being authentic and sharing failures: more people relate to your failures than to your successes, and failure posts get friendlier replies.
- Especially in the age of AI, he thinks you should be yourself and not share only the good parts.

## Burnout, energy and the hardest phase

- He says burnout is "always here somehow." He barely takes days off, mostly because he loves the work.
- What works: about two hours of workouts every day, and lots of sleep. He calls sleep "by far the biggest impact" on creativity, motivation, stress and anxiety.
- He had two serious burnouts, in 2017 and 2021. In 2017 he quit his job, left his girlfriend and changed country.
- The hardest part is the beginning: "nothing is happening... nobody cares about what you do nobody's buying your apps." That drove his past burnouts.
- Recent mini-burnouts came from sharing money publicly on YouTube and the negative comments about his public image.

## Handling hate and growing outside your bubble

- Hate arrived as his story spread beyond the indie maker community to people who did not know his earlier failures.
- He admits he was a bit arrogant, sharing his success too often, and says he partly deserved it.
- He says some people struggle to understand that a young developer can build small products alone and make a living. Strangers can mistake lean validation (like selling a PDF) for a scam.
- He adapted the ShipFast site for the new audience, rewriting it for people coming from outside the community.
- He is aware of pressure to keep a consistent public image. He tries to tweet only things that are "battle tested."
- He feels it is harder to publish things now because he fears breaking the image he built, but says his identity is "shipping stuff," not everything being top notch.
- Side projects or a shift to info products can draw resentment from "software only" people. His view: the less you care about it, the more successful you will be.
- He was surprised ShipFast worked: he expected a couple hundred dollars and it changed his life, so he says you never know and should double down.

## Identity, direction and quick takes

- He is trying to stop seeing himself as a developer and to see himself as a creator.
- He loves creating apps and telling little stories with them, and expects to write more and make YouTube videos mixing business, startups, coding and lifestyle.
- He is frustrated with school and university and wants to show "there is another way."
- Quick yes/no answers: refunds yes, premium no, Product Hunt yes, LinkedIn yes, Hacker News yes, Facebook ads yes, focus on one app no, 12 apps in 12 weeks (not months).
- He is not strict on error tracking: he has a React error boundary and an email button, and says spending too long on it means you have not shipped.
