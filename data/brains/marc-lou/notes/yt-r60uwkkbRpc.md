## Start with something simple, not a SaaS

Marc argues there's a "gold rush" around SaaS, fed by stories of kids making a million with no-code, but that many simpler products are better entry points. The easiest first product is teaching what you already know: a course, a book, or a PDF. He doesn't over-think who the customer is. He picks something that changed his own life (e.g. getting consistently good sleep: bedtime, temperature, earplugs) and notes you could "make a PDF in a day" and sell it to anyone.

- A simple product is the ignition, not the whole business. A sleep PDF could later become a supplement or eye-mask business.
- Starting simple generates more ideas, a tiny audience, feedback and learning. "There's just so many things that just roll after that."
- Greg's add-on: tools like Gamma can turn content into lead magnets and landing pages, and design matters even for a simple offer.

## Launch with a paywall and a small audience built in public

For a first digital product, Marc picks the medium that fits his personality: video (YouTube, TikTok, Instagram) if comfortable on camera, Twitter if he's more introverted and better at writing. He shares what he is working on and builds content from his daily life.

- This won't make a buzz at first, but gets "five to 10 people" who watch and will be there at launch.
- Use "I" ("I did that, I learned that"), not "you". Early on you don't know anything, so don't adopt a teacher voice. Teach only once you have a bigger following and have learned a lot. He cringes at his own early 2021-2022 tweets that told people what to do.
- Learn headlines and putting ideas out there. Add a call to action to buy a small course or product.
- Develop a format by consuming content and borrowing small things from several creators. Marc took Pieter Levels' "I did that" mindset and many small projects, and Justin Welsh's clear, concise writing. Your identity becomes a mix of those plus what you discover along the way.

## Pricing: paywall, don't go too cheap, use competitors and AI

- Start with a paywall; avoid launching free things unless you're very passionate about the idea. A paywall teaches you a lot.
- Don't price too cheap: cheap gets undervalued, and 10 PDFs at $1 each doesn't pay the bills or keep you motivated. Price a bit higher than expected.
- Go with a one-time payment at the very beginning, especially for a course.
- Prices can be changed. Marc has played a lot with this: he started very cheap with subscriptions and a long free trial, then removed the free trial, raised prices, and sometimes removed the subscription.

## Why Marc tends to remove subscriptions

- Business owners assume a subscription equals monthly recurring revenue ($10 in January, $10 in December). For the customer, subscriptions are a pain (Netflix, Spotify), and they immediately think "what happens if I have to cancel and I forget".
- Objections at the moment of purchase hurt conversion rate. So he asks himself "do I need that subscription?" and tends to remove it.
- A friend's rule: "if you have a recurring payment you should provide recurring value". That fits business software used continually (e.g. a weekly scheduling tool).
- Many products can drop subscriptions for one-time payments or a credit system, which doesn't push customers into a big commitment and is easier to sell.
- Pricing he admires: a subscription that charged more in the first month and decreased after two to three months, so customers don't cancel because they'd lose what they'd already gained. He couldn't recall the company.
- Stripe's pricing is smart for the business but he dislikes it as a customer: about 3% per card, plus 1% for non-European cards, 1% for other currencies, 0.4% for invoices, so roughly 5-6% in total. It's likely his biggest expense, around $5,000 a month.

## Idea: gamify real-life habits

Marc likes gamifying things with real-world purpose: sleep, eating, working out, with points, competition with friends, or losing money if you miss goals. He's a gamer (WarCraft) and thinks gamification could power software on its own.

- Pokémon Sleep: the better you sleep, the more your Pokémon grow and evolve. Per Greg's lookup in the conversation, it made about $100M on 9.8M downloads. Marc's take: nostalgia combined with a real-life goal is something big.
- Greg's unlock: license a nostalgic IP. Such licenses can be cheap (his example: a Star Wars apparel license for about $50,000 for two years plus a 2-3% royalty).
- A niche version Marc has had in mind for years: gamify developer GitHub commits, using the contribution graph everyone knows, with a massive leaderboard that makes people want to push code. Greg suggested adding credit-card-style points redeemable for rewards. Marc said he'd probably build it, possibly years from now.

## Idea: legal and company setup for entrepreneurs

- This is the number one question Marc gets on every YouTube video: where did you set up your company, how does it work, how did you set up your Stripe account.
- He sees an info product first, then eventually a service or marketplace matching people with the legal entity they need. He says he is "terrible at legal stuff" and spends as little time on it as possible.
- The problem for non-US founders: endless options for where to pay taxes and open a company (Singapore, Indonesia, France, the US). Stripe Atlas gives 0% tax but may be illegal or flagged depending on where you live.
- Marc's own case: a French person living in Bali, married to a Korean. A French lawyer told him a Stripe Atlas company would be flagged in France, with possible double tax or trouble in an audit. Regulations also change (e.g. Dubai). His conclusion: "I'm not going to optimize anything, I'm going to do something simple."
- His view: the demand is understanding the system and where to set up first, not optimizing taxes. A single-person company with a contractor video editor gives enormous flexibility, but picking where to go is the hard part.
- Concept: "Stripe Atlas for indie hackers with maybe a little more support".
