## Free tools as a marketing engine for a solo developer

- Marc calls free tools "the marketing cure" for developers who like building but hate marketing: "you never actually do marketing, you build a tool you ship it."
- Because the tool is free, people share it. That brings constant traffic, builds an audience (he plugs his name under the tool) and produces customers.
- Example: Logo Fast, a free logo maker built in 3 days. It earned about $5,000 for him by sending traffic to ShipFast, his paid code base for shipping startups faster.
- The logic: "if someone needs a logo then they might need to build a website after," which is what ShipFast sells.
- He tracks clicks from the tool's side ad to ShipFast and sees in his database which purchases came from it, so he knows exactly how many ShipFast buyers discovered it through Logo Fast.
- The tool is a standalone app, fully separate from his other apps.
- Ahrefs is his model: it takes one feature of its main paid software, makes a limited version free, and does this for several tools. The free version gives a glimpse of the paid product.

## Checklist for a free tool that spreads

- It must be free. This is what makes it viral and lets you post it in every community, platform and social network.
- It has one feature only, available as soon as people land on the page. No buttons to click, no redirects, no email collection.
- It has an explicit, memorable name, like Logo Fast (a tool to make a logo fast). This helps word of mouth and Google ranking.
- No signup. Requiring one "is going to kill the conversion rate."
- Build it in a few days.
- Second example: Visualize Habit, a free tool that shows what tiny habits add up to over a year. Users can share or download the image. Next they can sign up for his Habits Garden habit tracker, which imports the habits they just created.

## Launching a free tool and why it pays off

- Launch on free platforms such as Reddit, Product Hunt, Hacker News, and directories like There's An AI For That and Futurepedia. Each has a tutorial for increasing the chance of going viral.
- Drive as much traffic as possible on launch day to create a buzz, and push people to talk about it on social media (TikTok, Instagram, etc.).
- Benefits he lists:
  - Reaches a wider market than the main product.
  - Ranks for new SEO keywords.
  - Chance to go viral, since it is free, valuable and easy to share.
  - Grows an audience: shipping a new tiny tool every week signals that you show up.
  - Makes a good first impression, as with Ahrefs' free tools.
  - Keeps a developer self-driven, because building the marketing tool is the marketing.

## Black Friday without discounting: bundles

- Last Black Friday he did not discount any product and still made about $111,000. He says the video also references $10K in 36 hours for the deal.
- He sees two schools: discount for a big spike, or never discount because it trains customers to expect discounts and the spike flatlines after. He says he does not know which is right and believes the truth is "somewhere in the middle."
- His approach: Black Friday is "a good excuse" for people to buy what they have been waiting for, but he does not want the product to look cheap.
- Instead of discounting, he bundled. ShipFast was $149. He bundled it with Indie Page and BuyDispute for the same $149, so the discount sits on the bundle, not on any single product.
- "Instead of lowering the value of one product I increase the value inside of the same price."
- If you lack other products, bundle a small course or PDF with the main product.
- Stand out: he makes weird videos, such as going live with someone famous like Jimmy Fallon, which gave people a reason to stay on the promo page. His promo tweet would not have hit 500 likes without that content. His advice: find something creative that feels like you, because many other owners compete for attention.

## Launching a course quickly: CodeFast

- After 9 months of work he launched a coding course, CodeFast, around Black Friday, and the course was about 80% complete at launch.
- Reasoning: the most common question he gets is how he learned to code. He taught himself in 2016, and it was hard. Since ChatGPT arrived in 2022, and especially since 2024 as AI became efficient inside code editors, he mostly writes English and reviews the code.
- His claim: building a tiny business needs about 10% of what a professional software engineer knows (landing pages, user dashboards). Most courses still teach how to get a job, so he saw an opening for people who want to turn ideas into reality.
- Structure: a mindset part (using AI for everything, shipping a smaller version of the app, tips on marketing and making money), about an hour of HTML, CSS, JavaScript and how the internet works, and a 7+ hour build of a full software product from scratch. That includes a landing page, user dashboard, Stripe or Lemon Squeezy payments, emails and auth.
- Lessons run 30 seconds to 1-2 minutes each.
- He launched at a lower price because it was unfinished, which he framed as an opportunity for buyers to grab the discount.
- The site was inspired by Gumroad and Linktree-style pages.

## Customer-first validation and payment model (advice from Andre)

Marc credits a friend's advice from 6 years ago, "sell it before you make it," as what started his business. The friend's points, which Marc endorses:

- Pick a niche you find interesting.
- Scrape emails from that niche and send about 10 per day.
- Get people to raise their hand, then get them on a call and make an offer. "Mathematically impossible not to get your first client."
- Marc followed this in 2018, when many recipients did not know what Zoom was, and made his first dollar online that way.
- Go to the customer first rather than building a product and selling one-to-many, which is "very very difficult." The fastest way to know if someone will buy is to ask them, and all you lose is a little time.
- One-time payments suit validation: "if no one buys your one time payments no one will buy your subscription."
- Subscriptions suit a single flagship app, because they act as financial accountability to keep developing it. One-time payments suit shipping many products.
- Marc notes that ShipFast came from his apartment in Korea and later became a million-dollar business.

## Sleep and health habits that sustain solo work

- Marc prioritizes sleep as an investment for a better day, after reading "Why We Sleep" by Matthew Walker about 3 years earlier. He used to sacrifice sleep for work and felt tired and emotional.
- He starts his wind-down an hour before bed, around 7:50 p.m., and sleeps about 8:45 p.m. It includes reading 15-30 minutes, 4-6-8 breathing, red light and no screens.
- A nasal strip for a deviated septum and side sleeping raised his deep sleep from about 1 hour to 2 hours a night.
- He and his wife quit sugar and avoid processed carbs, favoring fiber-rich vegetables, fruit and complex carbs.
- He kept up sleep, exercise (free YouTube workouts) and home cooking from day zero, which let him grind about 2 years with nothing happening.
