## Twitter audience: build in public for yourself, not to sell
- Marc started Twitter seriously because of Peter Levels. He wanted something left over if a startup failed: "I still have a little following... I can share it and get some traction and feedback."
- His first attempt, years earlier, was growth-hacking: follow/unfollow, follow people to get follow-backs. It reached almost 5,000 followers, but it was "a number, not a community." Engagement was lower than that of normal accounts of the same size, and he got no benefit from it.
- His second attempt (this year) started with playing a role and trying to teach, which "didn't really work out."
- The turn came when he just shared his own story: "I'm trying this, it doesn't work, I tried that and it worked."
- It took six or seven months to reach 1,000 followers. At that point he found his signature: "I love shipping apps and I don't overthink." Growth then accelerated. His account was about to hit 10,000 followers.
- He now uses Twitter mainly to feel good while building alone in his room. He doesn't monetize the audience. The feedback and the social side make building startups fun.

## Don't teach or sell until you have proof
- His analogy: if you're dating, you don't say "I have this and I can help you with that." You show what you do and love, and eventually someone wants to be with you.
- His view is that solopreneurs and indie hackers market themselves as humans but copy what businesses do. That doesn't work if you're not yet a notable name.
- Telling people how to do marketing or build startups needs proof or a following. Until then, "talk about your own experience, what's working for you, what's not," and let people decide whether to believe you.
- He applies the same idea to launching. Cold-contacting people to push a product page never worked for him. Create connections because you want to, and share what you're doing when they ask.
- He agrees with an approach he attributes to Antoine BM: don't claim your product is the best, show proof and let people judge. Even Apple says "the best iPhone ever" and then demonstrates it.

## Ship in days, because overthinking is the real enemy
- His New Year resolution project took three days from idea to final product. He never touched the code afterward.
- It got about 80,000 visitors in 24 hours and led to sales for his other startups. He calls it by far his most successful product in audience and traffic.
- The three-day limit forced simplicity. After shipping, his ego still said "it's not good enough," but "for people it was perfect."
- He says developers often hurt themselves by building complex tech ("Kafka Docker ML") for their own satisfaction. He decides up front whether he is building a business or pleasing himself with something beautiful.
- Example: he built end-to-end encryption for one product, and nobody used it a month later. Everything non-technical got used.
- When a client wanted a Facebook-sized product, he told them to pick one core feature to make shine and evolve from there. Many refused, so he concluded: "if people don't build better it's not because they don't know, it's because they don't want to."

## Early failures and what they taught him
- **First startup:** a "Tinder for sports." He spent a year on it with business cards, a logo, and NDAs, before he could code. When asked how it would make money, he had no answer, which ended it.
- **Korea:** he and a friend raised $100,000 to build a flight-price prediction app. It lasted nine months and ended because neither of them was a CEO.
- **After Korea:** he began working solo, with no investors, no co-founder and small ambition. He made mini-apps and sold couple gloves in the street at -20 degrees, which brought his first dollars from a business at 23.
- **Viral/escape rooms:** he cold-emailed and called escape rooms and sold the idea before building it: $300 one-time plus $99 a month. This was his first customer, when he was living on about $600 a month.
- **Covid:** that product was roughly $6,000 MRR before Covid and dropped to zero. It still makes about $1,000 a month.
- **Game Widget:** a follow-up to remove the frustrations of the first product. It is an in-site escape mini-game. About 10% of visitors play, about 10% of players purchase, and it adds about 0.6 points of conversion on a 2-3% baseline.
- He is now selling Game Widget, because he feels no connection to the market. There are only around 3,000-4,000 English-speaking escape rooms.

## Mindset: you are your own enemy, and you are not your client
- Most of the time, "you're fighting against yourself." Limiting beliefs stop you from trying things. His example: he never did ads, which kept him from money.
- He believes in coaching or talking things through, because "alone, it's impossible" to question your own reality. He uses Plato's cave as the metaphor.
- "You are not your client." You've been a buyer your whole life and never seen the selling process, so test pricing and psychology ideas instead of judging by your own reaction.
- He recently removed the free plan on his startup and says conversion got better. His reasoning is that people value paid things more than free ones.
- He prefers a process he enjoys for his own projects. When targeting others, he cares about their results.

## Habit Garden and gamification
- Habit Garden is a gamified habit tracker. Quests, in-game rewards and challenges make you more motivated, and your habits grow a tree. He says he is "trying to make people addicted to their habits."
- His gamification ideas come from World of Warcraft, where he had a character with 150 days of play time, and from a book on why games work (e.g. random loot).
- Problem: many signups think it's a regular habit tracker, probably because the onboarding doesn't explain the game. About 50-60 users are deeply engaged and getting a lot done.
- To fix it, he booked about 20 user calls in one week. He has users log in and try the app while he watches what they understand. He has also tried session recordings, but wants direct conversation.
- He also built a "gamified list" site of gamification apps, such as Plant Nanny, charity vocab apps and classroom RPG apps. It taught him a lot.
- Leaderboard idea: besides all-time rankings, a 7-day leaderboard lets lapsed users climb again.
- He would like to gamify startups: quests, clear daily goals, and no void.
