## Valuation: a simple 3x annualised revenue formula

Marc values a micro startup with an "over-simplified" formula: **Valuation = Annualised Revenue x 3**. He assumes a solopreneur or co-founder pair building software, AI tools or mobile apps (not info products), aiming for 5-, 6- or 7-figure exits with low operating costs (hosting, OpenAI credits).

- A habits tracker at $300 MRR sells for about $10,800 (300 x 12 x 3).
- An AI logo maker launched 3 months ago that made $4,000 total sells for about $12,000 (4,000 x 3).
- MRR can be extrapolated to a year; one-time payments cannot, so they are counted as earned.

He does not go deeper into valuation: "gaining 5% here or there isn't worth the hustle. I'd rather build more apps."

Until 2022 he believed a $300 MRR startup was worth nothing; he now says he was wrong.

## His two exits and what they paid

- **GameWidget** (first startup, March 2023): $80 MRR sold for $4,300. He flew to Singapore and bought a laptop.
- **Habits Garden** (second startup, about 10 days before the Oct 21, 2023 post): sold for $10,000, after roughly one year from idea to sale, documented in public. He flew to Bangkok and bought a monitor for his laptop.

Both were small weekend-project-scale products, showing that sub-$1,000 MRR projects can be sold for meaningful sums.

## Deciding whether it is time to sell

Marc uses three questions:

1. Can I 10x the revenue in a year?
2. Do I care about the market?
3. Do I need money right now?

His reasoning against simply holding a small earner: "every abandoned business goes to 0 quickly," so a juicy $500 recurring revenue is not safe to sit on. Selling gives temporary financial freedom, less stress and time to focus on a new project.

He also frames the upside of growing instead: a weekend project reaching $1,000 MRR gives "a 2-year pass to quit your job, move to Bali, and build apps full-time."

## The acquisition process: paperwork, payment, transfer

1. **Paperwork.** Sign a Letter of Intent (LOI) and an Asset Purchase Agreement (APA), which lists what is being bought (IP, marketing assets such as a Facebook page). You may also sign a non-compete: make sure it is not too broad and still lets you build apps you care about.
   - Platforms like Acquire handle the paperwork.
   - Ask ChatGPT to "explain like I'm 5" to understand what you are signing; ChatGPT can also draft the paperwork if you do not use Acquire.
2. **Payment.** The buyer secures funds with a third party such as Escrow. Once funds are secured, you send the assets.
3. **Transfer.** Move the domain, database, credentials and so on. The buyer inspects everything and tells Escrow to release payment.

## Transferring assets that cannot be transferred

Some platforms do not allow asset transfers, for example an AWS S3 bucket, a Mailgun email-sending domain or a Google Cloud Console project.

- Marc would not hand over credentials and move on, because you likely use those platforms for other projects.
- Instead, explain the limitation to the buyer and ask them to create their own account and API keys.

## Tips to sell faster

- **Ditch buyers who ask a million questions.** Highly skeptical buyers will not buy and waste your time.
- **Build in public.** It all comes down to trust. Potential buyers follow you and can wire money without much questioning, which is how Habits Garden sold. It also saves the platform listing fee (4% on Acquire).
- **Set deadlines.** Follow up and tell buyers you need an answer within 3 days.
- **Have the Acquire listing 100% ready before publishing.** All buyers on the platform are notified within 24 hours, when most opportunities land.
- **Lower the price if nothing happens.** If no one is interested after a month on Acquire, drop the price a bit; Acquire tends to lift your listing when significant edits are made.

He closes by saying selling a side project can give "the mental clarity to work harder on your solopreneurship journey."
