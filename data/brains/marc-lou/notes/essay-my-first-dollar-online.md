## From failure to the first $1: the two-year path to a first sale

- Marc graduated in Computer Science in 2016 and skipped a job in Paris. After watching The Social Network he decided he would be "the next Mark Zuckerberg."
- His first idea was "Tinder for sports lovers." He spent a year on it: learning to code, asking people to sign an NDA before hearing the idea, and designing a logo and business cards.
- The app never worked: users couldn't sign up, and he was the only user. When a teacher asked how it would make money, he had no answer after a year of work.
- Next came a Seoul AI startup with his friend Sacha. They raised $100,000 from angel investors and had 2 employees, trying to predict future flight ticket prices.
- Nine months later they had a bad product and no income. He describes it as "10% real work, 90% pretending."
- His own takeaway on the pattern: "Big goals, big failures."
- Total timeline: about 2 years from the first idea to a first dollar earned online.

## The gloves experiment: building before selling

- In December 2017, Marc was broke in a foreign country. He copied a Facebook ad for gloves for couples: one big mitten for holding hands.
- As a "product-obsessed engineer" he built the product instead of dropshipping. A Korean grandma knitted 50 "Glovers" over about a month and a half.
- He spent all his savings (a few hundred dollars) on Facebook Ads with a video and made 0 sales.
- He then sold the gloves at $20 each on the street at night in minus 15 degree winter. They sold about 2 per day.
- His lesson: don't build a physical or software product before proving anyone will buy it. Inventory and time were sunk before any demand signal.

## Resetting the goal: "Make $1 online with code"

- In April 2018, after the gloves, he set a deliberately small goal: make $1 online with code.
- To avoid another failure he "focused on problems, not products." His reasoning: every business wants to grow, so he picked a gamified marketing tool for escape rooms.
- He supported himself with acting gigs (about $200 per gig, one or two calls a week) while building.
- He also started working out every 2 days (20 minutes of intense jumping rope) for clarity of mind. His rule: "Eat healthy, work out, and sleep well. It impacts all your decisions."
- His argument for the small goal: "Forget big startups, try making $1 online first." Making a living from your craft is, to him, the deepest source of happiness.

## Selling before building: the VirallyBot first sale

- At a startup event he met Andrey, a marketer, who asked: "Why don't you sell it before you make it?"
- Marc is an engineer who loves building and dislikes marketing, but after 2 years of failure he decided to apply what Andrey taught him instead of questioning it.
- Tactic: a few cold emails to escape rooms. Daniela at Time's Up Escape Rooms in Australia agreed to a quick call.
- The call was on Friday 13th, 2018, from a cheap guest house in Osaka, Japan during a visa run: bad wifi, no sleep, and by his own account a cringe-worthy pitch.
- After 42 minutes she said yes. By the end of the day she had subscribed to the $99 monthly plan.
- Only then did he go back to Korea and build the product.
- Result: the SaaS VirallyBot made $70,000 total and still paid the bills at $600/month in 2023. One sales call "paid the bills for 5 years."

## Lessons Marc would give himself if restarting

- **Listen to relevant people.** He applied everything Andrey taught him on marketing without arguing.
- **Success comes faster than expected.** Make a radical U-turn, throw away the past, and restart.
- **Hard times build strong relationships.** His Korean girlfriend, who helped with the gloves project, is now his wife and best friend.
- **Don't fall in love with your product.** Ship and launch fast. "One feature with a buy button is enough." Customers will shape the rest of the product.
- **Perspective on scale:** he says making $55,000/month later was easier than making that first $1 online.
