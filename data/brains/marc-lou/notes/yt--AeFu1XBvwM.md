## Starting out: ideas, NDAs and why Marc now says to share them

- Marc's first project was a "Tinder for athletes" app (find a tennis partner). He couldn't code, stitched together downloaded GitHub code, and never shipped it.
- He did the opposite of what he preaches today: he had people sign NDAs and threatened someone with a lawsuit for saying he'd copy the idea. A year went by.
- The turning point was a founder asking: "How are you going to find users? How are you going to find people who pay for your app?"
- His view now: "I never understood the idea of launching something without talking about it", because you can't get a client while hiding your idea. Making a client sign an NDA "makes no sense".
- He accepts that copying is easier in the AI era, and the answer is to run faster. Value sits in distribution, marketing and image rather than in building the product.
- Example of copying: he says he copied a viral US idea (ads in place of an AI subscription, with the user taking 50%) in a week and called it Idle Pay.

## Selling before building: the escape-game cold email business

- After the Korean ventures and a glove product failed, a friend told Marc: "Why don't you just send a cold email and sell it before you've created it?"
- He took the "simplest business in the world": B2B businesses all want more customers. He chose escape games and cold emailed ones in Australia and Europe.
- The product was a Facebook Messenger chatbot with a micro escape game; finishing it gave the player a 10% coupon for the escape game.
- His first sales call lasted 43 minutes and was awkward (poor English, stuttering), but the Australian owner paid. It was his first dollar online after about 2.5 years of entrepreneurship.
- He grew it to roughly $3-5K MRR over about 2 years by sending emails and improving the product all day.
- Limits: clients needed hand-holding to use it, and he stalled at $3,000-3,500 per month. Covid took it from about 5 cases per month to zero.
- He also learned that a small business he saw as a "job" was an asset that could have been sold.

## Failed experiments and the "first step" principle

- Product failures he lists: Korean wool couple-gloves (Facebook ads gave $200 spent and zero clicks; he sold about 20 pairs by hand on the street), and a $2 PDF info-product on a Shopify store.
- For the PDF he ran Pornhub banner ads with his own face, got millions of impressions and no clicks, then put QR-code stickers in Paris public toilets and still sold nothing.
- On action: the first step feels impossible, but after it "the stairs, it's super easy". Most people never take the first step.
- The harder part comes after: doing the right thing, not just doing things. Ousama's reply was that you have to have done bad things first to understand the good ones.
- Advice Ousama gave him: "no matter what you do, just keep going."

## Building in public and launching about one startup a month

- After being fired from his first real job (a remote engineering role, about $10K/month), he had about $50K saved and discovered Twitter and Pieter Levels. Employment also made him realise he wanted to earn a bit less and do his own thing.
- He started by launching tiny products to build a daily habit, with no monetisation. His first was a mood-to-movie recommender ("I'm tired" gives action movies).
- He has launched about 35 products since late 2021, aiming for roughly one a month, and posts everything on Twitter.
- Launch channels were launch platforms and Hacker News, then Twitter. He went from 5 followers to about 1,000-1,500 in around a year.
- One early earner was a gamified habit tracker (quests, a garden that grows), reaching about €1,000/month after a few months.
- After about five years of consistent shipping, more followers brought more visitors and revenue, which fed the cycle.
- He publishes his revenue every month, copying Levels. His reason: "I'm a product of that".

## Boilerplate for developers: the step up in revenue

- In 2023, after about 15 small startups, he saw he rewrote the same code (emails, accounts) each time and packaged it as a boilerplate for developers.
- Revenue went from roughly €3-5K/month to about €50-60K/month and "it's never stopped since then".
- Ousama's summary of his current position: around €100,000/month with a lifestyle he enjoys (Cyprus plus about half the year in Korea).

## TrustMRR: verified revenue leaderboard and acquisition marketplace

- Origin: he saw a Pieter Levels tweet saying many people fake their income, and built a leaderboard in about 4 hours where founders connect a Stripe key so revenue is verified.
- A user asked to sell their startup, so he added a "for sale" option. It grew into a marketplace with NDAs, letters of intent, chat, due diligence documents and escrow.
- Monetisation: listing costs $29-$499, plus 3% of the acquisition. He also sells sponsorship slots at $500 per week. He says roughly half of revenue is ads and half is acquisitions.
- Timeline: launched September 2025, first revenue in October 2025, acquisition platform in January.
- Market data from the platform: 115 acquisitions, an average multiple of 1.5x (versus the 3-5x people expect for SaaS), and the largest deal just under $100K. Buyers and sellers are small, young products.
- He says deals are getting faster: someone can build a product, get a customer, and sell within weeks.
- He turned down an offer of 27 months of MRR (about $1M), because he treats his startups like children and no longer needs the money. He says he has reached 20-25% of that offer's value and wants to push to over $1M himself.
- Growth is all word of mouth. He said he doesn't do outreach, and listing is free.
- His reasons people list: proof ("I'm not a liar"), a backlink and traffic, a portfolio page, and idea discovery by visitors.

## AI, the market for solo builders, and personal lifestyle

- On AI's effect on startups: he expects more people to make a living and fewer to become very rich, comparing it to music. He thinks the proportion of products that take off hasn't really changed, and that building the product alone doesn't win.
- Ousama's addition, which Marc didn't contradict: distribution, brand and personal energy are the other variables.
- He switches between AI coding models constantly without worrying which one, and expects interfaces to standardise.
- On lifestyle: he does 1.5-2 hours of sport daily, aims for 10,000 steps, prioritises 8 hours of sleep (after reading "Why We Sleep"), has a blood test every 6 months, and his wife handles healthy cooking. He tried very low carbs for a year and dropped it as miserable.
- On ambition: he is not chasing a financial target. His view is "you need to know which seat you want to sit in and be happy to be in those seats".
