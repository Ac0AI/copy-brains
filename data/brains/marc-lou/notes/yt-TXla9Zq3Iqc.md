## Launching a course: Code Fast results and what drove them

- Marc launched Code Fast, a course teaching people to code in order to build tiny internet businesses, after 9 months of work.
- Results he reports: about $23k within roughly 17 hours, $68,000 at 36 hours, and close to $200,000 USD in a week.
- Traffic: about 11,000 visitors in the first stretch (about $4 per visitor, about a 2% conversion rate on a product costing at minimum $150), then almost 50,000 visitors in the week at $4.4 generated per visitor.
- His best earlier product, ShipFast, reached close to $2 per visitor at its best. He calls $1+ per visitor "amazing".
- Revenue came mostly from Twitter (about 7,000 visitors in the early count), plus YouTube, a little LinkedIn, and people who searched for it after hearing about it on social media. Purchases came from all over the world.
- He monitored traffic live with DataFast and watched sales in the Stripe dashboard.
- The one launch promotion was a single tweet with a skit video, which reached about half a million views and 1.3k likes.

## Finding ideas: solve your own problem, then use audience comments

- Marc says to build something that solves your own problem. He criticizes "what if I combine this tech plus that market" ideas as "pure blind guesses".
- Example: Invoice (his invoice tool) makes around $1,000 per month with zero maintenance and zero customer support. He built it because Stripe charges 4% to generate a PDF invoice, so he knew others had the same issue.
- For Code Fast, he read every comment on his YouTube channel. His best-performing "how to..." videos revealed what people want to achieve: buy a domain name, build UIs, make a responsive website.
- Repeated comments told him people have a problem and he has the solution because he knows how to code.
- He argues the idea must resonate with you:
  - ViralBuds, his first product, helped escape room businesses and made a couple thousand per month. He did not care about the niche and it never took off.
  - Everything that worked aligns with his goal of helping people be free with just a computer.
  - He says there is "a power that knows how much the product resonates with you".

## Entering a competitive market with one marketing angle

- Udemy already has hundreds of JavaScript and React courses, so demand was proven but the market was crowded.
- He argues that in a competitive market you need one angle that sets you apart.
- The angle: existing courses run 300+ hours because they target people competing for jobs, a zero-sum game where more skills win. Marc only cares about tiny websites with "me, my computer, a few of my customers".
- Code Fast therefore teaches only the minimum core skills for a tiny internet business, built for "entrepreneurs, not employees". That lets him cut about 90% of what a software engineer or employee has to learn.
- He also bundled the entire journey, from zero to a finished product, rather than one part of it. He bets this is one reason the launch worked, since beginners struggle to know what to learn and how backend, frontend, HTML and JavaScript fit together.

## Pre-launch checks and launch craft

- Before launching any startup he sends it to a few friends from different backgrounds for feedback, because "when I build a landing page I'm always in my head and sometimes I don't see things".
- On a previous launch, the friends told him the landing page was not good enough with two days left before Black Friday. He says it was one of the most stressful launches he has done.
- He says the launch itself deserves careful crafting, because conversion rate matters just like in real life.
- Even without an audience you can:
  - write a good story of how you came up with the product idea;
  - build a clear chart in Figma explaining the product;
  - give a strong first impression.
- He launched later than planned because the video took 2 days instead of 1.

## Video as an ad: entertaining content that gets shared

- Marc made a skit where he plays Morpheus from The Matrix, trying to convince Neo, who has a 9-to-5, to quit and learn to code.
- His reasoning for the video:
  - the internet is crowded, so you need something that feels like you;
  - people will give a minute of their time to something entertaining and may then visit the site and purchase;
  - it is "like an ad that's pleasurable to watch", and people share it with friends even if not interested in buying.
- He credits the video for the tweet's reach and says it would never have reached half a million views otherwise.
- An existing audience on Twitter and YouTube helps launches, but the content format drove the virality.

## Post-launch growth: affiliates, Facebook ads, AI teacher

- Affiliate program: affiliates earn 30% to 50% commission. Because the site converts well, he says affiliates were making over $1,000 per day. He added a slider showing potential earnings (for example, 5 referred people equals $300).
- Facebook ads, run with Nico Jeanin (author of the Makers Guide):
  - spend of about $70 per day, campaign running only a couple of days;
  - first day was $60 per purchase while Facebook learned the audience;
  - the next day was $13 per purchase.
  - He says it is early to tell how far it scales. On ShipFast he previously spent $50 and got $150 in revenue.
- Next step: an AI teacher trained on the course that knows the captions, the code, and where the student is, to explain and debug.

## ShipFast Perks: bundling discounts to raise perceived value

- Marc added a "Perks" feature to ShipFast, a list of discounts on popular products such as Vercel, with Resend being added. The bundle is worth over $1,000 in discounts.
- He handpicks products that help people save or make money, in line with his goal of helping 1,000 makers make $1,000 online.
- On the landing page he shows how much in discounts buyers can expect, which makes the ShipFast price look cheap by comparison. He hopes this improves conversion rate.
