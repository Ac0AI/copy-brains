## From regular kid to $1M solo developer: the timeline and failures

- Marc was born near Paris in October 1993. He disliked school and university, played World of Warcraft 8 hours a day (65 days in one year), and was nearly expelled twice.
- He reports going from living with his parents and $0 to $1 million as a solo developer in under 3 years, after 27 businesses, most of which failed.
- At the time of this video, seven of his internet businesses make $62,000 per month.
- His early failures:
  - A "Tinder for sports lovers" mobile app, built over a year with no thought about how it would make money.
  - A flight-price-prediction AI startup in South Korea with $100,000 from investors and a good MVP, but no way to get customers.
  - Glovers, mittens for couples, which ended with 50 unsold gloves and a few hundred dollars burned on ads.
  - Golden Plumber, a $4 PDF sold with toilet stickers in Paris, which made no sales.
  - Ads on adult websites, which made $4 in a month.
- His lowest point came at age 27, back at his parents' house with no savings. He then took a job as a software engineer at about $9,000 per month.
- He says the job restored his sense of purpose and gave him time to read and recover.
- Marc says he learned everything (coding, marketing, English) from YouTube, Google and the internet.

## Learning "sell before you build" and the first dollar

- Two engineer-led projects (the mobile app and the AI startup) failed for the same reason: great product, no way to get customers.
- A friend, Andre, told him "sell it before you build it". His engineer mind couldn't comprehend it, but he tried it.
- He didn't know what to sell, so he picked a universal idea: "help businesses get more customers".
- Product: a gamification marketing tool for escape rooms. It was a game the escape room gives customers, which promotes the business when they finish.
- He cold-called an escape room owner in Perth, Australia, while paralyzed by fear and awkward on the call. She said yes after 40 minutes.
- First sale on September 8, 2018: $300 setup plus $99 per month.
- That customer stayed 5 years and paid about $4,000 in total, from a single cold email.
- He built the software for her, then repeated the process: emails, calls, improve the software. He closed 3 customers by the end of the month.
- Routine: marketing in the morning, coding in the afternoon.
- Growth: $1,000 MRR, peaking at $4,000 at the end of 2019. He raised the price to $199 per month and tested annual deals at $2,000.
- Covid then forced escape rooms to close, he paused subscriptions, and revenue went from $4,000 per month to zero.
- The software later recovered to around $15,000 per month, then churn grew and he got bored after about 2 years.
- A follow-up escape room website widget (a game on the customer's site giving a discount coupon) lifted booking conversion from 3% to 3.5%, but peaked at $300 MRR because marketing tools sold poorly in that market.

## The five rules he set in December 2021

He restarted with $30,000 in the bank in Bali, after being fired from his job. He set five rules:

1. Prioritize sleep. He read "Why We Sleep" by Matthew Walker and says sleep impacted his mental and physical health more than anything else.
2. Share everything transparently on Twitter, to connect with other entrepreneurs and never feel lonely.
3. Ship the smallest version of the idea. No more spending a year in his bedroom overthinking.
4. Always launch the project and show it to as many people as possible.
5. Use process goals, not outcome goals. Example: "working out every single day" instead of a goal of losing weight, because it focuses on what he can control.

- He treats the journey as a collection of small projects. Even if one flops, it still grows his portfolio of little websites.
- He was inspired by Peter Levels, a solo developer who built tiny startups from anywhere in the world in public.
- Later he applied the same idea to YouTube: one video per week in less than 24 hours, no matter what.

## The "one idea, a few weeks, launch, move on" shipping machine (2022-2023)

- Process: one idea, a few weeks to build, launch it, move on. By the end of 2022 he had built 10 websites that way.
- Mood to Movie: recommends movies by mood. It went viral on Hacker News, bringing a couple thousand visitors and growing his Twitter following.
- Habits Garden: a gamified habit tracker with quests and gem rewards. It had a premium plan and reached $500 MRR.
- Most of these made little money, but he had fun and his audience grew.
- Fun skit videos for launches (with Joe Rogan, Leonardo DiCaprio, Matthew McConaughey, and one playing World of Warcraft in real life) took his Twitter from 1,000 to 10,000 followers. He says it was unconventional but people loved it.
- In early 2023 he ran out of steam and shifted to "painkillers and not vitamins": apps that solve real problems.
- He also required a paywall: "no more free plans and no more free users".
- Examples: IndiePage (portfolio page builder) made a couple thousand dollars in its first month. MakeLing (AI website generator) made an extra couple thousand but bored him because he didn't care about the market or customers.
- He sold projects to focus: MakeLing for $35,000, Habits Garden for $10,000, and the second escape room software for $4,000 (it was still making $50 per month).

## ShipFast: how a side tool became the biggest hit

- By August 2023 he had built around 15 to 16 websites and wanted to ship faster, so he made a codebase for himself.
- It worked so well that he decided to sell it, and called it ShipFast.
- He told his wife they would be lucky to make $100. It made $4,000 in 24 hours, his biggest hit up to then.
- It made $50,000 in its first month.
- He kept improving it by listening to users, adding components and opening an affiliate program. He built everything in public and shared revenue, which grew his Twitter following.
- He says ShipFast's value came from lessons learned shipping software, so he kept launching other startups at about one a month:
  - BuyDispute, which prevents Stripe chargebacks.
  - LogoFast, a free logo tool.
  - Zenvo, an invoice generator for European customers.
- He focused on startups that solve his own problems. Each made from a couple hundred to a couple thousand dollars per month, and he fed learnings back into ShipFast.

## YouTube growth and where he is now

- In January 2024 he made around $50,000 per month and started YouTube with the process goal of one video per week in under 24 hours.
- Nothing happened for 3 months. Then he published a video showcasing the 23 websites he built, and YouTube began pushing his videos.
- The channel grew from almost 0 to 990,000 subscribers by August 2024, and his revenue doubled.
- In April 2024 he made $143,000 across all projects combined.
- He says the money removed the stress of paying rent. The biggest change was mindset: "I am addicted to building startups".
- His lifestyle: surfs every morning, works on tiny businesses in the daytime, evenings with his wife, with an office that can move between Bali, France and Korea.

## Marc's closing advice

- "Tomorrow you'll wish you would have started today. Start now. Build something, build anything."
- When stuck, ask ChatGPT or Google.
- Make it fun through a challenge or by doing it with a friend.
- Each project teaches something, and the right project can change your life.
- He cautions people not to take advice from just anyone.
