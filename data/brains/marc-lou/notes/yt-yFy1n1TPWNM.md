## Launch-first validation and the one-month rule

- Marc says being launchable on Product Hunt is "300%" his entire focus when he builds something new.
- He ships a single feature rather than a full startup, because he has "a bunch of failed startups in the past" and once spent a year building a product nobody wanted.
- His marketing is mostly launching, then watching how people react.
- If a launch shows real traction and feedback (his example: a product called cheap fast), he goes all in on it.
- If a launch only earns a couple hundred dollars with no excitement, he keeps the product running and moves on to avoid another six months wasted.
- Last year his cap was one month maximum per product.
- This year, with people sending him questions, emails and feedback, he gets some pre-validation. He spends more time on products where he already sees a need.

## Building live on YouTube as pre-validation

- For Zenvo (a small tool for Stripe to avoid paying the fee for Stripe invoicing, launched on Product Hunt the day before the interview), he built live on YouTube.
- He streams every morning for 2 to 6 or 8 hours.
- Viewers help debug code, suggest headlines, and sometimes show up as potential customers asking for features.
- He calls this his pre-validation.
- His channel has about 5,000 subscribers, and each unedited live gets at least 2,000 views.
- He chose live building as a different approach to building in public than 280 characters on Twitter: "100% me, not edited, not scripted". He argues transparency "connects people, it sells".
- Streaming also forces him to sit at his desk and produce code.
- He had only streamed about three times: once a year before, once six months before, and once the week before the interview.

## ShipFast: launch, pricing and one-time sales

- ShipFast is a code boilerplate for programmers who want to create startups. It includes payments, emails and more.
- He built it after about 15 products, when he kept repeating the same setup: landing page, domain name, customer support, payments.
- He spent about a week gathering the code and writing documentation, and pushed it live before a holiday in Hong Kong.
- At the time he made about 3 to 4,000 a month. He woke up to about 3K already made within 2 hours of launch.
- It has made about $50,000 a month for the five months before the interview. The interview places it at about six months old.
- Pricing: a $169 tier and a $199 tier that has a few more features. He says the exact pricing was "pure luck and randomness".
- His reasoning is the anchor effect. The cheaper tier makes the higher one make sense, whereas a single price is hard to compare ("how many apples is that").
- He chose a one-time payment because it is easier to sell than a subscription: "if your product doesn't have a really good recurring value, there is no point of charging" recurring revenue.

## Dogfooding as the feature filter

- He still uses ShipFast as the boilerplate for every product.
- He gets tons of feature requests but does not build them, because listening to all of them would make a very complex boilerplate.
- Instead he only adds something to ShipFast when he needs it in his own product. Example: after shipping Zenvo with ShipFast, two landing page UI components he made were nice enough to reuse, so he added them back into ShipFast.
- He describes this as his filter to avoid unwanted features and build only what matters.
- The ShipFast homepage shows a live feed of GitHub commits and how recently the product was updated.
- He frames this as marketing and community spirit ("you share your transparency, we relate") rather than being a nerd. It signals to $200 buyers that the product is maintained.

## Community as a side effect

- He is "not a community type person", but someone asked if he had a Discord, so he opened one and made it part of the premium plan.
- People started to join, debug each other's code and vote on each other's launches.
- It has grown to roughly a thousand-plus members, with new projects launching on Product Hunt every week.
- He says seeing people actually ship is his "end reward".
- Buyers also say they don't know how to code but want to be part of the community.
- Arvid suggested turning it into a "ShipFast University", as Daniel Vassallo's Small Bets does, with lectures from Indie hacker friends saved as recordings. Marc wrote it down on his to-do list.
- Marc says part of him wants to stay solo and does not like the operational side of working with people.

## Weekly schedule and work-life boundaries

- He allocates Monday to Friday to building, mostly offline, coming online around 4 or 5 p.m. to browse Twitter.
- Weekends are for content: Saturday for the YouTube channel (videos take a lot of time) and Sunday for the newsletter and related tweets.
- He surfs every morning for about two hours, reads for about 30 minutes, then works until about 6 p.m.
- Last year he worked until bedtime, which worked while things grew, but when things stall or decline "your brain is totally up".
- This year he closes the office door at 6 p.m. and has a home setup that separates work from the rest of his life.
- He admits he feels self-imposed pressure to make the next hit after ShipFast: "I cannot ship something that will fail".
- He has lived in Bali for six years and rents a villa year to year, unsure about buying property there.
