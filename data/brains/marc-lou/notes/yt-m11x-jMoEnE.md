## How Marc's YouTube channel started and the weekly habit behind it

- The channel is older, but it was only a backup. Marc posted videos made for Twitter there "in case I lose everything" until about 10 months before this interview.
- He then committed to one video every Saturday, "no matter what". He calls the lesson of the past three years "being dumb and just stick to what goal and do it every single time".
- He reached 100K subscribers in roughly 10 months. He does not call himself a YouTuber. He sees YouTube as "a window on what I do", where he shares stories from building apps.
- He had roughly 80K Twitter followers when he started, and early videos got 2-3K views mostly because he shared them there.
- Early production was poor. He used a laptop camera and a USB podcast mic that he had mounted backwards for three months.
- His advice to beginners: "just start your Channel right now... make a habit... if it's a crappy video with this crappy microphone it doesn't matter... do it every single week and then later luck will find you".

## The breakout video and its effect on revenue

- The first video was "my nine sources of income as a solopreneur". He copied the idea from a similar "my 7/11 sources of income" video that had views. His thumbnail showed him with a computer and his revenue figure in the background.
- Two or three months in, one video popped, and the whole channel lifted with it. It was a talking-head video about his simple tech stack, and it was about his real results.
- He framed it around a contrarian angle. "A lot of people over engineer their tech stack", while his simple stack built about 30 apps and was making $1.5k per day.
- It got 50K views in the first three days and later reached about 400K.
- He notes that new videos often show nothing in week one, then take off. He calls this "seeds... they all grow together".
- Revenue on one of his best-selling products went from roughly $35-40K in February to $74K in March. April and May then hit about $125-126K, when three videos went viral.
- Twitter had been producing about $30-40K per month, and he says the jump came mostly from YouTube.
- Later, in a 30-day window, YouTube drove about $750 of ShipFast sales. That was roughly the same as Twitter and less than Google (branded searches like "ShipFast review"). He says sales from YouTube were mostly during the peak.
- He does not push products in videos. He leaves links in the description because he finds being pushy awkward.

## Where video ideas come from

- Questions from his Twitter audience. When developers asked what tech he uses, he turned that into a video.
- His real life and builds. He makes a video a week, a newsletter a week, about 50 tweets a week and a product a month, so he constantly finds problems.
- Repeated viewer questions. After his viral videos, people kept asking how he learned to code, which became his learn-to-code course.
- He argues the best content comes from living something worth documenting. His retrospective advice: "really focus on having a life that is inspiring... document everything... I think that's 80% of the work".
- The remaining 20% is packaging: a curiosity-triggering title, a catchy thumbnail, and an intro that makes viewers feel "if I click away... I'm losing something". He says "not really a content creator", so production effort is minimal.

## Packaging, remixing and resources

- He learned thumbnails and titles from creators on Twitter (he names Aprilynne Alter for thumbnails, and a newsletter on creator hooks, and a French creator with "Wo" in his name). He would binge their threads.
- One lesson from that: understand "the foundation" and treat YouTube "as a Marketplace for attention".
- His most-viewed thumbnails were each inspired by someone else's. His method: search a similar title, then ask "what if I apply this kind of thumbnail to my own video", and mix elements into his own style. "Everything's a remix."
- Hindsight on the first video: "solopreneur" is understood on Twitter but not mainstream YouTube. He would say developer or entrepreneur and show a ring of app and payment logos around his face.

## Format experiments, pivots and gear

- After the hits, he tried to repeat what worked. He then tried a Casey Neistat-style video and a Daniel Dalen-style lifestyle vlog. Views dropped, and some viewers said they came for something else.
- He admits "I'm not going to be able to reproduce this kind of format". Success was based on three years of building 23 websites.
- He bought a roughly $5,000 camera setup (Sony ZV-1, Rode mic, Joby tripod). The channel "is down since I purchased those equipments" and he would not recommend buying it.
- He started with a Rode NT-USB (about $100-150) and the MacBook webcam.
- An editor does basic cuts, subtitles and icons for about $200-300 per video. He later switched to a lifestyle-oriented editor.
- His next direction is broader topics (health, entrepreneurship, getting your first customer) in face-cam videos for people unsure about school or career, with vlogs from time to time. He avoids short-form.
- Feedback from the host encouraged him to remake the tech-stack video with an updated angle. Marc says he worries about milking content, but concedes he may "remove my ego from the equation".
- He says negative comments are easy to over-focus on. His rule is to ship weekly and U-turn when nothing works for too long.

## How he finds product ideas and grows tools

- He builds a lot and notices repeated problems. After building 10 websites in 2022 he wrote the boilerplate that became ShipFast.
- He noticed money landing in his bank was less than what Stripe showed, so he built a product that avoids a 0.4% per-invoice fee. It makes $1-2K per month.
- His free tools grow through SEO. Three or four of them bring 600-700 visitors a day and advertise a paid product.
- He uses Rewardful affiliate programs. His course had earned about $7,000 through affiliates four days after launch.
- A friend, Nico, runs Meta ads for him. Marc gives him cards and a free hand, and it is profitable at about $50 in, $150 out.
- He likes humorous fake-movie ads (green screen, AI scripts, Photoshop AI), inspired by a scheduling-tool ad with The Shining. They don't do well on YouTube but go viral on Twitter.

## Marketing principle: be the proof

- His own philosophy: "be the teacher you wish you had." He contrasts a gym coach who is ripped with an unhealthy teacher.
- For ShipFast, he shows he shipped 20 apps with it. He suggests the host's tool do the same: build channels from zero and show the results using the tool itself.
- His short-term goal is to get so good at marketing that he can show anyone how to make $1,000 with a laptop, and to buy a house in Europe, likely Portugal.
