## Starting with cold email to a business you'll never visit

Marc's first money online came from cold email in early 2018. He had zero revenue and was doing movie gigs for a couple hundred dollars a month to survive.

- Idea: build something that helps a business he cares about get more customers. He chose puzzles and games for escape rooms.
- Prospecting: search "escape room" on Yelp (and Trip Advisor) in any city, since he would never go there and would reach them over the internet.
- He opened each business's website, found the contact page, and sent a manual email.
- Core advice: "pick something that exists already where you see money is already flowing," then add your own signature. His was gamification.

## How the cold email itself was written

Every email was personalized and went straight to the point.

- Name: he pulled the owner's or a staff member's name from Yelp reviews, where owners reply to customers or customers mention helpful staff.
- Pitch: "I'm trying to help escape room businesses increase their booking rate."
- Extra personalization: he mentioned how many Yelp reviews they had and noted something they could do better.
- He positioned himself as the bridge to more customers.
- Call to action: pick any slot on his calendar.
- He admits that as an engineer this felt "super uncomfortable."

## The $4,000 outcome from one call

In mid-2018, while on a visa run in Japan with $0, one lead (Danela, an escape room owner) agreed to a call.

- He says the call was awkward and he was "completely thinking like an engineer."
- She said yes. He built a Facebook Messenger bot with automated games (10-20 built), such as a murder mystery where players pick the suspect.
- Players who finished earned a 5% coupon for the escape room, which lifted the business's conversion rate.
- She spent around $3,000-$4,000 in total, paying on and off for 4-5 years.
- He says there was no customer support: "we did one call, that's it."
- The business peaked slightly above $4,000 MRR and closed in 2020 when Covid killed the escape room industry.
- Overall he credits cold email alone for about $770,000 in revenue plus roughly $10,000-$15,000 on PayPal (he is unsure of exact figures), and one customer who spent $13,000.
- His conclusion: cold email for any B2B business is "really, really underrated."

## Selling the outcome and handling the call

Marc's framing for sales calls:

- Sell the business outcome, not the product. He told her the cost, and that used well she would get 3x that money back.
- Reduce risk: in the worst case he could refund her if his calculation was wrong, so "no string attached."
- Treat a marketing tool like an investment with an expected return.
- Use calls to hear objections and ask questions. You may find your product is a poor fit and another product would fit, and people will share their struggles.
- Ask "is there anything you struggle with that I can make easier for you." He finds this more genuine than pushing.
- If someone says "I can't afford it," he reads it as them not seeing the value, since they could afford it if the value were there.

## Validate before building: sell it before you make it

- Most entrepreneurs never make money because they build things people don't want. Spending 3 months building without asking customers usually ends with a product nobody wants, because "we convince ourselves this is a good idea."
- His friend Andre told him "sell it before you make it." Marc collected escape room emails worldwide and pitched first.
- Calls and cold emails, though hard, taught him the foundations of marketing as a developer.
- You may not build the exact business you imagined, but you end up making something people want.

## A daily cold email pipeline

Marc structured his day around outreach:

- Mornings were for marketing and sales: find 5-10 new escape rooms on Yelp and Trip Advisor.
- Pipeline stages: stage 1 is the lead with no email sent, stage 2 is first email, stage 3 is second, and so on.
- He used four templates, one per stage, so no lead was missed.
- Sending was automated: if no reply, the lead moved to the next stage.
- After the fourth email he moved on and closed the lead.

## DataFast: growing to $1M valuation and learning from churn

Marc's target for DataFast (his analytics software) is a $1M valuation. He uses a 4x multiple on annualized MRR as "a generous multiple for software." At the time of filming MRR was $642 and the product was about a month or two old.

- Asking non-signups why: he asked on Twitter why visitors didn't sign up. One reply said migrating all their data wasn't worth the hassle, so he is focusing on features that help people grow with actionable data.
- Customer journey feature: shows everything a customer did before signing up (for example, found DataFast on X, visited twice, then paid), with the aim of tracking any goal and later giving actionable insights.
- Stripe webhook automations: bots listen for failed checkouts and cancellations and email the customer. His cancel email says he noticed they cancelled and asks why, "I'm just curious." He gets decent replies, often showing the user didn't see the value yet.

## Working constraints and avoiding burnout

- Marc says starting with about an hour a day is plenty, and constraints create creativity.
- His weakness as a full-timer is having too much time, which leaves him less creative, less happy and burned out.
- He recommends keeping your main job while building, since there is less pressure to succeed fast. Going full time makes your brain expect results.
- He is trying to stop obsessing over views, likes and revenue, writes down the metrics to track, plans one video per week, and now takes one day off a week.
- He values trusted people who believe in you, because the early stage is "pure faith."
