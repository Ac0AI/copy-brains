## Competition is for losers: monopoly is the goal inside a company

- Peter claims everyone starting or joining a company should "always aim for a monopoly" on the inside. Whether a given monopoly is good or bad for society is a separate discussion.
- He argues the conventional view that capitalism and competition are synonyms is backwards: "capitalism and competition are antonyms." A capitalist accumulates capital, and in a world of perfect competition all profits are competed away.
- Example of too much competition: he was once talked into opening a restaurant in San Francisco, "one of the worst ideas ever." It has crazy competition and is undifferentiated, so you are unlikely to make money.
- Example of a monopoly business: Google, which definitively distanced itself from Yahoo and Microsoft in 2002 and has made enormous amounts of money since.
- He quips that perhaps the top 20 people at Google understand what makes it successful, while the 20,000 below them have no clue.
- The Wall Street Journal retitled his chapter "All Happy Companies are Different" as "Competition Is for Losers," which he accepts as punchier.
- He inverts Tolstoy: all happy companies are different because they found something unique to do; all unhappy companies are alike because they failed to escape the essential sameness of competition.

## Why monopoly vs. competition is poorly understood: the distorted narratives

- Peter says the real difference between monopolies and competitive businesses is always bigger than the apparent one, because both sides misdescribe themselves.
- Monopolists understate their monopoly. Google does not talk about the 98% of revenue from search. It talks about being in "technology," a vast space where it competes with Apple on Android phones, Facebook, Amazon, and Detroit via self-driving cars.
- Competitors understate their competition. A restaurant founder pitching investors, who know restaurants lose money, invents a tiny narrative such as "the only British Nepalese fusion cuisine in downtown San Francisco."
- Result: both categories blur together, so the underlying idea is poorly understood.
- Practical test: when deciding what a business really is, strip away the narrative the company tells about its market.

## The psychology of competition: tracks, validation, and misdirected ambition

- Peter argues people are taught the losers are those who compete poorly (weaker grades, weaker teams). He says nobody considers that people too addicted to competition are at a systematic disadvantage.
- Autobiographical example: he grew up on a tracked path (a friend wrote in his eighth grade yearbook that he'd get into Stanford; he did, then Stanford Law, then a top New York law firm). At each step you won a competition and got to compete again, getting very good at it while losing sight of why.
- At the law firm, everyone outside wanted in and everyone inside wanted out. He left after seven months and three days; a colleague down the hall said it was reassuring to see it was possible "to escape from Alcatraz." Identities were so wrapped up in past competitions that leaving was inconceivable, though "you just had to go out the front door and not come back."
- Kissinger-style point on academia: battles are ferocious because stakes are small. When people are undifferentiated, differences shrink and they fight over less and less.
- Peter claims competition is "extremely validating" and therefore "super-misleading."

## Contrarian thinking: courage is scarcer than genius

- Peter's core interview question is "Tell me something that's true that very few people agree with you on." It is hard even when candidates know it is coming.
- His business version: "What great company is nobody starting?"
- The difficulty is social, not about brilliance: "courage is in far shorter supply than genius." He ties this to political correctness "properly understood" as a broad problem.
- Weak answers are things everyone already knows (the education system is screwed up, politics does not work). Good answers are uncomfortable ones the interviewer does not want to hear.
- On Silicon Valley founders seeming to have a mild form of Asperger's, he says to turn this into a critique of society: people without it get talked out of creative, original ideas before they are fully formed, picking up subtle social cues that an idea is "too strange" and drifting back to "opening that restaurant."
- On Harvard Business School: he says the largest cohort systematically goes into the wrong career each year, after two years of extroverted peers talking to each other, and they end up chasing the last wave. Examples: everyone wanted to work for Michael Milken in 1989, a year or two before he went to jail; HBS descended on Silicon Valley in 1999-2000 and timed the dotcom bubble end perfectly.
- Originality is hard because of imitation: "ape" meant both primate and to imitate in Shakespeare's time, and he cites Aristotle that man has greater aptitude for imitation. Imitation drives language learning and culture but also peer pressure, bubbles, and manias.
- No formula exists. It often starts with not focusing on the people around you and being passionate about a transcendent idea. Dressing like a hipster is not originality.
- His own minority experience (founding a conservative libertarian paper at Stanford) built resistance to the idea that "truth is collective."

## Avoid trends and buzzwords; start with a small market

- Peter says "all trends are overrated" and that he dislikes being asked about tech trends. Banal answers (more cell phones in five years) tell you nothing.
- He lists healthcare IT software and education software as overrated, SaaS enterprise software as "really overrated," and says big data and cloud computing should make you "think fraud" and "run away."
- Buzzwords are "like a tell in poker": they signal the company is one undifferentiated member of a category, which means lots of competition and a bad idea. Examples to avoid: the fourth online pet food company, the tenth thin film solar panel company, the one-thousandth restaurant in San Francisco.
- If a company is easy to describe using existing categories, that is a bad sign. Successful companies are hard to categorize and often get mischaracterized:
  - Google in the late 90s was filed as another search engine, but was the first computer-powered search; others were human-organized lists.
  - Facebook is filed as social networking, but others came first. Reid Hoffman started SocialNet in 1997 with avatars as cats and dogs. What mattered was real identity, which Facebook cracked first.
- Start with a very small market and take it over, then branch out:
  - PayPal started with about 20,000 eBay power sellers and reached roughly 30-35% market share in three to four months.
  - Facebook launched at Harvard with 10,000 students and went from 0% to 60% share in 10 days.
- Clean tech failure: 2005-2008 pitch decks opened with a giant "energy" market in the hundreds of billions or trillions. A thin film solar startup would have to beat nine similar companies, then 90 solar companies, then wind and gas, with fracking and China arriving unexpectedly. "When you're a minnow in a vast ocean," there is "no such thing as an empty ocean."
- He adds that failure is overdetermined (about five reasons), so you usually learn only one and "failure is very overrated" as a teacher.

## Globalization vs. technology: going from 1 to n vs. 0 to 1

- Peter argues the two words are wrongly used as synonyms. Globalization is horizontal, extensive growth: copying things that work, going from one to ten (like one typewriter to 100). Technology is vertical, intensive growth: doing new things, zero to one (typewriter to word processor).
- His history: the 19th century (1815-1914) had both. World War I reversed globalization (trade broke down, part of the world turned communist) while technology continued. Kissinger's 1971 China trip marks globalization restarting in earnest.
- For much of the last 40 years technological progress has been narrower, as "technology" came to mean information technology. In the 50s and 60s it meant biotech, medical devices, nuclear power, new energy, underwater cities, the green revolution, space travel, supersonic aviation, and flying cars.
- Summary: lots of progress in the world of bits, not the world of atoms. The political explanation: for 40 years bits were relatively unregulated while atoms were "more or less regulated to death."
- He says for the 21st century to succeed, globalization and technology must both proceed.

## Cultural anti-technology bias and the "developed world" framing

- Peter claims we live in a society that "dislikes science and technology in just about all its forms," despite being told there is lots of progress.
- His challenge: name one Hollywood science fiction film in the last 25 years where technology is portrayed positively (not dystopian, not killing people or destroying the world). He cites Terminator, Avatar, Elysium, The Matrix, and Gravity ("You would never want to go into outer space"). Hollywood mostly reflects the broader culture.
- He argues this makes Silicon Valley the center of the counterculture today.
- Geopolitical framing: in the 1950s-60s the world was the first world (accelerating technological progress) versus the third world (permanently broken), a pro-tech, anti-globalization split. Today it is "developed" versus "developing," a pro-globalization convergence story where the world becomes more homogenous.
- That framing is implicitly anti-technological: calling a region "developed" says things are finished, nothing new will happen, and the younger generation should accept reduced expectations compared with parents and grandparents. He says this view of the US and the West should be strongly rejected.
- His closing contrarian question: "How can we go about developing our so-called developed world."

## Q&A: failure, tech investing as a bet, mission vs. social entrepreneurship, education, and California

- **Failure and sentiment:** Peter doubts people learn much from failure. After dotcom failures in the late 90s, the lesson was "aim lower," a self-fulfilling prophecy. Easy success (the Google/Microsoft experience) teaches "it's easy," also wrong. "Hard is probably a pretty good lesson." Extreme optimism says do nothing because the future takes care of itself; extreme pessimism says do nothing because we are doomed; both converge on inaction. PayPal sold to eBay, a bittersweet intermediate outcome (they had hoped to create a new world currency), but it taught entrepreneurs that building a great company is hard but possible.
- **Mature tech stocks:** Most NASDAQ 100 companies are "bets against technological innovation." Investing in Microsoft bets no new operating system or mobile shift emerges, Oracle is a bet against the cloud, IBM a bet that people stick with 1970s software. They can be good cash-flow investments, but be clear about the bet. GM was a tech company in the 1920s; by the 1980s it was a bet against German and Japanese innovation.
- **Mission vs. social entrepreneurship:** A mission-oriented company is one where, if it does not work on the problem, the problem never gets solved. SpaceX (Elon Musk) going to Mars is his example. He says "social entrepreneurship" is ambiguous (good for society vs. good as seen by society), usually the latter, which attracts copycats, making the category "a dismal failure."
- **Bitcoin:** He admits a possible bias because PayPal failed at digital currency. Bitcoin is a brilliant innovation that created a new currency, but still needs to get the payment system part to work.
- **Higher education bubble:** Over $1 trillion in US student debt "went to pay for lies" about the quality of education. Bubbles involve public mania and abstraction. He sees education as an insurance policy against falling through society's cracks combined with a zero-sum tournament where a diploma from the wrong school is "a dunce hat in disguise."
- **California:** The tech and entertainment industries are heavily networked, so people stay despite high taxes (the 2012 marginal tax increase caused no exodus). He expects New York, where finance is more mobile, to collapse politically before California. The danger is policy going far wrong unnoticed, then collapsing off a cliff as with Detroit and the car industry, though he thinks California is still "a ways away."
