## Technology stagnation, definite optimism, and why the future must be built

- Peter frames the final class around "going from 0 to 1": creating new technology, and radically better technology building toward a singularity.
- He applies the same framework to individual lives: "There is a mini singularity whenever you start a company or make a key life decision. In a very real sense, the life of every person is a singularity."
- The default advice runs the other way. People are told to play it safe and be conventional, and "the future, we are told, is just probabilities and statistics. *You* are a statistic."
- Peter calls that "selling yourself short." Statistical processes, the law of large numbers and globalization are "timeless, probabilistic, and maybe random," but technology and a life are "a story of one-time events."

## The case against waiting for the singularity

- Peter poses the Kurzweil-style objection to the class: if progress follows an exponential curve that is "a law of nature," the singularity happens regardless of what individuals do, so one could "sit back, grab some popcorn, and enjoy the show."
- He puts the question to the panel as a real challenge ("Is there anything wrong with that argument?") rather than endorsing the passive view.
- His closing remarks give his own answer: "If we don't take charge and usher in the future, if you don't take charge of your life, there is the sense that no one else will."
- He tells students: "Don't be deterred by notions of luck, impossibility, or futility. Use your power to shape your own life and go and do new things."

## Secrets: white spaces on the map of knowledge

- Peter's closing claim: singular events are hard to teach or generalize about, but "the big secret is that there are many secrets left to uncover."
- He describes "many large white spaces on the map of human knowledge" that anyone can go discover.
- His instruction: "Get out there and fill in the blank spaces. Every single moment is a possibility to go to these new places and explore them."
- The advice is to "go find a frontier and go for it" and to choose to do something important and different, not follow the well-trodden path.

## Timing: not paddling too far ahead of the wave

- Peter treats timing as decisive for technology that is still speculative. If something is only barely plausible science fiction, it may not make sense to work on it yet.
- His example: a Chinese man who tried to launch a rocket into space in the 11th century. "No one was or should have been working on supersonic flight in the Middle Ages. That would be paddling way too far in advance of the wave."
- He proposes a middle path: a roughly 20-year goal with milestones along the way.
- He flags the tradeoff himself: too many milestones make the achievability of the end goal speculative.
- Even with an uncertain date for radical technology, he says the timing "does make a great deal of difference."

## Who builds the future: Peter's framing of the options

- Peter summarizes the panel's three answers to who forges the technological future: a top-down/bottom-up combination, social opinion molders, and small tribes.
- He chooses to "run with some version of" the tribe theory (a small cabal of tech people driving change) and steers the discussion from there.
- He asks whether people who think about radical technology get dismissed as crazy, and presses the panel on why, which sets up the discussion of cultural resistance to long-term technological thinking.

## Why now is the moment to start

- Peter says there is "perhaps no specific time that is necessarily right to start your company or start your life," but "some times and some moments seem more auspicious than others. Now is such a moment."
- The close ties the course together: new technology, new companies and individual life choices are all singular, one-time acts, and the obvious conventional answer for what to do with your own singularity is the wrong one.
