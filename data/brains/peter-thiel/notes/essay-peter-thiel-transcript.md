## Founding moments, durability, and building for the long term

- Peter left a tracked career (Stanford law, a big New York law firm, a bank) after a "rolling quarter-life crisis". His test: do something where "if you wouldn't do it, it wouldn't happen", not a career where "a hundred other people" would take your place.
- He argues the frontier is not as wide open as in the 19th century, and technology is where a small group can still start something new with a big impact.
- He prefers "founder" to "entrepreneur". "Entrepreneur" is overused and sounds like "I want to be rich, I want to be famous". A better mindset: there are important problems to solve, and a new business is the form in which you solve them.
- He favors a long-term plan over the "random walk" of iterating with no overarching vision. From chess: "a bad plan was better than no plan".
- Most value comes late. A PayPal financial model from March 2001 (27 months in) put about 80 percent of the company's value in profits in 2011 and beyond. So "durability is at least as important as growth".
- Founding moments carry a degree of freedom that later disappears. He compares the Constitutional Convention: Alaska has the same number of senators as California, which has 50 times the people, and that will not change. The key company DNA is set early.
- On teams: talent often beats experience, because experienced people have "anti-theories" about why things can't be done. At PayPal the team ignored advice that fraud would be overwhelming.
- Team members must work together, not just be brilliant. He asks how long the founders have known each other and how often they have worked together. Starting a company with someone met a week earlier is like "getting married to the first person you met at the slot machines in Las Vegas".

## PayPal: compounding growth, one-way payments, and being the last mover

- The core insight: only about 3 million US businesses could accept credit cards, but 150 to 200 million people had email. Linking money to email lets anyone with an email receive a payment, which solved the usual counterparty problem of needing both sides signed up at once.
- Growth: 24 people (the company's own staff) in October 1999, compounding about 7 to 10 percent daily. About 1,000 by mid-November, 2,000 by December 31, 100,000 by February 3, 2000, and 1 million by mid-April. Today about 120 million users (as of the 2014 taping).
- He wants positive feedback and snowballing, not "trench warfare on the Western front" where each step gets harder.
- It took off on eBay. eBay's own competing service was "a giant store that couldn't get its own cash register machines to work".
- On competition: "you don't want excess competition". The 20th person selling pet food online, or opening a restaurant in DC or San Francisco, has a terrible business. Good businesses have an "extremely unique character".
- The original plan was first-mover exponential growth so copycats could never catch up. In practice, "it's more important to be the last mover than the first mover". He cites Capablanca: to begin, study the endgame, because the last mover wins.
- That usually needs a technological edge. At PayPal it was fraud. The fraud problem deterred big banks, whose consultants said "too much fraud". Citigroup's competing effort failed because smarter people inside sensed it would not work and avoided it. Large corporations are "quite dysfunctional" with many bureaucratic levels and internal politics.

## The "political approach" to contrarian truth, and psychosocial bubbles

- His interview question: "Tell me something that's true that very few people agree with you on." He admits he is not sure it is a good question, since people answer poorly. Typical answers are a conventional truth claimed to be even more true (worry about climate change even more) or trivial, esoteric truths.
- He stresses it has two parts: something others don't believe and that is true. "Brilliance is hard, courage is in even shorter supply than brilliance."
- He sees powerful psychosocial forces homogenizing thinking: an Internet bubble in the 90s, a housing and finance bubble in the 2000s, and in his view a "government bubble" in the 2010s, all marked by deferring to others' supposed answers. There was a big bubble in the 1720s and 1920s, but four or five of similar size in the last 30 years. Like an EKG with repeated spikes, "it might be a mistake to say that everything is normal".
- Two ways to find unconventional truths: a natural/metaphysical approach (work out fundamental truth) and a political approach (ask what people can't say or think). He prefers the political approach as more straightforward, "a shortcut".
- Example, clean tech: from 2002 to 2008 venture capitalists mainly invested in next-generation Internet companies (mostly worked) or clean tech ("an unmitigated disaster"). Evaluating each technology on its merits would have been extremely hard. The political approach saw intensely held conventions, social status ("it seemed cool"), and no interest in whether it made sense. That is why roughly ten times as much good money was thrown after bad in 2005 to 2008.
- He notes that many Silicon Valley founders have mild Asperger's traits or social awkwardness, which he reads as an indictment of American society: well-adjusted people are deterred from unconventional thoughts quickly.
- The worst founder profile is the Harvard MBA: extroverted people without strong convictions who look to each other and ride the last wave. He cites a Harvard Business School study: in 1989 they all wanted to work for Mike Milken (a year or two before jail), technology only in 1999 to 2000 (the two worst years), and 2005 to 2007 private equity, hedge funds and real estate.
- He worries that a more transparent, connected world makes people self-censor more than 50 years ago. He cites Nietzsche: madness is rare in individuals but the rule in nations and crowds.

## Human-computer complementarity (PayPal fraud and Palantir)

- Two conventional fraud approaches both failed. One was human investigators (20 of them) hunting a needle in millions of transactions. The other was a "super-duper computer" that figures everything out, which would need general AI that does not exist.
- The winning paradigm was a hybrid: the computer flags somewhat suspicious things and trained people visualize the transactions. Getting the division of labor right was "extremely powerful".
- Palantir (started 2004) applies the same idea to counterterrorism. The pure-human model is characteristic of the CIA (no one knows what anyone else is doing). The pure-computer model is characteristic of the NSA (collect all the data, no idea what you have). The hybrid is "vastly more powerful".
- Palantir has a backend that combines databases that do not connect, and a front-end visualization engine for trained analysts to spot suspicious patterns. He says it helped connect dots in identifying bin Laden, and in SEC insider trading cases.
- His view: computers and humans are "fundamentally complementary", good at very different things. The dominant narratives (Luddite, or utopian where computers replace us) are wrong. "We have not asked the question enough of how can people work better with computers." A computer smarter than a human in every respect is "somewhere between science fiction and science fiction fantasy".
- On government technology: bureaucratic incentives work against sustained effort. He blames the DC contractor subculture, with billing by the hour and long, complex projects so "nobody really has responsibility". As a libertarian he was still shocked the Obamacare website failed: "this is not the Manhattan Project".

## Facebook: why he invested and why real identity won

- He and Reid Hoffman invested about half a million dollars in Facebook in summer 2004, after Zuckerberg launched at Harvard in February 2004 and was already on about 20 campuses. It was a good sign that the only thing it needed money for was more computers, because demand was so high.
- Others were too negative on social networks after earlier ones came and went. Peter looked at intensity of usage, time spent and network effects. He initially thought it could be a media channel reaching all college students, not that it would get this big.
- Why it beat earlier efforts: 1990s social networking assumed fictional alter egos (pretending to be a cat or dog in an AOL chat room). People wanted real identities. Starting at Harvard helped because people represented themselves accurately.
- MySpace (Los Angeles) was about narcissistic self-presentation and writing. Facebook was about learning about others, and over time "reading dominates writing". He says the names themselves were suggestive.
- It opened beyond college in late 2006 or early 2007, after two years of college-only. Later additions included multiple languages, age groups, and the newsfeed.

## Technological stagnation: specific success masking general failure

- Facebook is probably worth about as much as all other Silicon Valley companies started since 2000 combined. His framing: there are "specific successes that may be symptomatic of general failure". Facebook and Twitter are great businesses, and not to blame; the problem is that so few others are being built.
- Founders Fund's tagline: "They promised us flying cars and all we got was 140 characters." It is a dig at Twitter, which he says will last decades but is "probably not enough to take our civilization to the next level".
- Over 40 to 50 years there was big innovation in information technology (hardware, software, Internet, mobile) and much less in everything else. His reason: "bits are quite unregulated, atoms are very heavily regulated". Bill Gates in 1975 had no government checking his code; biotech and rockets face massive regulatory barriers.
- "STEM" is an abstraction. Only computer science and maybe petroleum engineering have been good careers for 40 years. Electrical engineering was mediocre in the 80s, and nuclear and aerospace were "catastrophic decisions for very talented people".
- Gadgets "distract us from the ways in which our larger surroundings are strangely old": phones in a 19th-century New York subway, a decrepit 50s and 60s San Francisco housing stock. "Bits making progress, atoms are strangely very stuck."
- Natural explanation (low-hanging fruit picked) versus cultural one. He is "very heavily on the cultural bias": a culture more risk-averse and less ambitious. People prefer the natural explanation because it exonerates them from responsibility. Evidence: the regulatory double standard, and SDI, which in the 1980s everyone assumed would work, while now it is assumed it never will, even though Israel's Iron Dome works remarkably well.
- Macro signs: wage stagnation since the early 1970s, most Americans believing the next generation will be worse off. Cultural sign: recent science fiction films (Terminator, Matrix, Avatar, Elysium) show dystopian technology; Star Trek retreads are a 60s throwback and The Jetsons a reactionary aesthetic.
- Energy: nuclear is still "the energy of the future" because of energy density, versus windmills and solar panels, which are less energy intensive and "retrograde". We could build much safer, cheaper reactors with political will and belief.

## Accept, deny, or fight stagnation

- Peter says there are three responses to stagnation: accept, deny, or fight. Culture is dominated by acceptance (Democrats: an era of limits, lower expectations) and denial (Republicans, especially the Bush 43 era: growth is automatic, fix one or two policies). Both tell you "there's nothing to worry about, there's nothing you can do". What is needed is to fight it.
- "Technological stagnation" is not meant to demotivate: "there's a problem and we could be doing a lot better".
- Belief is a necessary precondition: "thinking you can do something is a necessary precondition for being able to do it." Example: Andrew Wiles worked eight years alone on Fermat's last theorem after 358 years of attempts; if he hadn't thought he could, he never would have.
- Hysteresis: Nixon declared war on cancer in 1970, promising victory by the 1976 Bicentennial. Forty-four years later no president would declare war on Alzheimer's, though about one-third of Americans at 85 have some dementia.
- Where it can break through: space (some deregulation under Bush 43 enabled SpaceX), Uber and Airbnb (technology outpaces politics, then tens of thousands depend on it so it is not shut down). But medicine is hard without a less onerous FDA. Things needing political cooperation up front are "quite hard to change".
- Globalization is implicitly anti-technological: splitting the world into developed and developing nations treats the developed world as where "nothing new is going to happen". His question: "how can we develop the developed world?"
- Politicians lack concrete visions. The last such speeches he recalls: Martin Luther King's "I have a dream" and Reagan's Berlin Wall speech. Obama's slogan shifted from "hope and change" to "the change we need" because change poll-tested badly. Pessimism in Japan and Western Europe is "somewhat un-American", leaving an opening for an optimistic story.
- He wonders if the US constitutional system can work without growth: legislating works when the pie grows (a bit for you, a bit for me). The 1930s strained all democratic systems.
- He is bullish on Silicon Valley (the most charismatic place for talent) and bearish on New York, the tracked, overly financialized path. Since 2008, tracked jobs (lawyer, banker, consultant) work less well.

## The higher education bubble

- Peter calls education "perhaps the most anti-technological aspect of our society": if technology means doing more with less, it gives the same at higher prices. Real costs have gone up about 400 percent since 1980, after inflation, with no clear quality gain.
- He says we have an education bubble: things cost more than they're worth, with psychosocial dynamics and "abstractions away from reality". "Education" is "incredible abstract filler". Drill down: engineering, rigorous humanities, or education in the abstract?
- Investment vs consumption: people think they are investing by consuming, like buying a house with a pool as a frugal investment. His better frame is insurance against falling through the cracks. In reality it is a "zero-sum tournament masquerading as general insurance", where a third-tier diploma is "a dunce hat in disguise".
- Value is mostly selection and signaling, with little value-added learning. Test: if Harvard's education were as good as claimed, it would grow enrollment (say 1,600 to 3,000 over 20 years). Instead the only product that limits access this much is "a nightclub". Doubling enrollment would draw uniform opposition from alumni, students and faculty.
- Law schools: the top three (Harvard, Stanford, Yale) are very good, the next four are pretty good, 8 to 14 only for the top half, and for roughly 15 to 200 it is unclear whether it adds value for the bottom 90 percent.
- The college premium stopped widening around 2000 while costs kept rising, so years to pay off debt are rising and the relative premium has declined for 14 years. 2008 was a watershed as tracked jobs dried up.
- Reform: top universities, with rich endowments, resist outside influence. He compares the university system to "the atheist church": 2014 is like the Catholic Church in 1514, with a priestly class, indulgences in the form of debt, and a Reformation that will come from outside. He expects online alternatives to gain traction, and the non-elite colleges to change first.
- Elite schools dampen ambition: a Yale dean told incoming students "you're set for life". Peter says ambitions get "beaten out of them" and notes Caltech students convinced they can only be line engineers at Lockheed. His career version of the contrarian question: "what are you really good at that other people aren't that good at?" He wants a return to many paths, not a single track.
