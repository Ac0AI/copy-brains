## Stagnation thesis: slowdown in atoms, progress only in bits

- Peter has argued for about 20 years that since the 1970s progress slowed broadly. Progress came in the world of bits (computers, internet, mobile internet, software) but not in the world of atoms.
- Engineering fields tied to atoms (mechanical, chemical, aero-astro, nuclear) were already "a bad idea" for students to pursue by the 1980s.
- He calls it "not total stagnation but this broad slowdown," hard to measure because progress has many dimensions.
- The internet was an important growth driver in the late 1990s, bigger than anything else then, but perhaps not big enough to take civilization to the next level.
- He now thinks AI is perhaps bigger than the internet and has the potential to re-accelerate growth, "with all the good and bad things that come with that."
- His defense of the thesis today: now that AI exists, nobody disagrees that the prior decades were stagnation. It was proven in retrospect.
- He has "changed his mind to some extent" only in that AI may end the stagnation.

## Why stagnation deranges society: zero-sum politics and the broken intergenerational compact

- When progress slows, "there's a loser for every winner," so society becomes zero-sum and politics polarizes.
- The middle class in the US and Europe expects its children to do better than themselves. The intergenerational progressive compact (material, health and social progress from one generation to the next) is what holds society together.
- When that breaks down, "the whole society starts to derange in very strange ways."
- Common misconception: people picture the alternative to AI as a peaceful, social democratic zero-growth world. He says that is not true, and the no-progress alternative "is also far from neutral."
- Democracies and liberal societies are in trouble because they lack a broad growth story of the future. People no longer think things are getting better.
- AI is not a panacea, but without growth, zero-sum parties on the extreme right and left will keep gaining ground.
- Europe and the US are "gerontocracies," societies run for old people. Young people find it very hard to get ahead.
- His first step on tech stagnation: "I don't have a comprehensive solution but the first step is you have to talk about it."

## AI risk, slowing down, and the China competition

- On scenarios where a far-smarter-than-human system escapes control, he doesn't think probabilities are very high, but notes that even 5 or 10% "seems pretty bad."
- He rejects the conclusion that you should therefore always go slower. Doing nothing carries its own risk, and "you can never just turn the clock back."
- Slowing AI globally would need real, forceful one-world government. His classical liberal side calls that "a frying pan into fire... a cure that's worse than the disease."
- The realistic alternative is "fake global government": many conferences and empty statements, with a risk that the West slows while China does not.
- He says the Pope's AI encyclical works for the Communist Party of China: the CCP won't listen to the Pope, but people in the US might. He called the Pope "a useful idiot" for the CCP, while saying he is not claiming he is an agent.
- Asked about Chinese-spread anti-AI content on American social media, he is inclined to believe it but thinks it is not the main factor. "It's mainly what we're doing to ourselves," and he warns it is too easy to blame China.
- He wants the rivalry with China taken very seriously and is a "very tough critic of China."
- He pushes back hard on any moral equivalence between the US and China. He says it would be best for Europe to be a reliable ally.

## Moralizing technology: good vs. evil vs. bad

- Peter says society moralizes far too much and asks for the antonym of "good." The moral antonym is evil. The functional antonym is "bad."
- Examples: a Ferrari is an "evil" car (poor miles per gallon) but competent. A Trabant is a "bad" car: it doesn't work.
- "If you're evil, you're at least competent... you're at least getting something done."
- Marlowe's Faust (a fake scientist, bad science, straight to hell) versus Goethe's Faust (pact with the devil, dubious means, but it works out for the best and he is redeemed).
- He says it is better to be good than evil, but "I prefer Goethe's Faust."
- The real problem to discuss: in a world where "99% of businesses, companies, people, political leaders, business leaders are totally incompetent," badness, not evil, is the issue.

## Europe's decline: far behind in AI, and the fear-of-success hypothesis

- In his telling, the US is where AI is happening, China is a fast follower, and Europe is "extremely far behind," and it is extremely hard to catch up.
- Evidence from Palantir: sales to US corporations have accelerated tremendously in recent years because of urgency to transform companies for the AI age. Europe has much less urgency, with the attitude "don't do tomorrow what you can save for the day after."
- Two historical analogies:
  - Concorde: supersonic flight began in the US (Chuck Yeager, 1947), Europe was behind but caught up and overtook it. Not a free-market program, but something the US should support.
  - French Minitel (1990s): the internet was supposed to be French, and Europe dug itself into a deeper ditch.
- His assessment: on AI in 2026, Europe is further behind than Minitel was.
- Germany's usual explanation is a fear of failure. He pushes a different dimension: perhaps also a "fear of success," meaning not scaling something that works to an extreme degree like Elon Musk or Mark Zuckerberg, but selling or exiting.
- Stat he cites: of the 50 wealthiest people in the US, only 12 are Gen X or younger (born 1965 or later), and 9 of those 12 made their own money. In Germany, 20 of the 50 wealthiest are Gen X or younger, but all 20 inherited it. Not one built a large new scalable company.
- Each such founder creates thousands of millionaires and tens of thousands of jobs. Germany once built great new businesses (late 19th and early 20th century, and after 1945), but this "really broke down since 1995."
- He says he has no answer, but it is the question to think about hard.
- From an American perspective, it is in the US's interest for Europe to get its act together, build industry and be part of building the future. Where Europe is far behind, it should work with America much more closely.

## Debt, deficits and drift in Western politics

- Welfare states are very large, taxes "feel pretty high," and deficits are big. They might be sustainable at 0% interest rates, but rates are no longer zero, so there is "a compounding runaway deficit problem."
- Three choices: massively cut spending (the Milei answer), massively hike taxes (which would "completely shut down our societies and all growth"), or keep borrowing and kick the can down the road.
- For close to 20 years, since the 2008 crisis, the answer has been number three. He thinks that is close to ending, pushing countries toward socialist-level taxation or really tough austerity.
- He criticizes Merz: "the best idea Mertz has is just borrow more." He expects drift to continue for a long way because replacements lack answers either.
- Centrist leaders "systematically underestimate the scale of the problem." In Britain there have been seven prime ministers in 10 years, none with a plan to turn the society around. Each thinks becoming prime minister is the hard part.
- On Argentina and Milei: people are fascinated because "their past is our future," a century of subpar growth. He thinks Milei will be re-elected because the situation is so existential. He rejects the accelerationist Leninist idea that "the worse things get, the better," and worries things have to get a lot worse before they turn around.

## US politics: Trump, the Democratic establishment, DSA and 2028

- On Trump, he says to ask him in 10 years. His 2016 support reflected the establishment's failure to deal with long-term problems: growth, globalization and endless Middle East entanglements.
- On the two administrations making opposite mistakes: the first brought in people who couldn't agree, producing internal fighting. The second overcorrected with loyalty tests, staffing the government with "not very smart but very loyal people."
- He worries the exhaustion that hit the Republican establishment (the Bush family, 2016) is about to hit the Clinton-Obama center-left establishment. Pattern he cites: 2016 Trump won the Republican primary and Hillary barely beat Bernie Sanders, then the establishment won in 2020 and 2024, and it may not in 2028.
- The Democratic Party is the establishment party, so a Democratic Socialist takeover is a much bigger deal, "like, I don't know, the end of America." He does not want it and calls it "a real danger."
- Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) data: created in 1982 as a fringe fusion, about 5,000 members. After the 2016 Sanders campaign, membership rose from 5,000 to 50,000 and median age fell from 66 to 33. He says it has energy and charisma while establishment Democrats "are all lame."
- 2028 forecast: if Republicans get wiped out in the midterms, Democrats will think they win no matter what and won't pull out all stops to block a Democratic socialist. He puts the chance of a DSA-type winner in 2028 at about 50%, and says he argued this with Fukuyama, who answered "No way. Can never happen." Peter: "I don't think history works like that."
- He says he is "still a Vance partisan" but thinks the key question is who the Democratic candidate will be. He backed JD Vance because he thought he was thoughtful, open-minded and charismatic, and says he helped him financially in the 2022 Senate race.

## The 2009 "freedom and democracy" quote and the AfD/Linke discussion

- On his 2009 line that he no longer believed freedom and democracy are compatible: he says he typed the essay one night, asked a colleague whether it was controversial, and was told no. In retrospect, he says he should have been more careful and issued a correction two weeks later.
- His actual argument was for "depoliticization, a de-intensification of politics," aimed at libertarians who put too much faith in politics. He says he said nothing Montesquieu or John Stuart Mill wouldn't have said, and that he does not think voters' rights will be taken away.
- Weimar lesson: in 1932 the Nazis and the Communists together got over 50% of the vote. Once over half vote for parties that want to end democracy, "it's almost too late."
- On Germany: the Linke got 25% in Berlin, but 47% among 16-24 year olds, a very bad trend since those voters will decide things eventually. He makes the "pro argument" only to show that you must take young people's issues seriously (rents too high, impossible to build, hard to get ahead). He says the Linke has no solutions but at least talks about the problems nobody else does.
- On the AfD: it reflects the failure of centrist parties to find an inclusive economic future for Germany. He says they don't know how to balance the budget, and he thinks the AfD is wrong on Ukraine, China and Israel, even though he wants reasonable debate on all three.
- On why he cares about politics: "it's both toxic, but it's the air we breathe." He thinks he is not electable, and says it might be healthier and more profitable to ignore politics and focus on a business.

## Contrarianism, generalism, longevity and the Antichrist answer

- On contrarianism: "the point is not to be contrarian for its own sake," since that just puts a minus sign in front of consensus. "The real goal is always to try to think for oneself."
- He grew up in Silicon Valley, and says founders combine an outsider and insider dynamic. He says people exaggerate the outsider piece, which tends to become playing the victim. He credits Alex Karp (Stanford Law insider, Jewish outsider element) and Max Levchin (left Ukraine at 16, grew up in a poor part of Chicago, straight-A student, "incredible drive") as examples.
- He explains his independence by broad interests: history, politics, economics, science fiction, the future, psychology. Against the Adam Smith pin-factory specialization of late modernity, he says "there has to be room for at least one generalist." He still plays too much chess online and was a scholastic player, calling it beautiful and "somewhat dangerously addictive."
- On kids: having four young children changes his time horizons. His oldest daughter "will be 81 years old in 2100," so he wonders what the 22nd century will be like.
- On longevity: early-modern optimists like Condorcet, Francis Bacon and Benjamin Franklin thought death and all diseases could be cured. He calls the abandonment of that "a shocking social decline" and asks why we won't even try to cure Alzheimer's.
- Cancer example: Nixon declared war on cancer in 1971 and said it would be cured in 5 years. It is now 55 years later, with only some progress.
- He suggests if biology is understood as an information science, in theory processes could be reversed, but he rejects mind uploading: "I wouldn't find it acceptable to be uploaded... I don't think it would be me," and holds a physical, Christian-informed view of identity.
- He says transhumanism's problem is not that it is too extreme but "that it didn't go far enough" compared with Christianity's eternal body. On boredom with eternal life, his answer is that it beats the alternative.
- His target for the years ahead: dementia, finding a way to slow or reverse declines in people's brains. He says biotech investing is "surprisingly difficult."
- On the contrarian-truth interview question: he gave his speeches about the Antichrist three years ago, thinking the view was extremely unconventional. He now says it is less contrarian than he thought: most people think something like the Antichrist is right around the corner and that there is incipient totalitarianism worldwide. His meta-level conclusion: people call the idea crazy but "they actually secretly agree with me."
