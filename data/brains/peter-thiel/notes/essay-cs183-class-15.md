## The retrofuture thesis: go back to the future, but don't copy it

- Peter argues the best way to think about the future is to think about how the future used to be. Mid-20th-century visions included precisely predicted or controlled weather, flying cars, civilian submarines and widely useful robots.
- Most of those futures "didn't work": weather still "just happens to us," people still sit in traffic, and robotics produced the Roomba instead of the Lost in Space robot.
- Computer science is the exception. Moore's Law held, power consumption fell and connectivity grew. The Dick Tracy 2-Way Wrist Radio/TV is essentially an iPod nano, and Arthur C. Clarke roughly predicted the Internet in 1956's *The City and the Stars*. In CS there is little left of the old future to retrieve.
- Elsewhere, he suggests revisiting lines of research that were underexplored at some point in the past. Example: thorium power, which may have been neglected because the military favored plutonium/uranium R&D. It is "at least worth taking a look at."
- The catch: nostalgia is useless, and the old future failed. The goal is to tap the past, learn from it, and apply that knowledge in some new, important way. In his words: think about where the past failed, learn the right lessons, make it work this time, and approach old problems from a very different perspective.

## Energy storage: a physics approach instead of chemistry

- Peter frames the core problem: energy production costs vary a lot, and power is far more expensive at peak times. Because off-peak power is hard to store, there is a large arbitrage between peak and off-peak cost.
- Batteries are about 200 years old, and he sees the trajectory as asymptotic: limits on packing density, corrosion, and possibly chemistry itself. A better battery may be like "finding a new element on the periodic table."
- The chemical paradigm "has done wonders, but maybe it's done doing them." The open question is whether non-chemical approaches are worth pursuing.
- He highlights LightSail Energy, which treats storage as a physics problem. It uses Boyle's law: power compresses air into steel tanks, and decompressing it later releases the energy. Compression heats the air and lets power dissipate, so water is sprayed into the air to cool it.
- LightSail claims a trillion-dollar market. Peter says they should be pushed on whether that is a huge opportunity or "a rather huge competitive ocean where you can't actually defend yourself." He still calls physical rather than chemical storage a very orthogonal and promising approach.
- Historical note from the guest discussion: compressed air storage with water spray was tried in the 1870s and abandoned for unknown reasons. This is the retrofuture pattern in practice.

## Weather: statistics in an area where people gave up

- Peter says it is "sort of amazing" that we are still really bad at predicting weather, especially short-term. Chaos and complexity are the excuses, but he sees a sense in which people "just stopped trying." Forecasting looks like fortunetelling and could be made more accurate, which would be valuable.
- Weather control is largely abandoned territory because it is seen as dangerous. Cloud seeding strikes some as crazier than terraforming Mars. He describes a backlash against even thinking about it, with a "pick your preferred mix of impossible/undesirable/unnecessary."
- He points to The Climate Corporation (formerly Weatherbill) as fighting this consensus. It improves crop loss insurance by predicting the weather at a particular parcel of land and adjusting quickly with advanced computer modeling.
- He grants that a statistical approach deserves initial skepticism, but argues it is a statistical approach in an area where people grew wary of statistics because earlier attempts failed. That makes interesting statistical approaches possibly underexplored.
- Secret hidden in plain sight: hardly anyone looks at agritech, since by definition it happens outside Silicon Valley, so it tends to be overlooked and undervalued.

## Robotics: reconceive the robot instead of building the butler

- Peter notes 1950s-60s visions of a household butler/maid/cook/driver robot that would raise living standards by doing chores for free. That has not happened. Humanoid bots are expensive and limited; the state of the art is a laundry-folding robot that takes 45 minutes per piece.
- Experts say a Lost in Space-style robot is 25 to 50 years away. Peter reads that as being just beyond the horizon where people are accountable: "It will happen, but I don't have to do anything. Someone else will do it."
- His example of re-imagining robots is RoboteX, which treats the robot as an intermediary rather than a humanoid duplicate of human tasks. Robots go into dangerous situations first, for SWAT teams, hazmat experts and bomb squads.
- Detail: RoboteX robots have been effective at hostage situations. Amped-up bad guys psychologically break when a little robot enters and surrender on the spot. Organizations pay considerably to keep people out of harm's way.
- Lesson: pick a use case where the robot's limits don't matter and the value is high, rather than chasing the sci-fi form.

## Space: fix the cost structure, not just the rocket

- Peter notes the moon landing era lasted 3.5 years: Apollo 11 in July 1969 to Apollo 17 in December 1972. It has been about 40 years since anyone was on the moon, so Mars seems very ambitious.
- He says the Space Shuttle was "oddly Pareto inferior": it cost more, did less and was more dangerous than a Saturn V rocket.
- Launch cost per kg to orbit had barely changed in 40 years. The key insight is that traditional space work was accounted for on a cost-plus basis. Contractors built cushy margins into rockets, everyone had incentives to inflate costs, and things got more expensive and arguably more dangerous.
- SpaceX re-approached the problem by getting the cost structure right, driving launch costs down, and revisiting basic designs with new technology and materials. By most accounts the improvements were quite successful, and he says SpaceX is arguably ushering in a new era of space flight.
- He says the open questions are whether better telemetry software and radically new rockets can bring a new era of space technology.

## Selling to big customers: start small, get references, grow iteratively

- Rule of thumb from Peter: the larger the cost of a product, the slower the sales process.
- The classic mistake is the fantasy of a single $100 million contract that makes everything golden. In practice that "almost never works out that way."
- Even with a technology an order of magnitude better, you can't make the golden sale. People don't know what they want, buying is never completely objective, and the person writing the check will ask who else has bought. If the answer is no one, they may stop, since something is probably wrong.
- Example: a nuclear plant should in theory deploy RoboteX robots (they would have been useful at Fukushima), but you can't simply sell to the theoretically ideal customer.
- Successful enterprise startups use iterative sales models, with 50-100% growth per year for several years. Example: $5M in year one, doubling every year for a decade. Revenue doesn't 10x in year four, because no customer will do a deal 10x your largest to date; 2x your biggest deal is a more realistic hope.
- Strategy: get the smallest customer that is also a good reference customer, move quickly and collect references. A police department that buys a robot that stops a hostage-taker becomes proof the robot may be good for other things.
- Agreeing with a guest: start with people who are desperate for what you provide.
- VC analogy: VCs fantasize about one rich LP who removes the need to talk to anyone else, but LPs cluster like everyone else. "Everybody likes to act like they know what they're doing. But in reality no one has a clue."

## Selling the company, and why unique ideas are hard to fund

- Peter says "sales works best when it's disguised." If you want to sell, act like you don't, and declaring you'll never sell is a good first step. Parallels: if you want advice, ask for money; if you want money, ask for advice; politicians solemnly say they have no interest in running for President.
- M&A has two typical drivers. One is grinding out inefficiencies, like banks buying smaller banks and firing half the people. The other, in tech, is product synergy, such as PayPal and eBay. He calls that the exception: synergy rarely exists, and genuine complementarity between truly novel technology and what incumbents do is very unlikely.
- On fundraising, he says it is very hard for investors to invest in things that are unique, and the psychological struggle is hard to overstate. People gravitate to the modern portfolio approach and say their portfolio will hold different things, which he finds odd.
- Truly different things are hard to evaluate. Asking a would-be rocket founder about rocket experience gets "zero" (Elon had none before SpaceX), and a VC investing in rockets knows "nothing" since no one has invested in rockets in over 40 years. iPhone games are familiar to everyone.
- SpaceX example: NASA more or less required SpaceX to take outside funding in 2008. Founders Fund invested then. Various Silicon Valley VC firms warned that it was risky and maybe crazy, even though the company had already built rockets and attempted launches.
- The upside of the unfamiliar: if you are unfamiliar with rockets, so is everyone else. The competitive bar is lower, and you can focus on learning and substance over process, which is perhaps better than competing against experts.
