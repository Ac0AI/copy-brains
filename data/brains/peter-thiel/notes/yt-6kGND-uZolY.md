## Competition is for losers: monopoly vs. competitive businesses

- Peter argues all happy companies are different and all unhappy companies are alike, inverting Tolstoy: unhappy companies fail "to escape the essential sameness of competition."
- The Wall Street Journal excerpted the chapter and retitled it "competition is for losers."
- He says the most important and under-discussed distinction in business is that there are only two kinds of companies: competitive ones and monopolies.
- A one-of-a-kind company doing something different is a monopoly, and building one should be every founder's goal.
- He calls capitalism and competition antonyms. A capitalist accumulates capital, and in perfect competition all profits are competed away.
- Bad business example: opening a restaurant, which is super competitive and where nobody makes money.
- Good business example: Google, which has had essentially no competition in search since 2002, when it distanced itself from Microsoft and Yahoo, and has made enormous profits for a dozen years.

## How companies disguise monopoly and competition

- Monopolists avoid talking about it by pretending to be in a much larger market. Google would never say it is a search engine; it says it is a technology company competing with Apple on phones, Facebook on social, Amazon, Microsoft, and car companies with self-driving cars.
- Competitive businesses do the reverse, pitching a fictionally small submarket to look unique. Example: "the only British food restaurant in Palo Alto." He notes nobody eats only British food, so it still competes with other restaurants, even ones in Menlo Park or Mountain View.
- Result: "the people who don't have monopolies pretend to have them, the people who have monopolies pretend not to have them." The apparent difference is small, the real difference is huge.
- On Google, he notes that despite 20-30,000 talented people, few great companies have come out of it. Insiders are told success comes from talent, so leavers conclude it's just about hiring smart people, and the business strategy (monopoly) gets obscured. People talk about talent when they don't want to talk about monopoly.
- Twitter example: it is a fantastic monopoly-like business built on the 140-character idea, which nobody could catch once it took off. He says such businesses "don't have to be operationally that airtight," unlike a restaurant, which must be operationally tight on everything.

## Why people crowd into competition

- Psychologically we are taught competition is valuable and feel safety in crowds: if many people want something, it must be good, like joining a long line without asking why.
- He notes that in Shakespeare's time "ape" meant both primate and to imitate, and says human nature is herd-like.
- Competing makes you better at the thing you compete on (SAT prep, swim team), but at the price of losing sight of what is really valuable.
- He quotes Kissinger on Harvard faculty: "the battles in Academia are so ferocious because the stakes are so small." When differences are small, you must fight harder to differentiate, so everyone squeezes through the same tiny door while a huge gate around the corner goes unexplored.
- On social conformity: the socially well-adapted get talked out of original ideas before they're formed, picking up cues like "that's a little too weird." Hence people with Asperger-like traits do well in Silicon Valley, which he frames as a critique of society.
- Harvard Business School studies over 30 years show the largest share of graduates goes into the wrong field because they all ride the last wave: Michael Milken in 1989 (one or two years before he went to jail), internet and tech in 1999-2000 just before the bubble burst, housing and private equity in the last decade.
- His conclusion: "there is no wisdom in crowds," an "anti-Malcolm Gladwell thing," just ferocious competition.

## Secrets: conventions, mysteries, and the in-between

- Peter's contrarian interview question: "Tell me something that's true that almost nobody agrees with you on." He finds it shockingly hard for people to answer.
- Two reasons it's hard: people are taught truth is whatever everybody agrees on, and an answer people already agree with is by definition the wrong answer, which takes courage to avoid.
- He splits knowledge into three categories: conventions (everyone knows), mysteries (almost impossibly hard to figure out), and secrets (hard to figure out, unknown today, but discoverable with work).
- A secret or idiosyncratic body of knowledge is often at the core of great businesses.
- PayPal example: the team was deeply interested in cryptography and currency on the internet and fantasized about a new world currency (it was on their t-shirt). They failed at that, but the exploration shaped how they built the payment system that worked.
- In the 17th-18th centuries empty spaces on maps were secrets you could explore; in the 19th century basic chemistry let you discover new elements. Geography and basic chemistry are now closed fields, but he says it's a mistake to extrapolate that all fields are closed.
- He sees plenty left in information technology (computers, internet, mobile internet) and thinks many other areas of science and technology have progressed less fast but hold great discoveries and businesses.
- On disclosing secrets: tell nobody and it does no good; tell everybody and it dilutes. He defines a company as "a group of people who have a secret," telling just the right number of people.
- He is skeptical that patent laws, which give legal monopolies on discoveries, work well in practice, though he likes the idea behind them.
- In law, injustice also involves a secret: something unjust persists because most people don't see it. He doubts we've found all injustices and says we're not at the end of history.
- His example guess for 2100's verdict on 2014: many political and business leaders were borderline sociopaths. He also suggests football may be where smoking was in the early 1960s: sensed to be bad (brain damage) but not yet articulated.

## Globalization vs. technology: 0 to 1 vs. 1 to n

- Peter sees two modes of progress in the 21st century: globalization (copying what works, going from 1 to n, horizontal or extensive growth) and technology (discovering new things, going from 0 to 1, vertical progress).
- He draws globalization on the x-axis and technology on the y-axis to show they are not synonyms, even though they're used interchangeably.
- China is the paradigm of globalization, with a straightforward 20-year plan of copying what works from the US, Western Europe and Japan, skipping steps and avoiding some bad ideas.
- History: the 19th century had both globalization and technological progress. After 1914 (first world war) globalization reversed while technology kept moving very fast. After 1971, when Kissinger went to China, globalization restarted in earnest, but he argues the last 40 years have seen slower technological progress, "a lot in computers, not so much elsewhere."
- The vocabulary changed accordingly. In the 1950s-60s: first world (progress happening) vs. third world (permanently screwed up). Today: developed vs. developing, a convergence theory in which everyone becomes alike.
- He finds "developed world" anti-technological, since it implicitly says nothing new will happen in the US, Western Europe and Japan and that the young should not expect lives better than their parents'.
- His call: resist the label and ask "how do we go about developing the so-called developed world."

## Investing: skeptical of themes, backing one-of-a-kind missions

- Asked about big themes for the next 5-10 years, Peter says he is skeptical of themes because each contains many undifferentiated companies: "you don't want to be the fourth online pet food company," the 10th thin-film solar company, or the 20th pizza restaurant in Palo Alto.
- He calls education software, healthcare IT, big data, cloud computing and SaaS overwrought themes, noting it's not even clear what "big data" and "cloud" mean.
- What works: one-of-a-kind companies where people are on a mission and the thing wouldn't happen elsewhere if they didn't do it.
- Example: his PayPal colleague Elon Musk, whose SpaceX mission to go to Mars attracted great rocket scientists. Companies going to Mars are not a theme, just one unique thing.
- Airbnb (Peter says they are large investors) is framed by others as a "sharing economy" theme, but he sees a one-of-a-kind company freeing up inventory in housing and expanding the tourism market.

## Good versus bad monopoly, and antitrust

- Peter says monopolies deserve their bad reputation in a static world where nothing changes, because there the monopolist is just a rent or tax collector that artificially restricts supply.
- A good monopoly invents something new. Apple's iPhone was the first smartphone that worked; Apple had a monopoly on smartphones for years and he argues still does through brand and network effects. That kind of monopoly doesn't create scarcity but a whole new thing, which is why people line up for iPhones.
- The policy question is how static technology is at the moment.
- He thinks government intervention has come too late: IBM was pursued in the 1970s just as computing shifted from hardware to software, and Microsoft in the late 1990s when things were shifting to the internet anyway, with no reason to think Microsoft would have dominated the internet as it did the desktop.
- He is skeptical of the EU's intervention against Google and says Silicon Valley underestimates how much that is building up.

## Substance over status, education, and the Thiel Fellowship

- A personal resolution he made years ago: value substance over status and prestige.
- Advice to his younger self: he'd probably still go to Stanford and maybe law school, but ask far more why, since too much of his choices was driven by prestige.
- Law firm story: he lasted seven months and three days at a big New York law firm that everyone outside wanted into and everyone inside wanted out of. A colleague told him "it's so reassuring to see you leave Peter, I had no idea it was possible to escape from Alcatraz," when all he did was walk out the front door. People's identities get wrapped up in what they compete for.
- Thiel Fellowship: 20 students get a two-year stipend to stop out of school and work on a business, nonprofit or other project. It was starting its fifth year, with 83 people through the program.
- Results he cites: of the first year's 24, five went back to school after two years; others started successful companies; about $60 million in venture capital raised in aggregate; more applicants each year; peripheral programs such as Summit Series for younger entrepreneurs.
- He rejects being portrayed as against all college. His point is that higher education is too tracked and conventional: "if we say that you go to Yale or you go to jail, that's not the way we want our society to be structured." He cites student debt that limits career choices.

## Founders, alignment, and law lessons for business

- Peter says law school taught him to think about incentives and alignment: make sure people are aligned both formally (structure set correctly) and informally (natural tendency to work together).
- Founder prehistory question: "how did you meet, what's the prehistory of the company?" A bad answer is meeting a week ago at a business networking event and deciding to start a company because both wanted to be entrepreneurs. That's "like saying I married the first person I met at the slot machines in Las Vegas." A good answer is knowing each other a long time, with complementary skills (for example one technical, one business).
- Non-lawyers in business tend to make one of two mistakes: treating law as irrelevant (breaking lots of laws, getting in trouble fast) or as all-important (getting handicapped and unable to do anything). The right stance is critical but not all-important.

## Technology, government decline, and libertarianism

- Peter describes himself as libertarian: socially liberal, fiscally more conservative, and temperamentally a "political atheist" who doesn't believe the political system is where problems get solved. He therefore focuses on technology as the way to change things.
- He doesn't treat libertarian ideas as timeless absolutes; he thinks libertarianism is more true today than in the past because of government decline.
- The government used to do much more for science: the Manhattan Project built a nuclear bomb in three and a half years, and a New York Times op-ed days after Hiroshima argued it proved how much faster science moves when the Army orders scientists around, versus letting "primadonna scientists" take 50 years. He says nobody writes op-eds like that anymore.
- He says you couldn't do a Manhattan Project or Apollo program today, and calls for exploring this "decline of competence."
- How to resist conventional thinking: no formula, but awareness of how prone we are to it. A slightly-better-than-average answer admits it is unoriginal; saying "we need more education software companies" is a bad answer, but acknowledging it's unoriginal puts you ahead. Originality is "incredibly hard, incredibly rare," and we underestimate social influence, like thinking ads work on everyone but us.
- Star Wars vs. Star Trek: at PayPal they were pro-Star Wars. Star Trek is the communist series, with transporter technology and no money; Star Wars is the capitalist one, with Han Solo's debts driving the plot. If you're creating a new payment system, you don't want to model it on a Star Trek world.
