## Peter's view of the US fiscal problem: deficits, interest rates and Social Security

- Peter says the US has "a runaway deficit problem," and calls it dangerous because interest rates have returned to positive territory.
- Interest on the debt peaked at about 3.1% of GDP in 1991, fell to about 1.5% in the mid-2010s, and has crept back up to about 3.1-3.2% of GDP, which he calls an all-time high as a percentage of GDP.
- His explanation: from 2008 to 2021 rates were roughly zero, so the US could borrow far more without paying for it. Now rates are positive like the 1990s and mid-2000s, and the debt is huge.
- Part of the debt is held by foreigners, because chronic US trade and current-account deficits leave foreigners with more dollars than they want to spend on US goods. They reinvest them in houses, stocks and mostly government debt.
- Why people ignore it: "people have been talking about this for 40 years and crying wolf for 40 years."
- The three options are raise taxes a lot, cut spending a lot, or keep borrowing.
- His own answers lean "very libertarian": smaller government, a higher Social Security age, and means-testing Social Security so people who don't need it don't get it.
- He says this is "insanely unpopular," so he calls it unrealistic.
- Why Social Security is hard to reform: it was rationalized as a pension rather than welfare, which he describes as "an intergenerational Ponzi scheme." Under that fiction nearly everyone is paid. If it were honestly called welfare, it could be dialed back.
- He notes the proposal to uncap the Social Security tax (currently capped around $150k) would effectively be a 12.4% income tax hike on all income above the cap.

## California as "Saudi Arabia": why a badly governed state doesn't collapse

- Peter's model: California is like Saudi Arabia. It has a "crazy religion" (wokeism in California, Wahhabism in Saudi Arabia) that few believe but that distorts everything. It also has "oil fields" (the big tech companies) that pay for everything.
- Government and real estate are the channels that redistribute the oil wealth, producing a bloated, inefficient government sector and distorted real estate.
- It isn't how you'd design a system from scratch, but it's "pretty stable." People have predicted Saudi Arabia's collapse for 40-50 years, and "if you have a giant oil field you can pay for a lot of ridiculousness."
- Numbers he gives: California has about 40 million people and about $4 trillion GDP, similar to Germany (80M) or Japan (125M). That means California's per-capita GDP is roughly double Germany's and triple Japan's.
- He says roughly four of the 8-9 companies worth over $1 trillion are in California: Google, Apple, Nvidia, Meta, with Broadcom close.
- Tax flight is inelastic. If a state raises revenue 10% and 5% of people leave, it still collects more.
- Counterpoint he raises: Detroit was heavily networked around the car industry, then mismanagement unraveled it. Networks protect a place until they don't.
- Tech is more tightly networked on California than finance is on New York. That is why finance moved to Florida more easily than tech moved anywhere.

## Where Peter would live: a city-level screen of the zero-tax states

- His logic: you don't live in a state, you live in a city, so he screened the zero-income-tax states for a decent city.
- Four have no real cities (Alaska, Wyoming, South Dakota, New Hampshire).
- Washington (Seattle) has "the worst weather in the country."
- Nevada (Las Vegas) he isn't a fan of.
- Texas he likes as a state, but he dislikes the cities. Houston is an oil town. Dallas has an inferiority complex toward LA and New York. Austin is a government town, a college town and a "wannabe hipster San Francisco town."
- That leaves Nashville and Miami/South Florida as his top two. He could live in Nashville "no problem."
- He's stuck in California because he moved there in fifth grade (1977) and is "a sucker for the weather."
- He bought a Miami place in September 2020, and it's up about 100%. He says moving has become much harder since: prices doubled, and mortgage rates went from about 3% to 6.5-7%, so buying costs "four times as much."
- He admits he's talked about leaving for years, but "talk is often a substitute for action." He has been undecided between leaving the state or the country (Florida, New Zealand, Costa Rica).
- He says the relative outperformance of the US and its absolute stagnation are related. "Everywhere else is worse" becomes a way to avoid discussing what's wrong at home.

## AI as the biggest thing in decades: the Turing test, the internet analogy and the 2010s debate

- The 2010s AI debate was anchored on two visions: Nick Bostrom's Superintelligence (2014), with godlike intelligence, and Kai-Fu Lee's AI Superpowers (2018), with AI as mostly low-tech surveillance, where China wins because it has no ethical qualms.
- His claim: ChatGPT delivered neither. It passed the Turing test, the Holy Grail of AI for 60 years (1950-2010), "at least for an IQ 100 average person." He says we weren't close in 2021.
- His "psychological repression theory" is that the Bostrom-vs-Lee debate kept people from thinking about the Turing test because it was about to happen.
- He calls AGI a "hopelessly vague concept." Passing the Turing test matters more for humans because AI is either a complement or a substitute for humans. Superintelligence would only matter for "competition for being God."
- Analogy: AI in 2023-24 is like the internet in 1999. It's clearly going to be big and dominate the economy, but it's also a bubble where nobody knows the business models. Real application took about 15-20 years, not 18 months, and he expects the same for AI, "about two decades."
- On Tech Stagnation: in an era where nothing big happens, "we have no way of processing it when something pretty big happens." He says Bitcoin was underestimated for its first 10-11 years for the same reason.
- His scale: the internet was really big, Bitcoin moderately big, and passing the Turing test "really big," on the same scale as the internet.
- He is still unsure AI will lift living standards: "the internet was a massive achievement, how much it raised people's living standards is a much trickier question."
- His one-liner: crypto is libertarian, so AI is communist, because AI's natural structure is a big company and it looks dominated by Big Tech in the Bay Area. Crypto could move out of California. AI makes it hard to leave.

## Technological stagnation: a world of bits versus a world of atoms

- His thesis since the early 1970s: not absolute stagnation but relative stagnation. There has been "very limited progress in the world of atoms" and a lot in the world of bits (computers, internet, mobile, AI).
- In 1967, "technology" meant computers, rockets, supersonic planes, new medicines, the Green Revolution and underwater cities. Now it just means information technology, and he says this narrowing tells you progress has been "weird."
- Examples of atoms stagnation:
  - The Concorde was decommissioned in 2003.
  - Airport security makes travel slower.
  - Highways have more traffic jams.
  - "We're literally moving slower than we were 40 or 50 years ago."
- Screens distract from it: you look at an iPhone on a 100-year-old subway and don't notice your lived environment hasn't changed.
- His Stanford undergraduate experience in the late 1980s: every engineering field except computer science was stuck or regulated. He lists mechanical, chemical, nuclear and aero-astro as bad choices, with electrical engineering still OK because of semiconductors.
- Computer science then had the status of a fake field, like social science. His linguistic tell: when something is called "science" (climate science, political science, social science), it signals an inferiority complex to real science.
- He says he isn't questioning that climate change may be happening. His point is that the field isn't a place with "really vigorous debates" about, for example, methane versus carbon dioxide, and that the label signals dogma. He allows the steelman that hardcore environmentalists don't want a high standard of proof because they think there's no time.
- On the Club of Rome's 1972 "Limits to Growth": the 1970s fear was overpopulation and running out of oil, and by the 1990s it had morphed into pollution and climate.
- His read on the environmental argument: an American standard of living for the whole planet would need roughly 300-400 million barrels of oil a day, versus about 100 million consumed globally now, and fracking might add about 10 million. He suspects the real argument was that the planet can't afford that, so growth must be dialed back.

## Why nuclear power stalled: the dual-use theory

- Official story: Three Mile Island (1979), Chernobyl (1986) and Fukushima (2011) killed it.
- Peter's alternate theory: the real cause was India getting the bomb in 1974-75. The US had transferred reactor technology to India, believing it couldn't be weaponized, and it was weaponized easily.
- The consequence: civilian nuclear power is "only one step away" from weapons. That forces a choice between a double standard (the US keeps nuclear weapons, nobody else may have reactors) or effective global governance, "which doesn't sound that good either." The compromise was to regulate so heavily that new plants became too expensive.
- Evidence he cites: even China, which is building the most reactors, has built fewer than expected, because it copies over-safe Western designs. Nuclear was around 4-5% of Chinese electricity in 2013-14 and the share hasn't risen in ten years, since coal is cheaper.
- He believes modern small reactor designs would be much cheaper per kilowatt-hour with no giant containment structure, but they aren't allowed, and the dual-use problem remains.
- His point on energy density: history moved from wood to coal to oil to uranium, with each step more compact and taking up less environment. Solar and wind go backwards by covering deserts and landscapes. So nuclear was supposed to be the 21st-century energy mode.
- The broader stagnation theory: "why" questions are overdetermined (risk aversion, worse education, picked-over low-hanging fruit). One underrated factor is that much science and technology had a "dystopian or apocalyptic dimension" after Los Alamos in 1945 and thermonuclear weapons in the early 1950s.
- He asks whether stagnation is "a feature or a bug." If physicists kept accelerating on supersonics, hypersonic weapons and modular nuclear reactors, "maybe the whole world would have already blown up." He calls it "the stagnant path of the Multiverse" with a partially protective effect, though he still finds it deeply deranged in other ways.

## The deep state, Epstein and Bill Gates: Peter's speculative takes

- Why the deep state is weaker now: he says the 1940s through 1960s saw crazy secret projects (Manhattan Project, MK Ultra). He doesn't think the CIA does "anything quite like MK Ultra anymore."
- His reasoning: after the Church Commission hearings in the late 1970s, formalization made bureaucracies "a lot less functional." He gives the 2000s torture memos as another example.
- He sees the NSA/FISA process as out of control from about 2003 to 2017-18, culminating in the 2016 Russia conspiracy theories.
- MK Ultra and the 1960s counterculture:
  - He notes that Ken Kesey's LSD trials started at the Menlo Park veterans hospital and that Timothy Leary's work at Harvard began as MK Ultra projects.
  - He suspects MK Ultra was bigger than we realize.
  - He raises "inner space vs. outer space": we landed on the moon in July 1969 and Woodstock started three weeks later. He wonders whether the turn to interiority (therapy, meditation, identity politics, psychedelics) acts as a substitute for changing the outside world rather than the first step to it.
- He says he is a "pretty hardcore libertarian" on drug legalization. On the FDA and MDMA he says: "if it really works you don't need a double blind study," and that the FDA moved the goalposts because people know whether they got the drug or a placebo.
- On Epstein:
  - Peter says Reid Hoffman introduced him to Epstein in 2014, after Epstein's first arrest, and that he was described as one of the smartest tax people in the world. He calls it a moral weakness not to have asked more questions.
  - He thinks the underage-sex angle is "overdone and everything else is underdone," like a limited hangout.
  - He cites Alex Acosta's 2017 testimony that Epstein "belonged to intelligence." The real question is which part of US intelligence he worked for. He dismisses the Israel theory, saying "if you're working for Israel you don't get protected," and cites Jonathan Pollard's 25 years.
  - He suggests the secret-club dynamic: people were not so much compromised as invited into a club where they were "made."
- His Bill Gates theory (explicitly speculative):
  - Gates and Melinda had no prenup, and Epstein reportedly advised on the marriage and on philanthropy structures.
  - Committing the marital assets to the foundation would box Melinda in, since "it's all going to the Gates Foundation anyway."
  - He argues Gates going into overdrive on Covid in 2020 locked this in, because Melinda filed in early 2021 and would look like a "crazy anti-science person" if she resisted.
  - He thinks the left-wing philanthropy structure was a way to control a spouse, and says this fits the timeline well.
  - He adds that Melinda received about a tenth of what a half split would have been.
- On philanthropy: in America it's seen as what a good person does, while in Europe it signals "you must have murdered somebody." He thinks the European intuition is more correct, and that virtue signaling is now more suspect than "vice signaling."

## Birth rates, imitation and why talk substitutes for action

- Peter's explanation for collapsing birth rates is "overdetermined" (plastics, screens, careers, housing costs). He anchors on social and cultural factors: "people have kids if other people have kids and they stop having kids when other people stop."
- His evidence of the breadth:
  - Every US state is below replacement, even Mormon Utah, and so are Iran, Italy and South Korea.
  - He says Korea's fertility rate is about 0.7, a third of replacement, so each generation shrinks by about two thirds.
  - Israel is the exception.
- Political lock-in: once the pyramid inverts, old voters favor benefits for themselves ("so Grandma can have a spare leg" instead of "so Johnny can read"), and parents get penalized economically. A Japanese demographer's thesis is that once it flips, it doesn't flip back.
- Extrapolation he gives: at one child per woman, each generation halves. 2^33 is about 8 billion, and at 30 years per generation that's about 990 years to one person left. He calls it a very long-term extrapolation.
- Contrast: Paul Ehrlich's 1968 "The Population Bomb" predicted exponential growth, and we flipped to exponential decay. A stable equilibrium at exactly two kids is very hard to hit.
- Imitation theory of humanity: following Aristotle ("man differs from the other animals in his greater aptitude for imitation"), he says humans are giant imitation machines. Imitation explains learning, language and culture, and it also drives conflict (you want the banana because I want it).
- He adds that language is a radical discontinuity from primates, and imitation had to be channeled into ritual and religion. He ties this to René Girard, scapegoats and the origins of kingship.
- Talk versus action: his Korea anecdote has a conglomerate CEO agree "it's a total disaster" and then consider the matter closed. His conclusion: "talking about things is the first step but it also becomes the excuse for not really solving them."
- He applies the same skepticism to therapy, which promises self-transformation but often ends in "self-acceptance." His closing line: "strategy is often a euphemism for procrastination."

## AI risk, China, regulation and the case against the optimists' story

- Peter says he thinks the scary dystopian AI narrative is "way more compelling" than the Silicon Valley one, and that the Luddite and effective-altruist side is winning the argument this time. He says he doesn't like the effective altruists or the Luddites.
- He frames it as: do you worry about Dr. Strangelove (blow up the world to build bigger bombs) or about Greta (make everyone ride a bicycle)? Today people worry about Strangelove, not Greta.
- Prediction: AI will be "surprisingly" regulated, as "so many other vectors of innovation" have been. Computers progressed over 50 years because they were mostly inert and air-gapped from the real world, which kept regulation light. If the FDA regulated AI, progress would slow a lot.
- On Silicon Valley's pitch (humans merging with AI): he thinks there's maybe a 1% chance it works as advertised, and it's more plausibly "corporate propaganda" where companies say the right things, cut corners and it goes haywire. His critique is that pro-AI people are doing a bad job convincing the average person it will be good for them.
- On global governance: he notes RAND's push for "global compute governance" and says it sounds even worse to him.
- On China: he gives the CCP's best counter-argument, which is that it may regulate AI because it fanatically wants control and AI could undermine party power. He cites the Jack Ma/Alibaba/Tencent crackdown, in which tech leaders went from national champions to enemies of the people over about the last seven to eight years. He doesn't fully trust this and says China could also lose control of its own AI.
- On the military: drones need AI because a human operator can be jammed, so there's a logic to taking the human out of the loop, "but there doesn't seem to be a good end game."
- His rebuttal to the "AI becomes God and aliens are AIs" story: faster-than-light travel implies warp-speed weapons with no defense. So an advanced civilization must be either totally totalitarian (a "mind meld" with no independent agency) or perfectly altruistic, "demons or angels." He argues altruism is hard to reach by evolution, and that status competition is cultural, not just scarcity.
- His PayPal Galaxy Quest anecdote: Star Trek's premise was a post-scarcity world, but a 1999 PayPal event with Star Trek's "Scotty" (James Doohan) hit the "forbidden question" about William Shatner doing Priceline ads. He draws from it that scarcity (only one captain) persists even in sci-fi utopias.
- On UFOs: he is "strangely under-motivated" by the topic. His luggage-at-the-airport argument: waiting 77 years with no progress is like waiting 77 minutes for luggage, at which point "you should stop."
