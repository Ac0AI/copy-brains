## The 9/11 turning point: why wealth transfers and economic theory failed to contain violence

- Peter opens with September 11, 2001 as the start of the twenty-first century. It called into question the nineteenth- and twentieth-century framework of "deterrent armies, rational nation-states, public debates, and international diplomacy."
- He asks how talking, or even great force, could deter "a handful of crazy, determined, and suicidal persons who seemingly operated outside of all the norms of the liberal West," given that technology lets a tiny number of people inflict unprecedented death.
- He argues the new compromise trades less freedom for more security: more airport x-ray machines, more security guards on planes, more ID cards and privacy invasions, fewer rights for some of the accused. He says the ACLU's "inviolable individual rights" language was "rendered an unviable anachronism" overnight.
- He calls the post-WWII consensus of large wealth transfers to the developing world (World Bank, IMF, hundreds of billions in cheap loans or grants) naive in hindsight. The money was largely squandered on white elephant projects, and often ended up in Swiss bank accounts held by Third World dictators.
- The deeper failure: the blow came from upper-middle-class, college-educated Saudis, not from "the favelas of Rio de Janeiro" or "Tibetan yak herders." Bin Laden inherited a fortune now worth an estimated $250 million, and "had he been born in America, bin Laden could have been a Rockefeller."
- He concludes that economically motivated political thought, "from The Wealth of Nations on the right to Das Kapital on the left," is incomplete, and that the essay's agenda is to reexamine the foundations of modern politics.

## Why the modern West abandoned the question of human nature

- Peter says Enlightenment political philosophy abandoned the older questions: what is a well-lived life, what does it mean to be human, how do culture and religion fit in. "The death of God was followed by the disappearance of the question of human nature."
- If humans are approximated as rational economic actors (he notes even Adam Smith and Karl Marx agree), then people who seek glory for God or country look odd. But if such people are commonplace and can assert themselves with explosive force, that account of politics needs reexamining.
- The older tradition (Augustine's Christian virtues, Machiavelli's pagan virtues) saw humans as potentially evil or at least dangerous. It split into two incompatible streams, Athens and Jerusalem, which he describes via Pierre Manent as two "millstones" that wore each other down, producing the modern "individual" who defines himself by refusing all sacrifice.
- The early modern era tried to force agreement through violence: the Reformation, Counter Reformation, and the Thirty Years' War, which by some estimates killed well over half the population of Germany.
- The Peace of Westphalia (1648) is his date for the birth of the modern era. Questions of virtue and religion became private, and polite people learned not to discuss them. Hobbes boasted of running away from a religious war, and "Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori" came to be seen as an old lie.
- The Enlightenment was therefore "a major strategic retreat": the question of human nature was dropped "because it is too perilous a question to debate."

## Locke and the American compromise: capitalism fills the vacuum

- Peter argues that economics and capitalism filled the vacuum, with John Locke as the main proponent and the United States as the greatest practical success. He says one exaggerates only slightly in calling Locke America's definitive founder.
- He reads Locke as understated and cautious: in The Reasonableness of Christianity he denounces atheists while quietly teaching that honoring parents does not apply to "unnaturally careless" parents, that wives may separate, and that self-preservation is "the first and strongest desire God planted in men." Avarice stops being a mortal sin and infinite accumulation of wealth becomes acceptable.
- Locke reduces human nature to power (following Hobbes) and leaves an unknowable "X" in place of human nature. That unknowability grounds classic liberalism: freedom of religion, speech, property and commerce, because nobody is in a position to challenge another's desires. He quotes Milton Friedman: "Capitalism is simply what humans do when they are left alone."
- He says America gradually shed Christian religiosity while keeping its outward appearances, with no catastrophic war on religion as in France or Russia, and that conservative moralists never realize the drift "had been a part of that original conception."
- Locke's blind spot is origins. He says there is little value in the state of nature, so we can ignore how property was first acquired and look forward. Peter counters with oil: most of its value exists in nature and the labor added is proportionately small, yet it is a large share of world wealth. The original expropriation of oil built as many as half of the greatest fortunes of the twentieth century. He cites Balzac's "behind every great fortune there lies a crime" and says the Rockefellers "had forgotten their own family history."
- Since 9/11 the West must "awaken from that very long and profitable period of intellectual slumber and amnesia that is so misleadingly called the Enlightenment." He concedes that in the long run power may go to those who follow Locke's capitalist rules, but "none of this will matter if we are all dead in the short run."

## Carl Schmitt: the persistence of the political and the problem of choosing enemies

- Peter presents Schmitt as the extreme alternative to Locke. Schmitt agrees with Westphalia that there will never be agreement on religion, virtue or human nature, but says humans are equally condemned to be divided by those questions and forced to take sides.
- Politics is the choice between friend and enemy. "The high points of politics are the moments in which the enemy is, in concrete clarity, recognized as the enemy"; the enemy is "our own question as a figure."
- Schmitt argues you cannot escape politics unilaterally. A population that declares it no longer recognizes enemies "joins their side and aids them." Examples: the Kellogg Pact of 1928, and the pre-revolutionary Russian and French aristocrats who sentimentalized the goodness of the people while 1793 was coming. A world state is a logical impossibility, since it would end the friend/enemy distinction.
- Applied to 2001: Islam retains a strong religious and political conception of the struggle (more important than life and death), while the West is confused about what it is fighting for. Bin Laden could quote Cromwell and Pope Urban II "requiring almost no changes at all." The West prefers to see police actions against a few criminal sociopaths and no longer believes in a "Gott mit uns in heaven."
- Peter's challenge to Schmitt: a side that, like Hobbes, values earthly life above all will run away, and running from an enemy who keeps fighting means losing. But a Schmittian response, a new crusade, would be a Pyrrhic victory that destroys what distinguishes the West from Islam. "One must choose one's enemies well, for one will soon be just like them."
- He concludes that on Schmitt's premises the West must lose the war or lose its identity, and that after 1945, in a world of nuclear weapons, Schmitt's drastic solutions are impossible.

## The opposite danger: the end of politics, Kojève, and the Antichrist

- Peter considers the inverse of Schmitt: a worldwide abandonment of politics rather than a world state. Kojève's end of history means humanity reverts to animal-like "conditioned reflexes," with no philosophy and no wisdom.
- Schmitt's version: what remains is "culture, civilization, economics, morality, law, art, entertainment." Peter sketches it as violent video games instead of wars, amusement park rides instead of heroic feats, soap-opera "intrigues" instead of serious thought, "a world where people spend their lives amusing themselves to death."
- Schmitt asks "upon whom will fall the frightening power implied in a world-embracing economic and technical organization" and says it cannot be assumed things would administer themselves. Such a world needs a "religion of technicity" with faith in unlimited power over nature.
- In Schmitt's medieval-Christian frame this "Babylonian unity" can be brought about only by the Antichrist, who seduces people with "peace and security." Peter quotes: "God created the world; the Antichrist counterfeits it... the age of security dawns; everything has been taken care of, a clever foresight and planning replace Providence."
- He links this to the self-administering world of Snow Crash and The Matrix, and warns the price of abandoning yourself to an artificial representation of reality is always too high "because the decisions that are avoided are always too important." The Antichrist swindles people out of their souls by making them forget they have them.

## Leo Strauss: esoteric writing and an exceptional framework for security

- Peter frames the impasse: the Enlightenment never became global and came at a high price of self-stultification, while a return to the older tradition is too violent. He offers Leo Strauss as an attempted synthesis.
- Strauss holds that even in the most liberal regimes there are deeply problematic truths, so great thinkers of the past wrote esoterically, addressing "trustworthy and intelligent readers only." His thought experiment is a historian in a totalitarian state who publicly defends the official line but writes "three or four sentences" between the lines that arrest "young men who love to think." Strauss notes esoteric writing has the advantages of both private and public communication without the downsides, namely a small audience and "capital punishment for the author."
- Peter says Strauss therefore escapes relativism and nihilism: there is a truth about human nature that can in principle be known, and the great writers agreed on it more than their exoteric disagreements suggest. He notes the Straussian open agenda is only "reading the Great Books for their own sake."
- Recurring Straussian themes: the city and humanity, founding and origins, and religion and the best regime. He highlights that Strauss hints even America's greatness owes something to "her occasional deviation" from freedom and justice, and that there are politically incorrect taboos even in America.
- On action, Strauss holds "a universally valid hierarchy of ends, but there are no universally valid rules of action." In extreme situations there are no limits that can be defined in advance on just reprisals, and it is best to leave "these sad exigencies covered with the veil."
- Peter's own policy conclusion: America's checks and balances ("setting ambition against ambition") frustrate ambitious people, and merely political careers end in failure, but there are domains outside the conventional legal system. He quotes Strauss that "the most just society cannot survive without 'intelligence,' i.e., espionage." Instead of the United Nations, "filled with interminable and inconclusive parliamentary debates," he points to Echelon, the secret coordination of the world's intelligence services, as "the decisive path to a truly global pax Americana." He agrees with Tennyson on ends and differs only on means.
- He flags the self-referential risk: awareness of the city's problems makes unreflective defense of it impossible, so the Straussian project may become "a hastener against its will" (in Schmitt's terms, failing to preserve the katechon).

## René Girard: mimesis, the scapegoat, and the apocalyptic end of the Straussian moment

- Peter turns to Girard as the thinker who may show the Straussian moment to be brief. Girard also believes there is a disturbing truth about the city and humanity that the Enlightenment whitewashed, and predicts a post-Darwinian "Origin of Religions" that will transform the sciences of humanity.
- Core claim: following Aristotle, "Man differs from the other animals in his greater aptitude for imitation." Human brains are "gigantic imitation machines," and the independent human self is a modern myth. Imitation extends to desire: "keeping up with the Joneses" escalates into rivalry. Envy, the mortal sin closest to mimetic rivalry, remains a cultural taboo even among avant-garde postmoderns. Humans have no instinctual brakes strong enough to limit mimetic rivalry.
- Girard calls the social contract the "fundamental lie of the Enlightenment." Instead, the war of all against all ends in a war of all against one: a scapegoat whose death unites the community and brings a limited peace. That murder is the secret origin of religious and political institutions, concealed by myth that the victim was guilty. Myth turns victims into gods, rituals reenact the founding murder through sacrifice, and every king is a sort of living god (the tomb is "the first pyramid").
- The cat is now out of the bag: we know the scapegoat was not as guilty as the community claimed, so archaic rituals no longer work. The myths that distinguished legitimate from illegitimate violence are unraveling, which may leave humanity without "limited and sacred violence" to protect it from unlimited, desacralized violence.
- Peter's applications: nuclear weapons are a dilemma, and mimesis could drive many states to acquire them "for the mimetic prestige they confer," so the situation is never static. He defines a "liberal" as someone who knows nothing of the past and still believes in natural human goodness, and a "conservative" as someone who knows nothing of the future and thinks the nation-state can contain unlimited violence. The risk is a synthesis of violence and globalization with all boundaries abolished (geographic, civilian/combatant, children, even self versus other, as in suicide-murderers). "Terrorism" is the word for this unbounded, apocalyptic violence.
- "Apocalypse" originally meant unveiling. Girard's version is "the end of the world, the Christian apocalypse, the bottomless abyss of the unforgettable victim."

## Strauss versus Girard on timing, and what the Christian statesman should do

- Peter says the key dispute between Strauss and Girard is when the disturbing knowledge will burst into general awareness. Strauss, like Nietzsche's madman ("I come too early"), thinks the truth of mimesis and the founding murder is so shocking most people will never believe it, so it can stay with a philosophical elite. The danger is its popularization. Pierre Manent attacks Girard on this ground: Girard would bring "the destruction of humanity in the fallacious guise of non-violence."
- Girard's reply: there will come a day when no esoteric knowledge is left. Events will be "infinitely more eloquent than whatever we write," and the truths will become "simpler still; they will soon be accessible to anyone." Peter explains the difference as historicism: for Girard the Judeo-Western revelation is slow but irreversible.
- Peter ends with the individual choice between Jerusalem and Athens, citing Thomas More: Christ wept and left no example of laughter, while Socrates left no example of weeping.
- His prescription for the Christian statesman: no return to the archaic world or to Schmitt's robust political, no accommodation with the Enlightenment ("so many of its easy bromides have become deadly falsehoods in our time"), and no retreat into Bible study awaiting the Second Coming, which would end one's role as a statesman.
- Unlike Strauss, the Christian statesman knows the modern age is not permanent and that "all will be revealed, that all injustices will be exposed."
- His rule of thumb: "in every close case," side with peace. There is no formula for what counts as a close case. The cumulative decisions may decide whether the postmodern world is "the limitless violence of runaway mimesis or the peace of the kingdom of God."
