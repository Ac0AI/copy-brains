## The 2000 funding frenzy and the dot-com crash at PayPal

- Peter merged Confinity (PayPal, co-founded with Max Levchin) with Elon Musk's x.com in March 2000. The combined company had about $15 million in the bank.
- Growth was exponential: a $10 signup bonus plus a $10 referral bonus meant a $20 customer acquisition cost. The customer base grew about 5-7% a day, hit 100,000 users on February 3, 2000 and reached a million by mid-April 2000.
- Burn grew with it. There was no revenue, and the CFO projected the $15 million would run out in about six weeks.
- Peter describes the fundraising as far crazier than any "are we in a bubble again" moment today. In South Korea, three investor groups competed, and a professor hid behind a palm tree in the hotel to spy on them.
- One group bought Peter a first-class ticket on the spot when his credit card failed. The next day they wired $5 million without signed paperwork. They would not say where the money came from or where to send it back.
- PayPal closed $100 million on Friday, March 31, 2000. The market bottomed out the following Monday.
- Burn stayed above $10 million a month for seven months, through September 2000, and a large fraud problem emerged in rapid succession.
- Peter recalls the NASDAQ at 5,000 on the "day the internet ended" in mid-April, falling to 3,100 and recovering. By late August it was back at 4,200, and after Labor Day 2000 it fell for two years.
- Having lots of money had a downside: he thinks it slowed them in settling on a business model.

## Finding the business model and surviving the nuclear winter

- The early-2000 model was to upsell users to financial services. Online banking customers were valued at about $50,000 each against a $20 acquisition cost.
- By fall 2000 the company had settled on payments alone, taking a slice of the money flowing through the system.
- The tagline was "always fast free and secure," which raised the question of how to charge. David Sacks's product team came up with upgrading users from customer to business account for a 2.9% fee per payment, on one click.
- Peter notes that many companies built on acquiring customers and upselling later found customers left the day they tried to upsell. PayPal had very little attrition because it already held the payment information.
- The company hit break-even in September 2001 and was the first US company to file for an IPO after 9/11, at the end of September 2001. It went public in February 2002.
- The last money came in around March 2001. While the team was on the road for three weeks selling stock, the NASDAQ and the comparable public companies used for IPO pricing dropped by half.
- The press turned hostile. A San Jose Mercury News column titled "Earth to Palo Alto" said the world needed a company with a 29-year-old average executive age and a possible terrorist money-laundering tool "as badly as we need an Anthrax epidemic."
- An ideologically hostile SEC reviewer made the process open-ended, for example demanding a disclosure that PayPal could not do business in Louisiana.

## eBay, bilateral monopoly, and why selling PayPal made sense

- PayPal targeted eBay from January 2000. By April or May about 30-35% of eBay sellers used it.
- eBay's rival product, Billpoint, was a partnership with Wells Fargo. The bank did not want fraud risk, and Peter says you can always make a product fraud-proof "if it's unusable." You can take all customers if you accept all the fraud, or stop all fraud if you accept losing all the customers.
- Peter compares eBay to a store whose owners could never get their cash registers to work, while PayPal ran the registers.
- He calls the relationship a bilateral monopoly, which makes negotiation very complicated. About 75% of PayPal's volume was on eBay, so PayPal could only sell to eBay, and others feared eBay would shut it off.
- There were about five serious negotiations: late 2000, spring 2001, fall 2001, and March 2002 right after the IPO. The March talks fell apart and a PayPal-eBay war looked likely.
- In June 2002, PayPal sent 30 people to the eBay convention in Anaheim and handed out PayPal t-shirts. eBay saw its power sellers wearing them and decided to buy the company.
- The deal was announced Monday, July 8, 2002. Peter says the price was about the same in June as in March even as the market kept falling, and much higher than it would have been without the IPO.
- Peter says staying independent would likely have made PayPal much bigger 10-12 years later. But there were scary risks, including an investigation by Eliot Spitzer over offshore sports-betting payments. PayPal got the notice on Tuesday, July 9, a day after the acquisition was announced.
- Selling a company is like selling your kid, but he still thinks it was right: "most tech M&A deals don't make sense because most of the time there're no synergies," and this one had tremendous synergies. Volume rose sharply after the acquisition.

## Why PayPal alumni succeeded: the lesson was "hard but possible"

- Peter thinks the key lesson of PayPal was that building a great company is hard but possible, across years of regulatory, fraud, marketing and business-model challenges.
- Companies that fail teach that it's impossible, so people aim lower next time. Companies where everything works from day one, like Microsoft or Google, teach that it's easy. Both lessons are wrong and discourage real effort: "impossible, no point; easy, no need."
- The PayPal team was not a harmonious, finish-each-other's-sentences group. It had real conflict.
- His view of conflict: it happens "when different people want to do the same thing," not when they want different things. A sociopathic boss gives two people the same job to generate a fight.
- Startups have fluid roles, which causes overlap. David Sacks said the product was "a single seamless whole," which put the product team in conflict with fraud, customer service and everyone else.

## Elon Musk: McLaren crash, Tesla and SpaceX

- Peter describes Elon as incredibly smart, hardworking and ambitious, and "definitely not the person you wanted to compete against." They first met as competitors four blocks apart on University Avenue in Palo Alto.
- On the day of the March 2000 market peak, they drove Elon's McLaren F1 up Sand Hill Road to a VC meeting. It had cost about $1 million and was worth roughly a third of his liquid net worth. Elon lost control during a demo, the car spun 360 degrees in the air and crashed. Peter says they hitchhiked to the VC meeting and saw a chiropractor afterwards. Peter had no insurance on the car.
- A VC told Peter years later that he declined to invest because the CEO lived in a one-bedroom condo and falsely claimed his car was worth more than his house. It was true.
- The conventional wisdom around 2002-04 was that Tesla and SpaceX would fail. The belief was there would never be another American car company, because a movie about Tucker showed why, and no new rocket company had appeared in about 60 years.
- Founders Fund invested in SpaceX in 2008, about six years in, when people thought they were crazy.
- Elon's reading was that the last successful new US car company was Jeep in 1941, so "it's about time for a new car company."
- Peter's takeaway is that they picked weak competition. US car companies would never build an electric car, and aerospace was run by badly managed quasi-government conglomerates.
- He says avoid intensely competitive businesses. "Opening a restaurant" is his paradigm of a bad business idea.
- Tesla and SpaceX were stories of complex coordination and vertical integration rather than one breakthrough. Peter sees the same in the iPhone, where no single part was massively better but the pieces fit together.

## Is it a bubble? Where the real bubble is

- Peter says he's not fully confident, but he doesn't think the current tech boom is a bubble in the dot-com sense. Past bubbles (Japan in the 80s, tech in the late 90s, housing and finance in the 2000s) required heavy public involvement.
- Companies are going public very late in the cycle. He recalls roughly 30-40 tech IPOs a year versus about 300 in the late 90s, and says he didn't check the exact numbers.
- His candidate for the real bubble is government money printing and government bonds, with real interest rates around -2%.
- Assets most like government bonds are most exposed: corporate bonds, housing, high-dividend stocks, and old tech stocks like Oracle, IBM and Microsoft, where the discount rate is the key variable.
- Startups are valued on growth rather than the discount rate. Peter says about three quarters of his net worth is in illiquid Silicon Valley tech stocks, "the furthest I can get from" government bonds.

## Monopoly, Commodity and "bets against technology"

- Peter says many NASDAQ 100 companies are really bets against technological innovation.
- Microsoft bets nothing like Linux appears and that operating systems don't shift to mobile. IBM bets on the 70s-80s software model and lots of service people. Oracle bets against cloud computing. In the 70s and 80s, GM was a bet against Japanese and German car innovation.
- These can still be fine investments, but the companies keep a "technology" narrative from when they actually were tech companies. GM was a technology company in the 1920s; Microsoft in the 80s and probably the 90s.
- He distinguishes two questions: building monopolies as a founder, which he says you always want to do, and the public policy question of when monopolies are good or bad.
- Monopolies don't necessarily keep innovating. You reach monopoly by doing something very innovative, and the hope is to keep innovating from there.
- While founders run the company there's usually enough pressure to keep going. When founders are replaced by "politicians who act like CEOs," incremental stagnation sets in. His example is Steve Jobs shifting Apple from home computers to consumer electronics after 1997.
- The default monopoly motto is "we don't care because we don't have to," as anyone dealing with a cable company knows.
- He does not think government needs to regulate monopolies heavily, since in innovative sectors they decay anyway. A monopoly lasting a few decades is still far better than a restaurant.

## Nuclear power and the climate

- Peter thinks people worried about climate change should be much more open to nuclear power, though they tend to oppose it.
- He suspects it is a generational issue. The anti-nuclear stance belongs to baby boomers who came of age in the 70s, and a nuclear renaissance may be possible once they lose power in about a decade.
- He thinks the turning point was less Three Mile Island (1979 in the usual telling, which he cites as 1978) than India getting the bomb in 1974 or 1975 using reactor technology the US had provided. That showed nuclear power's dual civilian and military use, and drove the anti-nuclear sentiment.
- He says Founders Fund has started to look at nuclear investments.
