## The Core Question: Luck vs. Skill in Startups

- Peter says the biggest philosophical question under startups is how much luck is involved in success. It is nearly impossible to answer rigorously, because statistics are meaningless with a sample size of one. His thought experiment: start Facebook 1,000 times under identical conditions; 1,000 successes would mean skill, 1 would mean luck. Such experiments are impossible.
- He argues the starting view is mostly a bias. The internal narrative says talented people worked hard and made it work; the external narrative says right place, right time.
- He believes people are "extremely biased towards the luck side of things" and that skill plays a much greater role than people think.
- His weak argument against luck is repeatability: Steve Jobs (Next, Pixar, Apple), Jack Dorsey (Twitter, Square), Elon Musk (PayPal, Tesla, SpaceX, SolarCity). He finds it very odd to argue they just got lucky.
- A successful entrepreneur who credited skill alone would be seen as "ridiculous, arrogant, and wrong," whatever the proof. Nobody is allowed to show how he controlled everything.
- A startup class only makes sense if luck can be "channeled and mastered." A guide to slot machines would be odd. He notes this is class 13, and says: "Luck isn't something to circumvent or be afraid of. So we have class 13. We'll dominate luck."

## From Jefferson to Gladwell: How Views of Luck Shifted

- Peter cites Jefferson: "I'm a great believer in luck, and I find the harder I work the more I have of it." From the 18th century through the 1950s or '60s, luck was something to be mastered, dominated and controlled.
- Today's default is closer to Malcolm Gladwell: success stems "as much from context as from personal attributes." You can't control your destiny.
- The startup community shares this ethos. Paul Graham attributes much startup success to luck, and Robert Cringely wrote *Accidental Empires*. Peter says he isn't picking on them; they show the dominant view.
- He splits luck into past luck (birth country, genes, inheritance) and future luck. He thinks the past debate is less fruitful. The real question is whether the future is determinate or indeterminate.

## The 2x2: Optimism/Pessimism and Determinate/Indeterminate

- Peter frames the future on two axes: optimistic vs. pessimistic, and determinate (knowable, controllable) vs. indeterminate (too many chance events).
- If the future is indeterminate, you diversify and chase optionality. People spend 10 years building a diverse resume (junior high, high school, many clubs) for an unknowable future.
- If it is determinate, you hold firm convictions and do the one best thing.
- The four quadrants:
  - **Determinate optimism** (US until the 1950s-'60s): e.g. go west and get 640 acres; specific projects to improve society. Fits engineering and art.
  - **Indeterminate optimism** (US roughly 1982 to at least 2007): the future will be better, but not in ways you can know. Wait for progress, do incremental things. Fits law and finance.
  - **Determinate pessimism** (China): there is a calculus, but it gives no cause for celebration. "China will get old before it gets rich." He ties the one-child policy, pollution and coal-mine deaths to this view. Fits wartime rationing.
  - **Indeterminate pessimism** (Japan since the 1990s, Europe today): the future isn't great and you don't know what to do. Fits insurance. He calls it the worst of all worlds.
- He notes a widespread view that the US is drifting toward China's quadrant. The pessimistic quadrants are stable and self-reinforcing.

## Savings, Investment and the Missing Future

- Optimism means a low savings rate; pessimism means a high one.
- US savings is about 4%, or about -6% including government deficits (-10% of GDP). China's is close to 40%. Peter says it is hard to consume your capital when you believe you'll get old before you get rich.
- Determinate worlds have many things to invest in; indeterminate ones have low investment. US corporations generate about $1 trillion a year in free cash flow and hoard it, because "almost by definition, you wouldn't have free cash flows if you knew where or how to invest."
- The puzzle: low savings plus low investment. People are optimistic about a future nobody is working toward. He asks whether indeterminate optimism can be stable at all.
- Money circles through the system with nobody knowing what to do with it: founder to bank, bank to institutional investors, to a diversified stock portfolio, to companies chasing free cash flow, back to investors. Money matters more in an indefinite world because "having is always better than specific things."
- Government bonds: 1.8% yield against 2.1% expected inflation gives a -0.3% expected real return over the decade. "You get negative returns when people run out of ideas."
- Investors now focus on managing risk. Hedge funds rarely ask what will happen; the VC question has become access to good deals. Peter says VC should in theory have little in common with a statistical approach.

## Calculus vs. Statistics, Substance vs. Process

- Determinate worlds run on calculus: a rocket to the moon must be calculated precisely, not iterated. He jokes that many '90s companies "had launch parties but no landing parties."
- Indeterminate worlds run on probability: bell curves, random walks, distributions. There is a push to replace high-school calculus with statistics. Peter says "today, all prophets are false prophets," and cites Larry Summers on the economy.
- Indeterminacy makes people think procedurally rather than substantively. He ties this to the HP board debate: Perkins' substance (talk tech and engineering) vs. Dunn's process.
- Politics: pollsters become more important than politicians. He says McCain chose Palin from polling data, since she had an 89% approval rating in Alaska. Government now mostly shifts money around instead of backing specific projects like the space program.
- Other cultural markers he cites:
  - Sci-fi: Clarke's *2001* (1968) imagines specific, auto-updating news; Gibson's *Neuromancer* (1984) has "the color of television, tuned to a dead channel."
  - Philosophy: Marx and Hegel (optimistic, determinate); Rawls and Nozick (optimistic, indeterminate); Plato and Aristotle (pessimistic, determinate); Epicurus and Lucretius (pessimistic, indeterminate).
  - Physics: belief in many-worlds among theoretical physicists rose from about 10-15% to 55-60% over 50 years, with no experiment proving it. Physicists call it "aesthetically better."

## The End of Big Projects

- The US once believed in grand definite plans. Examples: the transcontinental railroad; Robert Moses, who held twelve high-level posts and rebuilt New York state over 30-40 years, at one point more powerful than the mayor or governor.
- People then asked who would do big projects well, not whether anyone should. Moses' Greenwich Village highway was blocked in 1965, and no new state highways were built after.
- The Reber plan: in the 1940s John Reber, a San Francisco teacher and amateur theater producer, proposed two dams across the Bay, about 20,000 acres of fill, a 32-lane highway and high-rises. It drew Congressional hearings. Today it would be dismissed as lunacy, and "the John Rebers of the world have long since learned to keep their plans to themselves."
- Definite optimism yields underwater cities and cities in space; indefinite optimism yields finance.

## Life Extension, Cities and Indeterminacy in Practice

- Peter says people treat actuarial tables as binding (about 1 in 1,000 odds of death for a college student per year, 50% for a 100-year-old, 1 in 10,000 reaching 100, 1 in 10 million reaching 110), so life extension gets a bad rap.
- From 1600-1850 people searched for the fountain of youth. Once belief in a literal fountain died, the belief that things could change died too.
- His key line: "Belief in secrets is an effective truth. Belief in luck is an *ineffective* truth; it will stop you from actually doing things."
- Evolution is the model for indeterminate optimism: never-ending, monotonic progress without a plan. He doubts it extends to economies:
  - Los Angeles could have been a magnificent planned city but became incremental sprawl.
  - São Paulo: the airport is 5 miles from downtown, a 10-minute helicopter ride that can take 3 hours by car at rush hour; about 20 million people; high-rise slums.
  - Mumbai and Lagos are similar cases of decline.
- On the environment, markets solving problems iteratively is indeterminate optimism and "we're all screwed" is indeterminate pessimism. Definite ideas like iron filings in the ocean (to feed CO2-absorbing phytoplankton) are not in the public debate.

## Implications for Startups: Design, Plans and Secrets

- Peter lists the startup symptoms of indeterminacy: Darwinian A/B testing, iterative processes, machine learning, no thinking about the future, short time horizons. He says they aren't self-evidently wrong, but not self-evidently right. Darwinism takes billions of years, startups don't have that, and it is carnage for participants. Iteration and machine learning often excuse the last two.
- The 2007 finance bubble peak was the peak of indeterminate thinking. Apple is "the absolute antithesis of finance," with deliberate design at every level, definite multi-year plans and methodical rollouts.
- Apple stock: Jobs took over at $3 per share, and it traded at only $6 in 2003. Institutions underweighted it because they can't value a secret plan. He gives no investment advice.
- The return of design: Airbnb, Pinterest, Dropbox and Path have an "anti-statistical feel," a "telepathic link" with what people want that works faster than A/B testing a huge search space.
- Companies with real plans tend not to sell. PayPal sold in 2002 only because it had run out of ideas. A definite plan anchors people against selling; "there is no reason to stop when you can do so much more."
- In an indefinite world, investors value secret plans at zero, so a company with a good one is undervalued. The ability to execute a long-term secret plan is "incredibly powerful."

## Career Advice: Have a Plan, Not a Resume Line

- Peter says young people iterate one resume line at a time, which gives luck too much dominion and is a crowded strategy.
- You can form a definite plan even in law or finance. The usual path of hopping to another firm for more money is "a recipe for disaster." Better: stay at one bank or firm for 10-15 years until you're the only one who knows what's going on. "You should plan on being partner or managing director from day one."
- "Your plans can change, but if you don't have them, you're just floating with the current."
- His closing hope: computer science is as deterministic as it gets, so it is odd to view tech startups through an indeterminate lens. An alternative title for the lecture was "Control Alt Delete": "The best edit is often a complete re-write."
