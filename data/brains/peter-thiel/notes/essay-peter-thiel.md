## Stagnation is a story of atoms, not bits

- Peter says the stagnation is a failure in the world of atoms, not bits. Computers, the Internet and mobile have seen heavy innovation; supersonic travel, space, new energy and new medicine have not. He calls it a "two-track" pattern of innovation.
- Core explanation: "bits were unregulated and atoms were regulated."
  - A software company costs maybe $100,000 to start.
  - A new drug through the FDA costs on the order of a billion dollars.
  - If the FDA regulated video games, they would need double-blind studies to show games aren't addictive.
- He says it is overdetermined, with many causes. His own response is not lobbying regulators to be reasonable; it is helping people "succeed at the margins."
- He also points to hysteresis: a history of failure discourages people, so failure begets failure. "No halfway sane parent would encourage their kids to study nuclear engineering today," while software draws people in because of past success.
- Signal successes in other fields could set precedents and turn a vicious cycle into a virtuous one.

## How the stagnation ends: human agency, not an automatic trend

- His most natural hope is technology at the boundary of bits and atoms, with information technology broadening out to affect the physical world. Self-driving cars are his example of a significant innovation that would change a decent amount at the margins.
- Views on specific fields:
  - AI "feels slightly overhyped."
  - Biotech could see a lot happen but is heavily regulated.
  - He thinks AI is further away than the consensus believes, and that its story is "happening more slowly."
  - Self-driving cars might replace at most 1 percent of the workforce and raise productivity by a few percent; phased in over a decade, that would not be that transformative.
- He rejects the premise of asking for an indicator that the stagnation has ended. He does not think the future is a fixed thing that just exists, and he disagrees with Kurzweil's accelerating-chart vision where you "sit back, eat popcorn, and watch the movie of the future unfold." If everyone takes a nap, "the great stagnation is never going to end."
- His overarching agenda is to find some way out of the stagnation "by literally helping people to start companies that will change the world."

## Government has lost the capacity to do big things

- Peter is pessimistic that government is where the stagnation gets reversed. "A letter from Einstein to the White House would get lost in the mail room today. You could not even do Apollo."
- Expectations have fallen: in the 1980s the SDI debate was first-strike weapon versus great defensive technology; today people say it was fiction that would never have worked.
- He thinks the 1930s and 1940s had real technocratic competence, and that the Manhattan Project and Apollo would be unthinkable now. The 1970s were when institutions started working much less well, and Nixon's war on cancer was "the first signal one that really went wrong."
- Government is a poor investor in technology because it lacks the ability to evaluate it. By his count, no more than about 35 members of the House and Senate have technical degrees, and two-thirds are lawyers.
- Solyndra example: a cylindrical panel has 2πr the surface area of a flat panel of width 2r, so it is 1/π as efficient, which ninth-grade geometry shows. The Energy Department, run by Nobel laureate Steve Chu, couldn't use that. The Republican critique blamed process and kickbacks, the Democratic defense pointed to a portfolio process; he wants focus on substance, not process.
- Private-sector coordination at scale is the alternative: Silicon Valley start-ups, and larger companies like his PayPal colleague Elon Musk's SpaceX and Tesla, which require complex coordination of many pieces. This is "not as big as we could do" with a well-functioning government, but he says that isn't realistic.
- On democracy: he now says it is unclear the US is a democracy at all. It is a representative republic modified by courts and largely superseded by unelected technocratic agencies. Many are "deeply sclerotic," yet the alternatives (for example Congress dictating Fed policy) seem worse.

## Conformity, original sin, and how Peter selects people

- Peter places "original sin" in society more than in individuals: society is real and powerful but on the whole problematic.
- He says many successful Silicon Valley entrepreneurs seem to have a mild form of Asperger's, "missing the imitation, socialization gene." That is a plus for innovation, but it is also an indictment of society: everyone else gets "talked out of our interesting, original, creative ideas before they are even fully formed" and ends up opening the safe restaurant everyone understands, which is hypercompetitive.
- His own path: highly tracked in Northern California (an eighth-grade yearbook note predicted Stanford), then Stanford, Stanford Law, and a big Manhattan law firm. "From the outside, it was a place where everybody wanted to get in. On the inside, it was a place where everybody wanted to get out." He left after seven months and three days, and a colleague said it was like "I had no idea it was possible to escape from Alcatraz." He calls this a quasi-conversion experience: the value system was deeply corrupt and needed to be questioned.
- Two routes to challenging convention: being oblivious to it, or becoming aware of how conventional conventions are and thinking for yourself.
- He worries conformity is more acute than in the 1950s and 60s; the eccentric scientist or professor is going extinct because research universities leave less room for them.
- What he looks for in people is combinations of near-opposites ("Zen-like opposites... like 175 degrees"):
  - Really stubborn and really open-minded.
  - Idiosyncratic and different, but able to work in teams.
  - For founding teams, ask about the company's prehistory: how they met and how long they have worked together.
- He prefers mild versions of attitudes. Extreme pessimism says there's no point in doing anything and extreme optimism says there's no need to; "they converge on doing nothing." He credits German pessimism (his family spoke German at home for his first 12 years, no TV) as a corrective to Silicon Valley's "optimistic and somewhat desperate" culture.

## Contrarian questions, monopoly, and the Straussian reading of Zero to One

- His interview question: "Tell me something that's true that very few people agree with you on." It is hard to answer not for lack of ideas but because the answers aren't things people want to say.
- He gave "the education system is badly broken" as a now-uncontroversial example (a TechCrunch piece drew about 350 comments, roughly 70 percent in his favor). The idea that every successful business aims for a monopoly is on "the border" of what he'll say; "the really good ideas are way more dangerous than that."
- The Straussian reading of Zero to One is that "perhaps you should not be an entrepreneur." He is skeptical of "entrepreneur" as a goal, calling it a vague, empty term like "I want to be rich." If the core advice is to build a monopoly, that discourages people who don't have such an idea. "We should be starting more good businesses and fewer bad ones," not more start-ups in the abstract.
- He said he did not think the tech scene was in a bubble like 1999-2000 (as of this conversation), because the public as a whole wasn't involved the same way.
- Vertical versus horizontal progress:
  - Horizontal progress is globalization, copying what works from 1 to n, and has something like a formula.
  - Vertical progress is technology, going from 0 to 1, and has no formula.
  - Every moment in the history of science, technology and business happens only once: "The next Mark Zuckerberg won't start a social network, the next Larry Page won't start a search engine."
  - He suggests being passionate about something you are good at that other people are not doing.
- Nonprofits: the contrarian nonprofit question is which great causes are deeply unpopular. He only wants to fund unpopular causes and asks "Why is your cause popular? Why is your cause unpopular?" Keep a counterfactual sense of mission: if you weren't doing this, nobody else would be. "Social" can mean good for society or good as seen by society, and the second tends to dominate, yielding "lemming-like, sheep-like clones."
- Advice to an institution: "I would always be long substance, short status." Chasing respectability means softening the edges.

## Globalization, geography and company names

- Bet against globalization: 2007 was likely the peak of both the finance boom (a 25-year boom from 1982 to 2007) and globalization, "like maybe 1913." Be in places and industries not levered to globalization.
  - Long California, short New York.
  - Long Texas, short Virginia (DC and New York are centers of globalization; California and Texas are inward-focused).
  - Relatively long Japan; skeptical of London and New York.
  - Smaller countries are underrated for adaptability; New Zealand is attractive as far from the Middle East as you can get.
- Japan: he suggests it is now perhaps the least conformist, least imitative country, no longer the Meiji or 1950s copying Japan. It no longer thinks it can gain much by copying the West, and this is a critique of the West.
- Brazil: inward-looking, with four false dawns of bullishness over about 150 years, each tied to globalization hopes. Facebook's international spread was a proxy for inward-versus-outward focus; Brazil was harder to break into than the rest of Latin America. Example: OGX's founder, worth $30 billion in 2011 and about -$1 billion later, held offshore oil concessions but there were no Brazilian oil service companies to develop them.
- China: hard to evaluate; he suspects it is underestimated but hard to invest in, since you would need to be a well-connected Communist Party member.
- Fracking: roughly $450 billion went in over four or five years, and it is unclear whether it earns a return at $50 oil. The fortunes made in fracking were much smaller than in IT, which suggested it was a more marginal innovation.
- Company names are "often very predictive of future failure or success":
  - PayPal was a friendly name, "the friend that helps you pay"; Napster ("you nap some music, you nap a kid") was a bad name, and regulators shut it down within a few years.
  - Airbnb sounds like an innocent virtual bed and breakfast; Uber sounds like a bad German name from the 1930s ("What are you exactly above? Maybe the law?"), worse from a regulatory view.
  - Facebook beat MySpace: reading dominates writing over time, Facebook was about real identities at Harvard, and MySpace started among wannabe actors writing fictional narratives about themselves. "The whole product arc was implicit in the names."

## Science, money, inequality, longevity and careers

- Science has gone "dramatically for the worse." Government money in the 1930s-60s accelerated research but politicized it. A good scientist is nearly the opposite of a politician (scientist interested in truth; politician has "a very troubled relationship with the truth"). A Gresham's law applies: grant-writing experts drove out eccentric scientists. Extreme specialization lets expert communities obscure this from the public. "When you use the word science it's often a tell, like in poker, that you're bluffing": we have political science and social science, but physics and chemistry are not called "physical science."
- Money: microregulations matter more than monetary policy. He would rather abolish most regulation and keep the Fed than keep zoning laws and other crazy rules and switch to PayPal, Bitcoin or gold. He thinks money should be linked to something real, citing the gold standard's discipline.
- Inequality: separate whether it is rising (probably in the US, unclear globally), why (he blames globalization more than technology, and it is overdetermined), and what to do (many remedies are worse than the disease; higher marginal rates incentivize loopholes). The real issue is stagnation, not inequality. In San Francisco housing costs are the biggest variable, and he'd break the "unholy alliance between urban slumlords and pseudo-environmentalists" that blocks urban development.
- Security versus privacy is a fake dichotomy of doing more with more or less with less; real innovation is doing more with less.
- Longevity: life is worthwhile in itself, and death is "kind of a bad thing." Wanting to live longer needs no special mission; the acceptance-and-denial attitude toward mortality converges on doing nothing. His straight-line forecast uses life expectancy rising about 2.2 to 2.5 years per decade since 1840, putting him in his early to mid-90s, plus perhaps 10 more years for an upper case of 100-110.
- Careers: he distrusts abstract general advice, since if everyone followed it, it would be wrong. Software still has too few people, and petroleum engineering is another underexplored field. Vocational careers like plumbing can pay about what the average doctor makes but are seen as uncool. Smarter people are often more prone to trendy thinking because they pick up cues more easily.
- Private cities: he'd be interested if one could be started for around $50 million rather than $50 billion, and thought Romer's African charter-city idea was prohibitively expensive.
- Chess: he always opens e4 even though d4 may be marginally better, because below world-champion level he enjoys the added risk and volatility.
