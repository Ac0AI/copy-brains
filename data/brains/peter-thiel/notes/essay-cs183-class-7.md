## Why power law distribution is the key structural fact of venture capital

- Peter calls "exponential power" "far and away the most important structural element of venture capital," yet "surprisingly poorly understood."
- Successful companies have an exponential arc: say 50% annual growth, compounding for years. Valuations rise exponentially during that period.
- A fund's J curve: early investments fail, management fees come due, then exponential winners lift the fund above water. Many funds never get there.
- The naive model has three buckets: failures go to zero, mediocre ones return about 1x, great ones return 3-10x. Peter says this misses that returns are "incredibly skewed."
- Bad VCs think all companies are created equal. The better a VC understands the skew, the better the VC.
- Example: in Founders Fund's 2005 fund, the best investment ended up worth about as much as all the rest combined. The second best was worth about as much as number three through the rest.
- Rule of thumb: "a VC portfolio will only make money if your best company investment ends up being worth more than your whole fund."
- Exponential thinking is hard because life is mostly linear. People vastly underestimate exponential things.

## How venture funds work: 2-and-20 and what it means for returns

- VC began in the late 1940s. Before that, wealthy individuals and families invested in new ventures directly. The Sand Hill Road version arrived in the late 1960s with Sequoia, Kleiner Perkins and Mayfield.
- Mechanics: VCs pool money from limited partners (LPs), invest it in portfolio companies, and hope those companies grow so everyone makes money. VCs must both win over LPs and pick winners.
- The standard model is 2-and-20: an annual management fee of 2% of the fund, plus 20% of gains beyond the original investment (the carry). A $200m fund earns $4m a year in fees.
- The fee is theoretically just enough to keep the firm running; the real payout is the carry.
- Funds last several years because companies take years to grow. Many investments go to zero, and the winners must return all the money and then some.
- Management fees aside, VCs only get paid if they return all invested capital plus more. So the key question for every deal: "Is there a reasonable scenario where our stake in this company will be worth more than the whole fund?"
- PayPal sold to eBay for $1.5bn. Its early investors held enough that their stake was worth about their fund's size, so they roughly broke even on the fund. PayPal's series B investors did well on PayPal but still did not break even on their fund. Like many early-2000s funds, theirs lost money.

## Portfolio construction: concentrate instead of spreading

- Given a power law, Peter wants a fairly concentrated portfolio. Investing in 100 companies "to cover your bases through volume" suggests sloppy thinking, because there are not that many businesses you can hold high conviction about.
- A better model: invest in maybe 7 or 8 promising companies from which you think you can get a 10x return. Founders Fund tries to invest in 7 to 10 companies per fund, aiming for a 10x return.
- In theory the math is the same with 100 companies each expected to return 100x, but in practice that looks less like investing and more like buying lottery tickets.
- The curve is "fractal-esque all the way up." It is about as hard to go from $10m to $100m as from $100m to $1bn or $1bn to $10bn. Going from $100bn to a trillion is harder "because the world isn't that big" (Apple's market cap was $500bn, Microsoft's $250bn).
- VC is not private equity, where you shoot for consistent 2x or 3x returns. VCs' eggs are in the founder's basket too: "They have a few more baskets than you do, but again, because of the power law, not many."
- Even later-stage investors targeting 3-5x with low mortality still face a power law; the curve is just less steep.
- Angels are saturated and tend to be less aware of power law dynamics, so they often overestimate a given company.

## Up rounds, flat rounds and the pro rata heuristic

- Backtesting Founders Fund's portfolios, one heuristic worked "shockingly well": always exercise pro rata rights when a smart VC leads an up round, and never increase your investment in a flat or down round.
- Why the inefficiency may exist: people do not believe in power laws. A big valuation step-up feels too large, so most VCs underprice it. Insiders also discount the change because the office and daily life still look the same.
- Flat rounds should be avoided because they signal that something has gone badly wrong. Flat rounds are driven by people expecting maybe a 2x return.
- Peter says not to be mechanical about the rule, but it forces you to think about power laws.
- Even with dilution, a company does well if every round is an up round. A single down round tends to be disastrous, mainly because it destroys relationships among all the players.
- If you take money from a not-so-intelligent investor who offers a huge valuation, take it only if it is the last money you will take.
- Peter's PayPal example: the most successful up round was a 5x valuation increase, pitched with a forward-looking narrative rather than "take x and multiply by 5." His point: "The real value is always in the future." Without a specific future to point to, people anchor to the past and ask how it can be worth 5x what it was three months ago.

## What the power law means for founders, employees and careers

- Equity percentage needs a modifier: where your company sits on the power law curve can matter as much as or more than your stake. Having 1% beats 0.5%, all else equal, but "the 100th employee at Google did much better than the average venture-backed CEO did in the last decade."
- This is not an argument against joining startups. It means thinking hard about where a given company will land on the curve.
- Peter rejects the view that the curve is random and you are just a lottery ticket. He says that is wrong and promises to explain why later.
- Key life decisions and starting businesses also produce such distributions, but people think about them too moderately. Reality is "probably more extreme."
- Some things really are flat: the Postal Service is his example, where what you see is what you get. People are trained to think that way, so they miscalculate in tech startups, where skew is "really vast."
- Co-founders through a power law lens: one co-founder means giving up half the company, two means giving up 2/3. If you pick the right people, the outcome is likely more than 2x or 3x better, so co-founders work well. A core team of two with equal shares tends to work very well, or sometimes one brilliant founder far above everyone else.
- A paradox: people who build the really great companies are usually hesitant to sell them, not for lack of offers. People heavily motivated by money are never the ones who make the most money in a power law world.
- He prefers hedgehogs to foxes in business, citing Isaiah Berlin: "foxes know many little things, but hedgehogs know one big thing." You should still try to know lots of little things too.

## Raising venture money, competition and a startup's own power law

- Peter says a startup that insists it will make money in many ways is troubling. Revenue also follows a power law: "one source of revenue will dominate everything else." Making money with A is key; making money with A through E is "terrifying, from an investor's perspective."
- If a company has a winner-take-all dynamic or a power law at play, take venture funding. "Giving up 25% of your business is worth it if it enables you to take over your industry." If you do not need to move as quickly as possible, rethink taking VC.
- He says you never land just one investor: "Chances are you have at least two people who are interested or you have zero." The cynical explanation is that most VCs lack confidence in their decisions and wait to ape others.
- A slide at the end of a deck saying you want to sell for $20m in 18 months would raise a big red flag.
- A power law also operates within a VC's portfolio: there is a perverse misallocation where problem companies consume the most board time, and reputational costs to leaving boards. Peter says there is a gap between VCs' public branding (loving attention to all companies equally) and reality. "The unspoken truth is that the best way to make money might be to promise everyone help but then actually help the ones who are going to provide the best returns."
- On incubators: in a long-cycle incubator, people start in similar boats but end up in very different ships because of the power law, which creates jarring intercompany dynamics.
- He will not take a stand for or against Y Combinator, but says attending an incubator that is not Y Combinator is perceived as a negative credential, "like getting a degree at Berkeley" instead of Stanford.
- Failure is not free: "One still shouldn't take failure lightly, though. There is still a reasonably high cost of failure."
- Asked who the smart VCs are: "The usual suspects."
