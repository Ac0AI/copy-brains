## Why competition is a trap and monopoly is the goal

- Peter argues the common view that capitalism and perfect competition are synonyms is wrong. He frames them as opposites: capitalism is about accumulating capital, while in perfect competition "you can't make any money."
- Competition is deeply embedded in American culture and education as character-building. Peter's concern is that people keep competing forever when the optimum is to stop competing, "to become a monopoly and enjoy success."
- Perfect competition can be fine for people who don't need to make money (just keep the lights on), and some fields like sports and politics are inherently competitive. He notes it is easier to build a good business than to become the fastest person alive or get elected President.
- He treats competition as appropriate for learning, and credentials as sometimes reflecting real accomplishment, but warns about chasing credentials as a habit.
- Competition also demoralizes. Top students reach elite universities, find the bar has been raised, and compete harder instead of questioning the bar. He says Harvard "sends its students into the eye of the hurricane" while Stanford is the farthest of the top schools from perfect competition (structured heterogeneity of engineering, humanities, athletics).
- On Stanford vs. Berkeley: asking which grads started the most valuable company in each of the last 40 years, Stanford "probably wins by something like 40 to zero." He calls that "monopoly capitalism, far away from a world of perfect competition."
- War is the perfect picture of competition ("everyone just kills everyone"). If life is war, you spend all your time preparing for it or doing it. Peter asks whether "life is war" is "just a strange lie we're told" and suggests sometimes you should run away and find something else to do.

## Confusing difficulty with value: the Supreme Court clerkship story

- After graduating from Stanford Law in 1992 and clerking a year on the 11th Circuit, Peter was among a handful of clerks who reached the interview stage with two Supreme Court Justices. He didn't get the clerkship.
- Years later, after building and selling PayPal, an old law school friend asked, "So, aren't you glad you didn't get that Supreme Court clerkship?" Peter's reading: winning would probably have meant more insane competition, and no PayPal.
- The wry version: Rhodes Scholars "all had a great future in their past."
- Core lesson: "in the race to compete, we learn to confuse what is hard with what is valuable." Intense competition makes things hard, so intensity becomes a proxy for value, but value is a separate question.
- He cites Kissinger's line about academia: the battles are so fierce because the stakes are so small.
- The question he poses: at what point does it make sense to step out of competition and shift your life trajectory toward monopoly?

## Why companies lie about monopoly and competition

- Monopolies have an incentive to hide it. A CEO bragging about a great monopoly invites antitrust audits and scrutiny, so the incentive is to "shout from the rooftops that you're not" a monopoly, even if you are.
- Non-monopolies have the opposite incentive: they want investors, but in a world of perfect competition investors shouldn't invest because no company can make a profit. So they pretend to be unique.
- Result: monopolies pretend they aren't, non-monopolies pretend they are, and rhetoric shrinks the apparent range between them. Peter concludes reality is "probably more binary—monopoly or competitive commodity business—than we think."

## How to spot market-definition lies (intersection vs. union stories)

- Really big markets tend to be very competitive, so a competitive business is tempted to rhetorically shrink its market.
- Example: a British-food-only restaurant in Palo Alto. The relevant market might be restaurants generally, or include Menlo Park and Mountain View. The incentive is not to ask, and to tell a story about why you're different when a bearish investor cites that 90% of restaurants fail within 2 years. Peter asks whether people who eat only British food in Palo Alto, the only ones you'd have pricing power over, even exist.
- PayPal vs. Castro Street in 2001: Mountain View had many lunch places (Indian, Thai, Vietnamese, American, with sub-splits like South Indian, cheaper vs. fancier). PayPal was then the only email-based payments company in the world and employed fewer people than the restaurants did, yet from a capital formation perspective was worth more than all the restaurants' equity combined.
- Movie pitches work the same way: "Andrew Luck stars in a cross between 'Hackers' and 'Jaws'" (a college football star joins elite hackers to catch the shark that killed his friend) is unique, but "query whether that intersection would be any good."
- The rule: **non-monopolies narrow their market and tell intersection stories** (British food ∩ restaurant ∩ Palo Alto). **Monopolies insist they're in a huge market and tell union stories** about tiny fish in big markets.
- Google as worked example, depending on how the market is defined:
  - Search engine: Google 66.4%, Microsoft 15.3%, Yahoo 13.8%. By the Herfindahl-Hirschman index, 66% squared is well over 0.25, so a monopoly.
  - Advertising company: U.S. search advertising $16b, U.S. online advertising $31b, U.S. advertising $144b, global advertising $412b. Google then has under 4% of global advertising.
  - Tech company (robotic cars, TV, Google Plus, Android): consumer tech is a $964b market.
- Eric Schmidt's line fits the union story: "The Internet is incredibly competitive, and new forms of accessing information are being utilized every day." Peter's subtext: we have to run hard to stay in the same place, no different from the pizzeria in downtown Palo Alto.

## Reading cash and margins as monopoly signals

- Cash on balance sheets (as cited in 2012): Apple about $98b, growing about $30b a year; Microsoft $52b; Google $45b; Amazon $10b.
- In perfect competition you'd have to reinvest all that cash just to stay where you are. Growing $30b a year makes you question whether things are really that competitive.
- Gross margins cited: Apple around 40%, Google about 65%, Microsoft around 75%, Amazon 14% (still huge for a retailer; grocery stores are probably around 2%).
- In perfect competition marginal revenue equals marginal cost, so high margins suggest a company is two businesses combined: a core monopoly (search, for Google) plus side projects (robotic cars, TV). Cash piles up because the monopoly piece is cheap to run and it doesn't make sense to pump it all into side projects.
- Amazon reinvests just 3% of its profits, which Peter describes as "more easy jog than intense sprint."

## The four ways to own a market

- A company must have some combination of **brand, scale cost advantages, network effects, or proprietary technology**.
- **Brand**: the hardest to pin down; Peter calls it "a classic code word for monopoly." It means people don't see products as interchangeable and pay more. Example: most people strongly prefer Coke or Pepsi, so both generate huge cash flows. If you build a brand, you build a monopoly.
- **Scale cost advantages**: apply with high fixed costs and low marginal costs, where you get more efficient as you get bigger (Amazon online, Wal-Mart in retail).
- **Network effects**: the nature of the product locks people into a particular business.
- **Proprietary technology**: technology no one else can use.
- Apple, "probably the greatest tech monopoly today," has all four: building both hardware and software gives it the whole value chain; Foxconn scale gives cost advantages; developers and millions of repeat customers give network effects; and the brand is "something extra that's hard to define." An otherwise identical product from another company would have to be priced lower.

## The three-step recipe: pick, monopolize, expand

- Steps to a valuable tech company: (1) find, create, or discover a new market; (2) monopolize it; (3) expand the monopoly over time.
- **Goldilocks principle** for the first market: not too small, not too big. Too small means no customers; PayPal's original idea of beaming money on Palm Pilots meant "no one else was doing it, which was good. But no one really needed it done, which was bad." Too big is too competitive and hard to get a handle on.
- Choosing the market is "not a rhetorical exercise." It is about the objective truth of the market, not intersection or union framing.
- The scaling story takes one form: "find a small target market, become the best in the world at serving it, take over immediately adjacent markets, widen the aperture of what you're doing, and capture more and more." Once large, network effects, technology, scale or brand should make it hard to follow. If there's no compelling narrative of how the market scales, "you haven't yet found or created the right market."
- You can't get the future exactly right, but thinking about it improves the narrative and the odds. Example: Bell's telephone market started tiny, was easy to own, then expanded until network effects made it hard to break into.
- The best way to fail is to invert the recipe by starting big and shrinking (Pets.com, Webvan, Kozmo.com). Failure paradigm: not being honest about objective market conditions; "you can't succeed by believing your own rhetoric over reality except by luck."

## Case studies: Amazon, eBay, LinkedIn, Twitter, Zynga, Groupon

- **Amazon**: started as an online bookstore (cataloguing every book is hard but manageable in scale), with the vision of scaling to the world's general store from the outset. The name, evoking the diversity of the Amazon, was elastic enough to cover every *thing* later.
- **eBay**: started small as a platform for the unexpected; first Pez dispensers, then beanie babies. Marketplaces tend toward natural monopoly: buyers go where sellers are and vice versa (why companies list on one stock exchange). In 2004 it became clear auctions suited unique goods with intense demand and limited supply (coins, stamps), not commodity products sold by Amazon, Overstock and Buy.com. It's "still a great monopoly business," just smaller than expected.
- **LinkedIn**: 61 million U.S. users and 150 million worldwide, pitched as a network for everyone but largely used for headhunting. Peter notes a joke long/short idea: short companies whose staff are joining LinkedIn to post résumés, go long the ones suspiciously quiet. Key question: is the business network the same as the social network? If it is fundamentally discrete, LinkedIn likely owns that market for a long time.
- **Twitter**: began as a small niche product (anyone as a microbroadcaster) and scales into a media distribution center. Monetization is an open question, but on durability it's hard to beat: "you can copy it. But you can't *replicate* it"; at 141 characters you break SMS compatibility, at 139 you're missing a character.
- **Zynga**: Mark Pincus's line, "Not having clear goal at outset leads to death by a thousand compromises." Zynga executed well (Farmville, aggressive copying of what worked, best-in-class monetization of in-game perks). Its durability question is whether it's a creative business, which would make it like a Hollywood studio whose fortunes rise and fall. The better narrative is "hardcore psychometric sauce": "we know how to make people buy more sheep, and therefore we are a permanent monopoly."
- **Groupon**: started small and scaled aggressively; claims brand as its moat. The bear case is no proprietary technology and no network effects, so if the brand is weaker than claimed, long-term trouble.

## Finding the tech frontier and timing entry

- Peter argues the "disruptive tech" story is overdone: "Disruptive companies tend not to succeed. Disruptive kids get sent to principal's office." Napster was disruptive, probably too much, and broke too many rules. Better to find a frontier and go for it.
- **Fog-on-the-water framework**: you're in a boat on a foggy body of water that could be a pond, lake or ocean. The longer you've been out, the longer your expected remaining journey; time passing means you're likely crossing an ocean. The frontier is temporal, not geographical: it's about *when* things happen.
- **Timing**: building a car company in the 19th century was too early, and it's far too late now (some 300 companies were built in the 20th century, a few survive). The best time to enter may be much later than conventional wisdom's "early," but not too late. You want to make "the last great development, after which the drawbridge goes up and you have permanent capture." In his phrasing: go long on tech, succeed, then short tech.
- Examples of "last" companies: Microsoft is probably the last operating system company; Google is the last search engine company (algorithmic approach was a quantum improvement that can't be much improved on).
- Fields assessed:
  - Lithium batteries: probably past the time to start; innovation may be too slow and the technology too set.
  - Aerospace: seemingly terminal markets sometimes aren't; SpaceX thinks it can cut launch costs 70-90%. If nothing has happened in an industry for a long time and you dramatically improve something important, "chances are that no one else will come and do that again, *to you*."
  - Bioinformatics: promising, but hard to know where it will be in 15 or 20 years; avoid fields moving too fast, like the 1980s disk drive companies.
  - Artificial intelligence: "probably an underrated field." People are burned out because it was overrated for decades, but progress is increasingly relentless (chess improving; computers probably beating humans in Go in 4 or 5 years). No one knows how far it will go.
  - Mobile internet: ask whether it's a gold rush and whether to dig or sell shovels. Google and Apple already sell the shovels, there may not be much gold left, and the market is too big and too competitive. Maybe you can own a valuable niche, but be skeptical.

## The test for a good frontier: why should the 20th employee join?

- Peter's test: "Why should the 20th employee join your company?" A great answer means you're on the right track; the question is deceptively easy.
- Your indirect competition for talent is companies like Google, so the sharper version is why the 20th engineer would join you rather than Google for more money and prestige.
- The right answer must be that you're creating a monopoly business. Early companies are driven by the quality of their people, so you need a compelling monopoly story to attract the best.
- You can't out-recruit a great monopoly business unless you have a powerful narrative that you're becoming one too. He advises not competing with Google at its core monopoly of search.
