## Monopoly versus competition as the basic fork in business

- Peter argues the Tolstoy line is inverted in business: "all happy companies are different" because each found something unique that gives it a mission and a monopoly of sorts; "all unhappy companies are alike" because they failed to escape "the essential sameness that is competition."
- He says there are really only two kinds of businesses, monopolies and those that never make money, and the difference gets obscured on both sides.
  - Monopolists pretend not to be monopolies so they avoid regulation, and tell a story of fierce competition.
  - Competitive businesses pretend to be unique to attract investors (e.g. a Palo Alto restaurant claiming the best sushi, or "the only British food in Palo Alto").
  - "The apparent difference is much smaller than the real difference."
- Examples of businesses to avoid: the fourth online pet food company, the 10th thin-film solar panel company, a restaurant in any city. These are competitive but not "capitalist" in the sense that they don't generate profits or capital.
- The airline industry is his cautionary case: a big business that, in the US, has made no profits cumulatively in 100 years because competition destroyed them. He would rather have started Google than a pizza place in downtown Palo Alto, or even an airline.
- As a founder, the goal is always to aim for a monopoly: do something no one else is doing. Capitalism "works best when you do something that's very unique, very differentiated."
- Incremental iteration ("things that are just incrementally better") lands you in an intensely competitive dynamic.

## How monopolies form and erode: Ford, brand, and which moats he prefers

- Asked about Ford's cycles of profit and near-failure, Peter says early Ford was a vertically integrated monopoly with enormous economies of scale, the classic version that worked best.
- In recent decades differentiation is trickier. The main one is brand, which he calls "the weakest form of Monopoly."
  - Brand is real: people will pay more than manufacturing cost because they like it (Mercedes and BMW, Coke versus Pepsi loyalists, some people preferring Ford to Chevrolet even when products are undifferentiated).
  - He admits he doesn't understand why it works, and calls it a slippery concept.
- His investing preference: companies with a major technological advantage, vertical integration, or other kinds of monopoly he considers "somewhat more robust" than brand.
- Marketing and advertising do work and can build a real brand, but he prefers not to invest in those companies.

## Sales and distribution matter as much as product; plans beat no plan

- He rejects the engineer's conceit that "the world will beat a path to your door step." There is always a sales component, even when it's hidden. Apple appeared to need none, but sales didn't happen in a vacuum.
- The best salesmen aren't transparently salesmen. Steve Jobs was a phenomenal salesman who "didn't look like a used car dealer."
- As an investor, he wants a distribution plan even if the product is great. It's the component people underestimate most, especially engineers. People "always overvalue what they do well": lawyers think only law exists, scientists only science.
- On "retain optionality," he says a bad plan is better than no plan. Founders should think through how the market and product will develop over 5 to 10 years.
  - Changing course later is fine, but "we have no idea what we're doing, we'll figure it out as we go" is a bad starting point.
  - The Silicon Valley version of putting talented people in a room to brainstorm until a product emerges has been tried a few times and "has never worked."
  - Good business plans are rarer than talented people, which makes them valuable.

## Technology versus globalization, and why the developed world has stagnated

- Progress comes in two modes: globalization, which copies what works (horizontal progress, one typewriter to 100), and technology, which invents new things (vertical progress, typewriter to word processor). "Zero to one" is about the second.
- China's plan for the next 20 years is to copy everything that works in the West. He thinks nothing in Chinese culture prevents invention, but China is still poor and can gain a lot from copying. The developed world (US, Western Europe, Japan) has to invent, so technology matters more than globalization for it.
- He says the whole developed world has entered an era of relative stagnation. Per capita incomes have still risen some in the US over 40 years, but there is a "sense of malaise," worse in Western Europe, with a belief that the next generation won't do as well as its parents.
- Technology has been redefined as information technology, a much narrower meaning than 40 or 50 years ago. Progress is a "very powerful but very narrow cone" in the world of bits, not atoms, like a Cartesian dualism between a virtual world advancing and a material world that isn't.
- Areas where he sees missed progress:
  - Energy: no real move beyond increasingly expensive fossil fuels.
  - Food: the Green Revolution has run out of steam.
  - Medicine: the 1970s expectation of defeating cancer, 44 years on, with little progress.
- Pressed on driverless cars, fracking, 3D printing and genomics, he says critics always point to things "a few years from now." They are plausible, but the last 40 years have been slower. The atoms world is heavily regulated: hard to build a nuclear plant or a rocket, supersonic planes are too loud, new drugs cost a billion dollars to get through the FDA. He sees no natural law stopping more; he blames cultural and political issues.

## Growth, supply-side thinking, and why stagnation is politically dangerous

- Standing still isn't a civilizational option for a representative democracy. Medieval societies or Egypt could stay static for a thousand years, but a republic depends on a growing pie so compromises can be win-win-win. Once the pie stops growing, politics gets polarized and the system "doesn't work that well."
- He disagrees with Larry Summers, who treats stagnation as a Keynesian demand problem to fix with money printing, lower rates, deficit spending and more consumer credit. Peter calls that "completely crazy": consumers already borrow too much and baby boomers are approaching retirement in an actuarially bankrupt state.
- His view is classical: "the only side that really matters is Supply," meaning overall productivity. The question is why it's so hard to create new things, which has less to do with Bernanke or Yellen at the Fed and more with micro regulations that drive the supply side.
- Asked if he sounds like Reagan, he says close, but in this era marginal tax rates matter less and regulation matters more.
- On Obamacare: even as a hardcore libertarian he wouldn't have predicted it couldn't get a website to work, a far simpler product than an Apollo rocket or the Manhattan Project.

## Why government lost the ability to build, and where problems get solved now

- The government once coordinated complex projects (atomic weapons, the Moon landing). Now it mainly provides insurance, and its solutions to big problems are transfer payments.
- He sees an inability to do complex coordination and even to describe a future very different from the present. Redistributive and utilitarian considerations dominate: giving Grandma more for Medicare beats investing in research for new drugs. Discretionary investing steadily falls and non-discretionary spending rises. His line: "a letter from an Einstein would get lost in the White House mail room."
- He says the last speech he remembers describing a really different future was Reagan's "tear down that wall" (and before that Martin Luther King's "I Have a Dream"). Today it "doesn't poll test."
- Popular culture shows only bad futures: The Matrix, Terminator, Avatar, Elysium, and Gravity (where you'd want to be back on an island and never go into space). In that world you won't get broad political support for something very different.
- He doesn't claim startups solve every problem, but says that is how problems will actually get solved, "not through mass movements."
- He prefers Silicon Valley to DC: politics is important but "almost endlessly frustrating," so the key is figuring out what you can do today without waiting for political reform. He thinks the 2008 crisis helped by pulling talented young people away from Wall Street and DC (a two-to-three-year DC bubble has ended) toward Silicon Valley.
- He is "very long Silicon Valley, very short New York" as an index bet. Finance had half of US profits by 2007 and faces regulatory headwinds and a long renormalization. Engineering (except computer science) has been a bad career for years, so there is room for real engineering rather than financial engineering.
- On New York copying Silicon Valley (Cornell's Roosevelt Island campus), he is skeptical of copying: "the next Mark Zuckerberg won't be building a social networking site, the next Bill Gates won't build an operating system." Also, "the something of somewhere is often the nothing of nowhere" (don't be "the Harvard of North Dakota").

## The education bubble and what to do instead

- Peter says education is a bubble like housing in the 2000s or internet stocks in the 90s.
  - College costs are up 400% after inflation since 1980.
  - Student debt is over a trillion dollars.
  - Federal policy subsidizes the loans and makes them impossible to discharge, even in personal bankruptcy, which he calls a form of near indentured servitude.
- Education is an abstraction: is it STEM, or real liberal arts? Economically it could be an investment, a four-year party (consumption), an insurance policy, or, as he argues in many cases, a tournament whose value comes from excluding people.
- He calls it "a very very expensive IQ test": an IQ test costs $25, a Harvard education a quarter of a million dollars.
- Test of exclusivity: a normal business with the world clamoring to get in would expand, but a Harvard president who sharply increased enrollment would be fired. It is like Studio 54, where more exclusivity means more value, so it masquerades as positive-sum while being zero-sum.
- He compares universities to the Catholic Church in the early 16th century: a single path, a "secular atheistic" church where salvation is a diploma, with corrupt-feeling indulgences and costs rising. The reformers' message, and his, is that there is no formula and people have to figure out how to save themselves, which is disturbing because people want a formula.
- He has never said nobody should go to college; he wants more choices, so it isn't "you go to Yale or you go to jail." He expects heterogeneous alternatives: MOOCs, amped-up vocational training, Udemy-style six-month programming courses that lead to real jobs. He founded the Thiel Fellowships to entice bright kids to drop out and start companies.
- Advice to his 18-year-old self: "value substance more than status." Is it for prestige or because there are important things to learn? Go long substance, short status.

## Planning your own life, agency, and meaning

- Looking back, Peter says his 24-year-old self had no plan whatsoever, and that was a bad plan; he lasted seven months and three days at Sullivan & Cromwell. His bias was that education substituted for thinking about the future: undergraduate degree, then graduate degree, then law, consulting or banking because it "generates options."
- He calls adding line items to a resume "not the best way to go about it," even though elite education pushes people that way. He doesn't think a 24-year-old can know his 44-year-old self's goals, but a bad plan beats no plan, and he'd tell his younger self to think about these things much more.
- On AI and The Singularity: computers and humans are good at very different things, so they are mostly complements, not substitutes. Fears of replacement are "largely unfounded." Strong AI is extremely far away if it ever happens, and past AI predictions (such as those from the 60s) were badly mistaken.
- He rejects Kurzweil-style framing that treats the future as something that happens on exponential curves while you "sit back and eat some popcorn." Better to talk about human agency and what future we choose to build.
- Extreme optimism ("the future will take care of itself") and extreme pessimism ("nothing you can do") both lead to inertia. A healthier attitude treats the future as open-ended and up to us.
- He is "not a techno utopian": technology isn't an absolute good (nuclear weapons are an example of a catastrophic innovation), but with 7 billion people there is no going back to a pastoral Neolithic existence. We have to go further, and giving up technological progress, which has driven Western civilization for centuries, "would represent an incredible decline."
- He sees no contradiction between building technology and reading Go or Shakespeare or Greek philosophy; you can do both.
- On happiness, he prefers meaning. Meaningful lives come from doing important things that otherwise wouldn't get done: "but for you, but for this venture or this company, this important thing would not get done." He warns against being "just a cog in a machine" whom a thousand other people could replace, and says to aim for that kind of meaning.
