## Applying the 2x2 of determinacy and optimism to energy

- Peter reuses his matrix of determinate/indeterminate and optimistic/pessimistic futures as the framework for energy.
- Determinate optimism: one specific energy type is best and should be developed. Peter thinks this quadrant "arguably makes the most sense," yet it is the least talked about today.
- Determinate pessimism: nothing is considerably better, so ration and conserve.
- Indeterminate optimism: better, cheaper sources exist but nobody knows which, so build a portfolio.
- Indeterminate pessimism: the sources will probably be worse and costlier, so take a portfolio approach anyway.
- Planned indeterminacy doesn't work. "I intend to get rich and famous" is an aspiration, not a plan. A portfolio only works with granularity, e.g. "if I do x, it leads to 5 specific options at a certain time, then I choose the best."
- In practice statistical thinking breeds radical indeterminacy, and aspirations replace plans.
- He rejects the conventional wisdom that detailed plans are worthless because everything changes. Winning without a plan is hitting the jackpot, and most people don't. To maximize mastery over outcomes, plan.
- He splits energy into two markets: power generation (wood, coal, gas, nuclear, biomass, hydro, solar, feeding the grid) and transportation (oil, plus electricity for buses and trains, where you carry the power with you). The first question is always which market you are actually in.

## Where each country and era sits on the matrix

- 1950s-60s: nuclear was the one thing nearly everyone expected to be better. Eisenhower's 1953 Atoms for Peace speech said it would be "too cheap to meter." Transportation was also determinately optimistic (space jets, ever-faster supersonic planes).
- Today determinate optimism in energy is dead. Agreement on a single better, cheaper source is gone.
- The U.S. sits in indeterminate optimism. Republicans want to remove regulation to get a range of better things; Democrats want to subsidize cleantech to get them.
- China is determinately pessimistic: the future is coal and oil. It buys oil fields in Africa and mines domestic coal, with roughly 3,000-5,000 coal mining deaths a year, "essentially fighting a small war each year."
- Japan and Europe show indeterminate pessimism: bikes, tiny cars, long train commutes.
- High-speed rail is determinate pessimism. It requires rearranging where everyone lives, and "gone are the days where Robert Moses could unilaterally re-architect the state of New York."

## Energy sources follow a power law

- Historically one source dominates at a time: wood until the mid-19th century, coal from mid-19th to early 20th, petroleum from the 1930s-40s. Natural gas has now overtaken coal as number two, and nuclear is a distant third.
- Britain ran out of trees, which is one reason America's living standard was higher.
- Peter's ex ante logic: solar, wind and nuclear are very different, so it would be "extremely odd" if their price and effectiveness were nearly identical. Expect one dramatically better source, with the second and third filling niches.
- People still think about energy through a statistical/portfolio lens ("you can't predict the future"), ignoring the power law just as they do elsewhere.

## Demand and resource constraints

- China's GDP growth and energy consumption moved 1:1, both about 8% a year over the last decade. In energy terms that means no real efficiency improvement: "You get more only if you expend more."
- World oil use is 85 million barrels a day; the U.S. uses about 18 million (just under 25%) and China 9 million. China has 4x the people. At U.S. per capita use China would need 72 million barrels a day, roughly all world production; even smart-car downsizing gets to about 45 million.
- Oil price inelasticity is on the order of 10:1: a 10% demand rise means a 100% price rise.
- Only 1 billion of 7 billion people live in the developed world, so lifting the other 6 billion needs enormous energy increases. When everybody in Uttar Pradesh gets a fridge, it cancels out Western conservation.
- Resource constraints look more real than population constraints. Ehrlich's 1968 Population Bomb said 3.5 billion was beyond carrying capacity; there are now twice as many people.
- Peak oil: in 1956 Shell geologist M. King Hubbert saw new discoveries falling, with a 20-30 year lag to production, and predicted U.S. output would peak in the mid-1970s. He was right. The Texas Railroad Commission, which set world prices, gave way to OPEC by 1973. OPEC quadrupled prices from $3 to $12 a barrel, and again in 1989 to $40.
- By the Hubbert projection the world is now where the U.S. was in 1970.
- Peter floats a provocative reading: the 2007 "financial" crisis may partly be an energy crisis. Oil hit $140, the economy was contracted to contain it, oil fell to $32, and it is back to $100. "Crises about money and central banks may not just be about money and central banks."
- He offers the working thesis that most major conflicts in the last 2000 years involved energy: the Pacific theater of WWII (oil embargoes), Churchill nationalizing Anglo-Persian oil fields before WWI, and West Virginia's secession giving the North about 20x the Union coal of the South.
- Oil's cheap Indian summer overlaps the U.S.'s indefinite optimism from 1982 to 2007.

## Why cleantech failed: the 10 questions

- Cleantech drew more investment than the Internet did in the last decade, partly from environmental and social motives (Al Gore's Nobel, John Doerr's 2007 TED talk).
- Good intentions did not make it profitable. Peter lists ten things a startup must get right, or at least have a plan for: markets, mimesis and competition, secrets, incrementalism, durability, teams, distribution, timing, financing, luck.
- "8 out of 10 is sort of a B-, and 5 of 10 earns you an F." Cleantech companies were often starting at 0 or 1 out of 10.
- The sector was ambiguous about what was scarce: resources or the environment. They imply different remedies. Believing in an environmental problem but no resource problem favors subsidizing cleantech; believing in resource scarcity but no environmental problem favors rationing conventional supply. Stiglitz noted peak oil theory may have been pushed by environmentalists, since carbon emissions are solved once oil runs out.

## Market framing, mimesis, secrets, incrementalism and durability

- Energy is a commodity and the market is huge, and in huge markets you can't protect yourself from the monsters.
- Market-size games, using a hypothetical Solyndra-like company: 100 MW against U.S. solar of 950 MW is 10.5% of the market; against global solar of 18 GW it is under 1%; against global cleantech of 420 GW it is smaller; against 15,000 GW of global power generation it is "just a dot in the ocean."
- Some firms shrank their market rhetorically to look dominant. Others pitched trillion-dollar markets, which is "even more dangerous" because it looks like perfect competition. Peter says you would be better off opening a pizza restaurant in Palo Alto.
- In perfect competition "the battles are so fierce because the stakes so small." People still fight for scraps because it feels cool.
- Social entrepreneurship ("doing well while doing good") usually ends up doing neither. Companies should have a specific mission and solve a discrete, important problem. Peter asks whether something is actually good for society or merely approved of by society. If good just means what the masses approve, everyone does the same thing: everyone has a solar startup with a slightly different story.
- A secret can escape mimesis, but with 10-15 year time horizons a small secret doesn't build a decisive lead. Most wind, solar and battery progress was incremental, with no step function.
- Durability: solar has thin-film, multi-junction concentrator, crystalline Si and emerging photovoltaics, each with many executions, and you must also beat wind and hydro. The goal is to dominate your market for 20-30 years. With steady incremental progress, a marginal addition rarely gets last-mover advantage. Like 1980s manufacturing, things improved but no great, lasting companies emerged.

## Team, distribution, timing, financing and the three requirements

- Culture: cleantech teams were shockingly non-technical and zero-sum. Peter's tell: "when cleantech people started wearing suits and ties." Tech people wear t-shirts and jeans; cleantech people looked like salesmen, and were. If a product is incremental and of doubtful durability, you must be a great salesman to convince people it's dramatically better. Salespeople and athletes are important but shouldn't run things, because they think only about defeating people like themselves.
- Distribution: companies couldn't deliver the power. A huge solar farm in Southern California still needs lines to L.A., and grid connection was treated as uninteresting but often proved decisive. Peter Orszag said little of the Stimulus went to infrastructure because zoning permits for power lines would take too long, so the administration avoided building unconnectable wind farms.
- Timing: "start paddling before the wave" is right in principle, but if the wave is years away it's unclear when to start, especially when cost curves shift fast.
- Financing: because of the power law, VCs need 10x returns. Solyndra took $1.65 billion in late-stage venture-type financing, so it had to become phenomenally large. His rule of thumb: never invest in companies seeking under $1 million or over $1 billion. Under $1 million, things may be too easy and it's just a timing game; over $1 billion, the story gets implausible, especially with many near-identical competitors.
- Three requirements of innovation:
  - Incremental technology: cleantech did fairly well, with falling costs, but there were no secrets and "the questions almost answered themselves."
  - Complex coordination: left to luck. The attitude was "I'm a scientist; delivering it to L.A. is not my job." When everyone thinks that, it doesn't get done. The Internet allows the "Asperger's approach" of succeeding without talking to anyone; cleantech requires getting many people to act.
  - Breakthrough technology: for the most part nobody tried, and nobody asked the biggest questions.

## Solyndra and what the aftermath revealed

- Peter says what was striking about Solyndra was how people talked about it afterward. Nobody asked whether the technology worked, which is the substantive engineering question of a determinate world.
- In an indeterminate world people ask legal and financial process questions. Republicans said officials were too entangled with the company; Democrats said the process was legitimate and transparent.
- From a definite optimist's view, if the technology worked, minor wrongdoing over funds is a footnote. A better defense would have been for the President to say "here are 2 or 3 technologies that we think are going to work" and get serious about them.
- He calls the episode a government version of the HP board fiasco.

## Thorium as a determinate, optimistic bet

- Peter proposes thorium as an example of determinately optimistic energy thinking, "a big secret." He says this is an illustration of how to think, not a pitch to start a thorium company.
- Background: in the 1940s the government found nuclear power in plutonium, uranium and thorium. Thorium has no fissile isotopes, so it can't be weaponized, and the government wanted bombs, not power. Atoms for Peace began as a warning about thermonuclear obliteration and was retooled to promote non-weaponized nuclear energy. With no need for more weapons today, he says thorium is worth revisiting.
- Claimed advantages:
  - Far more abundant than uranium: enough to power the world for a million years at current consumption.
  - Cleaner: uranium reactors use about 0.7% of the fuel with lots of enrichment waste; thorium is a self-contained cycle.
  - Reactors don't need hundreds of atmospheres of pressure.
  - No runaway reaction, for the same reasons it can't be weaponized.
  - About 1/10 the cost of other nuclear: roughly $250 million per plant versus $1.1 billion for uranium.
  - Together, roughly an order of magnitude better than what is currently possible.
- Scoring against the ten questions, thorium gets about 6 of 10 immediately, versus 0 for most cleantech:
  - Mimesis: solar is hot, thorium isn't.
  - Secret: thorium was underexplored for political reasons.
  - Not incremental.
  - Durability: an order of magnitude cheaper means you could own the energy market.
  - Timing seems right.
  - Financing: about $250 million raised over several years against milestones seems reasonable.
- Still unsolved: market, team, distribution and luck, plus regulation and coordination. Open questions include which countries to target and whether any nuclear engineers are left to hire. Solve these before moving forward to maximize mastery over luck.
- His other IT ideas: use software to shift daytime power consumption (smart appliances and thermostats, since prices swing during the day) and transport tech like self-driving cars and defeating traffic. He calls the Internet cleantech's "closest cousin": eBay is a recycling company, Amazon cuts suburban sprawl, Airbnb cuts hotel construction.

## Government: sell, take, or replace

- Technology's relationship with government takes three forms: selling to it, being subsidized by it, or replacing it. All are hard. VCs dislike government-dependent companies because selling to government is difficult, large subsidies are harder still, and the government objects to being replaced, so keep such a plan secret.
- The U.S. budget deficit runs about 10% of GDP. Optimistic projections reach about 2%; less optimistic ones reach 6-7% and rise again after 2020. Peter calls this a secret hidden in plain sight that nobody knows what to do about, so we're not allowed to discuss it.
- The implication is to avoid relying on subsidies, since there probably won't be money when you need it.
- Contrast SpaceX with Solyndra. SpaceX sells technology to government and may replace parts of it now that the government has decommissioned its own rocket programs. Its risk is government running out of money, but that is minimal next to the risk of relying on heavy subsidization.
- His anchor for the future of energy: the optimistic determinate quadrant. Ask the same questions as for Internet businesses: "What can be done that's better and cheaper? Can you do more for less?" That is "the classic definition of technology." First find the big breakthrough, then improve it incrementally. "The first step, as usual, is to think big and think boldly about the future."
