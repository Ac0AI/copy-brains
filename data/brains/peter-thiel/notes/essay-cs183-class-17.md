## Why AI is a huge, strange space, and why the timing and opportunity favor it

- Peter argues the range of possible AI is far larger than the range of human or even alien intelligence. Evolution can't produce "supersonic birds with titanium wings," but AI is built, not evolved, so it isn't limited by gradual natural selection.
- Peter's spectrum example: a mouse, a moron, and Einstein. People assume AI lands just past Einstein, but he sees no a priori reason the scale can't go much higher. It "might be easier for a mouse to understand the relativity than it is for us to actually understand how an AI supercomputer thinks."
- A future with AI would be unlike any other future. Biotech yields humans who function better but are still recognizably human. A retrofuture resurrects old ideas. AI could be radically different and strange.
- He draws a theological parallel: God may have been to the Middle Ages what AI will become to us. Will it be all-powerful, will it love us? Incomprehensible questions, but still worth asking.

## Luddite vs. Ricardo: does AI replace or complement people?

- The history of technology is largely a history of technology displacing people (plow, printing press, cotton gin). Peter sees two paradigms.
- The Luddite paradigm: machines are bad, destroy them before they destroy you.
- The Ricardo paradigm: technology is fundamentally good. Per Ricardo's gains from trade, displaced people are freed up to do more. If China makes cheaper cars, buying them is good for the US; Detroit workers lose jobs but can be retrained, and total value rises.
- Applied to AI: if humans and computers are good at very different things, they trade and AI is a complement, not a substitute.
- Caveat: this depends on the relative magnitude of advantage. If AI is dramatically better ("3000x what humans can do across everything"), it may make no sense for it to trade with us. "Humans, after all, don't trade with monkeys or mice." In extreme cases there is something to the Luddite view.
- Peter reframes this as control. Most technology gives people more control over the world with no cliff. AI may hit an inflection point where it goes superhuman and humans lose control altogether. He jokes of a cloud supercomputer that "calls itself Zeus and throws down lightning bolts at people."
- Peter also notes the popular concern has shifted from intelligent computers to empathetic ones: a computer that doesn't find human eye movement emotionally provocative is seen as inferior, "like Vulcans."

## Is it too early for AI? Timing risk and the consensus trap

- Peter says timing matters a lot here, and it may still be too early. Futures fail often: 1970s supersonic airplanes (too noisy), 1990s handheld iPad-like devices, 1999 smartphones. Siri is "probably still a bit too early today." Whether the timing is right is hard to know ex ante.
- The conventional view is to pick biotech 2.0 over AI, since bioinformatics is being applied to humans sooner while AI applications are further out. Peter notes "the conventional view isn't always right."
- At a "5 Top VCs, 10 Tech Trends" event in Santa Clara, his prediction that biology would become an information science got 100% agreement from the audience, with no dissenter. His reaction: "Perhaps that should make us nervous. Unanimity in crowds can be very disconcerting."
- The prediction that all cars would go electric drew 92% disagreement. Peter's quip: there are many reasons to be bearish on electric cars, "but now there is one less."
- On whether Moore's law would continue to accelerate, the audience split 50-50. Peter's point: if computing can more than double every 18 months, something like AI would seem to arrive within a few years, yet most people thought AI was much further off than biotech.

## Hidden limits: exponential hope vs. asymptotic reality

- Peter's test for comparing fields is whether there are serious, possibly hidden, limits in each.
- Biotech example: telomerase lets cells split without bound, which stops aging, but unbounded splitting looks like cancer. Aging and cancer may cancel each other out: fix one problem and you create another. It's unclear whether such barriers exist or can be overcome.
- AI's leading candidate for an invisible barrier is code complexity. Past a point there may be so much code that no one knows what's going on and debugging becomes impossible. He suggests something like this happened to Microsoft Windows over decades: "It used to be elegant."
- The fundamental tension he names: "exponential hope versus asymptotic reality." The question is whether and when the asymptote sets in.
- He also raises the track-record worry: after a long history of small advances (e.g. the War on Cancer, 40 years in), things may just be harder than people think. People in the 1980s thought AI was around the corner. He asks how we know AI isn't the same, and likens it to waiting for luggage at the airport, where the probability of arrival rises each minute until it eventually drops.

## Three reasons AI beats biotech 2.0

Peter gives three distinct advantages to focusing on AI:

1. **Engineering freedom.** Biology is a recipe, not a blueprint: "add food and water and bake for 9 months." If the cake gets messed up, the cookbook rarely tells you how to fix it. AI is much more of a true blueprint, less dependent on a precise sequence of steps, so you can build from scratch in different ways.
2. **Regulatory freedom.** Biotech is heavily regulated: about 10 years and $1.3 billion to develop a new drug, with 4,000 people at the FDA and many precautionary principles. AI is an unregulated frontier. You can launch as fast as you can build software, it may cost $1 million or a few million rather than $1 billion, and you can work from your basement. Synthesizing Ebola or smallpox there would get you in trouble; hacking on AI would not. Maybe the legislature "simply has no mind for AI-kind of things."
3. **Underexplored and contrarian.** On a 2x2 of underexplored vs. heavily explored and consensus vs. contrarian, biotech 2.0 sits in the heavily explored, consensus quadrant, "which, of course, is the worst quadrant." AI is underexplored and contrarian. After decades of talk without delivery, many people have turned pessimistic and moved on, which could be very good for those who do focus on it.

- Peter adds a joke that PayPal, at Luke Nosek's urging, offered cryogenics in its employee benefits ($50k neuro, $120k full body), but the sales rep couldn't get a dot matrix printer to print the policies. Maybe the way to make biotech work is to push harder on AI.

## Intelligence augmentation: Palantir and the PayPal fraud model

- Peter frames Palantir's approach as intelligence augmentation, not strong AI, falling squarely within the Ricardo gains-from-trade paradigm. The key is the right balance between human and computer.
- His view on counter-terrorism: the best way to stop terrorists isn't regression analysis on past behavior to predict the next move. A more game-theoretic approach is better.
- His PayPal parallel on fraud: humans couldn't solve it because there were millions of transactions; computers couldn't solve it because the fraud patterns kept changing. Having the computer do the heavy computation and humans do the final analysis, "while a weaker form of AI, turns out to be optimal in these cases."
- He describes the three companies in the room as taking different routes: Vicarious (brain-inspired principles, starting with a vision system, then general intelligence), Prior Knowledge (Bayesian methods for patterns and causation in large datasets, deliberately unlike human reasoning), and Palantir (augmentation).

## Peter's pressure-test questions for AI founders

In the guest conversation, only these lines are Peter's. The questions he asks show what he looks for in an AI startup:

- **Timing:** "Why is *now* the time to be doing strong AI as opposed to 10-15 years from now?"
- **Approach:** why is brain emulation (or, for Bob, augmentation instead of strong AI) the right approach?
- **Time horizon:** being 5-7 years from the goal is one thing; 15-20 years or beyond is quite another.
- **Recruiting:** AI differs from the web and mobile apps most engineers gravitate toward, so how do you hire?
- **Secrecy and competition:** "Are there other people who are working on this too? If so, how many, and if not, how do you know?" He adds that it's a bad idea to open a pizza restaurant in Palo Alto even if you're first, because others will come, so what is the strategy for avoiding competition?
- **Danger:** how dangerous is the technology?
- **Science project risk:** is the team developing technology looking for a problem, with no applications yet?
- **Track record:** how do you know AI isn't another long history of undelivered expectations?
- On convincing people to accept human-computer collaboration over 100% human or 100% computer, which people usually frame as antagonistic, he asks Bob how to persuade academics and Google-type researchers that Palantir's paradigm is better.

## Long-failed ventures and the pivot to the New World

- Peter's example of a long-lived failure: Xanadu, which tried from 1963 to 1992 to connect all the world's computers. It ran out of money and died, and Netscape arrived the very next year and ushered in the Internet.
- He tells the "probably apocryphal" Columbus story: everyone thought the world was much smaller than it was and they were heading to China. When the crew wanted to turn back, Columbus convinced them to postpone mutiny for three more days, and they landed on a new continent.
- The lesson he draws is about persistence and timing under uncertainty: sometimes the breakthrough lands just after a long stretch of seeming failure, but, as with futures that arrived too early, it's hard to know in advance which case you're in.
