## Why investors must bet on globalization

- Peter treats the "secular apocalypse" as any world where capitalism fails: runaway technological disaster, or wars and crises so disruptive they push the developed world toward fascism or anarchy.
- He says asking directly how to price the apocalypse leads to "paradoxes, absurdities, or at best money-losing investment schemes." A mutual fund manager gains nothing from pondering thermonuclear war, because there would be no funds left.
- So he runs an inverse thought experiment: what must happen for there to be no apocalypse, and which sectors do surprisingly well if the world stays intact?
- He warns that thought experiments cannot be falsified. They "exist largely in the mind."
- He ties the apocalypse to nuclear proliferation: 1 nuclear power in 1945, 5 at the Cold War's height, 7 known by 2008, and perhaps 20 by 2020 or 50 by 2050. He says "there exists some point beyond which there is no stable equilibrium."

## Globalization is divergent, and anti-globalization is incoherent

- Peter argues globalization is far from complete: trade barriers remain, nation-states persist, and the wage gap between a Detroit and a Shenzhen car worker is vast. Yet by trade share, migration and connectivity it matches the 1913 peak.
- Because the world is far from a stable equilibrium, the future will be "much more or much less globalist than the present."
- The good path is narrow. Its width is set by the spread of destructive technology and the difficulty of improving human nature.
- Every version of anti-globalization is incoherent. It "cannot be a global political agenda," since global anti-globalization gatherings dissolve into self-contradiction or become cover for a bad version, such as worldwide communist revolution.
- Even North Korea participates, exporting heroin, ballistic missiles and counterfeit currency to import cognac, German cars and prostitutes.
- Globalization can end only by accident or miscalculation, meaning world war. With no winners, every path away from it ends in catastrophe, so "investors have no choice but to bet on globalization."
- He notes this is only the necessary condition. Identifying which version of globalization saves the world is the harder part, which he leaves between the lines.

## Bubbles as miscalculations about globalization

- Peter's thesis: "Almost every financial bubble has involved nothing more nor less than a serious miscalculation about the true probability of successful globalization."
- 1720 France: John Law's Mississippi Company, backed by New World proceeds, approached the value of all France at its peak. Louisiana then had only a few thousand Frenchmen, mostly criminals. France's finances never recovered, and the next fix, the 1789 tax increase, triggered revolution.
- 1720 Britain: the South Sea Company aimed to open the South Seas to trade. Spain's Philip V refused to open its South American ports, and the bubble burst. Parliament banned joint stock companies for about a hundred years, and the British market did not regain its 1720 level until Waterloo in 1815.
- Later cases: 19th-century railroads, pre-1914 Russia, and the 1920s boom in cars and radio. RCA in 1929 was "the AOL of 1999": the Dow fell about 88 percent while RCA fell over 97 percent.
- 1928's Kellogg-Briand treaty, which outlawed war, came near the peak. Smoot-Hawley in 1930 was effectively a global trade war. Global markets bottomed in 1949 when China went Maoist.
- Japan's late-1980s bubble rested on the belief that Japan Inc. might run the planet. The Nikkei was almost half of world market capitalization in 1989, and the land under the Imperial Palace was valued above all of California.
- Recent real estate booms concentrated in cities that will centralize the future world: Shanghai, Dubai, Manhattan and, above all, London.
- Bubbles need great uncertainty, so they form in assets most levered to globalization. He says nobody could start a mania in a utility or a regional business in secular decline.

## The Great Boom: the last bubble or no bubble

- Peter says the last quarter century had more asset bubbles than all prior history combined: Japan, Asia, the internet, real estate, China, Web 2.0, emerging markets, private equity and hedge funds.
- Extremes cited: a 20-standard-deviation derivatives move in 1998; the Nasdaq at 5,000 in 2000, farther from equilibrium than the Dow at 350 in 1929; a Japanese 10-year yield of 0.44 percent in 2003; and the Shanghai A-shares index at a P/E of 62 in 2007.
- He argues these are facets of "a single Great Boom of unprecedented size and duration," with globalization retold through different markets.
- This time, globalization either brings freedom and prosperity "that can scarcely be imagined," or capitalism and perhaps humanity end. "The real alternative to good globalization is world war."
- So the Great Boom "either is not a bubble at all, or it is the final and greatest bubble in history."
- Pricing logic: if a share is worth $100 with successful globalization and the odds are 1 in 10, it is theoretically worth $10. But in every world where investors survive it is worth $100, so they may rationally pay up to $100.
- The mirror image is selling catastrophe insurance and reinsurance. He notes Buffett's shift at Berkshire from "value" stocks to global insurance and reinsurance, "one of the purest bets on the optimistic thought experiment."
- Extreme valuations are an indirect measure of how narrow the path looks. He argues March 2000 may have been "a peak of clarity": investors saw the Old Economy as doomed and the New Economy as the only chance.
- The same desperation shows up in individuals: the aspiring actor in LA, and Israeli entrepreneurs building companies where everything could be destroyed overnight. He links the subprime boom to tens of millions heading toward retirement "in an actuarially bankrupt state," forced to gamble on housing.

## Real versus fake: China, technology and hedge funds

- Peter's test for any globalization bet is the difference between the real thing and the financial label, which he marks in quotes: China vs. "China," technology vs. "technology," hedge funds vs. "hedge funds."
- China: he calls its rise the most important political trend of the new millennium, projecting it as the leading economic power in about 20 years at 10 percent annual growth.
  - It benefits the world only while deflationary. If China consumes much more energy, savings on cheap goods turn into costlier gasoline. He estimates Chinese oil use at one-fourth the U.S. per-capita rate would add almost 20 million barrels a day and could push oil past $200 a barrel.
  - There is no good scenario in which China fails.
  - But the 2007 Chinese market resembled the Nasdaq of early 2000: exponential-growth story, speculation, overvalued or unprofitable companies. The winners of 2014 may be private, tightly controlled firms today, as Google barely registered in early 2000.
  - China is run for insiders, who know when to sell: "'China' will underperform China."
  - A long "China" position is not a bet that financial globalization succeeds. It is a bet that internal contradictions persist, since trapped capital is what fuels the stock market.
  - He adds that even if the 20-year story is true, there was no urgency to buy at the frenzy's peak, just as with the internet in 2000.
- Technology:
  - Web 2.0 companies had almost no IPOs, so deploying substantial capital was nearly impossible, and venture returns face an extreme distribution of outcomes.
  - He quotes Glengarry Glen Ross: "First prize is a Cadillac El Dorado... Second prize is a set of steak knives. Third prize is you're fired." He says internet businesses are global and winner-take-all.
  - A "technology" company that sells pet food online using Super Bowl ads may not be technology at all. "The solutions to hard engineering problems are not necessarily valuable, but it is unusual for the solutions to easy engineering problems to have much value in the long term."
  - Large firms like Microsoft, Dell, IBM, Cisco and HP are effectively "short innovation": they gain from modest change and are threatened by massive innovation. He names Google and Intel as truly innovative.
  - He urges entrepreneurs and investors to return to hard problems, chiefly security, meaning real defense against destructive technologies. He calls for a "New Defense" sector and sees its absence as a sign of Silicon Valley's "complacent myopia."
- Hedge funds:
  - A real hedge fund moves capital from less to more efficient uses. A "hedge fund" just replicates past strategies "until they don't work."
  - "Short low-risk, long high-risk" is not the same as "long good globalization." Overvaluing emerging markets while ignoring corruption fails to reward behavior that makes good globalization likelier.
  - Good globalization needs long time horizons, but subprime credit, payday loans, housing consumption dressed up as investment, and energy depletion are all high-risk trajectories that hurt the Great Boom.
  - He asks where market limits lie: cheap imports vs. indentured labor in Burma, cheaper diamonds vs. a war in Congo, and markets in human organs or "the stuff of the minds of men." There, he says, the optimistic experiment "may have gone terribly wrong."

## Contrarian macro view in 2008

- Peter says "classic wisdom has failed." He argues the failures of LTCM, Soros and Tiger came from not being macro enough, not from tactical blunders. They lacked imagination about radically divergent futures: total collapse or good globalization.
- LTCM's 1998 collapse followed Russia's default, a nuclear-armed oil power going bankrupt, which was an "unimaginable apocalyptic scenario." Panicked central-bank rate cuts then fed the final stage of the 1990s tech bubble.
- He calls it "a world of retail sanity and wholesale madness." The great opportunities lie in the wildly mispriced macro context, not in shrinking spreads on esoteric products.
- What frightens him is "the unwillingness of the best and brightest to try and make any sense" of the larger dimension.
- His contrarian stance: he would be more optimistic if others were less naively optimistic. In every possible future, today's bubbles burst, including Miami condos, negative-interest mortgages and a negative savings rate, and their ideology becomes "lint in the winds of history."
- Near-term, he expects a globalization backlash as bubbles implode and the doctrine stops "literally delivering the goods." He reads the Fed's accommodations as a desperate effort to stave off a recession that would end the globalization debate for years, at the cost of reviving inflation and oil spikes in dollar-linked economies like China and the Middle East.
- A near-term backlash is not the end of the world, but a wholesale rollback "could represent the ultimate catastrophe."
- He says the challenge is to steer between "the Scylla of outdated wisdom and the Charybdis of nihilistic cleverness." The contest will be close, with individual choices decisive. He rejects the reigning faith in efficient markets, calling his own a faith "that seemingly still cannot be named."
