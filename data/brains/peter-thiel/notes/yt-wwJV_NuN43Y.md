## The 2024 election as the collapse of liberalism and the "machine"

- Peter did not expect a close race. He thought the polls still had not corrected their 2016 and 2020 errors, and he expected a solid Trump win. He calls the result a "collapse of liberalism" and of the Democratic party, not just the failure of "a somewhat senile Biden" and "a somewhat goofy Kamala Harris."
- 2016 was Trump against the Bush Republicans, and Hillary Clinton did not take him seriously. In 2024 the Democrats gave it their all. They spent two to three times as much in the final months, prosecuted him and tried to get him off the ballot, and still lost.
- He argues the result refutes "demographics is destiny" and identity politics. Millions of people changed their minds between 2016 and 2024, which means arguments can still move voters. Trump and Vance made a case. The Democrats had "zero substance," so there was nothing to differentiate from Biden.
- His historical claim is that identity politics last worked in 2008, in a pre-internet world, when Obama could tell black voters he was black and white voters he was post-racial. That tailoring no longer works. Making "diverse" concrete means picking one person and alienating everyone else, and the Democrats now seem able to elect only old straight white men.
- On "retroactive continuity," he says a Harris win would have made 2016 the fluke. In his reading, 2020 was the fluke, one last Biden-era win for an old regime.
- He calls the Democratic party a "machine" rather than a cult, because a cult has a leader and this has no individuals. He cites the 2020 primary (Biden fourth or fifth in Iowa and New Hampshire, then rammed through South Carolina) and Harris's replacement of Biden as extremely non-democratic. He feared a Harris win would have made the machine unbeatable and turned the country into a one-party California, worse because people can leave California but not the US.

## Why the elite pipeline stopped producing strong Democrats

- He traces a decline in credentials: Clinton (Yale Law, Rhodes), Obama (Harvard Law) and Hillary Clinton (Yale Law), then Biden (University of Delaware), Harris (Howard, UC Hastings), Walz ("even more mediocre"), Newsom (Santa Clara), Shapiro (Georgetown Law). "There is nothing Elite left."
- He can believe the elite schools are corrupt and still select very smart people. His twist is that a conservative who survives Yale Law, one of perhaps five in a class of 170, is well trained to understand what is wrong with liberalism. A liberal graduate would not defend liberalism any better.
- He suggests Democrats may avoid a truly smart nominee like Pete Buttigieg because a smart person defending the deficits, inflation and open border would be more embarrassing than Harris's word salad.
- He says there is no room for individual thought on the left. In the 1980s and 90s Stanford and Hollywood were liberal but had eccentric professors and candid private conversations. He says that is gone, including "in small groups at dinners behind closed doors."
- He thinks celebrity has lost its mystique, because celebrities now read as left-wing "ditto heads" who are not allowed to say anything individual.
- On the Democrats' prospects, he says "you go bankrupt gradually then suddenly." The 20th-century order (New Deal liberalism, political correctness, the university) lingered as a zombie into the 2000s and 2010s before unraveling.
- He started the Thiel Fellowship in 2010 to pay kids to drop out of college. In 2019 he heard an MIT president recite the same bromides as 2005 and thought nothing would change. He says five years later the collapse has been large.

## The Silicon Valley preference cascade

- He describes a preference cascade. If everyone knows they are lying and knows everyone else knows, the equilibrium is unstable and can flip fast. The Butler assassination attempt and Elon Musk's endorsement gave people cover.
- He says Elon was "incredibly important" and "incredibly courageous," and asks what would have happened to him had Trump lost. Peter has known Elon for almost 25 years, and says for the first 20 he was left of center. Tesla was a clean-energy company and the Republicans were the people who did not believe in climate change.
- His explanation for the shift is that the left's "intellectual straight jacket" is not what Musk, Tulsi Gabbard, RFK Jr. or others signed up for. He says it is never enough to agree with 80%, and you must be 100%.
- His metaphor is the Empire versus a ragtag Rebel Alliance, uncomfortably heterogeneous, with a "teenage Chewbacca," a Princess Leia type and an "autistic C3PO policy wonk."
- He says much of the tech shift was prosaic corporate governance. Founders told him privately how hard it was to manage ideologically deranged millennial employees. In 2017 one big San Francisco founder said he might not be able to take more money from Peter because Peter supported Trump. By 2019 that company's hiring goal was to move headcount out of the Bay Area as fast as possible, because anywhere else is "less woke and less crazy and more productive."
- He explains the ambiguity of failing wokeness. For years the instinct was "a little bit more" wokeness. At some point it was exhausted.
- On his own role, he did not give money to the Trump campaign this time, and says the money would not matter (Trump was "insanely outspent" and won). At Aspen he deliberately said Trump would win by a big margin, which that liberal audience heard as scandalous because "the actual is the ideal" and predicting a win sounds like saying Trump deserves one.

## Stagnation, "Make America Great Again," and the second Trump term

- His fantasy in 2016 was that Trump would force a conversation about stagnation. He calls "Make America Great Again" the most pessimistic slogan any presidential candidate had used in a hundred years, because it starts by saying we are no longer a great country. The first step is to admit that, and then perhaps the country can become great again.
- He lists stagnation problems: slow progress, a broken intergenerational compact, young people finding it much harder to get their footing, and an economy doing worse. He doubts the country was ready for the conversation, least of all the Democrats.
- He had worried about the first term as "crazier and more dangerous" than he expected: prosecutions, people going to jail, a circular firing squad and "uncompensated volatility" for those involved.
- On staffing, he thinks the second term will have a stronger bench. He blames the first term's hires, not just Trump's firings, citing Mattis (a general sitting on Theranos's board) and Bolton, who was picked for agreeing on Iran and disagreed on everything else. Trump, he says, is better prepared and the buck stops with him.
- He cautions that elections are "relativistic" (which candidate do you hate less) and governing is "absolute," so Trump will fall short of the pedestal. He expects problems to be harder than eight years ago: the border, Russia-Ukraine, Iran, China-Taiwan. He says the world feels like it is "sleepwalking to Armageddon."

## Trade, tariffs, immigration and the Georgist housing problem

- He thinks tariffs are directionally a good idea, with nuance. Free trade theory assumed comparable labor standards, regulation and free flow of capital and implied near-equilibrium trade, so a chronic deficit signals something off.
- He argues the deficit has sectoral winners. Wall Street banks are effectively "long the trade deficit," because the dollars China earns get recycled into US assets. In 2006 the roughly $800 billion current account deficit required about $800 billion of "fake" financial products, and the correction fed the 2008 crisis. Losers include Rust Belt swing states (Pennsylvania, Ohio, Michigan, Wisconsin), and fixing that is what JD Vance needs to do to win in 2028.
- He also gives a game-theory case. Threatened tariffs pressure partners to open up. He never wants trade deals negotiated by ideological free traders, who think they need to negotiate nothing.
- On 60% tariffs on China, he thinks it would badly hurt Chinese companies and be only mildly bad for US consumers, since production would shift to places like Vietnam or India rather than back to the US. The iPhone cannot be built economically in the US. A shift to Vietnam, "an evil communist country but not bent on world domination," would suit US geopolitical interests.
- On immigration, he doubts the 11 million deportation figure will literally happen. He is himself an immigrant, born in Germany, and his parents came to the US in 1968. He says Europe's immigration problem is cultural, while the US is still good at assimilation and its challenge is economic.
- He uses Henry George to argue that real estate is inelastic. Add 10% to a city's population and house prices might rise 50% while salaries do not, so the GDP grows but it is a windfall to boomer homeowners and landlords and a hit to young people and the lower middle class. He calls the Anglosphere experience "a Georgist real estate catastrophe" and says rent matters more than the price of eggs.
- His "cloning" thought experiment: 100 clones of Barry Weiss with competing talk shows might be ethically debatable, but the economics are the point. A Bangladeshi Uber driver in New York should not want many more Bangladeshis. He notes that public schools in Los Angeles have to teach in 30 languages and that the open border strained the social fabric (fentanyl, cartels).

## Universities, student debt and bankrupt blue states

- He supports eliminating the Department of Education but says to pick battles. A concrete lever is the overhead charged on NIH grants, which can be reset by executive order. The aim is more money to scientists, less to the "woke administration" and diversity bureaucracy, without defunding universities.
- On student debt, he says it rose from about $300 billion in 2000 to approaching $2 trillion. Graduates of 1997 had mostly paid off by 2009. For the 2009 cohort, the median student with debt owed more in 2021 than at graduation. Debt grew faster than the economic value of degrees, outside a few fields such as computer science. It takes about 12 years to realize it is not working.
- He is somewhat sympathetic to forgiving the debt as realistic level-setting, but not a taxpayer bailout. The hit should fall on bondholders and the colleges. If colleges were liable for unpaid debts, most would go out of business.
- He expects an implosion without Trump needing to act. Illinois, Chicago, New York and possibly San Francisco and Los Angeles are close to bankrupt. COVID spending in 2020 and 2021 was in part a bailout of failed blue cities and states. Trump only has to "sit on his hands," as Ford did with New York City in the 1970s, which was popular with voters. He says "good money after bad" is the thing to refuse.

## Foreign policy: Iran, the two world wars and appeasement

- On Ukraine, he thinks NATO's relentless expansion may not have been a good idea (the US would not want Russian troops in Cuba or Mexico), but that in 2024 the US cannot simply retreat without a route, and Trump does not want a repeat of Afghanistan.
- He distinguishes isolationism, the utopian Bush 43 neocon approach (the 2005 second inaugural was Fantastical) and a realistic middle. He says if the US deferred to Israel on the Middle East it would have a saner and more realistic policy, since the Israelis are realists about Syria and Saudi Arabia and see Iran as the one problem. When he first met Netanyahu in January 2009, a month before he returned as prime minister, the only thing Netanyahu wanted to discuss was Iran.
- His argument against an Iranian bomb is about proliferation and impunity. After the Soviets got the bomb in 1949, the Korean War started in 1950. After China got it in 1964, the Vietnam War exploded in 1965. In both cases the nuclear power could back proxies with impunity, and he fears the same from Iran backing Houthis, Hamas and Hezbollah. He adds that Israel and the Saudis had misgivings about the Iraq War because weakening Iraq strengthens Iran.
- He expects Rubio, Waltz and Stefanik to be hawkish on China and to prioritize China more than Biden or the Europeans would.
- His history of the 20th century: the two world wars teach opposite lessons. World War II says do not appease. World War I says do not have entangling alliances with hair-trigger escalation. To avoid World War III you must learn both. His contrarian weighting is about 60-70% World War I and 30-40% World War II, because we always fight the last war, and a World War III would look like gradually escalating conflagration that could happen overnight.
- Pre-1914 was also a deeply connected, globalized world. Norman Angell's bestselling 1910 book The Great Illusion argued that a world war made no sense economically, as little as London invading Hertfordshire, and it happened anyway. He says to ask where Angell was wrong and why the "great illusion" was not one.
- Asked about the far-right revival of Buchanan-style revisionism around World War II, he says he would never defend Hitler and thinks it is mostly a left-wing talking point. He adds that a self-fulfilling nihilism is a bad bet for any individual, since a white supremacist who believes white people are all losers will "eat a lot of donuts and move into a trailer."

## Science, skepticism, populism and the internet

- His framework: science began as a two-front war against both excessive dogmatism and excessive skepticism. Today scientists can list the Skeptics (on climate change, vaccines, Darwin) but cannot name one place science is too dogmatic. He says that shows lost balance and that "science" in scare quotes is now more dogmatic than the 17th-century Catholic Church.
- On vaccines, he does not believe they lead to autism, but doubts current science could find it out if they did, because results would be suppressed. Autism has risen dramatically with no good explanation, and the question should be askable. He thinks it was a mistake to suppress COVID skepticism prematurely, since the Skeptics were right on the lab origin and the bat-market "politically correct Theory."
- He admits there is a point where skepticism goes too far, but says directionally institutions have been very unhealthy. He sees RFK Jr. and Joe Rogan as fresher than the consensus echo chamber, even if not right about everything.
- His NSA analogy: the DMV and post office are transparently dysfunctional, but the NSA is more likely badly mismanaged because no one can see in. He applies this to science: humanities corruption is visible, and the esoteric sciences (no progress curing cancer in 50 years, string theory) may be worse. He cites the fired Stanford president Marc Tessier-Lavigne's alleged decades of fraud as harder to see than Harvard's plagiarism case.
- On populism, he calls populism and democracy a "Russell conjugate": synonyms that are emotional antonyms, where it is democracy if people vote right and populism if they vote wrong. He says he shares concerns about both, since rampant majoritarianism tramples minorities and property. The US checks it with republicanism and the Constitution.
- His revision is that the US is not too populist. It has become less of a constitutional republic, replaced by an unelected technocratic bureaucracy or "deep state," which an advanced technological society with secrets partly requires.
- On the internet, he is hesitant to make everything about tech, since making it omnipotent makes it a scapegoat. He thinks it made things transparent and that markets get more efficient with transparency while scapegoating works less. Trump was "the first internet president" because the internet exposed how fake other politicians were.
- He argues no science fiction anticipated the internet's small-packet, 140-character modality, which hints at a deeper change in human consciousness. He still doubts how much it added to GDP, and says people are "embedded in the atomic world," so a shiny iPhone does not replace a decrepit subway.
- He recommends Martin Gurri's The Revolt of the Public, which predicted the dynamics without mentioning Trump. He cites Fukuyama's The End of History as the book that got it wrong. He says history did not end, with 9/11, the rise of China and Xi becoming president for life in 2017, and that human agency still matters.
