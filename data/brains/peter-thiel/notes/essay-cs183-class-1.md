## Retail sanity, wholesale madness: the "0 to 1" framing of progress

- Peter describes the world as having "retail sanity, but wholesale madness": details are well understood, the big picture is not. The challenge in business and life is to integrate micro and macro into one coherent narrative.
- He notes humanities majors learn about the world without career skills, while engineers learn technical detail without knowing why, how, or where to apply it.
- The best students, workers and thinkers join those questions into a single story.

## Horizontal vs. vertical progress: globalization vs. technology

- Peter splits progress into two kinds. Horizontal/extensive progress means copying things that work; its one-word name is "globalization". Vertical/intensive progress means doing new things; its one-word name is "technology".
- Intensive progress is going from 0 to 1, as opposed to the 1 to n of globalization.
- Example: in 50 years China will be a lot like the US now. Cities, cars and rail systems get copied, maybe with some steps skipped.
- Most people focus on globalization over technology. Speaking of "developed" vs. "developing" nations is implicitly bearish about technology, because it assumes convergence on the "developed" status quo. Society believes in a technological end of history "almost by default".
- The two interact. If 1 to n is blocked by resource constraints (not everyone can have a car without environmental catastrophe), only 0 to 1 solutions help. Technology matters "even if all we really care about is globalization".

## Technological stagnation after the 1960s

- Peter says that for most of recent history, from the late 17th-century steam engine to about the late 1960s, technological progress was tremendous, perhaps relentless.
- The industrial revolution changed how people make money: from taking it from others (plunder) to trade. Of perhaps 100 billion people ever born, most lived in essentially stagnant societies where success meant claiming value, not creating it.
- The 1960s were the peak of optimism: people believed in and thought about the future, and were confident the next 50 years would bring unprecedented progress.
- With the exception of computers, it didn't happen. Per capita incomes still rise but the rate is decelerating, and median wages have been stagnant since 1973.
- He compares the result to Alice in Wonderland: people run harder (longer hours) just to stay in place.
- Computers are the exception: Moore's/Kryder's/Wirth's laws held, and hardware plus agile development are a model for other industries. That is why CS is the logical place to start recapturing progress.

## Why 0 to 1 can't be taught, and the limits of case studies

- Peter argues that teaching innovation is almost a contradiction in terms, because education is about 1 to n: observe, imitate, repeat. Infants learn existing languages rather than invent new ones.
- Crossing T's and dotting I's (incorporation, pitching VCs) gets you maybe 30% of the way. Then you must do something important and do it right, which can't be taught.
- Echoing Tolstoy: all successful companies are different, having solved 0 to 1 in different ways; all failed companies are the same, having botched it.
- Case studies of PayPal and Facebook are of limited use because it's hard to know what was path-dependent. The next great company may not be an e-payments or social network company. The business school case method is "more mythical than helpful".
- Exceptionalism is a problem for anyone doing something new, who must ask "am I sane? Or am I crazy?" Example: some 20,000 people move to Los Angeles each year to become famous actors, and very few do. Startups are probably less plagued by this than Hollywood, but not immune.

## Determinism vs. indeterminism: the calculus metaphor

- In 1 to n you can analyze statistically. In 0 to 1 it isn't a statistical question: "the standard deviation with a sample size of 1 is infinite."
- Society thinks about the future statistically and probabilistically, as random. If markets follow a random walk, there's no point out-calculating them.
- Peter offers calculus as the alternative metaphor: figuring out exactly what will happen. Apollo had to know where the moon would be and whether the rocket had enough fuel. "No one would want to ride in a statistically, probabilistically-informed spaceship."
- Startups are like the space program: going 0 to 1 has to favor determinism over indeterminism.
- The practical problem: people who claim to know the future are called prophets, and "in our society, all prophets are false prophets". Steve Jobs finessed the line; people sensed he was a visionary but he didn't go too far, and he "probably cut it as close as possible (and succeeded accordingly)".
- Separating luck from skill is difficult or impossible and invites fallacious reasoning. Entrepreneurs should at least have a handle on the question.

## Four theories of the future of intensive progress

- Convergence: growth rose quickly after the industrial revolution, then technology decelerates and growth becomes asymptotic.
- Cyclical: advances and retrenchments repeat. Probably true historically, but hard to believe now; losing all accumulated know-how "strains credulity".
- Collapse/destruction: some technological advance does us in.
- Singularity: technological development produces an AI or intellectual event horizon.
- People overestimate the likelihood and explanatory power of convergence and cyclical theories, and so probably underestimate destruction and singularity.

## Why companies, why startups: coordination costs and small teams

- Peter applies the Coase Theorem: companies exist because they optimally balance internal and external coordination costs. As an entity grows, internal costs rise and external costs fall.
- Totalitarian government is the extreme: external coordination costs are zero, but internal coordination is hard (Hayek and the Austrians showed central planning doesn't work). Independent contractors are the opposite extreme: zero internal costs, but paralyzing external costs. Firm size is finding the right mix.
- Above about 100 people, employees don't all know each other, politics matter, incentives change, and signaling work can matter more than doing it. These costs are almost always underestimated.
- Red flags: professional investors should and do reconsider before investing in companies with more than one office; even a multi-floor office can cause coordination problems. Hiring consultants and outsourcing key development are also serious red flags.
- Path's limit of 150 "friends" is illustrative, and ancient tribes apparently had a natural size limit near that number.
- If a business's size and complexity scale like the square of the number of people, startups are uniquely positioned to lower internal costs and get things done.
- A computer couldn't model all of a company's costs even if it modeled the economics, since it would have to model human irrationality, emotion and interaction.

## Why do a startup, the costs of failure, and where to start

- Peter's negative answer: you can't develop new technology in existing entities. Big companies, governments and nonprofits can't recognize financial needs (the federal government over- and under-compensates), and can't give personal recognition, respect or fame. Anyone on a 0 to 1 mission needs to be surrounded by others who want the same, which happens in startups.
- On motives: money alone is a weak reason. Research shows happiness rises with income only up to about $70,000 per year. Fame is questionable. A better motive is a desire to change the world (he points to the US in 1776-79 as a startup of sorts). Culture matters: in Japan entrepreneurs are seen as reckless and the respectable path is lifelong employment. He raises the line "behind every fortune lies a great crime" and asks whether founders are criminals.
- Financial losses from a startup's lower pay aren't that high. Non-financial costs are higher: a failed startup may teach nothing useful, or teach you to fail again and become more risk-averse. "You aren't a lottery ticket," so failure isn't just 1 of n tries.
- A failed 0 to 1 startup has low financial and low non-financial costs; you'll learn a lot. A 1 to n startup has especially low financial costs but higher non-financial ones. Example: if "Groupon for Madagascar" fails, "it's not clear where exactly you are."
- Three starting questions: What is valuable? What can I do? What is nobody else doing? In academia the cardinal sin is plagiarism, not triviality; in business, valuable output is what counts.
- Intellectual version: "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" Business version: "What valuable company is nobody building?"
- Test: an answer like "our educational system is broken and urgently requires repair" is wrong because many agree with it. Right answers take the form: "Most people believe in X. But the truth is !X."
