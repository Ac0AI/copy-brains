## The core formula: value created vs. value captured

- Peter says a business is valuable when two things hold: it creates X dollars of value for the world, and it captures Y% of X.
- He stresses that X and Y are independent variables. X can be huge with Y tiny, and a modest X with a decent Y still makes a big business.
- Airlines vs. Google search: US domestic airline revenue was about $195 billion in 2012 versus just over $50 billion for Google. Airlines still matter more to most people.
- Airline margins are thin and "the entire 100-year history of the airline industry the cumulative profits in the US have been approximately zero." Airlines went bankrupt episodically and were recapitalized.
- The market cap of the whole US airline industry was roughly a quarter of Google's. Search is much smaller than air travel but much more valuable, because of different Y.

## Two kinds of business: perfect competition or monopoly

- Peter holds a binary view: "there are exactly two kinds of businesses in this world": perfectly competitive businesses and monopolies. There is "shockingly little that is in between."
- Perfect competition is easy to model, efficient in a static world, and politically favored. But in hyper-competitive businesses you often don't make money, because all the capital gets competed away.
- He treats capitalism and competition as antonyms. A capitalist accumulates capital; perfect competition destroys it.
- Monopolies are more stable, longer-term businesses with more capital. Creating a new monopoly is a symptom of having created something really valuable.
- He calls this the most important business idea people don't understand, because people constantly lie about the nature of their business.

## How companies lie about their market

- Monopolists pretend not to be monopolies, to avoid regulation. Non-monopolists pretend to be monopolies, to differentiate and attract capital. So the apparent difference is small while the real difference is large.
- A non-monopolist describes its market as the intersection of small categories, so it looks like the only player. A monopolist describes its market as the union of big, different markets, so it looks competitive.
- Non-monopoly examples: "the only British food restaurant in Palo Alto" (too narrow; nobody eats only British food and people can drive to Mountain View). Hollywood pitches that mash genres, like a football star joining elite hackers to catch the shark that killed his friend. Startup pitches that stack mobile, social and other buzzwords.
- His verdict on these: "the something of somewhere is really mostly just the nothing of nowhere," like "the Stanford of North Dakota."
- Google example: with about a 66% share of search, it rarely calls itself a search engine. It says it's an advertising company: search ads are about $17 billion inside a far bigger ad market, global advertising is close to $500 billion, so it's about 3.5% of that. Or it says it's a technology company in a roughly $1 trillion market, competing with car companies, Apple, Facebook, Microsoft and Amazon.
- Evidence of real monopoly power in tech: Apple, Google, Microsoft and Amazon keep piling up cash with very high margins, so much they don't know what to do with it.
- When evaluating an idea, he asks: "what is the actual market," not the story told about it, since you can always tell a fictional story about a market that's much bigger or smaller.

## Start with a small market and expand

- Counterintuitive rule: go after a small market first. Monopoly means a large share of a market, and the way to get there is to take over a really small market, then expand in concentric circles.
- Going after a giant market on day one is a mistake. It usually means you haven't defined the category correctly and there will be too much competition.
- Amazon started as a bookstore with all the books in the world, then expanded into other e-commerce. eBay started with Pez dispensers, moved to Beanie Babies, then auctions for all kinds of goods.
- PayPal started with eBay power sellers, about 20,000 people. At the time (December 1999 to January 2000) they looked like terrible customers, "just people selling junk on the internet." PayPal built a much better product for them, reached roughly 25-30% market penetration in 2-3 months, got walk-in traffic and brand recognition, and built from there.
- Facebook started with 10,000 people at Harvard and went from 0 to 60% market share in 10 days. Business school analysis would call that market too small to have value, and it would have had little value had it stayed small. The value came from growing it concentrically.
- Counterexample: clean tech in 2005 to 2008. Many things went wrong, but almost every PowerPoint started with the energy market worth hundreds of billions or trillions. "Once you're sort of a minnow in a vast ocean" you have tons of competitors and don't even know who they all are.
- Be a one-of-a-kind company in a small ecosystem. Don't be "the fourth online pet food company," "the 10th thin film solar panel company," or "the 100th restaurant in Palo Alto." A large existing market usually means tons of competition and is very hard to differentiate in.

## Characteristics of a monopoly business

- There's no single formula, and "every moment happens only once." The next Zuckerberg won't build a social network, the next Larry Page won't build a search engine, the next Bill Gates won't build an operating system. Copying them means not learning from them.
- Tolstoy inverted: "all happy companies are different because they're doing something very unique; all unhappy companies are alike because they fail to escape the essential sameness that is competition."
- Proprietary technology: his rough, somewhat arbitrary rule of thumb is a technology an order of magnitude (10x) better than the next best thing. Amazon had over 10 times as many books. PayPal moved money in a fraction of the 7-10 days a check took to clear on eBay, which he puts at about 10 times as fast. The iPhone was the first smartphone that worked.
- Network effects: they make monopolies stronger over time, but are very hard to get started. The tricky question is why it's valuable to the first user.
- Economies of scale: high fixed costs and low marginal costs are typical of a monopoly-like business. Software is especially good here because its marginal cost is zero.
- Branding: he says he never quite understands how it works and never invests in companies where it's all about branding, but he thinks it's a real phenomenon that creates value.
- Software also gets adoption fast. If a small market has slow adoption, others have time to enter; with fast adoption you can take over the market before they do.
- Google has all four: ad-network effects, the PageRank algorithm as an order-of-magnitude lead (somewhat weaker now), economies of scale in storing sites, and brand. He gives it "three and a half out of four" today.

## Be the last mover and prize durability

- Silicon Valley loves the first mover. Peter says you want to be the last mover, the last company in a category. Microsoft was the last operating system for decades, Google is the last search engine, and Facebook will be valuable if it turns out to be the last social network.
- Most of a company's value sits far in the future. When he did a discounted cash flow for PayPal in March 2001, about 27 months in, growth was 100% a year and the discount rate about 30%. About three quarters of the value came from cash flows in 2011 and beyond.
- He says the same math applies to Airbnb, Twitter, Facebook and the YC companies: roughly 75-85% of the value comes from cash flows in years 2024 and beyond.
- Hence "we always overvalue growth rates and we undervalue durability." Growth is measurable now; whether a company is still around in a decade dominates the value and is more qualitative.
- Each monopoly trait has a time dimension. Network effects tend to strengthen as the network scales. Proprietary technology is trickier: you need to explain why yours will be the last breakthrough, or improve fast enough that nobody catches up.
- Disk drives in the 1980s: a better drive could take over the world and be replaced two years later. 15 years of vast improvement benefited consumers but not the founders.
- A future full of other people's innovation on your problem is great for society but "not that good for your business."
- Chess analogy: the first mover (white) has only about a one-third pawn advantage. He quotes Capablanca: "you must begin by studying the end game." The question to ask is why a company will still be the leader 10, 15 or 20 years out.

## Why inventors often capture nothing, and what does work

- Because X and Y are independent, great innovations often reward nobody. He says Y has been about 0% across the board in science. Einstein created special and general relativity and didn't become a millionaire. Scientists are "duded into thinking that they live in a just universe that will reward them."
- Railroads were hugely valuable but mostly went bankrupt from too much competition. The Wright brothers flew the first plane and made no money.
- Textile mills in the first Industrial Revolution (roughly 1780 to 1850) improved efficiency about 5-7% a year for 60-70 years. Yet in 1850 most British wealth was still held by the landed aristocracy. Competition among hundreds of factory owners competed it all away.
- He sees only two broad categories in the last 250 years where innovators made money. First: complex, vertically integrated monopolies, like Ford and Standard Oil in the Second Industrial Revolution. They need intricate coordination and are capital intensive, and he says they're done surprisingly rarely today.
- His example is Elon Musk at Tesla and SpaceX. He doesn't see a single 10x breakthrough in batteries or rockets. The strength is integrating many pieces, more vertically than competitors.
- Tesla integrated car distribution so distributors couldn't capture the money, as happened in the rest of the US car industry. SpaceX pulled in subcontractors, whereas big aerospace firms use single-source subcontractors that charge monopoly profits and make it hard for the integrators to make money.
- He calls vertical integration an underexplored mode of technological progress. The second category is software, with its economies of scale, zero marginal cost and fast adoption (bits versus atoms).
- He warns against rationalizations. "Scientists aren't interested in money" may obscure that Y is 0%. And "Twitter people made billions, so it must be worth more than anything Einstein did" confuses X and Y.

## Why competition is a psychological trap

- "Competition is for losers" is provocative because we think losers are bad at competing. Peter asks whether competition itself is the problem, a psychological blind spot as well as an intellectual one.
- Humans are deeply mimetic. "Ape" meant both primate and imitate in Shakespeare's time. We take competition as validation, but "when lots of people are trying to do something that is often proof of insanity."
- Example: about 20,000 people a year move to Los Angeles to become movie stars and about 20 make it. He thinks the Olympics are slightly better because you find out quickly whether you're good.
- He quotes Kissinger on Harvard faculty fights: "the battles were so ferocious because the stakes were so small." When objective differences are small, people compete ferociously to maintain an imagined difference.
- Personal story: he was "hypertracked" through Stanford and Stanford Law, then a big New York law firm where outsiders wanted in and insiders wanted out. He left after seven months and 3 days. A colleague told him: "I had no idea that it was possible to escape from Alcatraz."
- Competing does make you better at what you compete on, but at a price: you stop asking what is truly important and valuable. His closing line: don't rush through "the tiny little door that everyone's trying to rush through," and go around the corner to the "vast gate that no one's taking."

## Q&A: lean startups, risk and how to avoid the herd

- Telling monopoly from competition: ask what the real, objective market is, ignoring the narrative, and remember people have incentives to distort it.
- On lean startup and iteration, Peter says he is "personally quite skeptical." Great companies usually made a Quantum Improvement, not surveys. Their founders were "not that easily deterred by what other people thought," and he says some had mild forms of Asperger's. He argues we're too focused on iteration and not enough on figuring things out ourselves.
- On risk: if you spend the time to mitigate it by finding out what people want, "you often will have missed the boat." He also notes a law-school track looks low-risk but may be high-risk for failing to do something meaningful, so risk is a complicated concept.
- First mover vs. last mover: Google wasn't the first search engine but was dramatically better on one dimension (automated PageRank). Facebook wasn't the first social network. Reid Hoffman's SocialNet (1997) imagined "I'd be a dog and you'd be a cat" in a virtual space, while Facebook was first with real identity.
- Palantir: it started in the small intelligence-community market with a proprietary human-computer synthesis approach, rather than the dominant paradigm of substituting computers for humans.
- Mobile payments copycats (Square, PayPal and others) differentiate by shape, "one is a triangle, one is a square."
- On the herd instinct, he cites a Harvard Business School study of an extroverted, low-conviction cohort who "systematically end up doing the wrong thing": chasing Milken in 1989 (a year or two before he went to jail), tech only at the 1999-2000 bubble peak, then housing and private equity in 2005-2007.
- He offers no easy fix. His starting point is to never underestimate the problem, because we assume it afflicts other people, the way we think ads only work on others. He says it afflicts all of us "to a very profound degree."
