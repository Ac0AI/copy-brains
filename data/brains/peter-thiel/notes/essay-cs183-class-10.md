## Technology futures: software eating the world and the 2x2 of competition

Peter frames the Internet as a "Wild West" frontier of the past 20 years: few rules, room to do almost anything. He notes the world of stuff has been heavily regulated for 40 years while the world of bits has not, so computers and finance were the best places to be. Finance got too innovative and regulators noticed, so there is probably less to do there now.

- He flags quieter threats than PIPA and SOPA, chiefly software patents.
- Absent regulation, no one can shut you down just because you are small. Patents do exactly that.
- Big companies with economies of scale can afford such regulation or skirt it; small companies cannot.

Peter Thiel's patent-fight view: in litigation there are four parties, the two sides and their two sets of lawyers, and lawyers are almost always scared of losing, so defense lawyers talk clients into settling.
- High litigation costs can be worth it if you only have to fight a few times.
- The danger is fighting and winning without setting a deterring precedent, so the suits keep coming and you are worse off.
- Patent problems are ones you want to have: no one would come after you without good technology.

## Software eating the world: which sectors are next

Peter agrees with the thesis and lists sectors already eaten: telephone directories, journalism, accounting brokerages, and arguably music. Incumbents often miss it. The New York Times declared in 2002 that the Internet was over, and the record industry cheered when it took down Napster. "Those celebrations were premature."

Candidates he names for being eaten next:
- Healthcare: better EMR technology, analytics and transparency, but heavy regulation and bureaucracy.
- Education: many attempts to computerize and automate learning.
- Labor: Uber and Taskrabbit circumvent traditional regulated models.
- Law: computers may replace many legal services. Things stay inefficient because people "very oddly" trust lawyers more than computers.

He says not to bet against computers in these spheres, and it may not be wise to become the kind of doctor or lawyer that technology could make obsolete.

Further out: broadcast media, space and transportation, biology shifting from an experimental to an informational science, and the intelligence and government sectors.

## The 2x2: compete with or work with computers, compete with or work with China

Peter builds a 2x2. One axis is compete with computers vs. work with computers (anti- vs. pro-technology). The other is compete against China vs. work with China (anti- vs. pro-globalization).

- Globalization axis: collaborating beats competing. "Competing is too hard. People never really make any money. They just beat each other to bloody pulp." Even if you can win against China, it is a Pyrrhic victory.
- Technology axis: do not compete with computers. Even if you can do square roots faster than a computer, they will catch up. Human vs. computer chess has gone badly for humans since 1997.
- Working with China is better than competing, but too many people are myopically focused on it, so it may be too competitive now. Most focus on globalization instead of technology.
- By indirect proof, that leaves working with computers.
- Caveat: indirect proof is tricky. Everything may point one way and still be wrong if your inputs or perspective change. It is still a useful tool: if nothing else works, look closer at what seems better.

## Timing: when the future arrives and why predictions miss

Peter argues you must study how people in your shoes got the future wrong if you hope to get it right. Instead, "we tend to abdicate thinking about the future entirely."

- Wrong predictions are old: Lord Kelvin in 1895 said "heavier than air flying machines are impossible"; Patent Commissioner Charles Duell in 1899 said "everything that can be invented has been invented."
- Some predictions were too optimistic: in the 1960s people expected nuclear-powered everything and flying cities. Why that did not happen is worth thinking about.
- More interesting are cases where people were right on substance and wrong on timing.

Examples of right-but-early:
- Mobile: everyone who tried it in 1999 failed. No one thought the best mobile investment would be buying Apple stock.
- Apple's Newton launched in 1993; the iPhone took another 15 years to get the timing right.
- Rockets were developed in China in the 13th century, but that was not the time to go to the moon.
- Napster was too early and probably too disruptive; now there is Spotify.
- AI "just doesn't quite work yet."

His conclusion: "If we learn the right lessons, the future of the past can come back and become true."

## Surfing: paddle early, and err toward the future

Peter on the pitfalls of thinking about the future: either it is too far away, so you are right on substance but wrong on timing, or it is already here and everyone else is doing it.

- The goal is to catch a big wave, so you paddle hard. Sometimes there is no wave, "and that sucks."
- You cannot wait to be sure there is a wave before paddling, or you miss it entirely. "You have to paddle early, and then let the wave catch you."
- At the margins, it is better to paddle where there is no wave than to paddle too late and miss a good one.
- Starting the next great social networking company is current-wave thinking. "Social networking is not the next wave." So the bias should be toward the future.
- "Then again, the bigger bias should be to not err at all."

## Distribution, complex sales and gatekeepers

- Peter says never take it at face value when successful companies claim they do no sales or marketing; that claim is itself probably a sales pitch.
- He argues it may be time to rethink complex sales. The 1990s scarred people because complex-sales businesses failed, and business development deals were hard to get in the early 2000s, but such deals can be very advantageous.
- His example: Google's BD deal with Yahoo was phenomenal for Google. Google does not talk about it because it wants to talk about engineering; Yahoo does not because it is embarrassing.
- On mobile, the big worry is that any great distribution technique will be disallowed and then copied by Apple and Android. It is a big market, but it is far from clear you can wrest power from the gatekeepers.

## Boards, experience and power laws

- Boards: for effectiveness, keep the board small. Three people is best, since coordination gets worse with each person added. To make a board do nothing, make it enormous. Non-profits sometimes have 50-person boards, which gives the quasi-dictatorial head effectively no checks.
- Experience: the founders of Google, Microsoft and Facebook had little prior experience. The questions are what translates, and how.
  - A failed 10-20 person startup might teach what not to do, but it may have failed for other reasons, or leave you scarred and averse to risk.
  - Big companies like Microsoft or Google are great, but everything works automatically, and "there are shockingly few companies started by those people." One theory is that they are too sheltered from startup processes.
- Better than asking where to go is asking what to do: what do you believe in, what makes sense, what will work.
- "If there is indeed a power law distribution in company outcomes, it's really important to get into the single company you think is the best." The company's stage matters less than the substance of what you are doing.
