## The core question: what important truth do very few people agree with you on?

Peter treats the question "what important truth do very few people agree with you on?" as the starting point for a company. To a first approximation, the correct answer is a secret: an unpopular or unconventional truth. Restated for business, the question is "what great company is no one starting?"

- If there are many good answers, many great companies can still be created. If there are none, starting a company is probably a very bad idea.
- So the number of secrets left in the world is roughly equivalent to how many startups people should start.
- The question is easy to ask and hard to answer. When people try, they often conclude it is impossible, swinging from one extreme to the other. Peter says that is too big a move: answers not coming easily does not mean they do not exist. Finding secrets is "neither easy nor impossible, just hard."

## Easy, hard and impossible: the taxonomy of truths

Peter sorts truths by how hard they are to obtain, and says the distinction decides whether you pursue lucrative ventures or guaranteed failure.

- **Easy truths** are accepted conventions that nearly everyone knows.
- **Mysteries** are impossible to figure out. His example is superstring theory: you cannot really design experiments to test it, so the question is whether it is just very hard or a fool's errand.
- **Secrets** sit in between: difficult but possible.
- Discovery means removing the cover from a secret. Pythagoras's triangle math was once the subject of mystery cults and later became convention.
- It also runs the other way. Conventions can be forgotten and become secrets again, as people stop believing what earlier generations believed.
- Only big secrets that are true matter. Silly secrets (gossip), esoteric ones (tarot, numerology) and small incremental ones are of little importance.
- "Those who believe something is hard might still try. If you think something is impossible, you won't even try."

## Past secrets, and the biggest one left

Peter lists the secrets covered so far in the class as a template, and notes that each has a human side as well as a natural one.

- **Monopoly vs. competition:** "Capitalism and competition are antonyms." Monopolists pretend they are not monopolists ("Don't regulate us!"), and non-monopolists pretend they are ("We are so big and important!"), so firms only look similar on the surface.
- **The power law:** startup outcomes follow a power law. Investors find it psychologically hard to admit their best investment is worth more than the rest of the portfolio combined, so it stays hidden. People are uncomfortable talking about inequality and ignore or rationalize it.
- **Distribution:** it matters far more than people think, and people in distribution work to hide what they do. "Salespeople do best when people do not know they're dealing with salespeople."
- **The biggest secret:** that many important secrets are still left. Forty or fifty years ago this was convention and everyone believed much remained to be done. It has since become a secret again.

## Why people stopped believing in secrets

Peter says the common view that no secrets are left is plausible, and he explains where it comes from.

- **Extreme version:** Ted Kaczynski (IQ 167, Harvard, math PhD, Berkeley professor) argued in his manifesto that human goals are either easy or impossible, so people are depressed and technology should be destroyed so that people can work on hard problems anew. Peter calls this the easy/hard/impossible trichotomy with the hard middle removed.
- **Milder forms:** the hipster who ironically rejects useful technology, and fundamentalists generally. Religious fundamentalism has easy truths and the mysteries of God, with heresy in between. Environmental fundamentalism says protect nature, then "Mother Nature knows best." Market fundamentalism says prices are easy truths that must be accepted, not questioned.
- **Geography:** the map has no white spaces left. In the 18th century you could become an explorer. About 72% of the planet is ocean and some 90% of the inhabited ocean is deep sea, with only about 200 hours of human exploration, but few people find it magical. The frontier of knowledge seems to have closed with the geographic one.
- **Four sociological drivers:**
  - **Incrementalism:** do what is asked a bit better than others and get the A. Academics are incented by volume, not importance.
  - **Risk aversion:** secrets are likely to be wrong, and dedicating your life to something no one believes in is unbearable if you are wrong.
  - **Complacency:** the Harvard and Yale law deans tell incoming students they are set. Peter suspects that is only true if you do not believe it.
  - **Egalitarianism:** the idea that some people have insight others lack has fallen out of fashion. In 1939 Einstein's letter to Roosevelt about atomic weapons was taken seriously; today it would get lost in the White House mailroom.
- Peter concedes these attitudes serve most people well. Distrusting prophets is a good heuristic, and it is probably impossible now to start the cults people joined 30 years ago.

## The case against the case against secrets

Peter argues that the belief in a world without secrets has strange implications.

- **Injustice:** every injustice involves a secret that a small minority understands. Views in the 1950s and 60s that things were unjust became convention once the majority was won over. Saying no secrets remain means we are either fully just or should not try to be.
- **Markets:** disbelief in secrets implies efficient markets, but there have been decades of extraordinary inefficiency. You were not allowed to say in 2000 that people were acting irrationally about Internet companies, or in 2007 that there was a housing bubble. The Fed's model said the worst-case loss was no more than $25bn.
- **Political dissent:** extreme criticism of government rests on some secret truth. Disbelieving in secrets means no dissident can ever be right. Each party tries to discredit the other by tying it to conspiracy theorists (Tea Party, Occupy Wall Street).
- **Corporate governance (HP):** in 2004-2005 Tom Perkins, an engineer and Kleiner Perkins co-founder, wanted the HP board to discuss new technology and hard substantive problems. Patricia Dunn argued science was beyond the board's competence and it should focus on process. Leaks about the Compaq acquisition led to wiretaps, which were illegal, so a board obsessed with process broke process rules. Peter's lesson: "It's hard to work toward a radically better future if you don't believe in secrets."
- **Evidence for secrets:** hard problems do get solved. Andrew Wiles started on Fermat's last theorem in 1986 and proved it in 1995, and he would not have tried if he thought it impossible.
- **Web 2.0:** many small secrets add up. Twitter's 140-character messages are individually trivial but aggregate into something powerful. WikiLeaks showed how many secrets exist, and the order in which they are revealed can matter more than what they are.

## How to find secrets: ask what nature won't tell you and what people won't tell you

Peter says there is no formula and no complete list, because no one person knows every secret and a widely distributed list would turn secrets into conventions. He offers a method instead.

- **Filter first:** discard anything small or false and keep the big, true secrets.
- **Two kinds:** secrets of nature (science, the world) and secrets about people (things people hide). Ask "What secrets is nature not telling you? What secrets are people not telling you?"
- **Start with people:** he thinks human secrets are underappreciated. What people will not say is a good signal of where to look, and the most interesting secrets sit at the intersection of the two kinds. His lab analogy: if someone sneaks in every night and messes with your results, you will not understand what is happening if you only think about nature.
- **Physics bias:** the view that natural secrets are foundational leads to treating physics as the fundamental science. He jokes that physics PhDs are hard to work with because "they know fundamental things, they think they know all things." A PayPal candidate interrupted his interviewer with "Stop! I already know what you're going to ask!", was wrong, and was not hired.
- **Questions to ask:** what are we allowed to talk about? Which areas can people not look into? What is forbidden, implicitly off-limits or taboo?
- **Tradeoff:** natural secrets are transparently hard but politically safe (no one cares about superstring theory), while human secrets are more dangerous because more is at stake.
- **Worked examples:** the monopoly secret comes fast from asking what people running companies cannot say. The power law secret comes from asking what VCs cannot say. Both can also be reached by Econ 1 reasoning or by plotting startup outcomes, but the people question is faster.

## Where Peter points the search: nutrition, biotech, cleantech, agriculture, government

Peter shows the method by applying it to specific fields, framing each as a place where structure or taboo has kept people from looking.

- **Nutrition:** it is not a real major at Stanford or other real universities. Most big studies were done 30 or 40 years ago and there is little incentive to study it now. A company Founders Fund found interesting is a "Manhattan Project for Nutrition" with the six best people. He argues the food pyramid (low-fat, heavy on grains and carbohydrates) was probably more a product of Kellogg's lobbying than science, and obesity has exploded since.
- **Biotech:** stem cell and cancer research have surprisingly little overlap. The pro- and anti-stem-cell camps are both politicized, and neither wants to dwell on the fact that injected stem cells divide and multiply like cancer. Perhaps some cancer cells behave like stem cells, and research at that intersection looks promising.
- **Cleantech:** very few cleantech investments worked, because investors and entrepreneurs got involved to make an environmental statement and fashion was decoupled from business merit. "Saying you're doing something because it's cool is decidedly uncool." Rather than swear off the field, one can use the insight, and Elon Musk did with Tesla: high-end electric sports cars that build a brand first, with rich buyers underwriting R&D for cheaper mid-market cars. In 2005 Tesla seemed crazy and better solar panels seemed the way to go; seven years later Tesla has a fantastic brand and Solyndra does not. A monopoly is built by starting with a brand and building a tech company under it.
- **Agriculture (from the trade deficit):** the US trade deficit, around 4% of GDP, is probably unsustainable and awkward to discuss. If it must close, raising exports is more plausible than cutting imports, and the US's biggest comparative advantage is probably agriculture. Agritech looks counterintuitive for tech investors, which he takes as a good sign.
- **Government:** the debate is big versus small government, but nobody discusses "do more with less," which is the definition of technology. Pro-government people dislike criticizing government and anti-government people prefer abolishing it, so applying technology to government is ignored even though it could benefit everyone.
- **General rule:** find things that are hard but doable, and "don't simply accept others' definitions of the frontier."

## What to do with a secret: tell, hide, or time it

Peter treats the decision of whom to tell as a core strategic question.

- **Two extremes:** tell no one and keep it safe, but no one works with you and it dies with you. Tell everyone and you may build a team, but more people may compete with you.
- **Type matters:** intellectual and natural secrets are cheap to share. Secrets about people can be costly. He quotes Faust: "Mankind has always crucified and burned" those who "reveal their feelings to the crowd below."
- **Timing:** the right time to bring people in is rarely all at once at the start, and rarely never. An intermediate answer is usually best.
- **Speed vs. secrecy:** if you think others are about to find the same secret, move as fast as possible and tell whomever you need to.
- **PayPal in summer 1999:** after failed models such as beaming money between Palm Pilots, the team realized linking money and email would be powerful. It looked like a big secret but not a hard one, so they scrambled and shared it liberally. The risk is that listeners compete instead of joining. In June 1999 a management candidate told Peter in his interview that he wanted Peter's job. Peter liked his job and did not hire him, and the candidate tried to launch a competitor a few weeks later.
- **Fraud at PayPal:** fraud was endemic in banking but nobody discussed it. Banks do not say "we have hundreds of millions stolen every year and no idea how to stop it"; they build loss budgets and reserves and stay quiet.
- **Small and vocal vs. big and quiet:**
  - In Silicon Valley most secrets are small and get copied quickly, so the play is hypergrowth: reveal fast and get your exponential curve going so no one can catch you.
  - For bigger secrets that you alone hold, there may be no need to reveal early. Keep a low profile, use trade secrets and unique expertise, and build over several years.
  - The incremental fast-growth companies are very visible. People working on bigger ideas on longer timelines are in stealth, so there may be far more people going after hard secrets than we think.

## Secrets and choosing a job: hidden companies

Peter applies secrets to picking where to work, going beyond knowing the power law in theory.

- Companies are bimodal: a few great ones and many that do not work. Media and the tech community broadly agree on which are best, so the obvious plan is to shoot for startup A with B as a backup.
- That plan works in a world with a power law but no secrets. "In a world with many secrets, the best companies may be hidden." The power law still holds, but the perceived ranking may be wrong.
- Your task is to identify hidden companies with the potential to be the best, and to ask what potentially great company people are overlooking. Taking the perceived best-to-worst ordering as given is the fundamentalist view: the market, media and blogs know better than you.
- This does not mean joining obscure companies. Esoteric truths are not the aim; important truths that are hidden are.
- He closes with Tolkien: "Still round the corner there may wait / A new road or a secret gate." His message is to take the hidden paths.
