## Value comes from durability and capture, not just creation

- Peter says great companies do three things: they create value, they last, and they capture some of the value they create.
- Creating value is necessary but not sufficient. Disk drive companies of the 1980s created a lot of value with better drives, but were all replaced by others.
- Capturing value is separate from creating it. Isaac Newton created immense value and captured little of it. Airlines create value for the public and employ many people, but "have never really made any money."
- On first mover advantage: a first mover can fade away. He argues "more important than being the first mover is being the last mover."
- He cites Capablanca: to succeed, "you must study the endgame before everything else."
- Last movers build non-commoditized, relationship-driven businesses that create value, last and make money.

## Valuation: multiples, PEG and why tech value sits 10-15 years out

- Multiples and comparables "sort of" work, but Peter warns that social heuristics substitute for rigorous analysis. If everyone at an incubator invests at a $10M cap, the company gets deemed worth $10M.
- Software companies are often valued around 10x annual revenue. Guy Kawasaki's joke formula: pre-money valuation = ($1M x engineers) - ($500k x MBAs).
- P/E ratio = market value per share / earnings per share. It ignores growth.
- PEG = P/E divided by annual earnings growth. He says PEG should be less than one, and is a good indicator to watch while growing a business.
- Real valuation sums discounted future cash flows (time value of money), because money today is worth more than money later and the future carries risk.
- In a growth model you want growth rate g above discount rate r. Growth must eventually decline, otherwise the company approaches infinite value.
- Old Economy businesses in decline hold most value in the near term. If a company can maintain present cash flows for 5 or 6 years, value investors see a good investment.
- Tech and high-growth companies usually lose money at first. A typical model could see about 2/3 of the value created in years 10 through 15.
- He argues startup culture wrongly thinks in months and quarters, in "Old Economy mode," and "pointedly ignores, and even resists, 10-15 year thinking."

## PayPal and LinkedIn as long-horizon valuation examples

- PayPal: 27 months in, its growth rate was 100%. The team expected deceleration but a rate still above the discount rate. The plan was for most of the value to arrive around 2011.
- That horizon undershot. The discount rate came in lower than expected and growth was still a healthy 15%, so most of PayPal's value now looked likely to come around 2020.
- LinkedIn: market cap around $10B and a P/E of about 850. Discounted cash flow makes it make sense: about $2B of value expected between 2012 and 2019, and the other $8B reflects 2020 and beyond.
- His conclusion on LinkedIn: the valuation only makes sense if the company is durable, "around to create all that value in the decades to come."

## Perfect competition vs monopoly

- In perfect competition no firm makes economic profit. Profits attract entrants and erase themselves; losses force exits. The firm is a price taker whose scale is negligible against the market.
- A monopoly owns its market and is a price setter.
- Peter says economics textbooks spend most of their time on perfect competition and treat monopoly as a small exception. He asks whether monopoly is instead "a valid alternative paradigm in its own right" and perfect competition only the default in textbooks.
- Most great tech companies have one decisive advantage, such as economies of scale or uniquely low production costs, that makes them monopoly-esque. A drug company with a patent can charge above its production cost.
- "The really valuable businesses are monopoly businesses": last movers whose value is sustained instead of eroded by competition.
- His point about intensity: "the more intense the competition, the less likely you'll be able to capture any value at all." Under perfect competition you "compete hard, but you can never gain anything for all your struggle."

## The case for and against monopoly

- Cons he lists: lower output and higher prices than competitive markets, price setting, price discrimination (capturing more consumer surplus), and complacency that stifles innovation because profits arrive either way.
- The counter-argument: monopoly can incentivize innovation. If a company builds something dramatically better than the next best thing, the gap between its price and marginal cost is "the creators' reward for creating the new thing."
- Monopolistic firms can do better long-term planning and fund deeper projects because of durability. Under perfect competition, profits are zero and that durability does not exist.
- Adam Smith's view, quoted: "People of the same trade seldom meet together... but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices." Peter says the idea that monopoly is bad is usually accepted as a given and deserves questioning.
- Legal and measurement tools he walks through: the Sherman Act ("monopoly power will not be found unlawful unless it is accompanied by an element of anticompetitive conduct") and three DOJ tests.
  - Lerner index = (price - marginal cost) / price; 0 is perfect competition, 1 is monopoly. Hard to compute externally, but tech companies know their own numbers and should watch it.
  - Herfindahl-Hirschman index: sum of squared market shares of the top 50 firms. Under 0.15 is competitive, 0.15 to 0.25 concentrated, over 0.25 highly concentrated and possibly monopolistic.
  - m-firm concentration ratio: sum the shares of the 4 or 8 largest firms; over 70% means a concentrated market.

## Why people overvalue competition, and why small markets win

- Peter says people are biased toward perfect competition because it is easy to model, efficient in a static world and politically salable. The name itself, "perfect," is normative.
- He calls it irrelevant in a dynamic world: if there is no equilibrium, you can capture some of the value you create, which perfect competition prevents.
- Competition is overrated and taught unquestioningly: high school, college, grad school, then the "rat race." His example is Big Law, where young lawyers work insanely hard for partner odds that "aren't in your favor." He concludes "winning by a large margin is better than ruthless competition, if you can swing it."
- Globalization starts from "the world is flat," which frames work as crazed competition and a race to the bottom. Technology starts from the idea that the world is Mount Everest and much of it is unique.
- "The bigger the market, the better" is, in his words, "utterly, totally wrong." The restaurant business is a huge market and "not a very good way to make money."
- Better to find or make a small market, excel and own it. In a huge, well-defined market it is hard to be number one and hard to see where the predators are.
- He returns to the question to keep asking: what valuable company are other people not building?

## PayPal's decisive advantage and the value of networks

- Payments had considerable economies of scale, so PayPal could not compete head-on with big credit card companies. It had to undercut them through technical and product innovation.
- Technical problem: fraud. There was far more of it than expected and it was hard to stamp out. Enemies included "Carders World" and a hacker named Igor. PayPal's fraud software was named "Igor."
- Product innovation: cheaper funding sources. Getting users' bank account information drove down blended costs. By modeling how much money sat in an account, PayPal could make advance payments, largely bypass the Automatic Clearing House system, and make payments instant from the user's perspective.
- His takeaway: it is "absolutely critical to have some decisive advantage over the next best service," because even a few competitors quickly create a very competitive dynamic.
- On venture capital: VCs rely on a discrete network of people, not a large pool of business. The network is the core value proposition and is driven by relationships, so VC is "anti-commoditized," personal and idiosyncratic, like great businesses.
- The "PayPal network" is a set of friendships built over a decade that became a sort of franchise.
