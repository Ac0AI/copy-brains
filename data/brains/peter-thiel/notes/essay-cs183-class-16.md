## Life expectancy trends and why Peter treats longevity as a bet against bad luck

- Peter notes that even without a biotech revolution, life expectancy has risen steadily, by roughly 2.3 to 2.5 years per decade since the mid-19th century.
- Plotting the longest-lived demographic in each country gives a very straight line, "analogous" to Moore's law. In 1840 life expectancy was about 45-46 years; before that it was flat for thousands of years.
- Every day you survive adds about 5 or 6 hours to your life. He asks whether the line will continue, slow, or accelerate: a short burst that stagnates, or the start of a fierce acceleration.
- The U.S. lags the global maximum by several years (bad food, inactivity), but the upward trend is relentless.
- Keeping people alive longer has been an exponentially harder problem for a century and a half.
- He frames longevity as "the opposite of bad luck": death comes from accidents, so the real question is whether and how far luck can be overcome.

## Death as accident: micro, macro and cosmic, and the case against pure probabilistic thinking

- Accidents sit on a spectrum: microscopic (a DNA mutation starting a cancer), macroscopic (a car crash), cosmic (an asteroid strike).
- Solving longevity completely means removing all of them, but macro and cosmic accidents are likely to stay probabilistic. He would take those on later.
- If the microscopic accidents are solved, the best estimates put lifespans at between 600 and 1,000 years.
- From the 17th to the mid-19th centuries the prevailing view was that accidents could be overcome. Bacon's *New Atlantis* was the classic accident-free utopia, "new" because its people had complete mastery over nature, unlike the old Atlantis the gods destroyed.
- That view has receded since about 1850. He attributes this partly to actuarial science and life insurance, which reduced life and death to probability functions (a 30-year-old has a 1 in 1,000 chance of dying in a year; at 100 the chance is 50%).
- He warns that "unchecked probabilistic thinking can be dangerous" because it defeats one's ability to shape the future. His example is *No Country for Old Men*: if everything is a probability distribution, eventually your luck runs out and you resign yourself to it.
- The alternative is to think and avoid playing games that depend too heavily on luck.
- Historical footnote: around 1700, claims of living forever seemed stronger because salesmen claimed to be 150 years old, and poor record-keeping let them get away with it. A 70-year-old in London was seen as wretched, while a 150-year-old might get a pension from the King.

## Why drug discovery and biotech investing are too luck-driven

- Peter argues that modern drug discovery is "too much a matter of luck." Scientists start with about 10,000 compounds, screening cuts them to maybe 5 that reach Phase 3, and maybe 1 is approved by the FDA.
- Starting a biotech company is usually brutal: most last 10 to 15 years, with little control along the way. What looks promising may not work, there is no iteration or sense of progress, and the outcome is binary at the end of a largely stochastic process. You can work 10 years and not know if you wasted your time.
- In Internet businesses, the basic rule is that the company succeeds if every financing round is an up round. In biotech that is very hard; investors get tired, and some candidly say they don't care about valuations because everything gets wiped out in their favor in the inevitable down round. "Why negotiate valuation if luck dominates everything?"
- Luck-driven, stats-driven processes worked well for a few decades, but the costs may be rising because the easy discoveries have been found. Developing a new drug cost $100 million in 1975 and $1.3 billion at the time of the lecture.
- He says probably all life sciences investment funds have lost money, and that biotech investing has been roughly as bad as cleantech.

## Can computing make biology determinate? The search-problem view of biotech

- Peter calls drug discovery "fundamentally a search problem" with an extremely large search space of possible compounds. The key question is whether computing can reduce the role of luck.
- Biological processes involve some luck against irreversible degradation, and traditional therapeutics largely mirrors them. Computational processes are reversible: "you can dive in and re-program things."
- The open question is how far biological problems can be reduced to computer problems.
- Sequencing costs are collapsing: about $500 million per genome in 2000, about $5,000 at the time, and probably $1,000 within a year or two.
- The Human Genome Project was seen as revolutionary in the late 1990s but hasn't lived up to the hype. Peter suggests it may have been too early or too costly, but the deeper reason may be that the main problem is not sequencing at all: "we just don't know what to do with the data."
- He says exactly how much of biology is computational is still an open question.

## Three computational-biotech companies Peter highlights

- **Stem CentRx** is the closest to traditional biotech, with a heavy computational piece. It aims to cure all cancer by targeting the cancer stem cells that drive tumor growth. Chemo is hard to dose: too low is ineffective, too high kills the patient. Targeting the driving subset precisely would be less destructive and more effective. Mouse studies were very promising, with human results expected over the next year or two.
- **Counsyl** is a bioinformatics company aiming to be the default for pregnancy genetic screening. It built a single simple test covering about 100 genes for inherited traits and focuses only on Mendelian diseases, since more complicated genetic combinations are poorly understood. It had identified a "tractable and well-defined subset of the problem." It screened about 2% of U.S. births and expected that to rise dramatically.
- **Emerald Therapeutics** is the most computational and was in stealth. Its goal is to cure all viral infections by reprogramming cells, building a molecular machine that tags virus-containing cells and releases a sequence making them self-destruct.
- He says the high paranoia around programmable anti-viral companies is understandable: these are "big secrets that play out over long time horizons, not web apps that have a 6-week window to take over the world."

## Timing, secrecy, and competition in biotech

- Peter asks whether the field is early or right on time, echoing Marc Andreessen's point that many late-'90s Internet ideas were right but too early: "how do you know you're not paddling too early?"
- He asks how a company can be confident no one else is secretly running the same strategy, and whether its own secrecy is actually working.
- If competitors like a hypothetical "Ruby Therapeutics" were out there, he asks whether it would make more sense to be open and collaborate, and how you recruit if you are secretive.
- On recruiting, he says referral-based hiring has worked well at every company he has been involved with, but you must keep a clear head. Ask MBAs for referrals and you get far too many; ask engineers and you may get shocking silence because they are shy. You have to make them comfortable referring people.
- He asks why Silicon Valley entrepreneurs default to web or mobile apps instead of computational biotech.
- On advertising, he jokes that it usually works best hidden but sometimes works when completely transparent. He finds opaque games tiring, such as the VC adage "If you want money, ask for advice. If you want advice, ask for money," and finds it refreshing when someone says "I really just want money."

## FDA bottleneck as a future opportunity, and Internet vs. biotech startup friction

- Peter calls it "very odd" that the FDA is a bottleneck on global drug development. There has to be a tipping point beyond which the U.S. no longer dictates what drugs are developed worldwide, and the U.S. may then have to compete with China on how quickly drugs get developed, a potentially huge paradigm shift.
- He argues things look bad now but the future may be promising. SpaceX was heavily regulated at first but persevered, and aerospace regulations have eased in the last decade. "The sheer unfriendliness of the baseline could be a great opportunity."
- On startup friction, he contrasts Internet businesses, where you can be up and running while doing hardly anything, with biotech's physical setup costs.
- His PayPal example: the biggest interface with reality was that, on Max's orders, people had to assemble their own desks. Luke Nosek found even that too much and hired a service called Delegate Everything, which sent an elderly woman handyperson to assemble his desk so he could do more work on the computer.
