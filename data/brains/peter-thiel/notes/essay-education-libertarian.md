## Peter's shift from political activism to technological escape

- Peter remains committed to "authentic human freedom as a precondition for the highest good" and still calls himself "libertarian". He opposes confiscatory taxes, totalitarian collectives, and "the ideology of the inevitability of the death of every individual."
- What changed over two decades is his view of how to achieve those goals. His most important change: "I no longer believe that freedom and democracy are compatible."
- He frames his own path as an education that other classical liberals face too: from political persuasion to disillusionment to a search for something beyond politics.

## Why political activism failed him

- At Stanford in the late 1980s he started a student newspaper to challenge campus orthodoxies. It won limited victories, notably undoing university speech codes.
- He judged the effort mostly futile: like "trench warfare on the Western Front in World War I", with lots of carnage but no movement in the center of the debate. They were "preaching mainly to the choir", though that did convince the choir to keep singing for life.
- As a young lawyer and trader in 1990s Manhattan he saw why so many grow disillusioned after college: "The world appears too big a place." Many of his saner peers retreated to "tending their small gardens" rather than fight "the relentless indifference of the universe."
- He observes that the higher one's IQ, the more pessimistic about free-market politics, because "capitalism simply is not that popular with the crowd." Smart conservatives coped with heavy drinking; smart libertarians escaped into alcohol "and beyond it".

## The 2009 case that politics is closed to libertarians

- In 2009 he saw the financial crisis as caused by too much debt and leverage, facilitated by a government that insured against moral hazards. The response was more debt, leverage, and government.
- Free-market advocates "have been screaming into a hurricane." Recent events "shatter any remaining hopes of politically minded libertarians", so educating the body politic "has become a fool's errand."
- The trend has run the wrong way for a long time. The last US depression without massive government intervention was the 1920-21 collapse: sharp but short, a Schumpeterian "creative destruction" that led to a real boom. The roaring 1920s were so strong that historians forgot the depression that started them.
- He calls the 1920s the last decade in American history in which one could be "genuinely optimistic about politics."
- He argues that since 1920 the vast increase in welfare beneficiaries and the extension of the franchise to women, "two constituencies that are notoriously tough for libertarians", have made "capitalist democracy" an oxymoron.
- In a later clarification he said it would be absurd to suggest women's votes will be taken away, and that he doesn't think any class of people should be disenfranchised. He has "little hope that voting will make things better", and says voting isn't under siege but other rights are: people imprisoned for using mild drugs, tortured by their own government, and forced to bail out reckless financial companies.

## Escaping politics rather than winning it

- His conclusion: "the great task for libertarians is to find an escape from politics in all its forms", from totalitarian and fundamentalist catastrophes to "the unthinking demos that guides so-called 'social democracy.'"
- He does not despair because he no longer believes politics "encompasses all possible futures of our world."
- The key question is means: escaping "not via politics but beyond it." Since "there are no truly free places left in our world", escape must come through "some sort of new and hitherto untried process that leads us to some undiscovered country."
- So he focuses on new technologies that may create new space for freedom.
- He says politics is "way too intense": it angers people, destroys relationships, and polarizes vision into us versus them. It is "about interfering with other people's lives without their consent", which is probably why libertarians have made little political progress. He advocates putting energy into peaceful projects that some consider utopian.

## Three technological frontiers for freedom

- **Cyberspace.** He has focused his entrepreneurship and investing on the Internet.
  - PayPal's late-1990s founding vision was a new world currency, free from government control and dilution: "the end of monetary sovereignty."
  - In the 2000s, companies like Facebook create space for new dissent and communities not bounded by historical nation-states.
  - "By starting a new Internet business, an entrepreneur may create a new world," which may force change on the existing order.
  - The limitation: these worlds are virtual, so the escape "may be more imaginary than real." Which account proves true won't be resolved for many years.
- **Outer space.** A limitless frontier and limitless possibility for escape, but with a barrier to entry: rocket technology has advanced only modestly since the 1960s. He urges redoubled efforts to commercialize space, with realism about time horizons. The libertarian future of Heinlein-style science fiction "will not happen before the second half of the 21st century."
- **Seasteading.** Settling the oceans sits between cyberspace and outer space. He says whether people will live there (answer: "enough will") matters less than whether the technology is imminent. It is more tentative than the Internet but much more realistic than space travel, possibly economically feasible now or soon. He eagerly supports it and wishes Patri Friedman "the very best in his extraordinary experiment."

## A deadly race between politics and technology

- He warns against technological utopianism: "the notion that technology has a momentum or will of its own, that it will guarantee a more free future, and therefore that we can ignore the terrible arc of the political in our world."
- His metaphor: "we are in a deadly race between politics and technology." The future will be much better or much worse, and it is open. He suspects the race may be very close, "even down to the wire."
- Unlike politics, in technology "the choices of individuals may still be paramount." He argues the world's fate "may depend on the effort of a single person who builds or propagates the machinery of freedom that makes the world safe for capitalism."
