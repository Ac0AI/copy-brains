## Why Peter wants political theology back

- He says late modernity is Adam Smith's pin factory "on steroids": hyper-specialization leaves people as "ever smaller cogs in an ever bigger machine," unable to see the big picture.
- Political philosophy and political theology integrate the facets of life. The Enlightenment set them aside because the questions seemed too hard or a "fool's errand," or too dangerous and divisive. Religion was settled by the 1648 Treaty of Westphalia, and "you're not supposed to have debates about religion."
- He calls that a reasonable 18th-century compromise, but argues that in the 21st century it is "maybe more dangerous not to think about things."
- He starts at human scale (politics, economics, society, culture) rather than micro or cosmic scale, which he calls a Socratic move.
- He sees a link between the university and the universe, and between the failing "multiversity" and the "crazed multiverse." His view is that you have to fix what went wrong with the university before you can make sense of the universe.

## Why he is neither a Calvinist nor a rationalist

- Tyler argues that "religious but not spiritual" makes Peter a natural Calvinist. Peter replies that he is "still mostly a libertarian."
- On the five points (total depravity, unconditional election, limited atonement, irresistible grace, perseverance of the saints), he doesn't know if he agrees with even one.
- Girardian argument: gods and scapegoats are deeply linked. Calvinism is "an extreme form of scapegoating," where "Mr. God did everything, he determined everything." He says to be deeply distrustful of scapegoating God for everything.
- Intellectual argument: if you are too depraved to think, "we shouldn't be having a conversation."
- Medievals trusted the intellect and distrusted the will; moderns reverse this. Effective altruists and East Bay rationalists are "much closer to Calvinism." Outwardly they are pure and rational, inwardly "it's all just spaghetti code," and "you can never be right about anything. Maybe you can be a little bit less wrong."
- His shared diagnosis of Calvinism, rationalism and "muddling through" is distrust of human agency, human thought, and our ability to make choices.
- Against Catholicism, his "two-word rebuttal" is "Pope Francis." He grew up Lutheran and says the Reformation had to come from outside, since a centralized church could not reform itself. He thinks the Protestant project lines up more closely with a libertarian view, but admits he is "not a perfect Lutheran."

## Old Testament, victimhood and the woke

- He holds a fairly orthodox Christian view of continuity between the Old and New Testaments. The Christian God is "maybe the original progressive," the first time the new is better simply by being new. Exaggerating the difference ends in saying the two Gods differ, and he says the progressive 19th-century higher criticism was "deeply anti-Semitic."
- The Girardian reading: the Bible repeatedly tells stories from the victim's side. Cain and Abel (founding of the first city, told from Abel's view) mirrors Romulus and Remus (told from Romulus's view). The Exodus is told from the Israelites' view, not the Egyptians' ("troublemakers and we got rid of them").
- On Tyler's pitch that history is bad so we should side with the woke, Peter agrees history was very bad and says conservatives err by whitewashing it ("happy slaves" is "a loser argument").
- He lays out three options:
  - Christian: history is terrible, so find a way to forgive.
  - Woke: history is terrible, forget the forgiveness.
  - Right-wing Nietzschean (Bronze Age Pervert): forget the history, "sick of this guilt trip."
- He thinks the in-between Christian position is the most tenable.
- Victimhood: he doesn't want a blanket rule against claiming it (he jokes he is "a poor and persecuted Peter person"), but absolutizing it is unhealthy. His test from the Harvard talk: "You can't say that you're a greater victim than Christ."
- His other canon is the Western great books (Shakespeare, Cervantes, Goethe), read with near-semi-divine seriousness, though he calls them less holy than the Bible.

## Carl Schmitt, Weimar and why history isn't cyclical

- Schmitt belongs to the 1920s Weimar thinkers, after Germany lost WWI, when neither throne and altar nor liberal democracy was viable. They reopened political theology questions set aside since the Enlightenment. Peter notes Schmitt got entangled with the Nazis, which was "very bad judgment."
- His image: the dwarves of Moria who "dwelled too deep" and woke the Balrog.
- He sees parallels between the US in the 2020s and Germany in the 1920s: liberalism exhausted, democracy perhaps exhausted, questions to be asked "very far outside the Overton window." He adds that he doesn't think we are in a cyclical world.
- The Schmitt insight he values is from *The Concept of the Political*: politics is defined by friends and enemies.
- His example is the Reagan coalition. A millionaire, a general and a priest have almost nothing to talk about at dinner, yet the coalition worked because they shared an enemy, anti-communism. When the Berlin Wall fell in 1989, the coalition disintegrated. Anti-communism was his own formative political idea as a teenager in the late '70s and early '80s.
- Where Schmitt goes wrong: politics may be a scapegoating machine that only works if you don't look into the sausage-making. His example is accusing a random elderly woman of witchcraft to unify a village, which "doesn't really work if you're that self-aware." Schmitt's optimistic Enlightenment rationality, describing politics as an arbitrary friend/enemy split, likely accelerated its disintegration.
- On cyclical history, he stresses what is "one-time and world-historical." He favors the Judeo-Christian linear view over the Greco-Roman one.
  - Thucydides' peace-then-war pattern is cyclical, and none of the details matter (he made up the speeches).
  - Daniel's succession of four kingdoms is unique and unrepeatable. Peter calls Daniel the real first historian, and says "Thucydides isn't even close."
- Science and technology give history a progressive character. We have nuclear weapons now, and "I don't know how you unlearn all the knowledge we've gained, even since 1900." He rejects Tyler's analogies to 17th-century England and the Roman Empire.

## The Antichrist, existential risk and the katechon

- Apocalyptic dangers today come from a nonreligious place: science and technology (nuclear weapons, bioweapons and the Wuhan lab question, climate, AI). Girard noted that the Church stopped Advent sermons on the end times after 1945, so people wouldn't link nuclear weapons to Armageddon. The first nuclear test was named Trinity.
- He wants one more risk on the list: "a one-world totalitarian government. I find that at least as scary as the others."
- Biblical frame: you are supposed to worry about Armageddon and also the Antichrist, "maybe more" since "the Antichrist comes first." The path runs between "the Scylla of all these existential risks and the Charybdis of this political totalitarian catastrophe."
- Fiction: the best Antichrist novels are pre-WWI (Robert Hugh Benson's *Lord of the World*, 1908; Solovyov, 1900). Their Antichrist takes over by hypnotic speeches ("daemonium ex machina"). A post-1945 version would be *One World or None*: the Antichrist rises "by constantly talking about Armageddon and constantly telling us scary millenarian stories."
- The Antichrist's slogan in 1 Thessalonians 5:3 is "peace and safety." He argues we've moved toward peace-and-safety rather than chaos. After 9/11 we got the Patriot Act and tracking of money flows, not chaotic terrorism, and it is now hard to have an illegal Swiss account or exit the US.
- He is more worried about the humans trying to stop AI than AI: "A force that's powerful enough to stop the AI is probably a force that's powerful enough to destroy the world, too."
- He cites a 2019 Bostrom essay on stopping AI risk (culture change so nobody has heterodox ideas, global policing, global compute governance), which "sounds to me at least as scary as the AI." Dr. Strangelove versus Greta: if everyone has to ride a bicycle, "that requires some real, real enforcement."
- Katechon: the mysterious restraining force that holds back the Antichrist. He doesn't put too much stock in it, because it is provisional and you can't identify it with an institution ("if you're not supposed to immanentize the eschaton, you're also not supposed to immanentize the katechon").
- The Girardian risk is that the katechon is mimetically entangled with what it opposes and can become the Antichrist. Examples:
  - Claudius restrained Nero, but both were Roman emperors.
  - Anti-communism (about 1949 to 1989) was the katechon of the 20th century, violent and hard to justify morally, yet it stopped communism. It then morphed into neoliberalism and Bush 41's "new world order."
  - Greta presents herself as a katechon stopping climate change.

## Why "muddling through" is a formula for disaster

- Tyler asks what variables he tracks for whether the West muddles through. Peter says it's not really empirical, and that granting the frame "you've already won half the argument."
- Post-Cold War globalization was the "you don't need to sweat the details" story, and he thinks the US-China relationship went differently than Fukuyama and others envisioned. Alarm bells would have gone off sooner without the muddle-through assumption.
- Globalization could lead to a neoliberal paradise or to the totalitarian state of the Antichrist. Libertarians weren't critical enough of the latter over the last 20 to 30 years and "didn't believe their own theories."
- "Whenever people think you can just muddle through, you're probably set up for some kind of disaster."
- Parallel to *Zero to One*'s chapter "You are not a lottery ticket": an investor who sets up a desk on South Beach and writes checks to everyone has a formula for losing all their money. He calls muddling through anti-intellectual: "It's not thinking about things."
- His default is that the default is really bad: "muddling through isn't good... You don't get to muddle through with Greta on her bicycle." This is why he favors AI even if the Luddites turn out to be right.

## Human agency, Girard, Shakespeare and Strauss

- On agency in the Bible: Girard called Christ "the first political atheist." Caesar Augustus, son of the divinized Caesar, was not simply Son of God, so the Roman Empire was not divinely ordained. That opens room for a less unitary order, and for the individual, over many centuries.
- He calls Ayn Rand "a pretty good Christian in this way": "you just can't sacrifice the individual... your mind... your reason."
- Shakespeare as proto-Girardian: Marx says conflict comes from objectively different class interests, but Shakespeare shows conflict is most intense when people don't differ at all (*Romeo and Juliet*: two houses "alike in dignity"). So Shakespeare would distrust extreme ideological differences.
- *Julius Caesar* shows reenactment as a trap. Brutus is pulled in by his ancestor who overthrew Tarquin in 509 BC. John Wilkes Booth, a Shakespearean actor, reenacted it with "Sic semper tyrannis."
- Peter notes Lincoln's 1838 Lyceum address, which describes a Caesar or Napoleon figure who would "enslave all the white people or freed all the slaves," and suggests Lincoln was speaking of himself. He says we should want to transcend cycles of reenactment.
- Strauss and Girard agree that some truths are disruptive when unveiled. The apocalypse means "unveiling," and unveiling the social order may deconstruct it.
- Girard: "To see Satan is to see Satan fall," so Satan appears only at the end of the world.
- Libertarian reading: in the temptation in the desert, Satan offers all the kingdoms of the world, implying governments are "more satanic than divinely ordained."
- He favors truth over utilitarian reasons for religion, as in the case of Ayaan Hirsi Ali's conversion.

## AI, centralization and the questions Silicon Valley doesn't ask

- He says Silicon Valley is bad at talking about AI: questions are "way too narrow" (will the next transformer improve 20 percent?) or "too cosmic" (the simulation theory). He wants the in-between questions.
- Wordcels versus math: he guesses AI is "much worse for the math people than the word people." Contacts told him that within three to five years models could solve all US Math Olympiad problems.
- His history of math bias:
  - The French Revolution favored math testing because verbal ability was thought to run in families, and math to be spread across the population, which made it meritocratic and egalitarian.
  - In the Soviet Union, honoring chess grandmasters and number theorists may have been a control mechanism, since they were "singularly clueless."
  - Silicon Valley today is "way too biased toward the math people."
  - His own late-'80s chess bias was undermined by computers in 1997. "Isn't that what's going to happen to math? And isn't that a long-overdue rebalancing of our society?"
  - On medical school: he doesn't want a neurosurgeon "doing prime number factorizations in their head."
- Questions he wants asked:
  - How much will AI raise GDP versus inequality?
  - Is it centralizing? His old riff: "if crypto is libertarian, why can't we say that AI is communist?"
  - Returns to scale on AI are so big that "even San Francisco will survive."
  - What does it do to US-China?
- On China: it may deploy AI faster and train it on all its people. But the US may be ahead, and Chinese models must be nerfed (e.g., can't say who Winnie the Pooh is). He asks whether a US edge, and a volatility-increasing technology China can't match, could accelerate China's timetable toward invading Taiwan "in the next year or two."
- He doubts the claim that AI is the only technology that matters. In the 1960s "technology" meant computers, medicines, spaceships, supersonic planes and the Green Revolution. Narrowing it to IT, then to AI, is "a manifestation of the centralizing stagnation that we should be trying to get out of."
- Centralization of computing is not predetermined. Late-'60s computers were imagined as centralized (2001, *Star Trek*'s Planet Beta supercomputer), the late '90s as decentralized (*The Sovereign Individual*, which influenced him), and the late 2010s as centralizing again (crypto excepted). He says "it's up to us" which way to push the technology.
- On open-source AI, he doubts it is the main variable, citing the Linux versus Microsoft precedent.
- Crypto: he is hopeful Bitcoin is on net on the "anti-one-world-order side," but notes physical cash may beat it for anonymity and that the crypto-anarchist utopia of the late '90s didn't happen. As a one-time way to move money outside a government's control, it is "still extremely good."

## Other views from the Q&A

- **World order:** He wants "a libertarian world order of many nations" where you can move between them, with a transnational layer not strong enough to control the nations. He notes the globalization paradox that people confuse a Hegelian synthesis with "a superposition of the thesis and antithesis." Bostrom and communism both reach one-world government through Enlightenment rationality and optimism about human nature.
- **Scaling the Thiel Fellowship:** Scaling means automating and turning it into a "cookie cutter," which deranges at scale, "like some kind of Mao's Little Red Book." Advice like "think for yourself" can't be formulized.
- **Personal path:** Highly tracked as a kid (Stanford, law school, a top Manhattan firm that "everybody wanted to get in" and "on the inside... everybody wanted to get out"), then a mid-20s quarter-life crisis. His lesson is to avoid the worst mimetic competition, though he doesn't think awareness of it fixes anything.
- **DEI:** He is "very against it," though not always the most important issue. His 1990s book *The Diversity Myth* described campus culture wars, but he calls the arguments ineffective because drivers were on a different level. He calls DEI a distraction from the economy, science and tech and religious questions, and says "a real Marxist would be much preferable to a diversity person." Walmart was the "proto-woke company" of the 2000s, using green rebranding to split the anti-Walmart coalition.
- **Biotech:** The FDA has a "global stranglehold." Most governments won't loosen below it, so there's less regulatory arbitrage than it appears. US pricing means for a biotech drug it is "US or bust."
- **Miami:** He is pro-Miami, mainly as an anti-New York story where finance doesn't have to be centered in New York. He worries about a Georgist outcome, where all value is captured by real estate owners as in San Francisco, New York and London.
- **Next learning:** He is a hedgehog (versus Tyler the fox) coming back to "the wonderful and terrible history of the world that we're living through as Christianity's unraveling our culture" and how to get to the other side.
