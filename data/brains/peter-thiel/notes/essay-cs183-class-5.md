## Culture sits between consultant-nihilism and cult

- Peter says everyone knows culture matters, but it is hard to say what makes a good one. The early Microsoft team "clearly got something right."
- He describes a 1-dimensional axis from consultant-nihilism to cultish dogmatism and wants a company somewhere in the middle.
  - A cult is a culture that fails: crazy and idealistic in a bad way, with members "fanatically wrong about something big."
  - Anti-culture is the dominant paradigm. Consulting firms are the classic case: people are mercenaries and nihilistic.
- If a company must lean to one extreme, he prefers being closer to a cult than to an army of consultants.
- Homogeneity alone is not enough. A robust culture is one where people share something that "distinguishes them quite sharply from the rest of the world." If everybody likes ice cream, that doesn't matter. A relevant, unique shared philosophy about something important does.
- Differences matter only when they go to the core mission. A key person on an ice-cream-only diet is quirky but irrelevant. Different kinds of problem-solvers on one team can strengthen the culture.

## Capitalism and competition are antonyms, but be ready to fight

- Peter says capitalism and competition are "better seen as antonyms than as synonyms." Don't set out to compete.
- That is no excuse to slack off: you probably need to work intensely, but on something others aren't doing, an area that is not zero-sum.
- Monopoly is the theoretical ideal to always pursue. You won't always find a non-competitive, cornucopian world, though, and may land in zero-sum situations. You must be prepared to handle them.
- He quotes Gandhi's advice to let yourself be slaughtered rather than owe allegiance to Hitler and Mussolini, and says Gandhi "probably would not have been a great startup advisor." His rule: try to avoid fighting, but where you must, "fight and win."

## Nerds, athletes and the 2x2 matrix

- Peter dichotomizes two extreme types.
  - Nerds (engineers, STEM people) are highly intelligent, good at problem solving, and naturally non-zero-sum.
  - Athletes are highly motivated fighters who think you only win if the other guy loses.
- A company of only athletes is biased toward competing. They like competition because they've historically been good at it, so they jump into crowded fields.
- A company of only nerds ignores that sometimes you have to fight, and gets "crushed by their own naiveté."
- His 2x2 matrix has zero-sum vs non-zero-sum people on one axis, and warring, competitive environments vs peaceful monopoly/capitalist environments on the other.
  - Examples of warring environments: Indian food joints on Castro Street, art galleries in Palo Alto.
  - Most startups are run by non-zero-sum people who believe the world is cornucopian. That is good, but they often pick competitive fields anyway because they don't know better, and "don't realize that they've decided to fight a war until it's all over."
- The optimal spot is monopoly capitalism with a tailored mix of zero-sum and non-zero-sum people. Pick an environment where you don't have to fight, but bring good fighters to protect the non-zero-sum people and the mission, just in case.

## Founders Fund and the cleantech cap table

- Founders Fund is a picky VC firm with maybe 20 dogmas of the form "Never invest in x" (mobile internet, cleantech and so on). Sometimes it seems impossible to invest in anything.
- Being contrarian, they tried a new strategy: find the best company in or for each dogma. Peter calls it more useful as a thought experiment than as a strategy.
- It led them to a cleantech company they would normally have skipped. The space is extremely competitive and no one really makes money, but this one looked good: run by scientists, great engineers and technology, everyone passionate about the mission. Term sheet talk began.
- Then the cap table showed the founders and employees owned about 20% and other VCs 80%, at a $35m valuation, still early stage.
- The founders' response was nonchalant: "We are so committed to making the technology work that we didn't care about the equity." Peter calls that noble but "also pretty bad."
- It killed the deal because of the questions it raised.
  - With such passivity, what will they do about competitors?
  - Can they build a sales team?
  - If they got run over so hard by investors, how will they fare against the entire world?

## Firing, and why companies die from the inside

- An early PayPal goal was to never fire anybody. The founders hired friends they could trust, but eventually had to hire more people they knew less.
- They hired an outside sys admin who showed up at 6pm on his first day and had poor hygiene. The "never fire" rule silenced objections.
- A couple of months later PayPal's systems crashed and the sys admin had made no backups. Luckily an engineer had secretly backed up everything daily, outside his job description. Order was restored and the sys admin was fired.
- Peter's takeaway: firing is like war, and war is bad, so try not to do it. But "if you wait until it's obvious to everyone that someone should be fired, it's far too late."
- Surface issues quickly. Details matter and people will disagree often. Concealing disagreements out of discomfort "doesn't fix things," they just sit and do damage.
- "Everyone moving together in lockstep is a big red flag, not an ideal." If disagreements aren't surfacing, key things are being covered up.
- The standard view is that external competition destroys companies. Peter says that may hold in the long run, but most failures happen in the short run, from internal blow-ups during a down period. Companies are "complex entities with complex dynamics," and those dynamics usually blow up before an outside predator strikes.

## Hiring and paying engineers and salespeople

- Salespeople: check how much they've sold, but apply a discount rate because they don't always tell the exact truth. Scott Bannister of IronPort asked sales candidates to submit their W-2s; exaggerators couldn't survive a simple test of what they'd earned in past commissions.
- Value is built over a long term. When eBay bought PayPal, all the PayPal engineers left. eBay had to hire them back as consultants at roughly 3x their old salaries because it couldn't manage the codebase without them. So target people who will stay a long time.
- Pay transparency thought experiment: emailing everyone what each person is paid is "the surest way to blow up a company." He says not to try it. But ask how upset people would be, whether that would be justified, or whether they could be persuaded the pay is reasonable.
- Engineering pay is hard because you compete with Google's prestige and money. Avoid competing on purely financial terms, where you're likely to lose. You need the compelling monopoly narrative from the previous class.

## Keynes, and vertical over horizontal search

- Peter cites Keynes: "When the facts change, I change my mind. What do you do?" But you don't want every new fact to call what you're doing into question.
- Searching for a great business, he asks whether your search space is broad but shallow (every possible business) or focused on one area and drilled deep.
- A super-broad, horizontal search is perhaps fine when first deciding to start a company. Returning to it at later stages is counterproductive. His example: "An internet company talking about being a cleantech company is lost."
- People overrate horizontal search and underestimate the sheer size of the search space. Better to do vertical search and value depth over breadth.
