## Scale is a trade-off: the Wriston legacy and what happens when things get big

- Peter frames the 2019 Wriston lecture around "problems of scale": more of something can be better (quantity), can unlock qualitatively different things (quality), and can supposedly improve the world (a normative claim).
- Walter Wriston scaled Citigroup from a bank serving a city to a bank serving the world (ATMs, credit cards, interstate banking, money-center banks). Peter recalls first hearing the name around 20 years earlier, when a friend told him to talk to Wriston while he was starting PayPal.
- PayPal shared the Wriston ambition: a "libertarian revolution" against central banks, liberating money from government control at a trans-political technological scale.
- Peter adds qualifiers. Margaret Thatcher thought joining the EU in the late 70s would crush the UK unions, then saw it as her worst decision because free trade came with a Brussels bureaucracy that regulated "the size of bananas."
- His core point: scaling gives a qualitative difference, but not always a good one, so one has to think hard about which kinds of scale play out well.

## Crypto vs AI: two competing stories of technological scale

- Peter says there are two tech stories about scale. One is crypto/Bitcoin, which has libertarian potential. The other is AI, big data, centralized databases and surveillance, which "does not seem libertarian at all" ("the big eye of Sauron watching you at all times in all places").
- His framing: "if we say that crypto is libertarian why can't we say that AI is communist."
- On what AI means in practice: the main applications use large data to monitor people and know more about them than they know about themselves. In the limit this could solve the Austrian-economics knowledge problem and make communism work, "maybe not so much as an economic theory but at least as a political theory." He calls it "definitely a Leninist thing."
- Evidence he cites: China loves AI and hates crypto, which he says tells you something. He also notes people are "creeped out" by it and that it should be labeled accurately.
- He suspects the real outcome (communist AI, libertarian crypto or a hybrid) will arrive more slowly than people expect.

## Wisdom of crowds, madness of crowds, and innovation that does not scale

- Peter says a majority vote is good and a supermajority better, but at 99.99% "you're sort of in North Korea." The wisdom of crowds works up to a point, then becomes madness.
- He applies this to Silicon Valley: network effects that were once positive have, at scale, tilted toward something "completely deranged," which he expects to be bad for innovation even if the businesses still work at scale.
- A critique of big tech he is sympathetic to: "innovation does not scale well." As the tech industry (or government) gets bigger, innovation arrives more slowly.
- He calls big science "the older brother who's fallen on hard times" and says it has scaled extremely badly, with groupthink in universities and big-science lies linked with university lies.
- He tells the Bob Laughlin story. After winning the Nobel Prize in physics in the late 90s, Laughlin assumed he finally had academic freedom and set out to investigate Stanford scientists he thought were taking government money for unproductive research. His grad students could not get PhDs and he was defunded.
- Peter's heuristic: "when there's speech that is completely forbidden and questions that are not allowed to be asked normally you should assume that those things are simply true."

## The Obamas' "thousands of amazing universities" as the biggest lie of scale

- Peter's candidate for the biggest lie the Obamas told, bigger than partisan ones like WMD or "if you like your doctor you can keep them," because it is all-encompassing and follows from getting scale wrong.
- Michelle Obama: "we live in a country where there are thousands of amazing universities." Barack Obama: "just because it's not some name brand famous fancy school doesn't mean that you're not going to get a great education there."
- Peter parses this as two lies. If it is not a name-brand school, you get "a diploma that's a dunce hat in disguise." If it is, you probably don't get an education either.
- He notes the Obamas' daughter went to Harvard, which shows they did not believe it.
- His re-scaled description of Harvard: a "Studio 54 nightclub," a tournament that is good for self-esteem and bad for morals. Not criminal, not to be shut down, but it does not deserve a tax deduction.

## Where the US can still scale against China

- Peter says the US strategy for roughly a century (Wilson progressivism, the post-WWII New Dealers' global institutions) was to lean into its bigness and go even bigger. That is a poor strategy against a rival that is also incredibly big.
- He lists four vectors of globalization: goods (free trade), people (migration), capital (finance) and ideas (the internet). The US still has a real advantage in only two, finance and the internet, and even those it distrusts. The US doesn't fully trust the banks or tech companies, and they don't fully trust the US.
- National-champion test: in the 1950s the GM CEO could say "what's good for GM is good for America." Today it would be "inconceivable" for the CEO of Goldman Sachs or Google to say that.
- Goods: seven of the ten largest container ports are in China; the largest US port, Los Angeles, is number eleven. Making the world safe for container shipping tilts toward the Chinese Communist "one world state."
- People: Shenzhen went from 60,000 people in 1980 to about 12 million (a factor of 200) while New York City went from 7.1 million to 8.4 million. Subways cost $3.8 billion per mile in New York versus $400 million per mile in Paris. "We can scale finance, we can scale tech, people we're really bad at scaling."
- Conclusion: the US should not compete with China on scaling people.

## Real estate, AOC and why zoning limits scale

- Peter says he is not a fan of AOC but steel-mans her argument against Amazon coming to New York: it would drive up rents and prices for everyone.
- He points to the Henry George theorem from the late 19th century: in a city that is too restricted and heavily regulated, the elasticity of real estate becomes complete, so any economic gain flows to landlords.
- AOC's mistake, in his view, is that this is also a libertarian argument: if welfare simply goes to landlords as a 100% transfer, one could argue for abolishing it.
- It is also an argument for rethinking migration, because the constraint is how hard it is to build housing and transportation.

## From quantity to quality: settling for greatness instead of American exceptionalism

- Peter's prescription: shift from scaling in size to quality. This means intensive growth rather than more of the same, more IP protection, fewer but real scientists, and fewer but genuinely elite universities. He says this may be where the US has comparative advantage versus China.
- He would not have the US go "sub-scale" like Israel or Switzerland, though he says he would like the US to be a tax haven and doubts it works at US scale.
- On the left, he blames identity politics: it distracts from asking about the scale of the whole country. He argues it is self-contradictory, since "identity" means both what makes you unique and what makes you the same, and "if you start with A and not A you can prove anything." He calls it a monster possibly in its death throes but with a lot of energy left.
- On the right, he says the doctrine to rethink is American exceptionalism, which he compares to the radical monotheism of the Old Testament or the Quran: nothing can be compared to God, and likewise the US can't be measured or compared. The result is being "exceptionally off" in different ways: $3.8 billion-per-mile subways, people who are exceptionally overweight.
- His closing line: "perhaps in the 2020s the United States needs to settle for greatness." In Q&A he says exceptionalism is too abstract to discuss details, that the China rivalry will force the scale questions, and that it is one of the few essentially bipartisan issues.

## Stagnation, automation and the "Google propaganda" about UBI

- Asked about UBI and robots replacing workers, Peter says he disagrees with all the premises and that the questioner "bought the Google propaganda hook line and sinker."
- Data: unemployment is 3.5% and productivity numbers are "still pretty anemic," so runaway technological progress does not show up in the economic data.
- Automation has been going on for 200-250 years since the Industrial Revolution. The rate slowed because the easily automated sectors (farming, manufacturing) were already automated, so even 5% a year of automation in manufacturing matters less as it is a smaller part of the economy.
- The remaining large sectors are non-tradable services (kindergarten teachers, nurses, yoga instructors), which are largely immune to automation and have grown bigger.
- He sees the UBI discussion as a "magic trick" like identity politics, drawing attention away from the real issue: Silicon Valley has not innovated enough, which is why there is political pushback against tech.
- On self-driving cars, Google's biggest item: promised for 10 years, now talked about less, with timelines expanding. He says "going from a horse to car is bigger than going from a car to self-driving car."
- His hope for the country is a return to "intensive growth" and a future that looks different from the present, rather than "an eternal Groundhog Day." Progress has been confined to a "narrow cone" around bits and IT: "the iPhones that distract us from our environment also distract us from how strangely old and unchanging our environment is," such as a 100-year-old subway. He wants innovation across the board rather than "some kind of narrow iPhone app."
