## Founders as extreme insiders and outsiders

- Peter argues founders are not average. He asks whether the founder distribution is a fat-tailed one, then pushes further: perhaps it is an inverted normal distribution, with both tails extremely fat.
- Founders may be complex combinations of extreme insider and extreme outsider at once. He says ideological narratives isolate and reinforce just one side, but "the truth about founders comes from both sides."
- PayPal's six-person founding team as evidence: four were born outside the United States, five were 23 or younger, and four built bombs in high school (Max in the Soviet Union, Yu Pan in China).
- Other PayPal oddities: Russ escaped a trailer park for the one math and science magnet school in Illinois. Max came to the U.S. weeks after the Soviet Union collapsed, "a citizen of no country," so the team decided he couldn't leave the country. Ken took a 66% pay cut to join PayPal instead of going into investment banking.
- Applied examples: Jack Dorsey went from nose ring and unkempt hair (outsider) to Prada suits (insider). Sean Parker had no college, underground hacking, Napster, a Forbes 400 cover, and extreme swings between insider and outsider.

## Four explanations for the founder distribution

Peter lists four explanations for why founders look so extreme:
1. Natural: founders really are different (Max Levchin really has 3 kidneys).
2. Nurtured: cultural feedback makes them different.
3. Self-created: founders exaggerate their own traits.
4. Other-created: everyone else exaggerates them.

- The first two assume the distribution is real. The last two treat it as possibly fictional.
- He says generally all four apply to some extent and are hard to disaggregate. They feed each other: people start different, get nurtured, learn to exaggerate, others exaggerate in turn, and the cycle repeats.
- Examples of cultivated or mythologized personas: Richard Branson's lion-like hair, Lady Gaga's "Born This Way," and Howard Hughes, who pretended to be crazy on the theory that no one would take the time to stop or compete with a crazy person. Hughes also claimed a December 25th, 1905 birthday.

## Mythology and the archaic sacrificial cycle

- Classical myth overlaps with the inverted bell curve: "There are monsters and there are gods. And very often they are one and the same."
- Founding myths follow the pattern. Oedipus was both king and abandoned foreigner. Achilles was perfect except where flawed. Romulus and Remus were orphans raised by wolves who became founders and lawmakers, and Romulus killed his brother over a boundary line.
- In archaic cultures there were no rules and a war of all against all. Peter says the social contract story never happened. The common resolution was to polarize all hostility onto one person (a scapegoat), then sacrifice him, restoring peace. The cycle repeated.
- The scapegoat is omnimalevolent (peace followed his death, so he must have been bad) or omnibenevolent (he died so others could live), probably both. Cultures that ritualized the process lasted longer.
- Finding the victim: Gaelic Scots baked a cake with one piece blackened with charcoal. The Gauls ran a footrace to the battlefield and sacrificed the slowest.
- The perfect scapegoat is both an extreme outsider (so the crowd doesn't see itself in him) and an extreme insider (so he can plausibly be blamed for the community's strife).

## Monarchy, tragedy and revolution

- Peter's working theory is that monarchy originated from scapegoats worshipped before being killed. "Every king was a living god. Every god was a murdered king." The scapegoat simply figured out how to delay his execution.
- Examples: the Aztecs crowned a quasi-god king for a period, then sacrificed him. Pharaohs may have started as stoned scapegoats, with pyramids as the piles of stones. A Zulu king was deposed and killed once white hair and wrinkles showed, which is why Zulu kings wanted hair coloring lotion on first contact with the British.
- Aristotle's view as he presents it: tragedy reduced ordinary people's anger toward successful people by showing that even the greatest have tragic flaws, transforming envy and anger into pity.
- Julius Caesar was a classic insider/outsider, and later Roman emperors had to be a Caesar. Marie Antoinette was an insider turned foreigner. He says all great revolutions are a rapid shift from insider to outsider.
- Robespierre argued the king should not get a trial: a trial meant the king might be innocent, which meant the people might be guilty, which was unthinkable.

## The sacrificial dynamic in modern culture and politics

- The 12-person jury has the same structure: the unlucky 13th is the scapegoat. Peter notes it is never a jury of your peers, since murderers are not judged by 12 murderers.
- Celebrities are anointed kings (King of Rock, King of Pop, Princess of Pop), then torn down. Britney Spears drove $100 million of a $400 million/year paparazzi industry, with 1,000 to 2,000 people chasing her. After death, stars are resurrected as god-kings, e.g. the Forever 27 club.
- Alexander the Great died at 32 after drinking marathons, yet lives on as a conqueror.
- Politics: each side obsesses over the other's scapegoat, when the truth is "some strange combination of both." Lincoln was an extreme outsider turned insider, assassinated with "Sic semper tyrannis." Kennedy was one of the richest presidents (about $1 billion in today's money) with a bootlegger father.
- Lincoln's 1837 Lyceum Address warned that "Towering genius disdains a beaten path" and that ambitious people won't be satisfied maintaining an edifice built by others. Peter's takeaway: "we have to be really careful because such people might exist."

## Tech founders as scapegoats, and the odds

- Bill Gates was treated like a god in the '90s, then ganged up on after Larry Ellison, Scott McNealy and other CEOs started a "We Hate Gates" club and stirred up the DOJ. From Gates's view he was on a winning arc and the haters were envious. Peter says when it turns, it turns fast, and falls are so big it's hard to recover.
- Howard Hughes became the wealthiest person in the U.S. by 45 across movies and aviation, then after the 1946 crash became addicted to painkillers and holed up for 30 years. Steve Jobs (dropout, LSD, phreaking, eccentric diets) was kicked out of Apple and replaced by John Sculley.
- Marc Andreessen sat on a throne-like chair on Time's February 1996 cover, was vilified after Netscape, then recovered. Mary Meeker, the "Queen of the Net," fell after the bubble and returned as a venture capitalist.
- Peter's numbers: 44 American presidents, four (9%) assassinated in office, four more nearly killed. If you want the crown, risk comes with it, and mistakes are hard to spot at the time.

## How founders can avoid becoming the victim

- Startups are structured as monarchy, "more tyrant than mob because it should be." Voting on new things yields lowest-common-denominator results. But pure dictatorship fails because you can't attract people. The best arrangement is a king-like founder who can do more than a democratic ruler but is far from all-powerful.
- 0 to 1 is monarchy; 1 to n is democracy. He frames "the 99% vs. the 1%" as the modern scapegoating mechanism, since the victim must be a single whole person.
- Extend the founding moment. Founding lasts as long as technological innovation continues, so delay the shift to ossified, process-based normality. Peter says this needs "a healthy amount of paranoia": treat every board meeting as a trial, where the board is at best a jury and at worst a mob. Most fatal wounds come from internal, not external, conflict.
- Minimize the title risk. Augustus never called himself king, only "first among equals."
- PayPal, October 2000: burn rate $10 million/month, about 4.5 months of runway. Peter returned as interim CEO after a 6-7 month search. When the board wanted a COO before the IPO (code for the #1 replacement candidate), he got David Sacks in, "perceived to be crazier than I was." He calls this a powerful insurance policy against being deposed.
- Co-founders (Hewlett and Packard, Moore and Noyce, Page and Brin) get in less trouble because a mob board cannot easily isolate a single scapegoat.
- The return of the founder: Apple went through 12 years (1985 to 1997) of conventional CEOs who couldn't build anything new, then changed course under Jobs from 1997 to 2011. Jobs survived the options backdating scandal partly because he was terminally ill, and there is less power in scapegoating someone whose life is waning. "Resurrections are possible. But you can only be resurrected after you die."
- Peter closes by suggesting society should focus more on people who don't play by the rules, since they may be the most important.
