## Startups, monopoly and the capitalism vs. competition distinction

- Peter argues most people treat capitalism and competition as synonyms, but "I think they're antonyms." A capitalist accumulates capital; under intense competition, "all of the capital is competed away."
- Example: the San Francisco restaurant industry is "incredibly competitive but not very capitalistic because no one ever makes any money at it."
- Counter-example: Google is "a very capitalistic company" that has made enormous profits for about a dozen years and has had no real competition since it definitively distanced itself from Yahoo and Microsoft around 2002.
- He advises against joining popular trends: "You do not want to be the fourth online pet food company in the late '90s. You do not want to be the twelfth thin panel solar company in the last decade."
- Once something is a trend, many people do it, so there is lots of competition and little differentiation.

## Mission over trends

- Peter prefers a sense of mission to a tech trend: working on a unique problem that people are not solving elsewhere.
- Example: when Elon Musk started SpaceX, the mission was to go to Mars. Whether or not you agree with it, the problem would not be solved outside SpaceX, and everyone there knew it, which motivated them tremendously.
- He says unique missions beat trends because "every moment in technology happens only once." Each is a unique constellation of technologies and people where the time is right for a new kind of thing.

## Contrarian thinking and convention vs. truth

- Peter's high-level contrarian answer: technology matters far more than globalization. People focus on globalization (copying things that worked) and not enough on technology (doing new things). He wants technology seen as the main driver of progress in the 21st century.
- He calls "What do you believe that very few others do?" a harder question than it sounds.
- The core philosophical question, in his view: what do people agree on merely by convention, and what is true? "We never want to let a convention be a short cut for truth."
- His indirect version of the question: "tell me something that's true that very few people agree with you on."
- Silicon Valley is "laden with conventional thinking," possibly more than the rest of society, because the future is uncertain and there are few markers. When people are uncertain, they look to what others say about the future.
- When "everybody, simply, is listening to everybody else," that is "the definition of a bubble or of a mass psychosocial insanity." Thinking for yourself and breaking through convention matters even more there.

## Investing: concentration over "spray and pray"

- Peter says investors spray and pray not because of portfolio theory but because they lack conviction and are too lazy to work out which companies will ultimately work.
- He objects to treating companies, and especially founders, as lottery tickets. It is morally bad and also bad investing.
- His reasoning: "small number times big number normally equals a small number." Thinking in lottery-ticket terms means you write checks without thinking and lose money.
- The alternative is to concentrate. A small number of positions (he cites five to seven companies per fund at Founders Fund) forces high conviction before writing a check of any size.
- He says Founders Fund's approach is the anti-lottery-ticket approach.

## Failure, success and relentlessness

- Peter says failure is "massively overrated." Most businesses fail for more than one reason, so failure is over-determined. You think it failed for Reason 1 but it failed for Reasons 1 through 5, so the next business fails for Reason 2, then 3, and so on.
- Conclusion: people don't learn much from failure, and it is damaging and demoralizing. The death of every business "is a tragedy," neither a Darwinian nor an educational imperative.
- When asked who he thinks of as successful, he names Mark Zuckerberg and Elon Musk (a serial entrepreneur who has built multiple mega-billion-dollar companies), and also Jeff Bezos.
- What they share: they are relentless. "Every day, they start over, do more, get better at it."
- He rejects the "Facebook was a fluke, right place at the right time" story: the more you know founders like Zuckerberg, the less plausible it is, because of how hard they work, how much planning there was, and how much vision there was from the beginning.

## Don't wait; competition's hidden cost

- Looking back 20 to 25 years, Peter wishes he had known "there was no need to wait." He went to college and law school and worked in law and banking, and only with PayPal did he fully realize you don't have to wait to start something.
- His test: if you have a 10-year plan, ask why you can't do it in 6 months. Sometimes the 10-year path is real, but check whether it's just the story you're telling yourself.
- On what he'd improve in himself: his younger self was "insanely tracked, insanely competitive." Being very competitive makes you good at the thing you compete on, at the expense of other things.
- A competitive chess player gets very good at chess but neglects other things because they focus on beating competitors rather than on something important or valuable.
- He says he has become more self-aware about the problematic nature of competition and rivalries, and doesn't claim to have extricated himself. His framing: "how do I become less competitive in order that I can become more successful."

## Growth, technology and unemployment

- Peter gives a micro and a macro answer on unemployment. Micro: no one-size-fits-all; look at why people are unemployed (e.g. a rust belt factory job that no longer exists vs. a 20-something with a poorly advised major and a mountain of debt).
- Macro: the economy needs more growth. At 4 percent annual US growth, he thinks these problems would broadly get solved, but growth has been 1 to 2 percent for 7 or 8 years, which is not enough to overcome structural unemployment.
- He holds that technology is the key driver of growth, especially in a developed nation like the US, so accelerating technological progress and innovation increases growth and opportunity.
- Zero to One has an alarmist and a hopeful side. Alarmist: if we don't innovate more, "we will have a bleak and stagnant century ahead of us." Hopeful: the great ideas have not all been found, the low-hanging fruit has not all been picked, and "there are many great secrets left to be unlocked in the decades ahead."
- He frames the issue as cultural: what we need to do "to get back to the future."

## Fighting death, education and Bitcoin

- Peter says the problem he is most passionate about is making real, continued progress against aging and death. About 100,000 people a day die, mostly from diseases linked to old age.
- He says people are in denial ("not a problem") or acceptance ("nothing you can do"), which are opposite extremes with the same passive effect. He wants an active mode: "there is nothing more natural for us than to fight death."
- On education, he dislikes the abstraction and favors learning over credentialing. He asks whether college is a four-year party (consumption), an investment, insurance, or a tournament, and compares elite universities to an exclusive nightclub like Studio 54.
- He sees engineering as "the opposite of education" because it is a specific skill. He expects less one-size-fits-all education, with big institutions "delivering less and less and charging more and more."
- Quoting a friend, he compares education today to the Catholic Church on the eve of the Reformation: people believe salvation requires a college diploma, and his answer is that "you're going to have to figure out your salvation on your own."
- He defends going to Stanford for undergrad and law school as not hypocritical because he never claimed one size fits all. The oddity is a society where the most talented all get tracked to the same elite colleges, subjects and careers. If doing it over, he'd ask whether he was doing it for prestige or from passion for the work.
- On Bitcoin: PayPal created a payment system but never quite a new currency, while Bitcoin created a new currency but not yet a new payment system. People speculate on it as a store of value rather than transact, so it must become a payment system too.
- His daily habit: sustained conversations with smart friends and long-time colleagues, not "an MTV approach where you talk to a new smart person every day."
