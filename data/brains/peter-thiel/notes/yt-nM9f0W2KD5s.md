## Stagnation began around 1970 and is the core problem

- Peter dates the era of relative stagnation and slowed progress back to about 1970, so close to half a century.
- 1968 is roughly when the progress story still held; by 1973 it "somehow was over."
- Cultural markers he cites: the Moon landing in July 1969 with Woodstock three weeks later, and the 1973 oil shocks.
- In his telling, the world of bits (computers, internet, mobile, software) is the big exception while the world of atoms has been much slower for about 50 years.
- At Stanford in the late 1980s, most engineering fields (nuclear, aero-astro, chemical, mechanical) were already bad fields to enter; computer science was the good one.
- Even within tech the mood has shifted. He notes the iPhone looks the same as it did seven or eight years ago, and self-driving cars are further away than people expected.
- His line: "We've been working on the Star Trek computer in Silicon Valley but we don't have anything else from Star Trek," no warp drive and no transporter.
- He has pushed this stagnation thesis for the better part of a decade. In 2008-2010 it was fringe even in Silicon Valley; now he sees many people partly coming around.

## Nature or culture: why progress slowed, and where he sees room

- He goes back and forth between two explanations: nature ran out of useful ideas, or the culture and institutions became deranged. He thinks it is probably a dialogue between both.
- On physics he is pessimistic. Quarks do not matter for chemistry, which is what matters on a human level.
- On biology he thinks much more could be done, and the cultural explanation seems plausible: it is heavily regulated, and the cultural institutions for research are restrictive.
- One speculation: biology selected for people with weaker math, while good math students went into physics, math or chemistry.
- He thinks it is optimistic to say "we could have done better." There is more space on the frontier than the world of bits, and we have "just chosen not to" advance in atoms.
- He also sees extreme hyperspecialization as a core problem. In 1800 Goethe could understand nearly everything; in 1900 Hilbert could understand nearly all of mathematics.
- Today, each narrow field of experts polices itself and talks up its own progress (string theory, cancer research, quantum computing). Outsiders lack the authority to say "not much is happening."
- His political cut: specialization should make you suspicious, because it is harder to evaluate and so easier to exaggerate or lie.
- Institutional rule on polymaths: "no polymaths allowed." Saying you like computer science and also music or the Hebrew Bible signals you are not serious.

## Universal institutional failure: embedded growth expectations and pathology

- Eric's framing, which Peter engages: institutions were built on expected growth, and when growth ran out they became pathological. Law firms and universities are pyramids (partners hire associates, professors train grad students).
- Peter says the honest alternative was to dial expectations down. A university could have shrunk PhD programs, made them shorter and more selective. A law firm could have kept the same share making partner at lower pay.
- That honest reset was not the path taken. The incentive was to pretend it works, because pretending also signals that you are one of the few who succeed in it.
- Analogy: 20,000 people move to Los Angeles to become movie stars and about 20 make it. The ones who do are expected to say "it opened all the doors."
- Boom-bust cycles masked stagnation: the 90s tech bubble, the 2000s housing bubble, and in his view a government debt bubble in the 2010s. He notes California's last three recessions were more severe than the country's and its recoveries steeper, so volatility got read as dynamism.
- His simplified decade scorecard: 70s down, 80s up, 90s up, 2000s down, net flat but feeling exciting.
- Each decade had its own narrative lie: the "new economy" (lying about growth) in the 90s, the "Great Moderation" (lying about volatility) in the 2000s, and "secular stagnation" (lying about real interest rates) in the 2010s. All three are bullish stock narratives despite sounding less bullish over time.
- On the 1930s: he argues the real tailwind then was technology (aviation, talkies, plastics, secondary oil recovery, household appliances). By 1939, three times as many Americans had cars as in 1929. The crash was largely financial mismanagement of a period of real innovation.
- Two parallel systems exist: an institutional account and the open podcast and internet narrative. He expects the institutional account to fail eventually because it is so incorrect, and bets on "a decade not a century."
- On the mood in Silicon Valley: people are angrier than the stock market would suggest, because it is a look-ahead function and growth going forward is harder to see.

## The college system, student debt, and the Thiel Fellowship

- Student debt in the US was about $300 billion in 2000 and is now roughly $1.6 to 1.7 trillion. His quip: "1.6 trillion dollars worth of lies about how great the system is."
- Debt is "pernicious" early in a career. Owing $100,000 out of the gate either demotivates you or pushes you into higher-paying, less creative professions.
- Individually, going to an elite university may still make sense, though not number 100. He says it works individually even if it does not work for the country as a whole.
- Why the 2008 crisis was a watershed: tracking into banking and consulting blew up, and Gen X parents now tend to agree with his critique, while baby boomer parents 15 years ago would not have.
- His reform ideas on debt: make it dischargeable in bankruptcy, with the university bearing part of the loss so there is local accountability. He says the left-wing alternative is to socialize the debt.
- His analogy: elite undergraduate is like junior high football, probably not damaging by itself but not going anywhere. If you keep playing football in high school, college and the pros, it just gets bad, and the more successful you are the worse it works.
- The teleology (a clear purpose for each stage) is broken: undergrad prepares you for grad school, grad school for a postdoc, and the track does not work. He was most intensely motivated in high school because the goal was clear.
- On PhD programs, he cites a friend in Stanford's economics department. About 30 students start each year, take six to eight years, and by the end of year one the faculty has an implicit ranking in which the top three or four get academic jobs and the rest do not. "We're pretending to be kind to people and we're actually being cruel."
- A grad-student pipeline with more people than the field can use creates a Malthusian struggle: "ten graduate students in a chemistry lab and you have to have a fistfight for a Bunsen burner."
- He is not arguing for zero people in basic science. He wants tighter gates leading to real positions.
- The Thiel Fellowship worked as a "hack the prestige status" move: it was as hard or harder to get than admission to a top university. He says it is "not scalable."
- He thinks a college equivalency degree is a great idea but hard to implement, since HR people who went to good colleges have something like "Stockholm Syndrome" and would resist it.
- On the BA: "the BA is not as valuable as it looks. I also think the PhD is not as valuable as it looks."

## What he would change: housing, growth, and automation skepticism

- On inequality he points to runaway housing costs as the main driver of the last 20-30 years, and thinks stagnation matters more than inequality.
- Housing under strict zoning has become the baby boomers' retirement nest egg rather than a place to live. He would want to dial this back massively, an intergenerational transfer that hurts boomer asset prices but helps young people form families and households.
- Without growth, he argues, parliamentary democracy stops working: when the pie stops growing, the legislative horse-trading becomes zero-sum.
- He says the democratic systems of the last 250 years mapped onto rapid growth, and that the 1930s no-growth experiment produced fascist or communist systems.
- A world without growth is either "much more violent or much more deformed" (more state violence, zero-sum wars, or a pacified population "smoking marijuana all day").
- He says growth is not sufficient but is "our only chance"; without it he finds it hard to see a good future.
- On automation, he sees it as the same question as productivity growth, and doubts it is happening. Most US jobs are non-tradable services (waiter, yoga instructor, nurse, kindergarten teacher) that resist automation.
- Unemployment was around 3.6-3.7%, wage pressure was low and productivity numbers were weak, which he reads as evidence that automation is not happening at scale.
- Retail, with Amazon, is the main sector he accepts as under real pressure.
- He would worry more about "the lack of automation than excess automation." His comparison: secretaries gave way to executive assistants, not fewer assistants.
- On UBI: if automation is real, it shows up in productivity numbers and then something like UBI might be needed. If it is not happening and you do UBI, you "blow up the economy."
- Order matters: with 3% GDP growth sustainably, without fudging the numbers, there would be much more room for social programs. He is "very uncomfortable starting with the social programs without the growth."
- On redistribution rhetoric: he is skeptical, since redistribution from the powerful to the powerless is almost self-contradictory in practice and tends to end up as a shell game. He notes that California moved from a broadly middle-class centrist state in 1977 to a heavily Democratic, very unequal one.

## Violence, mimetic theory, and scapegoating

- Peter says his central preoccupation is reducing violence. He credits René Girard, whom he studied under at Stanford, for the framework.
- Girard's mimetic theory: humans imitate, first language, then desire, and mimetic desire leads to conflict, mass violence and mass insanity. Mimesis both enables culture and is dangerous.
- Existential effect on him: as a libertarian-leaning undergrad he believed in radically autonomous individuals. Girard corrected that and made him see how hyper-mimetic and hyper-tracked he had been at Stanford.
- Preference falsification fits this: people go along with what others think, then shifts happen much faster than expected.
- He recommends Girard's "Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World" as the magisterial work, part psychology, part anthropology, part history.
- Scapegoating works only when it is not understood: the more people see the mechanism, the less well it works. Girard's metaphor: the sacred is like phlogiston and violence like oxygen.
- He argues modernity responds by scapegoating the scapegoaters (going after historical oppressors), which is complicated enough that it still works.
- Archaic violence was a way to limit violence, "a little bit satanic but not very." He says the nation-state both contains and channels violence, and that a world that tries to abolish violence outright could need enormous violence or end up in chaos.
- His recollection of Girard's view on communism: Western intellectuals were communists because of the millions being killed, as a tell of utopia-building. Communism lost appeal after Stalin died in 1953.
- His line on useful idiots: sympathetic people on the left were likely intelligent but "were somehow still always the useful idiots," and in countries where communists took power it did not work out for them.
- On Germany and Jewish culture: he says pre-WWI Germany had unusually well-integrated Judaism with great synergy, and that the social democratic response to the Holocaust (everyone equal, nothing changes) does not capture the loss of "the best people." He was born in Germany and emigrated at about age one, then grew up partly in Namibia in a German-speaking school.
- His contrast: "California's optimistic but desperate and Germany's pessimistic but comfortable."

## Preference falsification, political correctness, and Trump

- Peter says he believed Trump was more likely to be elected than most people did, because of preference falsification.
- His anecdote: a prominent Silicon Valley angel investor told him a week before the 2016 election that he would vote for Trump but would tell people he was voting for Gary Johnson. Johnson's support dropped from about 6% to 2% in the final month, which Peter says largely went to Trump.
- He notes the Johnson pattern echoed Ross Perot in 1992 and John Anderson in 1980: third-party candidates as a gateway.
- He wonders whether people clicking on the 538 polling model several times a day was a tell that they sensed it was not a done deal.
- He sees political correctness as arguably the biggest political problem: people are uncomfortable saying what they think. His example is Silicon Valley as a one-party state.
- Two kinds of one-party state: one where everyone believes the same thing, and one where 85% believe it and the other 15% pretend to.
- His rule of thumb: 51% agreement suggests truth, 70-80% is almost surely right, but 99.99% means you have shifted from democratic truth to "North Korean insanity."
- He argues political correctness starts with correctness about politics, but freezes out debate on more important questions. "We're certainly not going to have a debate about string theory if we can't even have a common sense debate about politics."
- He thinks it distracts from stagnation. The left's distraction is identity politics (looking at parts instead of the whole). The right's is American exceptionalism, which he compares to the radical monotheism of the Old Testament God, so exceptional that you can say nothing about it.
- His examples of "exceptional" US problems: overweight people, very expensive subway systems, sociopathic universities, student debt, a self-destructive trade regime. He distinguishes exceptionalism from greatness, since many countries can be great and greatness is measurable on a scale.
- On Trump and foreign policy: he spoke at the 2016 Republican convention and at the Washington Press Club about a month before the election. He argued Trump would break from the neoconservative and neoliberal interventionism of Bush and Obama, which Hillary would likely have continued.
- He says it was important in the primaries when Trump spoke out against the Iraq war, and he does not think the Republicans will go back to the Bush foreign policy. He admits it has not moved as far from interventionism as he would like, but directionally it has.
- His frame: the president is like the mayor of the country but the dictator of the world, so foreign policy is the key lens.
- He concedes Trump does not act presidential the way previous presidents did.
- On the Democrats, he says they can win in 2020 but need more of an agenda than telling Republicans to "hurry up and die."

## Gawker, transparency, and waking people up

- On Gawker, he describes it as a "hate factory" and "scapegoating machine" that was powerful because people did not understand how it worked. Once the mechanism is seen, it is less powerful, and he says he expected to win the litigation.
- On transparency he sees a real privacy-transparency tradeoff. In theory it is a panopticon that lights everyone equally; in practice it is a weapon aimed at certain people, and "first strike" transparency is powerful. His example: the NSA against Snowden, and the NSA against Assange.
- Full transparency assumes people can pay attention to everything equally, which he calls politically implausible.
- It also causes self-censorship: if everything is transparent, politicians will not experiment with bold ideas and will be conventional and curated. The Supreme Court still does not televise hearings, which he suspects is the right call.
- Younger generations feel watched by "the great eye of Sauron," which he suspects changes behavior.
- On violence, individuals versus centralized power: transparency means more state control; privacy means more individual violence in the dark. You should not be too categorical about either.
- On climate and growth: he asks what you do about China and India, who are catching up and have far to go. He says Europe is about 8% of global emissions, and that "magical political thinking" (a California carbon tax inspiring China and India to follow) does not work.
- His alternatives: energy sources that are not carbon-intensive, possibly engineered carbon sinks and geoengineering, and being more open to nuclear power. He adds that you cannot have an economy without an environment, but may also not have an environment without an economy.
- On technology risk: he takes fears of AI weapons, bioweapons and nanotech seriously but says "I'm more scared of the one where nothing happens," meaning stagnation.
- On how to wake people up from institutional betrayal: the wrong way is nihilism (all paths blocked, nothing can be done, low energy). He says you must couple the critique with portals, other paths to take.
- What he would put back into institutions: basic science, as a nonprofit knowledge base for humanity. He says he does not yet know how to do it without institutions, and wants institutional reform so politicians do not replace scientists (a Gresham's law dynamic).
- He is deeply interested in longevity and radical life extension. His disappointment in nonprofits pushed him toward investing in biotech companies, though he worries some basic research is being sidestepped because it is too hard.
- He cites Aubrey de Grey, whom he funded, as an example of someone treated as crazy who nevertheless has a lot on the ball, noting his recent solution to a hard math problem.
