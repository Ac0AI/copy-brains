## Peter's view: technological progress has stalled outside computers

- Peter distinguishes the developed world from emerging markets. In China, India, Africa and Latin America "there's zero need for innovation," because those countries only need to copy things that work.
- He argues technology is "the only way that things get better in the developed world" (US, Western Europe, Japan), so that is where its health matters.
- His headline data point is US wages. Median wages have been stagnant from 1973 to 2012, while from 1932 to 1972 they went up by a factor of six.
- The earlier period matched real technological progress: better cars, aeronautics, supersonic jets, computers.
- He says this stagnation also shows up in socialist countries like France and in Japan, so he does not treat it as only a problem of capitalist countries.
- When Eric Schmidt blames bad politics, entitlements and demographics, Peter answers that technology "should be a large enough force that could power change" even with a somewhat screwed-up political system. In his view, treating everything as politics concedes that technology is no longer a big driver.

## Computers progressed while energy and "the world of stuff" did not

- Peter simplifies the last 40 years as "incremental but relentless progress on the computer side" and "basically no progress on energy."
- Oil was about $2 a barrel in 1973 and was north of $100 in 2012. He calls this "a catastrophic failure of energy innovation" that was offset by computer innovation.
- His agriculture example: the Green Revolution of the 50s and 60s roughly doubled world food production in line with population growth. It has decelerated dramatically over the last 40 years, which he links to a decade of rising food prices.
- On travel speeds, the Concorde was decommissioned in 2003. Counting low-tech airport security, he says travel speeds are back to about 1960 levels.
- His explanation is government. The FDA charges about $1.3 billion per new drug. Supersonic jets are banned for noise. Nuclear plants, fusion and thorium are not allowed. He says "we've basically outlawed everything having to do with the world of stuff and the only thing you're allowed to do is in the world of bits."
- That is why computers and finance were the two areas of big innovation in 40 years. He expects finance to be outlawed too, leaving only computers.
- He flags the risk that fracking could be outlawed even as Schmidt cites energy independence as an example of innovation.

## Moore's law is good for computers, but is it good for people?

- Peter says Moore's law is "good if you're a computer," and asks how it translates into economic progress for humans.
- He says he is in favor of the computer companies and wants more of them, but computing is the exception to the rule that there is not enough innovation, and the specific should not be confused with the general.
- He also pushes back on the claim that social media drove the Arab Spring. He says the fundamental driver was food prices rising 30 to 50% in the previous year, which left people facing starvation. Calling it a Twitter and Facebook revolution is, to him, "smug and complacent."
- He notes that Apple, Twitter and Facebook are profitable but do not employ the numbers that General Motors did in the 1950s.

## Google as a monopoly with cash it cannot invest

- Peter says Google has a "worldclass monopoly" in search. Monopoly is legal as long as it is not used to tie in or oppress other companies.
- He challenges Schmidt that Google holds $30 to 50 billion in cash, has no good ideas for investing it in technology, and so accepts roughly 0% interest. In his words, the cash "gets burned away over time through inflation."
- He argues that with an accelerating technological world and 0% interest rates, a company should be able to invest all its money in things that return it many times over. If it can't, that signals a lack of ideas.
- He credits Amazon as the only big tech company reinvesting all its profits, saying it has "enough of a vision of the future." Google, he says, does more than Microsoft or Apple, citing self-driving cars and asteroid mining.
- His harder charge is that the intellectually honest move would be to say Google is no longer a technology company. The search technology was developed a decade ago, so investing in Google is "a bet that there will be no one else who will come up with a better search technology."
- He compares it to a bank with enormous cash flow that cannot pay a dividend, because returning $30 billion would admit it is no longer a tech company. He says Microsoft and others are hoarding cash for the same reason.

## What Peter would change: imagination, long-term incentives and "vertical" growth

- Asked how he would improve the technology environment, he says the problem is multifaceted:
  - Regulatory and political issues.
  - "A failure of imagination on the part of people starting things."
  - Incentives that are too short term, so people cannot invest in multi-year projects. He says Google does this relatively better than most tech companies.
- He puts his nonprofit donations into technology projects that are not profitable but could be valuable in other ways.
- He calls taking technology as critical, not being complacent, and continuing to push "an incredible imperative."
- On globalization, he says it is a good trend but has nothing to do with technology. It was happening in the 19th century and is about the spread of ideas, and it would improve China and India.
- What he is interested in is "vertical growth, intensive growth, doing fundamentally new things." He says that needs a change in financial, corporate, cultural and political priorities.

## Finance as a symptom of blocked innovation

- Asked whether finance has crowded out long-term investment, Peter rejects the premise that there is a trade-off between finance and other innovation.
- He says it is easy to be anti-finance, but the economy became lopsided toward it "fundamentally because people weren't able to do other things."
- His example is why rocket scientists went to Wall Street in the 90s to create new financial products. He says it was because they couldn't get jobs as rocket scientists, since rockets and supersonic airplanes could not be built, not because finance paid too much.
- His analogy is that brilliant people in the Soviet Union became grandmaster chess players because "they weren't allowed to do anything else," not because there was something wrong with chess.

## Education as a bubble and the debt burden on a generation

- Peter lists a series of destructive bubbles: the tech bubble in the 90s, the housing and finance bubble in the 2000s, and a government bubble now, which he finds strange because government is "so clearly dysfunctional."
- He says that "if there's anything in our society that's a bubble it is education."
- His figures:
  - College costs have tripled or quadrupled, a roughly 300% increase after inflation since 1980.
  - It's not clear quality has gone up at all.
  - The number of years it takes to recoup college debt has been steadily increasing since 2000.
  - College was an increasingly good investment through 2000, but since then the gap between college and high school has been roughly flat because debt kept rising.
- He says Millennials are being turned into "something like indentured servants." Bush rewrote the bankruptcy laws in 2005 so college debt cannot be escaped even through personal bankruptcy.
- He calls education "hyped beyond belief," and says that if education is limited to engineering degrees, it could be cut back by about 90%.
