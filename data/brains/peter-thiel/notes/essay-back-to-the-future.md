## Decadence as a four-part diagnosis (Douthat's framework, which Peter endorses)

- Peter reviews Ross Douthat's *The Decadent Society* and treats it as a well-timed book on the central question of the 2020s: "How do we get back to the future?"
- "Decadence" here does not mean indulgence. It means "stagnation and complacency, a dissipation of creative energy, a jaded will merely to muddle through."
- Douthat's four aspects, which Peter presents as accurate:
  - **Stagnation**: technological and economic mediocrity.
  - **Sterility**: declining birth rates.
  - **Sclerosis**: institutional failure.
  - **Repetition**: cultural exhaustion.
- Peter says each aspect feeds the others:
  - Legal sclerosis is likely a bigger obstacle to flying cars than any engineering problem.
  - Poor transportation makes it more expensive to raise a family, which lowers birth rates.
  - Aging brings risk-aversion and arrests creativity.
- He calls it "a young man's book." Baby boomers wince at the word "decadence" because they cannot bear to think of themselves as old or of the society they dominate as dysfunctional. Douthat sees the sclerotic institutions clearly because his vision is not "distorted by out-of-date memories from a more functional era."

## Stagnation, measured by jets

- Peter uses aviation as the test case. Boeing's 707 (introduced 1958) cruised at 977 km/h and fed the optimistic self-understanding of a society proud to have entered the Jet Age.
- Over sixty years later, Boeing's latest plane, the 737 MAX, cruises at 839 km/h, "to say nothing of its more catastrophic limitations."
- The 707 was a success and the MAX "looks like a failure." The 747 (debuted 1969) is still flying fifty years later, which he treats as a sign of decadence.
- He endorses Douthat's line that over two generations "the only truly radical change has taken place in the devices we use for communication and entertainment," so that one 19th-century invention (running water) "still looms larger in our everyday existence than most of what we think of as technological breakthroughs nowadays."
- His test for progress is simple: look up from your phone and compare the streets and the expectations of life to 1969. Anyone can tell whether they have been transformed for the better, even if few can evaluate something like string theory.
- His summary of the physical world outside MIT's PR materials: it appears much the same as in 1969, "just with faster computers and uglier cars."

## Sterility, sclerosis and repetition

- **Sterility**: Peter says low birth rates are usually treated as personal preference or as a drag on the economy (hence an argument for immigration). He credits Douthat for going further: missing kids weaken a society's connection to the future. This explains a key current in "populist" skepticism of the elite consensus. Immigration replaces some missing workers but heightens anxieties about inheritance and loss. He praises Douthat for focusing on the dynamics that explain "our unique moment" rather than inveighing against an age-old evil.
- **Sclerosis**: government cannot get anything done. Time worsens it as:
  - Popular programs become informal social contracts that are nearly impossible to reform.
  - The administrative state gets "barnacled" by interest groups.
  - Proliferating regulations handcuff administrators.
  - Peter's gloss: "the New Deal could only happen once," and whatever competence existed at its experimental dawn no longer exists.
- **Repetition**: the fifties through the eighties each had distinctive styles in design, clothing, music and art. From the nineties to now feels like "one big remix": remakes of remakes, from J. J. Abrams remaking *Star Wars* to Scorsese remaking himself.
  - Boomers were the last rebellious generation, with a real adversary: refreshed bourgeois norms, a Christian revival, patriotic history burnished by WWII victory, and a common culture bound by radio, TV and mass media.
  - With the old family structure smashed, religion in decline, patriotism passé and the cultural marketplace fragmented, would-be rebels have nothing to resist, so they "playact the battles of a previous age."

## Science as the engine nobody can measure

- Peter says Douthat rightly stresses material stagnation but does not treat science deeply, the "ultimate engine of both technology and the economy."
- He cites Leo Strauss, who in 1958 noted an "appalling discrepancy" between the exactness of science itself and our incapacity to evaluate its progress.
  - We know the fine-structure constant to a dozen significant digits.
  - Ask the authorities to measure the health of science as an enterprise and the reply is "vehement and very vague." Example: the former MIT president claimed in *Science* (2018), "Never has the pace of discovery been so rapid, the range of achievements so broad, and the changing nature of our understanding so revolutionary."
- Such claims are hard to challenge because of the extreme specialization and implicit invincibility of thousands of scientific subfields. He asks whether things are "as rosy as the grant writers insist."

## Rejecting the establishment excuses for stagnation

- When a university president (almost all of them boomers) takes stagnation seriously, Peter says he hears two talking points:
  1. Spend more money on education and research.
  2. Progress is harder now because the "low-hanging fruit" is gone and we are up against the limits of nature.
- Peter, siding with Douthat, rejects both: more of the same is not enough, and stagnation is not a fate imposed by the universe.
- He frames this as "choosing agency over boomer complacency."

## Critique: why warp drive is the wrong goal

- Peter's one criticism: Douthat does not press his theme urgently enough. His exits are religious revival and technological acceleration toward interstellar travel, ending "So down on your knees—and start working on that warp drive."
- Peter says the urbane tone undermines the argument, as if winking that the author does not take it seriously.
- Warp drive is hard to take seriously because its physics are so far beyond current knowledge that it debilitates would-be researchers, and there are "reasonable doubts about the friendliness of faraway foreign species" (joking: "Some of them, I assume, are good aliens").
- His principle: "A renaissance will require motivational goals. To be motivational, a goal must be both ambitious and achievable."
- He hesitates to put faith in distant star systems: "The fountain of youth and the Tree of Life are not waiting for us in Tau Ceti or on Planet Vulcan in 40 Eridani." We need not be "loyal to the earth" like Zarathustra, but should expect salvation to be worked out in the solar system we have been given.

## The prescription: difficult-but-possible goals and moderation

- **For technologists**: pursue goals that are difficult but possible:
  - Cures for cancer and Alzheimer's.
  - Compact nuclear reactors and fusion power.
- **For statesmen**: deconstruct the corrupt institutions that have falsely claimed to pursue those goals on our behalf.
- Peter calls it a paradox that "the path to radical progress begins with moderation." Extreme optimism and fatalistic pessimism look like opposites but both end in apathy: if things were sure to improve or bound to collapse, our actions would not matter.
- He believes our actions matter, and "matter eternally."
- His closing warning: if we do not find the narrow, moderate path, "stagnation and decadence were all that kept immoderate men from stumbling into the apocalypse."
