## Thiel's law: a startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed

- Peter states "Thiel's law": a startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed.
- Beginnings are qualitatively different from later life, like the origin of the universe or of a country. The US Founders got most things right, but the ones they got wrong, such as Alaska having 2 senators like California despite roughly 1/50th of the population, can't really be fixed.
- This is the logic behind the Founders Fund name. If you focus on the founding and get it right, you have a chance. If you don't, you'll be lucky at best.
- At Google, a debate is settled by "The Founders have scientifically determined that x is true." Peter's point is that all the science is done at the founding moment.
- He ties the founding to 0 to 1 (technology) versus 1 to n (globalization). The founding may last as long as the company's technical innovation continues. Founders should arguably stay in charge while the paradigm is 0 to 1. Once it shifts to 1 to n, the founding is over and executives should execute.
- Rules can't prevent every breakdown, but the early stuff should be as right as possible.

## Trust and alignment: the 2x2 and the founding team

Peter's matrix crosses high-trust or low-trust people with low-alignment or high-alignment structure:

- **High trust, low alignment (anarchy):** Google from 2000 to about 2007, where talented people worked on all sorts of projects with few constraints.
- **Low trust, high alignment (totalitarianism):** Foxconn. People are "sort of slaves", the company installs suicide nets, yet it is very productive and "sort of works".
- **Low trust, low alignment (dog-eat-dog):** an investment bank is the example. Avoid it.
- **High trust, high alignment (the ideal):** people trust each other and share a good culture, with a structure that has them "rowing in the same direction, and not by accident".

One factor dominates all others: whether the founders are aligned with each other. If they aren't, "it will blow up the company" and nothing else will work.

- Investors should focus on how well founders know each other, how they interact, and whether skills and personalities are complementary. Any fissure in the founding team is amplified later.
- Example: in 1998 Peter made an early investment in a company Luke Nosek started with someone he met at a networking event. Luke was a chaotic, brilliant thinker and the other founder was a "by the books" deliverables person. It was doomed and went poorly.
- Choosing co-founders is like marriage. Marrying the first person you meet at the slot machines in Vegas might hit the jackpot, but chances are it won't.
- The founding team is "the single most important question in assessing an early startup", including how founders split equity and how well they work together.

## Set up as a Delaware C corporation

Peter's answer is categorical: a startup should be a Delaware C corporation.

- Incorporating separates personal and company affairs, lets others in, lets you sell equity, and adds legitimacy. "Larry Page and Friends" might work today but would not have in 1997.
- **S corps:** one class of shareholders only (no preferred vs. common), no stock options, a cap on shareholder count, and no IPO. They only suit companies that won't scale past a point.
- **LLCs:** similar to C corps, but issuing preferred stock, granting options or going public gets problematic. Specially drafted agreements can do it in theory, but in practice "it doesn't work so well".
- **Double taxation is the C corp's cost:** on $100 of income at a 39.2% corporate rate, $39.20 goes to the government, leaving $60.80. Taxed at 35% on the top personal tier, that is $21.28 more, leaving $39.52.
- That is why consulting and law firms aren't usually C corps. But C corps are easier to take public or sell, and acquirers (usually C corps themselves) value you as if you were double taxed anyway, so being an LLC doesn't make you a more attractive target.
- Over 50% of C corps incorporate in Delaware because its business law is clear and well understood, its chancery courts are fast and predictable, and its judges are good. There is also signaling value, since everybody does it.

## Ownership, possession and control

- Peter separates three things:
  - **Ownership:** who has equity and how much.
  - **Possession:** who operates the company day to day (job titles).
  - **Control:** who formally controls it, meaning the board, "most of whom really don't know your business that well".
- The DMV analogy: as a voter you indirectly control the DMV, but the window clerks and managers in possession decide whether they help you. Telling them they are your boss under representative government won't work. That gap between control and possession is misalignment.
- In many parts of the world these three can't be separated. If the majority owner also has exclusive control, minority shareholding has no benefit, so there are no minority shareholders and viable businesses are hard to build.
- Separating them creates its own misalignments, between and within the groups.
- A sole founder has 100% of all three and is fully aligned. Even one co-founder adds imbalance, though with two people it is still mostly aligned. Employees get lots of possession, small ownership and minimal control. Investors complicate it further.
- The founder's job is to prevent misalignment and fix it where it arises.

## Equity, salaries and vesting as alignment tools

- Equity is the classic alignment tool because everyone benefits from a higher share price.
- Cash salaries and bonuses create opportunities for misalignment. Founders Fund's rule of thumb is that **no CEO should be paid more than $150k per year**.
  - The lower a venture-backed CEO's salary, the better the company tends to do.
  - If diligence were reduced to one question, it would be the CEO's salary. If it's more than $150k, do not invest.
  - A low CEO salary makes the CEO believe the equity will be worth a lot, and it effectively caps everyone's pay, creating an equity-focused culture.
  - At $300k a year, when something goes wrong the CEO's incentive is to keep the well-paying job rather than fix things.
- "Either on the bus or off the bus" (Ken Kesey). Part-time work, other jobs, and consultants or advisors doing important work are big red flags because the equity matters less to them.
  - Exception that proves the rule: Peter passed on YouTube in summer 2005 because the founders were working on it part-time. Things then took off and Sequoia got the investment.
  - Never hire consultants. Their cash or immediately vesting equity gives bad incentives, and they break the bus metaphor.
- **Vesting standard:** 4 years, 25% at a 1-year cliff, then 1/48th per month for the remaining 3 years. Someone who leaves before a year gets nothing, though people who didn't cause trouble often keep what they earned.
- Founders need vesting too. A fully vested founder can quit and leave the co-founder "working for 2 people". Typically 20-25% vests immediately as credit for work before the first financing, and the rest vests over time.

## Equity mechanics: what actually matters

- **Common stock** is a fraction of ownership. The share count is meaningless without shares outstanding: 200k/10m equals 20m/2bn, and "2% is 2%".
- **Options** are the right to buy at an exercise price set at grant. It is set at or above fair market value to avoid an immediate tax event, so options are worth zero on day one. If FMV is $20 and the option is priced at $10, the $10 gap is taxable compensation.
- **ISOs** expire 10 years after grant or 3 months after an employee leaves, which locks employees in. They have favorable tax treatment. Any option that isn't an ISO is an NSO, where gains until exercise are ordinary income.
- **Restricted stock** is sold to an employee at a heavy discount, and the company can buy it back at that price. It is a mirror image of an option grant because the buyback right shrinks over time (a reverse vest).
- The takeaway: "What matters is your share of the company." Share count, share price and option pool share are irrelevant. This is 3rd grade arithmetic, and many smart technical people fail to divide, perhaps from anchoring that makes 1m/1bn look better than 1k/1m.
- "The surest way to blow up a company is to circulate a spreadsheet listing everyone's equity stake." Equity is keyed to when you join, not only what you can do. At eBay, secretaries who joined three years earlier may have made 100x what their Stanford MBA bosses did. Later, more important hires see that as unfair, and you can't please everyone.

## Fundraising math, convertible notes and Series A

- Angels must be accredited (net worth over $1m or income over $200k). Investors get preferred stock with protective rights, while founders and employees hold common. As a rule of thumb, common is priced around 10% of preferred at a Series A.
- **Angel math example:**
  - Two founders hold 1m shares each, bought at $0.001 per share.
  - An angel invests $200k at $1/share for 200k shares.
  - 6 employees and 2 consultants each get 100k shares at $0.10, 800k shares in total.
  - That makes 3m shares outstanding and a $3m valuation. The angel owns 6.7%, the 8 hires own 26.7% together, and each founder owns 1/3.
- **Why convertible debt can be better than an equity round:**
  - It postpones the valuation question to Series A investors, which helps when angels can't value companies.
  - It mathematically eliminates a down round, which matters where angels systematically overvalue hot companies such as Y Combinator ones.
  - It is cheaper and faster than equity rounds, which cost $30k to $40k in Silicon Valley.
  - Two structures: cap and discount (for example a $4m cap and a 20% discount), or no cap and no discount with warrants or options accumulating per round.
- **Series A process:** a term sheet, then about a month of final diligence on people, finances and technology, then closing and wire.
- **Option pool:** 5% is small and 15% is large. Investors want it created before the round so they avoid dilution, while founders want it created after, so it is negotiated. Too greedy and you keep more of something that may be worth zero. Too fearful and you give away too much.

## Investor protection, down rounds and the board

- **Liquidation preference** keeps founders from taking the money and running. A 1x preference returns the investment first, and an Nx preference returns it N times. Multiples and participating preferred skew incentives in intermediate exits: investors may want to sell and double their money while founders make nothing. Peter says the best arrangement is **1x, non-participating**.
- **Anti-dilution** reprices earlier investment after a down round. The full ratchet, which reprices as if the investor had just invested in the down round, "sounds like a form of medieval torture" because it effectively is, and it is bad for everyone but investors. Broad-based weighted average is more common, and narrow-based is sometimes used.
- **Never have a down round.**
  - With tough anti-dilution terms they wipe out founders and employees and make the company less appealing to other investors.
  - They make everybody mad: owners blame controllers, who blame possessors. "Companies are essentially broken the day they have a down round."
  - If one is unavoidable, make it catastrophic, so the angry people are wiped out and don't cause more trouble. If every round is an up round you make at least some money, but with a single down round you probably won't.
- **Other terms:**
  - Pro rata rights give VCs a free option on the next round, but a prior investor not participating is a red flag to new investors.
  - Sale restrictions align founders and VCs on growth but limit early employees' ability to cash out. VCs are wealthy and can wait, while employees may want some security.
  - Co-sale, preferred dividends, no-shop, redemption and conversion terms are worth understanding but are less crucial.
- **The board:** it is in charge of governance, and every person on it matters and must be a really good person. A typical board is two VCs, one "independent" and two founders. Five is large and more than five is suboptimal, since "less is often more". The ideal board is three people: one VC and two founders.
- **Dilution is relentless.** At IPO the Google founders held 15.6%, Steve Jobs 13.5% of Apple, and Mark Pincus 16% of Zynga. Holding more than 10% after many rounds is generally a very good outcome.
- **The no-investor alternative** gets short shrift but works:
  - Craigslist would be worth about $5bn if run more like a company than a commune.
  - GoDaddy never took funding, and Trilogy had no outside investors in the late 1990s.
  - Microsoft took one small venture investment before its IPO, at which point Bill Gates still owned 49.2%.
- Choosing VCs is like choosing co-founders and employees: who are the best people, and who do you want or need on board?
