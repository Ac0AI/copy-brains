## Technology stagnation: the core thesis

- Peter argues that modern Western civilization rests on "the twin plinths of science and technology," and that both liberals and conservatives silently assume natural-science progress "continues apace."
- He disputes this: "Progress is neither automatic nor mechanistic; it is rare." The West's rise is an exception to the human norm of a "brutal, unchanging, and impoverished state," and "there is no law that the exceptional rise of the West must continue."
- He frames the Great Recession (begun 2008) as a desperate need for science and technology, even though most people "cannot separate science from superstition or technology from magic." Desperate optimism will not save us.
- His closing image: "The first and the hardest step is to see that we now find ourselves in a desert, and not in an enchanted forest."
- He says the question to ask is whether Progress "is not doing as well as advertised," and whether exceptional measures are needed to arrest and reverse decline.

## Evidence of slowdown outside computing

- **Speed:** The centuries-long acceleration of travel (sailing ships, railroads, cars, airplanes) reversed with the Concorde's decommissioning in 2003. He cites a 1964 Popular Science cover, "Who'll Fly You at 2,000 m.p.h.?", as a relic of a bygone age.
- **Energy:** Real oil prices exceed those of 1979-80. Nixon's 1974 goal of full energy independence by 1980 became Obama's 2011 goal of one-third oil independence by 2020. Nuclear's 1954 promise of "electrical energy too cheap to meter" was defeated by environmentalism and proliferation fears. He says one cannot in good conscience encourage an undergraduate in 2011 to study nuclear engineering.
- **Clean tech:** He calls it a euphemism for "energy too expensive to afford" and, in Silicon Valley, a toxic term for near-certain ways to lose money. Without breakthroughs, the alternative to costlier oil may be dirtier coal.
- **Buffett's bet:** Berkshire put $44 billion, mostly in late 2009, into BNSF Railway. He notes 40 percent of rail freight is coal, so railroads do well if the 21st century regresses to the past.
- **Agriculture:** The Green Revolution raised grain yields 126 percent from 1950 to 1980 but only 47 percent since, barely keeping pace with population. He links runaway food prices to the 2011 Arab Spring: people "became more hungry than scared."
- **Medicine:** Congress promised victory over cancer in 1970 within six years; four decades later it is still elusive. Nearly a third of America's 85-year-olds have some dementia. Male life expectancy rose from 67.1 (1970) to 71.8 (1990) to 75.6 (2010), with deceleration. Big pharma will lose about one-third of revenue to patent expiry in three years and is liquidating research departments. He offers FDA intransigence, fecklessness of biologists, and biological complexity as possible causes.

## Computers versus everything else

- Information technology is the lone acceleration: Moore's Law has held far longer than anyone, including Moore, expected, and 2011 cellphones hold more computing power than the whole 1969 Apollo program.
- Palo Alto looks like a return to 1999. Thousands of startups launch yearly, and he says maybe two to six per year hit billion-dollar-plus valuations within five years. Google nearly doubled wages for top engineers in three years. Facebook has 750 million users.
- He asks whether supercomputers will create wholly new economic value or merely "reshuffle existing structures" (nature "red in tooth and claw"), and how to tell "progress from mere change."
- Progress in computers and failure in energy "roughly canceled each other out": like Alice in the Red Queen's race, we run faster to stay in place.

## Measuring progress through wages and incomes

- Because scientific progress is hard to measure (fields so specialized that "who can compare" nanotech, cryptography and superstring theory among 610 other disciplines), he uses economic data as an indirect clue. He also says he suspects scientists are often "lawmakers and politicians in disguise" in almost all fields.
- The key fact: real wages and incomes have stagnated broadly since 1973, the year oil prices quadrupled.
- Details since 1973: median incomes rose only 10 percent; mean incomes 29 percent, about 0.7 percent per year, much slower than the preceding four decades. Non-wage benefits (mostly health care) rose about $2,600 per worker, roughly 0.2 percent per year. If official inflation is understated by 0.9 points per year, mean wages and benefits are flat. Corporate profits rose from 9 to 12 percent of GDP.
- He suggests government inflation in education and health care (more spending, little or modest improvement) may be understated, and that gold prices might be taken seriously, implying real incomes did even worse.
- On the secondary issues (inequality, globalization, college premium), he says they "do not really change the larger point." Economic progress may lag technology, "but 38 years seems like an awfully long time."
- He cites Servan-Schreiber's 1967 *The American Challenge*, which predicted a four-day, 7-hour work week and 147 work days a year by 2000, as an example of how wrong the consensus was.

## Credit, leverage and bubbles as bets on the future

- "Like technology, credit also makes claims on the future." A credit crisis happens when earnings disappoint and the present fails past expectations.
- Lack of easy progress makes leverage both more dangerous (growth cannot cure liquidity or solvency problems) and more tempting (unleveraged real returns fall below pension and investor expectations).
- His account of the 1990s tech bubble leading to the 2000s housing bubble: investors who bet on technology growth that did not arrive sought double-digit returns through leverage in "safe" real estate. Both bubbles shared "a mistaken but nearly universal background assumption about easy progress."
- Conclusion: "Leverage is not a substitute for scientific progress."

## Politics and Keynesianism in a zero-sum world

- Modern political order is "predicated on easy and relentless growth." Without growth, "we can expect a loser for every winner," winners will look like a racket, and politics gets nastier.
- He calls Keynes "always a bit of a fraud," but says the trick seemed to work for decades because of 20th-century technological tailwind. Even in the 1930s, innovation expanded radio, movies, aeronautics, appliances, polymer chemistry and secondary oil recovery, and New Dealers pushed innovation hard. Those deficits were repaid by later growth.
- Today's policy leaders debate fiscal and monetary questions with erudition but hold a "cargo-cult mentality" about innovation. "The age of monetary bubbles naturally ends in real austerity."
- On the right, he sees a shift from Jack Kemp's optimism to Ron Paul's pessimism, and wonders whether supply-side economics was just code for Keynesianism. Lower marginal tax rates would not substitute for "hundreds of new nuclear reactors."

## What can be done: the state, culture and the lost future

- The state can push science (Manhattan Project, Apollo), and free markets may underfund basic research. But he says today's leaders are not engineers and do not listen to them: a letter from Einstein would get lost in the mailroom, and the Manhattan Project would never start or finish in three years.
- He knows of no U.S. political leader, in either party, who would cut health-care spending to fund biotech or cut the welfare state for major engineering projects.
- Culturally, Robert Moses and Oscar Niemeyer "belong to a past when people still had concrete ideas about the future." Voters prefer Victorian houses, and science fiction has collapsed as a genre.
- He dates the lost culture war over Progress to July 1969: men reached the moon, and Woodstock began three weeks later, when "the hippies took over the country."
- He argues the Progressive Left conflates civil rights with general progress ("a difference between the election of a black president and the creation of cheap solar energy") and so cannot ask whether things are getting worse. He wonders if identity-politics culture wars are the main reason the tech slowdown has been ignored for so long.
- After "40 years of wandering," he says it is hard to find "a path back to the future," and that we should reflect on the future more.
