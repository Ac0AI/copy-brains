## Distribution as an underrated determinant of success

Peter argues that distribution is the single topic people understand least. Team, structure, and culture get plenty of attention, and even "avoiding competition is usually better than competing" gets discovered and implemented. Distribution gets overlooked.

- The engineering bias holds that great products sell themselves. Peter says "nothing is further from the truth."
- The best product does not always win. Tesla's alternating current was better than Edison's direct current, but Edison was the better businessman and went on to start GE. Tesla also developed radio transmission, but Marconi took it and won the Nobel Prize.
- Claims that "this product sells itself" are almost never true. People say it to convince others the product is good, because nobody wants to say "our product is so bad that it takes the best salespeople in the world to convince people to buy it." He says to ask whether it is an empirical fact or a sales pitch.
- Great distribution can give a company a terminal monopoly even with an undifferentiated product. Product differentiation alone gets nowhere.
- Poor distribution, not product, is the number one cause of failure.
- Every tech company has salespeople. "If you don't see any salespeople, you are the salesperson."

## The math of distribution: CLV versus CPA

Peter frames distribution quantitatively.

- Metrics: customer lifetime value (CLV), average revenue per user per month (ARPU), monthly retention rate r, average customer lifetime = 1 / (1 - r), and cost per customer acquisition (CPA).
- CLV = ARPU x gross margin x average customer lifetime.
- In a frictionless world, a great business needs CLV > 0. With friction and uncertainty, it needs CLV > CPA.
- Worked example, a second-tier cell phone plan: $40/month, 24-month average lifetime, so $960 lifetime revenue. At a 40% gross margin, CLV is $384. The business is in good shape if acquiring a customer costs less than $384.
- Different customers have very different acquisition costs, so the distribution approach should match what is being sold:
  - Cheap products such as steak knives, sold to individuals at a couple of dollars each: advertising and viral marketing.
  - Roughly $10,000 packages for small businesses: marketing aimed at small businesses.
  - $1m-$50m sales to big businesses or governments: salespeople and business development people ("basically just fancy salespeople who do three martini lunches and work on complex deals"). As unit value rises, the process shifts toward people-intensive selling.

## Sales is hidden, strange, and not purely rational

Peter argues that selling and advertising are not about rational information sharing.

- Scale: about 610,000 people work in U.S. advertising, a $95bn market. About 3.2 million work in sales, a $450bn industry.
- Advertising works, but everyone believes it only works on other people. The ad industry also had to convince the people who buy ads that advertising works, which is self-referential.
- Sales pays well for those who are good. At Oracle, an engineer with 4-6 years of experience gets a $105k salary and $8k bonus, while a sales manager with the same experience gets $112k and a $103k bonus. At Google, salespeople earn a $96k base, $86k in commissions, and a $40k bonus, more than engineering counterparts. He does not say everyone should go into sales.
- Engineers are truth-oriented, since in engineering something either works or it doesn't. So they see attempts to change surface appearance, meaning sales, as dishonest (a point he credits to Marc Andreessen).
- Sales works best when hidden. In the Tom Sawyer story, none of the kids recognized the sale.
- Bill Gross story: at a late-2001 lunch, the IdeaLab founder told Peter his apathetic 14-year-old son had written a beautiful email to friends on why to sign up for PayPal and use the referral structure. Peter calls it a "literary masterpiece" with many nested levels of conversation. Gross was also tacitly signalling to the other investors how smart he was to have invested.
- Distribution roles should carry job titles that don't say sales, marketing, or advertising: "account executives" for salespeople, "corporate development" for fundraisers. People don't want to admit they're being sold to.
- A 2x2 of product (sells itself or needs selling) against sales effort (none or strong):
  - Sells itself, no sales effort: does not exist.
  - Needs selling, no sales effort: no revenue.
  - Needs selling, strong sales: a sales-driven company.
  - Sells itself, strong sales: ideal.
- Engineering is transparent, so it is easy to judge who is good. Sales is opaque, with gradations from amateurs to mediocrities, experts, masters, and grandmasters. Great salespeople are "much better than you think," and the skill is deeply hidden. Peter compares this to experts versus politicians in big companies: people drift to being politicians because it looks easier. He says "probably every President of the United States was first and foremost a salesman in disguise."

## Distribution methods by deal size: complex sales and mid-sized sales

Peter walks through companies that found unique, effective distribution.

- **SpaceX (complex sales).** Elon Musk won about $500m in government grants across SpaceX and Tesla. The U.S. space industry has close to 500,000 people spread roughly evenly over the 50 states, and congressional lobbying keeps almost the entire industry alive. Things are designed to be expensive, while SpaceX aims to cut launch costs by 90%. SpaceX has about 2,000 people and effectively took on the entire House and Senate. Peter calls Elon a sales grandmaster.
- **Palantir.** Sells to governments and large financial institutions, with deals from $1m to $100m. It has no "salespeople." It uses "forward deployed engineers" and a CEO who travels 25 or 26 days a month. The counterpoint to "not scalable" is that at that level people only want to talk to the CEO, and a large sales army "sounds bad."
- **Knewton (Founders Fund portfolio company, adaptive learning).** It chose a non-disruptive route and partnered with Pearson, the textbook company, rather than outselling incumbents school by school, which it would probably lose.
- **Yammer ($10k-$100k tickets).** David Sacks was vehemently anti-sales at PayPal ("Networking is not working!") but at Yammer built a scalable sales system and hired a top Salesforce person to run it.
- **ZocDoc.** Signs doctors' offices at $250/month, with market-by-market launches and an internal team of recruiters that only hires salespeople. At the lower end, things get more transactional and marginal.

## The missing middle and the Intuit example

Peter identifies a structural gap in distribution channels.

- Large-ticket items support a person driving the sale (Palantir, SpaceX). The far end has mass marketing and advertising. In between, for most small businesses, there may be no good distribution channel at all.
- ZocDoc example: TV ads would overpay because no channel only doctors watch. A sales team can't knock on every doctor's door. Most doctors aren't technologically advanced, so internet marketing is imperfect.
- A great product with no channel doesn't get sold. If you do crack the channel, you may have a terminal monopoly.
- Intuit is the classic case. Small businesses needed accounting and tax software and Intuit got it to them. Because it nailed distribution, Peter says it is probably impossible to displace today. Microsoft tried to acquire Intuit and the DOJ struck down the deal.

## Marketing and advertising: differentiate, and measure

Peter argues advertising is creative but competitive, so an edge has to be hard to replicate.

- In the 1950s and '60s advertising was iconic (Mad Men; Cary Grant in North by Northwest). As more people learned to do it in the '70s and '80s, entrants grew faster than the market and profits fell.
- The core problem is opacity. He quotes John Wanamaker: "Half the money I spend on advertising is wasted: the trouble is I don't know which half."
- Google was the first to let people figure out whether advertising works, through metrics like CPM, CTR, CPC, and RPC and straightforward ROI calculation. He says this is core to why Google is so valuable, since people pay a lot for advertising that demonstrably works.
- Priceline's marketing is powerfully differentiated and hard to copy. Its William Shatner is "in a league of his own." PayPal's James Doohan (Scotty) palm-pilot money-beaming event was a flop.
- Zynga combined viral growth with a lot of targeted advertising, monetized users more aggressively than expected, and used that revenue to buy more ads. Other gaming companies relied on viral growth alone.
- The internet bias that "advertising does not work" is a double standard, since many sites earn their living selling ads. Peter says there is a lot of room for creativity in distribution strategy.

## Viral marketing: it must be built into the product

Peter says viral marketing is easy to underestimate and cannot be bolted on.

- Two points: shorter viral cycle time is better, and viral coefficient must exceed 1 to get viral growth.
- PayPal started with 24 users, all employees, and used cash incentives to refer others. It reached 7% daily growth, with the user base roughly doubling every 10 days. Held for 4-5 months, that gives hundreds of thousands of users.
- Win the key segment first. Hotmail put a sign-up message at the bottom of every email, and later copycats were "a whole segment behind." The first mover that achieves viral growth can be the last mover.
- PayPal's first high-growth segment was eBay power buyers and sellers. Money moved fast there, which linked to virality. By the time others understood, the segment was locked in. Other segments, like sending money to family overseas, had much lower velocity.
- Other examples he names: Dropbox (file sharing implies someone to share with), Spotify's social angle, and possibly Pinterest, though he says it is unclear whether it is good or a fad.
- "Marketing people can't do viral marketing." There is no viral add-on or "tell your friends" button. The product's core use case must be inherently viral. He calls advocating it as an after-the-fact choice "wrong and lazy."

## The power law of distribution: find the one channel that works

Peter applies the power law to distribution channels.

- As with revenue streams (LinkedIn is the exception that proves the rule, per Roelof Botha), companies probably won't have several equally good channels. Engineers who don't understand distribution try a bit of sales, BD, advertising, and viral marketing, "everything but the kitchen sink," which he calls a really bad idea.
- One channel is very likely optimal. Most businesses get zero channels to work. One working channel makes a great business, and trying several without nailing one is fatal.
- Think hard about who is most likely to buy. For an enterprise software company, that means narrowing to the likeliest buyers rather than sending the sales team to talk to everybody.
- Distribution also covers selling the company to employees and investors. "This company is so good, people will be clamoring to join" and "investors will bang down our door" are the same error. When they seem to happen, something else is usually going on behind the scenes.

## PR, uncertainty, and distribution for fundraising and hiring

Peter extends distribution to media, investors, and talent.

- **PR.** Palantir had a strong anti-media bias for years. Media still mattered for investors and employees, since candidates Google the company and parents say things like "Palantir? Never heard of it. You should go work at Microsoft." PR is "the art of getting trusted, objective third parties to give you press," not plugging yourself on your own website.
- **Uncertainty.** People are very uncertain about what they want and insist otherwise. With poorly formed demand functions, people copy what they believe everyone else wants, as in fashion. In VC, a rational market would produce single-investor deals priced at the most bullish bidder. In practice you get zero investors or many.
- **Corporate development.** Once a company is worth roughly $30m or more, hire a full-time person who only travels and finds prospective investors. Example: a company worth about $300m, where valuation can swing about 2:1. Raising $50m at $300m gives away 16%, while raising it at $500m gives away 10%. A 6% delta is huge, so why not give the best person 1% of the company?
- **Hiring.** Traditional recruiters assume rational people who decide well once informed. Since the best people make the best companies, founders or one or two key senior people should spend 25% to 33% of their time identifying and attracting talent.
- **Authenticity myth.** People claim ads don't work on them and that their wants are authentic, yet fashionable people all wear the same clothes. The question to ask is how big the distribution problem is and whether the business can solve it. Distribution works best when hidden, and people only show the tip of the iceberg.
