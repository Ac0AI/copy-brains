## Where AI and the internet fit in the stagnation story

- Peter dates the "tech stagnation" argument to about 2008 and says progress in the world of atoms has been limited for roughly five decades, since the 1970s.
- When he was an undergraduate at Stanford in the late 1980s, majoring in mechanical, chemical or nuclear engineering, or in aerospace, was "terrible decisions". Only the world of bits (computers, the internet, mobile internet, software) kept progressing.
- He is unsure how much computing raised GDP compared with the first half of the 20th century. He calls the progress "very unbalanced" even though it produced great companies.
- He sees the situation as roughly unchanged for the last 25 years: decent progress in bits, much slower and harder-to-invest-in progress in everything else.
- The late-90s internet wave was a giant computing wave. He now says LLMs and ChatGPT are "probably a breakthrough" that he would rank "on par with the internet itself".
- Outside computing, he says, we are much further from "the science fiction future of the Jetsons".

## Why science slowed: centralization, risk aversion and the nuclear shadow

- Peter calls why questions "overdetermined". Some fields have had their easy discoveries made (no new continents, a new element on the periodic table is hard).
- He leans more on cultural explanations: "it's not that nature has run out of things for us" but that society became too risk averse and bureaucratized.
- Centralized government funding could accelerate science once. He cites the Manhattan Project (a New York Times editorial said decentralized scientists would have taken half a century; the Army delivered in three and a half years) and the Apollo program (Kennedy's early-60s speech, a man on the moon by the end of the decade).
- The long-term cost was large bureaucratic institutions and politicized, slower science. He compares it to agricultural monoculture, which boosts output once but is not a healthy ecosystem.
- Military use of science and technology made it ambiguous whether science was more good than bad. He puts the delayed response to Hiroshima at about 25 years: by the early 1970s the reaction was "we can destroy the world 20 times over, what are we doing?".
- That could explain why the smartest physicists stopped being funded to build bigger bombs and scientists in general became regulated: "maybe that's the price you have to pay to stop them from blowing up the world".
- He says Apollo-era risk taking was extreme (Yuri Gagarin died in a test flight years later) and that society then felt things were too dangerous, with too much risk of nuclear war and environmental degradation.
- He does not dismiss existential risks. His line: "we've resulted in a society that was locked down not just during Covid but we've been in a soft lockdown for something like 50 years", and he wants a way out.

## Where he thinks safetyism has gone too far

- Peter says it is hard to say where the safety line belongs, but gives two examples.
- Real estate: zoning is extremely strict. Looking out his window in Los Angeles he sees office buildings from the 1960s to 1980s and not a single crane on the horizon. For something as important as real estate, that tells him "we've gone way too far".
- Biomedicine: he has thought about it a lot and sees many promising approaches, yet "the barriers are just very, very high".
- His summary: "we're scared of the things that can go wrong but we're not scared enough of the things that will go wrong if we do nothing."

## Wokeness as a diversion from science and economics

- Peter frames his argument as "anti-anti-woke". The debates on DEI, political correctness and multiculturalism matter on some level, but he worries many are distractions, like a magic show that hypnotizes the audience while a man in an orange monkey suit jumps on someone's back. "Diversity is a diversion from more important things."
- The more important things include economics, science, religion and other political issues.
- Economics: the Marxist critique of cultural Marxism is that focusing on race and gender made people forget class. He also names runaway housing prices as the real problem that gets crowded out.
- He raises a "Marxist conspiracy theory" in which wokeness splits workers by race and gender so they can be paid less. His example is Walmart in the 2000s: under attack for low pay, it rebranded as a green company, which split the left-wing anti-Walmart alliance. A bit of green stuff was cheaper than higher wages, probably good for shareholders, and left the underlying economics unaddressed.
- Science: wokeness focuses on the humanities (English, history) while the real failure is that scientists aren't inventing new things. Research is "corrupt peer-reviewed", incremental, and run by "a stagnant malthusian sociopathic institution".
- He would be "fine with a little bit of wokeness if we were finding a cure for dementia".

## Science needs dogmatism and skepticism in balance; religion and AI simulation theory

- Peter says science must fight a two-front war against excessive dogmatism and excessive skepticism. Early modern science fought the dogmatism of the Catholic church and Ptolemaic astronomy. Cartesian-style total skepticism is also bad for science.
- His 2023 read: science is "100% anti-skepticism". It fights conspiracy theorists, vaccine, climate, stem cell and Darwin skeptics.
- His test: ask scientists in which fields science is too dogmatic. He says they could not name anything specific, which suggests science "has become as dogmatic as the medieval church was". Non-dogmatic scientists survive in children's books, not in grad school labs, where anyone heterodox gets "thrown off the overcrowded bus".
- On religion, he says God is possibly the biggest question there is, so large that society keeps peace by agreeing not to debate it. He thinks that costs us thinking about the most important things, "maybe at the price of something like a frontal lobotomy" for scientists and the religious.
- On the new atheists (Hitchens, Dennett, Dawkins, Harris), he argues that in 2005 new atheism was a politically correct way to be anti-Muslim, aimed at Bin Laden and Al-Qaeda. In 2023 the main geopolitical challenge is "Xi Jinping thought and the CCP", a consensus-driven "Borg" whose structure resembles East Bay rationalists and consensus scientific thinking, and the new atheists have nothing to say about it.
- He offers two explanations for the AI community's shift from militant atheism to simulation theory:
  - It was a status game in which computer scientists (bits) beat physicists (matter, energy, particles).
  - It partially solved the AI alignment problem. In a multiverse, AGI is in the future and unlikely to be friendly, so "chances are we're all just going to die". In a simulation designed by a super AGI, AGI is in the past and compatibility with humans was already solved, so it can be solved again.
- He says some of this is reasoning, and calls it a quasi-psychological explanation of people grasping for relief from the difficulty of building a friendly AI.
- On naming: if you are insecure enough to call a field a science (political, social, climate science), it often means the opposite. Computer science got the label because it was the major for people not good at EE or math, and it had an inferiority complex. He extends this to adverbs, which often mean the opposite of what they say.

## Existential risk, Pinker, and nuclear deterrence

- Peter accepts some progress since 250 years ago (e.g. George Washington's wooden teeth) and would not go back even 100 years, but says "something has hit the wall in the last 50 years".
- His specific objection to Steven Pinker is that violence is going down. As a psychology professor who "probably flunked chemistry", Pinker measures only kinetic energy (bombs dropped), while total energy is kinetic plus potential. The potential energy, the number of nuclear bombs and the potential destructiveness, is far higher than 50 years ago.
- He is not complacent that nuclear weapons haven't been used since 1945. He asks how we should treat a North Korean dictator video of a nuke hitting the Golden Gate Bridge, which we dismiss as a cartoon villain.
- On deterrence: it was supposed to work by thinking about nuclear weapons all the time and being scared, which worked during the Cold War (1949 to 1989). Over the last 33 to 34 years we have been "psychologically exhausted" and stopped thinking about it.
- He thinks JFK, LBJ or Nixon could have launched, but doubts Trump could have pushed the button after the 2020 election, and he is not sure nuclear weapons are completely unusable now.
- He says "I'm not a Luddite", you can't turn the clock back, and other existential risks are real: AI and environmental issues beyond climate change.
- On AI risk, he says Eliezer Yudkowsky has "gone kind of crazy" but his arguments "are not that bad", and Silicon Valley has no great rebuttals. The answer is to talk about the risks seriously without minimizing them like Pinker, and also without shutting everything down.

## Debt, US vs the world, polarization and taxes

- Debt: the US has about $32 trillion in federal debt. Warnings since the 1980s that debt would squeeze the private sector seemed wrong for about 40 years because of a bond bull market. Rates fell from about 20% in the early 1980s to roughly 0% after 2008, with mostly zero rates from 2008 to 2021.
- Servicing cost was about 1.6% of GDP in 2021 versus 3.2% in 1991. Now rates and inflation have spiked, and "I think we're headed for a very, very challenging decade" because "once the rates are above zero the crisis is here".
- The US reserve-currency status let it run bigger deficits for longer, which both helped and hurt.
- US vs the world: despite US problems, "almost everything else seems worse". His metric: companies founded since 1990 worth over $100 billion. There are 17 in the world, 11 in the US, 6 in China, and none anywhere else. He calls Europe's failure extraordinary and says a CEO of one of the 17 would now much rather be in the US than China.
- Polarization: parties look extremely polarized but differ little on his priorities (faster tech innovation, cutting the deficit, more housing). He uses Romeo and Juliet: families fight because there are no differences, which he calls "90% Shakespeare and maybe 10% Marx".
- Inequality and mobility: he thinks both are problems but anchors on stagnation. At 3% GDP growth, "everybody would be better off" and the problems would matter less. At 0 to 1% growth they become zero-sum and hard to solve.
- Tax view: billionaires pay a lower effective rate (maybe about 15%, mostly deferred capital gains) than millionaires on ordinary income (50% in California). Raising capital gains taxes would make billionaires not sell, so the Laffer curve effect would cut revenue. He thinks the US is near the maximum on billionaires.
- His libertarian answer, which he calls outside the Overton window: cut taxes massively on the middle class and millionaires. A bigger government that collects more would need a Western Europe-style structure, where the 50% rate kicks in at about $70,000 in Austria versus $1 million in California, which reduces mobility.

## Courage, conspiracy thinking and conventional wisdom

- Peter says courage is in shorter supply than genius. Heterodox, think-for-yourself thinking is harder than 50 to 70 years ago, partly because anything put on the internet stays forever.
- His example: he started the Stanford Review in 1987, and it was wildly heterodox, obnoxious and even cruel. It moderated in 2002, the exact point when articles started going online, because everything written would follow you for life.
- His "conspiracy" example is the ESG explanation for the oil majors. Oil demand is highly inelastic (about 100 million barrels a day; cutting 1 million barrels raises price roughly $10 a barrel). If Exxon, Shell, Chevron and BP CEOs agreed to cut output 30% and collude with OPEC, they would break Section 2 of the Sherman Act.
- Hiring ESG consultants who tell them to put half their profits into solar and windmills, which "don't really work", gets the same result: lower supply, higher prices and profits. He doesn't claim it is a deliberate plot, only that the market feedback rewards more of it either way.
- He gives a similar example: the late-1990s US tobacco settlement effectively cartelized the industry, turning tobacco into a tax source and a set of monopolies that "printed money".
- Closing question on bad conventional wisdom: almost all of it. There is no one-size-fits-all or cookie-cutter advice, and advice that works for everyone isn't differentiating. He is not interested in "timeless eternal truths" but in what makes sense to do "in 2023, right here, right now".
- He says "it's probably not a good idea to go around killing people", but that does not get you far in planning your life.

## Total addressable market and monopoly dynamics, including in venture

- Peter says a market is a necessary component of a startup, but people overstress TAM. A very large TAM probably means a lot of competition.
- Examples: the restaurant business has an enormous TAM and is "a terrible business to go into". Energy functions in a nearly commodified way, so it is hard for startups to find a differentiated niche.
- His preferred narrative is a tight, fairly narrow initial market plus a way to expand. "Build a fortress" there, then expand from that base.
- PayPal: the initial TAM was power sellers on eBay, about 20,000 people in early 2000. PayPal reached 30 to 40% market share in about three months. Expansion paths included payments on and off eBay, eBay's own growth, and gaining share on eBay.
- Crossing the chasm to the off-eBay market turned out to be quite hard, so PayPal sold to eBay in 2002. The strong initial TAM and expansion capability probably "got us to an m&a exit".
- For VCs, he applies the same monopoly layers: companies are layer one, investors are layer two. You want companies without too much competition "red in tooth and claw", and you don't want to compete too much with other VCs.
- Monopoly sources for VC firms parallel those for companies: brand, network effects, economies of scale, unique technology. Founders Fund had a founder-friendly brand from 2005, as the firm was started by founders. By 2023 many others claim it, so it is more cluttered.
- Network effects give idiosyncratic deal flow, but he says advantages must often be reinvented deal by deal.
- His poker rule for investing: "what do we understand about this that other investors do not understand?" If you can't answer, you are the sucker, like the poker player who can't tell who the mark at the table is.
