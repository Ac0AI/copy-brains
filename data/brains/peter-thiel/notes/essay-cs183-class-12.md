## Why war happens: theater, mimesis, and the Marx vs. Shakespeare frame

- Peter says conflict often starts as theater: threats, missile-pointing, and nations copying each other. His examples are the space race, Fischer-Spassky (1972), and the Miracle on Ice (1980). He calls the whole Cold War "essentially theater".
- Rivalry is a strong motivator. People work harder when racing an enemy, but the end of the rivalry is awkward (the 1975 Apollo-Soyuz joint flight).
- The Charles Atlas example: a skinny kid bulks up and beats the bully, and in doing so becomes the bully. His working theory is "you must choose your enemies well, since you'll soon become just like them."
- This is the psychological counterpart to the economics of perfect competition, where profits are competed away. The economic snapshot shows the problem but not why people still compete.
- He quotes Kissinger: "the battles are so fierce because the stakes are so small."
- Escalation runs: conflict, then obsession with the opponent, then growing similarity, then sometimes total destruction. Losers lose everything and winners can lose a lot too.
- Two models of conflict:
  - **Marx:** people fight because they disagree, and the bigger the difference, the bigger the fight. This is the internal narrative, and good-vs-evil is useful propaganda.
  - **Shakespeare:** from the outside, combatants look alike ("Two households, both alike in dignity"). They grow more similar as they fight and forget why they started.
- On Hamlet, he notes that true greatness means fighting "even for an eggshell". Heroes fight for what doesn't matter, while Hamlet is too fixed on meaninglessness to fight at all.
- In tech, he says it is "straight Shakespeare" in the great majority of cases.

## Tech examples of imitative, destructive competition

- **1970s computers:** IBM, NCR, Control Data and Honeywell all built minicomputers that were slightly different but conceptually similar. They missed the microcomputer, and IBM developed the microprocessor and eclipsed its rivals.
- **1990s pet stores:** Pets.com, PetStore.com, Petopia and about 100 others fought to dominate online pet supplies. They fought over Super Bowl ads and never asked whether the market was worth fighting for. The same went for Kozmo, Webvan and Urban Fetch.
- **Oracle vs. Siebel:**
  - Siebel was founded by a top Oracle salesman and copied Oracle almost exactly, down to the office design.
  - Oracle parked a huge ad truck in front of Siebel HQ to lure its employees away.
  - Oracle acquired Siebel in 2005.
- **Oracle vs. Informix:**
  - Informix put up billboards near Oracle HQ: "You just passed Redwood Shores. So did we."
  - Oracle answered with snail ads on TPC benchmark results.
  - The ads were aimed at each other and each other's employees, not customers.
  - Ellison's theory was that you always need an enemy, small enough that it can't beat you but big enough to motivate. Peter's takeaway is that fake enemies for motivation can turn into real enemies, and Informix self-destructed in 1997.
- **Square card readers:**
  - Square did software, hardware and the iconic white square device.
  - Copycats followed: PayPal's triangle, Intuit's cylinder, Kudos' semicircle.
  - He says copycat reader makers are in "a great deal of trouble" and that it is better to be the original or do something original elsewhere.
- **Microsoft vs. Google:**
  - Both were started by nerds obsessed with being the smartest, and he cites Gates' IQ-testing obsession.
  - They started in different businesses (Office, Explorer and Windows vs. search), then converged into direct rivalry (Bing vs. Google, Chrome vs. Internet Explorer, Google Docs vs. Office).
  - Meanwhile Apple overtook both: market cap $531B vs. $456B for Google and Microsoft combined, though three years earlier each was individually bigger than Apple.
  - His lesson: "fighting is costly. And those who avoid it can often swoop in and capitalize on the peace."

## The PayPal vs. X.com war and the merger

- Confinity's PayPal launched in late 1999. X.com (Elon Musk) was four blocks away on University Avenue in Palo Alto.
- X.com launched a feature-for-feature matching product, down to the same cash bonus and referral structure.
- December 1999 and January 2000 were intense, with people working 90-100 hours a week. The focus was beating X.com, not usefulness.
- One engineer presented a schematic of an actual bomb in a daily war update. It was axed and blamed on sleep deprivation.
- In February 2000 the leadership met at a restaurant equidistant from the two offices. They agreed to a 50-50 merger in early March, then raised money before the crash and had years to build the business.
- In early March 2000 PayPal had $15M in the bank and was about six weeks from running out of money. CFO Roelof Botha raised the alarm, but engineers cared only about beating X.com.
- Peter says the hard part after the merger was de-escalating. Rivalries are remembered more fondly when things work out and exaggerated when they don't.

## Fight rarely, win fast, and prefer peace (and monopoly)

- If you must fight, use overwhelming force and end it quickly, because a long war turns you into your enemy. He means real "shock and awe", not the fake kind that produces a 10-year war.
- Since a quick win usually can't be guaranteed, his main job is to find ways to avoid the war entirely.
- He returns to the class five 2x2 of athletes (zero-sum competitors) vs. nerds (non-zero-sum collaborators), against war/competition vs. peace/monopoly. A company should optimize for peace, with nerds to build the business and athletes to fight and win if competition is forced on it.
- The hybrid creates an internal problem: competitive people can fight each other. Most companies are killed by internal infighting, like an autoimmune disease. The proximate cause may be external, but the ultimate cause is internal.
- On the other side of the argument, Peter holds that the reasoning for expecting a long-run payoff from competition fails because the long run "never really arrives as planned". His example is Microsoft losing a billion dollars a year on Bing.
- He also argues people are bad at assessing probability. Pouring gold into taking the other guy's gold is irrational when success is unlikely, so he sees a crazy psychological component rather than pure calculation.

## Preventing fights inside the company

- Marx would say colleagues fight over big disagreements about direction. Peter says the Shakespearean answer is almost invariably correct: people fight because they want the same thing, usually the same role. People who want different things just go and own them.
- At PayPal the conflicts centered on the product team. David Sacks wanted the product to be one seamless whole, which meant product overlapped with everyone else and couldn't act without stepping on someone's turf.
- The CEO's job is to stop this at the source by keeping prospective combatants apart through clear definitions and precise roles.
- Startups are more dangerous than big companies because roles change constantly and you can't simply silo people.
- PayPal's approach:
  - Redraw the org chart every three months, repositioning people to head off conflicts before they started.
  - Evaluate each person on a single criterion, one thing only, different from everyone else's.
  - It was unpopular at first because people wanted three or four things, but it focused them on getting things done rather than on each other.
- "Focusing on your enemy is almost always the wrong thing to do."
- Title inflation at PayPal (lots of VPs, then lots of Senior VPs) was a status fight, since prestige is not a scarce resource. He admits it probably wasn't stable, but PayPal was acquired before it blew up.
- External war can forge internal peace, but at a cost: PayPal's engineers didn't care if the company went broke as long as it beat X.com.

## Having a plan, and being 10x better

- Peter's view is that a bad plan beats no plan, which he links to a chess lesson. Most people default to no plan.
- On law students: most had no idea what to do with their lives, and few expected to make partner. Many planned to work at a firm for a few years and "figure it out".
- His rule is that you should either like what you're doing, see it as a direct plan to something else, or see it as an indirect plan to something else. Adding a resume line every two years to "buy options" is bad.
- Hill-climbing metaphor: step back and look at the hill from time to time, or you may get old and find it was "a really low hill".
- People avoid planning because it is unfashionable to believe you have an edge and can make things happen.
- A marginal improvement doesn't win. His anti-spam example: the startup claimed to be better than about 100 competitors, but took half an hour to explain why. His line is "A half hour pitch on anti-spam is just more spam."
- The benchmark is a pitch as concise as "we are 10x better/cheaper/faster/more effective." (Reid Hoffman made the "plan to be 10x better" point; Peter endorsed it with the anti-spam example.)
- A secret plan from last class's theme is still the goal. He says probably not enough companies even have a plan, let alone a secret one.

## Network, secrecy, and Silicon Valley vs. New York

- On the tradeoff between secrecy and networks, Peter says you can't tell everyone your secret plan and must guard information. But you also need to talk and be somewhat open to get the benefits of the network, so it is "a very fine line".
- On how to tell whether your product is really different from a competitor's, when everyone has an internal story about being different, Peter put the question to Reid. He did not give his own answer.
- On New York vs. Silicon Valley, he says New York is "a pretty distant second", with some cool companies. His critique:
  - Media plays a much bigger role there, which induces competition. People focus on each other rather than on creating things.
  - People live on top of each other and are trained to fight and enjoy it. It may be motivating, but it points people at the wrong battles.
  - Original, great companies will keep coming out of Silicon Valley.
- He also noted that PayPal got to a million users in 4 to 5 months, versus about 460 days for LinkedIn. His point is that if you target a non-competitive space, slower growth is fine, and you want to grow fast but also be able to grow more slowly.
