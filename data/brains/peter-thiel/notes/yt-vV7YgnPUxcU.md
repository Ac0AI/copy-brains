## Peter's stagnation thesis: what it claims and how he checks it

- Peter still broadly believes the stagnation thesis he laid out in "The End of the Future" (National Review, about 13-14 years earlier). It was never absolute: the claim is that velocity slowed, not that progress stopped.
- From 1750 to 1970 (over 200 years) change accelerated: ships, railroads, cars and planes got faster, ending with the Concorde and Apollo missions. After that, things slowed in many dimensions.
- He always made an exception for "the world of bits": computers, software, the internet, mobile internet, and in the last 10-15 years crypto and AI. He asks whether that is enough to escape generalized stagnation.
- Epistemological problem: late modernity is hyper-specialized, so it is hard to tell whether progress is happening. You can't judge string theory, quantum computing or cancer research without devoting half your life to them, and you must then weight each field against the others.
- The ever-narrower groups of experts guarding their own fields are themselves "cause for skepticism."
- Common-sense test: a person time-traveling from 1860 to 1970 would find a completely different world. Someone from 1985 to 2025 would find cars a little different and no phones, but otherwise a similar world. The Back to the Future sequels (1955 to 1985, then 1985 to 2015) illustrate how far the real 2015 diverged from the film's future.
- Economic test: how do 30-year-old millennials live compared with their boomer parents at the same age?
- Intellectual test: how many breakthroughs are we getting, and what are the returns on going into research? He says there are diminishing returns to science and academia, and that this may be why institutions feel sociopathic and Malthusian. They must throw more and more at the problem for the same return, and eventually people give up and the thing collapses.

## Why stagnation happened and why it can't last

- Peter separates three questions about history: what happened, why it happened, and what to do about it.
- His reasons for the "why":
  - People ran out of ideas.
  - Institutions degraded and became risk averse.
  - Cultural transformations took hold.
  - People had legitimate fears that accelerating progress led to environmental or nuclear apocalypse.
- His 1970s account: Baconian science "ended at Los Alamos," and the runaway technology had become scary. On the moon landing and Woodstock: "we landed on the moon in July of 1969. Woodstock started three weeks later and with the benefit of hindsight that's when progress stopped and the hippies won."
- If we don't find a way back to the future, he thinks society "unravels." He defines the middle class as "the people who expect their kids to do better than themselves." When that expectation collapses, you no longer have a middle-class society.
- Feudal or static societies are conceivable, but not how the United States functioned for its first 200 years.
- Institutions are predicated on growth, especially budgets:
  - Reagan was "consumer capitalism, which is oxymoronic" (you borrow rather than save).
  - Obama was "low tax socialism, just as oxymoronic."
  - Peter prefers low-tax to high-tax socialism but calls it unsustainable: either taxes go up or the socialism ends.
- Why people aren't optimistic: nobody believes we've reached a stable end state. A true degrowth future (a small Scandinavian village) would be "super oppressive."
- He rejects the idea that Bush 43 was anti-decadent: the dominant Bush message after September 11th was to go shopping.

## Taking more risk, with examples from biotech, nuclear and "moonshots"

- His directional answer on risk: "a lot more." He declines to give a precise level.
- Biotech example: dementia and Alzheimer's have seen zero progress in 40-50 years. Researchers are stuck on beta-amyloid, which is "obviously not working" and "some kind of a stupid racket" of self-reinforcing people. Those with a fatal disease and researchers can both take far more risk.
- Culturally he wants a return to early modernity, when people believed disease could be cured and radical life extension was part of the project. He cites Francis Bacon and Condorcet. He speculates that science had to promise the same physical resurrection Christianity promised in order to compete with it.
- PayPal anecdote (1999-2000): cofounder Luke Nosek was into Alcor and cryonics, and the whole company went to a freezing party run like a Tupperware party, with a choice of full body or just the head (cheaper). The dot-matrix printer failed, so policies couldn't be printed.
- He calls this still technological stagnation, but notes it was the last fringe boomer belief in living forever. He is "always anti-boomer," yet sees something lost: no millennial believes science will cure all their diseases.
- The New Deal pushed and funded science and scaled it. Today an Einstein letter to the White House "would get lost in the mail room," and the Manhattan Project is unthinkable.
- "Moonshot" used to mean you went to the moon. Now it means something fictional that will never happen, as with Biden's cancer "moonshot."
- Nuclear power was supposed to be the power of the 21st century but was "off-ramped all over the world." The FDA effectively regulates drugs worldwide because other countries defer to it. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission does the same for reactors: you can't design a modular reactor and build it in Argentina, because nobody trusts Argentinian regulators over the US ones.

## Why he backed Trump, and what he thinks populism can do

- He frames his political investing as "venture capitalist for politics" at several levels: hoping to redirect "the Titanic" from the iceberg, and, more narrowly, hoping to have a conversation about decline at all.
- On "Make America Great Again": he asked whether it was an ambitious agenda or a pessimistic admission that America is no longer great. He had low expectations for what Trump would do positively.
- His hope in 2016 was that for the first time in 100 years a Republican wasn't offering "syrupy Bush nonsense," so a conversation about decline could start. In retrospect he calls that "a preposterous fantasy."
- His 2016 reasoning: nobody would be mad at him if Trump lost, and he thought Trump had a 50/50 chance because the problems were deep and stagnation was frustrating. He says people weren't ready for the conversation then and perhaps are more ready in 2025.
- Evidence of change: he debated Eric Schmidt (2012), Marc Andreessen (2013) and Bezos (2014), each arguing that everything was going great. He says those three have updated to varying degrees, and Silicon Valley has adjusted.
- His account of why much of Silicon Valley moved toward Trump by 2024: liberalism was the default, and when it didn't work the answer was always to do more of it. You "up the dose," spend hundreds of millions, go completely woke, "and everybody hates you," and at some point you pivot. He says it is not a pro-Trump thing as such.
- Populism is "still by far the best option we have." His rhetorical question: will Harvard cure dementia by puttering along doing what hasn't worked for 50 years?
- He sees two phases: a deregulatory, deconstructive one (for example nuclear deregulation, then new or better-designed plants, maybe fusion) and then actual construction. Both are entangled and need doing together.
- On politics generally he is "schizophrenic": it is incredibly important and incredibly toxic. It is zero-sum, the stakes feel high, and you end up with enemies you didn't have.

## Elon, Mars and the "woke AI will follow you" argument

- Peter's account of a 2024 dinner: he said that if Trump didn't win he wanted to leave the country, and Elon replied there was nowhere to go. About two hours later Peter thought of the rebuttal: Elon no longer believed in going to Mars.
- His claim: Mars was meant as a political project, building an alternative society, not just a science project. In 2024 Elon came to believe the socialist US government and the woke AI would follow you there.
- The Demis Hassabis exchange (a meeting Peter helped broker when Demis ran DeepMind):
  - Demis said he was building a superhuman AI, the most important project in the world.
  - Elon said his own project, making humanity an interplanetary species, was the most important.
  - Demis replied that his AI could follow Elon to Mars, and Elon went quiet.
  - Peter says it took Elon until 2024 to fully process this.
- Elon's other conversation: he predicted a billion humanoid robots in the US within 10 years. Peter pointed out that this implies growth would solve the budget deficit, yet Elon still worries about deficits. Peter reads that as not fully thought through, or as large error bars on the prediction.

## How big Peter thinks AI is, and why he is skeptical of the superintelligence story

- His placeholder: AI is "more than a nothing burger and less than the total transformation of our society," roughly the scale of the late-1990s internet.
- That internet added maybe 1 percentage point of GDP growth per year for 10-15 years, which was enough to create great companies but not obviously enough to end stagnation.
- He finds it unhealthy that AI is the only thing going: "I'd like to have more multi-dimensional progress," including Mars and dementia cures. "If all we have is AI, I will take it."
- He is skeptical that raw intelligence is the gating factor. He calls the fixation on IQ in Silicon Valley an ideology and perhaps a liberal one. The counter-argument he raises: the smarter people are, the worse they sometimes do, because society doesn't know what to do with them. That suggests the bottleneck is something wrong with society.
- He doubts there is a cure for dementia or mortality, and says the problems may be unsolvable. If not, the blocker may be whether society tolerates heterodox smart people, who he thinks you need for crazy experiments.
- Risk: if AI is only conventionally smart, or "woke" in the sense of conformist, it won't make a difference. He grants AI could deepen stagnation by flooding the world with merely okay output.
- His bottom line: he still thinks we should try AI, because "the alternative is just total stagnation." Drones plus AI in a military context could be scary or dystopian, but without AI "there's just nothing going on."
- Analogy to the internet: it did not produce the diverse idea explosion libertarians fantasized about in 1999, but he argues it was still better than the counterfactual.
- Critique of Silicon Valley: it argues at a microscopic level (the IQ Elo scores of AI, exact AGI definitions) and neglects intermediate questions: what AI means for the budget deficit, the macro economy and geopolitics.
- Taiwan example: an accelerating AI revolution might deter China because it has effectively lost, or push it to act because it's now or never. Either way it matters, and it isn't being thought through.

## Transhumanism and Christianity

- Peter would like the human race to endure but also wants radical solutions to its problems (long hesitation before answering).
- He says transhumanism's promise was changing a natural body into an immortal one. Using sex-change surgery as the foil, he says the critique isn't that such change is weird or unnatural but that it is "so pathetically little." We should want to change our heart, mind and whole body.
- Orthodox Christianity's critique, as he puts it, is that transhumanism doesn't go far enough: you must transform the soul and the whole self as well as the body.
- He says the word "nature" doesn't occur once in the Old Testament. His reading of the Judeo-Christian inspiration is that it is about transcending and overcoming nature, with "fallen" as the closest thing to a natural state.
- He doubts the AI builders' transhumanism is the center of gravity: cryonics now seems a retro thing from 1999, and uploading seems a step down from cryonics because he would rather keep his body.
- He doesn't say they are all lying, but describes the cornucopian language as not the center of gravity.

## The Antichrist, Armageddon and one-world government

- Peter wants existential risk framed to include another bad singularity: the one-world totalitarian state. He argues the default political answer to every existential risk is one-world governance: a UN with teeth for nuclear weapons, global compute governance for AI (logging every keystroke).
- The two framings of the same question:
  - Atheist: "one world or none," the title of a late-1940s Federation of American Scientists film that opens with a nuclear explosion.
  - Christian: "Antichrist or Armageddon."
- The plot hole in Antichrist novels: how does he take over the world? Peter's answer: he talks about Armageddon and existential risk nonstop, and says that must be regulated. That is the opposite of the Baconian picture of an evil tech genius with a world-taking machine.
- His claim: in the 17th century you could imagine a Dr. Strangelove or Edward Teller type taking over, but "in our world it's far more likely to be Greta Thunberg." The Antichrist's slogan is "peace and safety" (1 Thessalonians 5:3).
- He sees an environmentalism strong enough to be a candidate: in Europe the only visions of a different future on offer are green, Sharia and Chinese-style communist totalitarianism, and green is "by far the strongest."
- His argument that we already live under a mild version: 50 years of stagnation under submission to peace and safety, via the FDA and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission acting worldwide.
- Free will and God: he rejects the idea that God caused it. Citing John 15:25 ("They hated me without cause"), he says the Christian view is anti-Calvinist and that attributing everything to God scapegoats him. He thinks there is "a great deal of scope for human action, for human freedom," and that determinism would just mean accepting the lions.
- On the suggestion that his own tools (Palantir, surveillance, military tech) might serve the Antichrist: he says he obviously doesn't think that is what he's doing. He doesn't give a direct rebuttal beyond pressing the stagnation account.
