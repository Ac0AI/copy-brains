## "Late to the party": why Peter says you must know the 1990s to understand startups today

- Peter treats history as an "extended dinner conversation" that each generation is born into. A toddler in 1969 technically lived through the 1960s but missed the civil rights and Vietnam debates.
- He argues today's students (2012) are in the same position relative to 1999. To join the conversation about startups, you have to know the history of the '90s. He says it is questionable whether you can understand startups without knowing Webvan or recognizing the Pets.com mascot.
- History often turns out different from what people living through it thought. Even '90s entrepreneurs struggle to piece it together, and why things happened is harder than what happened.
- Most of the 1990s was not the dot-com bubble. The mania started in September 1998 and lasted about 18 months.

## Why money flowed into tech: the 1990s as a proof by elimination

- The decade started with the Berlin Wall falling (Nov 1989), then 2 months of euphoria, then a long, drawn-out recession (first such post-WWII). Technically it ended March 1991, but through the end of 1994 it still felt like recession. Manufacturing never fully rebounded.
- Culture reflected hopelessness (Nirvana, grunge, heroin). NAFTA and competitiveness worries fueled Ross Perot's third-party run. George H.W. Bush became the only one-term President in 30 years.
- In the late 1980s Stanford felt disconnected from the Valley, Japan seemed to be winning the semiconductor war, and focusing on tech was idiosyncratic.
- Netscape's August 1995 IPO made the public aware of the Internet. It was unprofitable, priced at $14, doubled before trading, doubled again on day one, and hit $160 within 5 months. Netscape poked Microsoft (posters on the Redmond campus), Gates redirected Microsoft to the Internet, IE followed, and Netscape's antitrust claims were what let it sell to AOL for over a billion dollars.
- Xanadu had the two-way network idea in 1963 but needed everyone to adopt at once. It kept attracting venture funding for about 29 years and died in 1992.
- Yahoo IPO'd in 1996 at a $350M valuation, Amazon in 1997 at $460M. NASDAQ was about 1400 in late 1998, only about 400 above August 1995.
- Meanwhile everything else was failing: Greenspan's "irrational exuberance" speech (1996), the 1997 East Asian crises, the 1998 Russian ruble crisis (banks bust and currency worthless: "Zero times zero is zero"), the LTCM blowup ("picking up nickels in front of a bulldozer," needing a multibillion Fed bailout), and a Euro that lost value after its January 1999 launch.
- His conclusion: old economy, emerging markets, Europe and leverage all looked broken, so "by the late '90s, a process of elimination left only one good place to put money: in tech."
- Caveat he flags: proof by contradiction is dangerous ("even if something's not A, B, C, or D, it doesn't necessarily follow that the truth is E"). But he says indirect proof had real purchase here.

## The mania (Sept 1998 to March 2000): what it looked like

- Peter says the best way to convey it is crazy stories, and that surfaces matter: "quite often, the surface of things is actually the heart of things." Themes: extremely social, irrationally exuberant, money everywhere, lots of sketchy people.
- Examples: 40-year-old Stanford grad students starting dozens of companies at once; billionaires from Idaho flying in to fund anyone with a polished pitch; broke founders paying $1,000 dinner bills in shares; an exclusive email list ranking the day's launch parties.
- Business models where the more you sold, the more you lost (the SNL pennies-at-the-bank skit): "It's okay... we'll make it up in volume." Adding ".com" to a name roughly doubled value overnight.
- Yahoo replaced Netscape as the most hubristic company. In 2000 it told PayPal to think about who it sold to, because a stock-for-stock deal needed a sound buyer; Yahoo considered itself one because its stock, it said, "always goes up."
- VA Linux: Larry Augustin nearly closed it in 1997. It IPO'd at $30 in 1999, traded to $300 on day one (biggest first-day gain ever), making his 10% stake about $1B. Six months later, at the end of lock-up, it was down 90%, then fell another 90%. He ended with $5-6M.
- Filtering the sketchy: Max Levchin's "aura test." Listen to someone for 15 seconds, decide if the aura is good, and walk away if not. Peter implies companies that did something like this were likelier to survive.

## PayPal in the mania: hiring, pivots, and growth by giving away money

- PayPal started December 1998, late in the boom. Founders agreed they couldn't afford to hire sketchy people, "So they just hired their friends instead."
- The original idea was beaming money over Palm Pilots, voted one of the 10 worst business ideas of 1999. One angel only wanted to know who else was investing, then consulted a fortune cookie, which said invest.
- Nokia Ventures put in $4.5M, but mobile Internet didn't work yet, so PayPal announced a pivot at the first post-investment board meeting to account-based payments to any email address. It felt too easy, so 1999 was frantic: move fast or fail.
- Ads were too expensive. BD deals with big banks went nowhere (the HSBC London meeting, where Luke Nosek and the team concluded over Häagen-Dazs that "BD didn't work"). They needed organic, viral growth, so they gave people money: $10 for signing up, $10 for each referral. They paid $20 per new customer.
- Result: 7 to 10% daily growth and 100 million users (his wording), but no revenue and an exponentially growing cost structure. Peter says it worked out but "does not mean it's the best way to run a company. Indeed, it probably isn't."
- Valuation by press: a Feb 16, 2000 WSJ piece gave a back-of-envelope $500M valuation, and the next round's lead accepted it as authoritative. In March 2000 a South Korean firm wired $5M to PayPal's law firm with no documents and refused to say where to return it ("You have to take it"). PayPal closed a $100M round on March 31, just before the crash.

## The crash and the schadenfreude era

- The collapse came in waves: marketing-driven e-commerce in the first half of 2000, B2B in the second half, telecoms in 2001. Military defense stocks were at the lowest in March 2000 (nobody believed in war) and then rose for most of the next decade.
- Hubris gave way to schadenfreude ("we were right all along") and cultural depression.
- PayPal broke even in 2001, fixed fraud and customer service problems, and filed its IPO in late September 2001, the first company to file after 9/11. The "Earth to Palo Alto" article ridiculed it, saying the US needed PayPal "as much as it does an anthrax epidemic."

## Lessons the world and Silicon Valley took, and why Peter says they were overreactions

- World's lessons: tech was a bubble; go from "clicks" back to "bricks" (housing, emerging markets); Buffett-style avoidance of tech; only profit matters; globalization over technology; the future is indeterminate and "all prophets are false prophets."
- Peter says these are "probably all wrong," driven by "hubris, envy, and resentment against the '90s." "When base emotions are driving, analysis becomes untrustworthy." People were right about much in the '90s, and March 2000 "was still a peak of clarity as well" as a peak of insanity.
- Silicon Valley's post-bust rules:
  - Incrementalism; grand visions out.
  - "Lean": don't know what you'll do, experiment and iterate.
  - Zero advertising: "If your growth isn't viral, it's fake."
  - Anti-social as the new social (Google as the iconic product for people who'd rather interact with computers).
  - Product over BD: smart non-engineers did BD in 1999, product in 2001; Larry Ellison-style salesperson CEOs gave way to Steve Jobs-style product CEOs.
  - Distrust rapid monetization; longer growth phase, later IPO, reinvest profits.
  - Don't discuss the future, or you look weird and crazy.
- His verdict: a "strategic retreat" that was necessary in part (why IPO early into a hostile market?) but "probably overblown." He asks whether you should really never advertise, never do BD/sales, or never talk about the future, and says "we should be open to idea that some or much of the retreat... was overreaction."

## Are we in a bubble? Why he calls it the wrong question

- In 2012 many said yes. Peter says the bubble-vs-no-bubble debate is the wrong question, and some froth (more Stanford CS majors than in '99, rising valuations) doesn't prove the thesis.
- His definition: a bubble requires "(1) widespread, intense belief that's (2) not true." But "people don't really believe in anything in our society anymore," so there can't be a bubble without intense belief. The bubble narrative comes from people "looking for a bubble," which is more overreaction to the pain of the '90s than analysis.
- Anti-bubble thinking ("everything will work, buy houses and tech stocks") is probably somewhat truer, but he says to resist it too. Both hold that truth is social.
- On contrarianism: "if the herd isn't thinking at all, being contrarian—doing the opposite of the herd—is just as random and useless."
- The truly contrarian move is to "think for yourself." The better question is what is valuable, then specifically: is company X valuable, why, and how do we figure that out. He says the next class takes this up.
