## Why a return to Eden is impossible

- Peter notes that the future will look very different from the past. The biblical arc runs from the Garden of Paradise to the City of God, the Holy City, Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven. In Revelation, the river and tree of life mean "the curse of death is lifted."
- He says "There is continuity with Genesis, but to Eden there will be no returning."
- He argues the Bible is not needed to see this. Earth could sustain about 10 million people on a comfortable pastoral or agrarian existence in Neolithic times.
- A planetary civilization of 10 billion people, which he expects Earth to be in the twenty-second century, requires a highly advanced level of science and technology as a precondition.
- "We cannot change this reality, although we are free to pretend otherwise."

## Faust over the Luddite: the alternatives to acceleration are not neutral

- Peter admits it is easy to mock Faust, who forgets his immortal soul to reclaim land from the sea. He asks why the options are mutually exclusive: "Can we not do both?"
- He acknowledges many perils on the current scientific and technological trajectory.
- His core point: "the alternatives to technological acceleration are far from ethically or politically neutral."
- He says "I badly miss the misguided optimism of a Faust": at least Faust was motivated to do something about everything wrong with the world.
- He ranks Faust morally above Nietzsche, whom he calls the first environmental philosopher. Nietzsche opposed both Christianity and the techno-scientific utopia, willing a return to Nature, "a counterfeit Eden bared red in tooth and claw."

## Without technology the world turns zero-sum

- Peter defines technology as "doing more with less."
- Without technological progress, there is a zero-sum world with a loser for every winner.
- He doubts a capitalist economic system could function without growth.
- He thinks representative democracy, which depends on win-win compromise, would probably stop functioning.
- In the Malthusian end state, resource constraints reduce marginal human existence to "eking out bare survival."
- Even the anti-Malthusian Julian Simon conceded this. Simon argued more people would be a great good only because he optimistically believed they would invent new things and raise living standards for everyone.

## We are far from the technological cornucopia

- Six billion people in the emerging world are beset daily by resource and pollution constraints.
- The Green Revolution in food production has decelerated tremendously since 1980. Average calorie consumption in rural India is lower today than in 1970.
- China has raised industrial output, but the failure of clean-energy technologies has meant massive environmental devastation.
- In the US, the rate of technological progress has been insufficient to guarantee higher living standards. For the first time ever, the younger generation has reduced expectations. There are not enough well-paying jobs for many college graduates, let alone everyone else.
- Many cannot afford healthy food and are nutritionally starved. He calls this "hidden Malthusianism" and says it perversely shows up as an obesity epidemic.
- In Manhattan, scarce affordable housing means rents rise far faster than wages. He frames this as a failure of technological innovation in skyscraper construction and transportation. Slumlords benefit at everyone else's expense, and the scarcity gets "dressed up with aesthetic sentiments."

## Distrust of technology is the post-Enlightenment consensus

- A scientific and technological utopia was the hallmark of the Enlightenment. Distrust of it is the hallmark of the post-Enlightenment, postmodern West.
- He treats the breadth of that distrust as a measure of how far postmodernity has displaced modernity.
- The Christian right and the Hollywood left agree on it, differing only in what they dislike: stem-cell research, fracking, or radical life extension (as against God's will or bad for the environment).
- Nearly every science-fiction movie of the past quarter century shows technology as a trap humanity builds for itself, with a menu of dystopias from The Terminator to The Matrix, Elysium and Avatar.
- A film whose arch-villain is a Luddite, an environmental extremist or an FDA regulator "does not get made." He says Hollywood both creates and reflects the broader cultural consensus.

## The twentieth century as a loss of hope

- Peter reads the twentieth century as a history of losing hope in the future.
- In hindsight, the nuclear age and the Manhattan Project may have been the key turning point: a great achievement that led to tremendous disillusionment.
- The disillusionment hit with full force in the 1970s, when the successor Apollo program collapsed and baby boomers redirected their energies into interminable culture wars.
- Scientists were put "on a short leash" and made to spend their time writing grant applications for modest extensions of existing paradigms.
- "The reign of science foretold in New Atlantis culminated and terminated at Los Alamos."

## Why the optimism has to be Judeo-Western, not atheist

- Peter says the optimism of Bacon and Hobbes belongs to a bygone era. He suggests optimism and atheistic materialism were always in tension. Engels could finesse the clash between endless dialectical progress and the heat death foretold by the second law of thermodynamics only by saying it lay far in the future.
- Atheist optimism meant escape from nature. Today's atheist pessimism means accepting nature, including its gruesome accidents and "the terrible rule of chance."
- Modern physical theories resemble Epicurean atoms moving randomly through the void, and such physics leads naturally to Stoicism and Epicurean hedonism.
- Judeo-Western optimism differs from Enlightenment atheist optimism in how far it believes chaos and nature "can and will be mastered": "The tyranny of Chance will give way to the providence of God." The movement from chaos to order starts in Genesis.
- Personal immortality requires a place where no accidents can happen. Lucretius and Epicurus had no such place, and Bacon and Hobbes skipped the question. "But with God all things are possible."
- Science and technology are natural allies of this optimism, provided one stays open to an eschatological frame in which God works through people to build the kingdom of heaven now, a kingdom both future and partly achievable in the present.
- Given a choice, he says to ally with atheist optimism over atheist pessimism. Faust's land-reclamation project may even be part of God's plan. In the Bible the sea is where the demon Leviathan lives and symbolizes chaos to be rolled back, and the end state is "and the sea was no more" (Rev. 21:1).
