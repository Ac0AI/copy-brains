## Why Farza went looking for a co-founder, and who Sharif is

- By mid-2025 Farza was tired of building alone: "I just wasn't having fun. And if I'm not having fun I don't think I'll build good things." He gets a lot of energy from working with others.
- He had been a solo founder for about 5 years (kanga, zipschool, buildspace). He calls those years painful and says they took a lot of "mental gymnastics" to stay sane.
- He wrote a note describing the person he wanted (farza.com/cofounder) and sent it to 15 friends who might know someone. He expected nothing from it, but figured he would learn something about himself.
- Sharif Shameem, one of the 15, replied that he wanted to work together. They met roughly a decade earlier through a HackerNews post: Farza was 21 and training AI models for video games, and Sharif was 19 and had just launched the GPU product VectorDash.
- They roomed together in SF and stayed close friends for about 7 years, but never worked together. Farza says the reasons were timing and maturity: at 21 he had a massive ego and "was an ass", and it took him a very long time to learn to work with others.
- Now 29 and 27, they were both comfortable with direct personal feedback, which made working together easy.

## Changing environment to find an idea: 14 days in Japan with no plan

- They decided on a Sunday, flew to Japan on Tuesday, and had no product idea. They went because they like Japan, and because they believe changing your environment produces new ideas.
- Their shared philosophy is "you gotta try stuff to find stuff", so the plan was to "aggressively try stuff."
- Setup: a quiet suburb of Tokyo (Setagaya City) with a nearby supermarket for pre-made food (lots of yakitori), so no time went to cooking or logistics. They worked from the apartment, Denny's and local cafes, and were the only foreigners in the area.
- Day 1: each prototyped something alone from 9AM, then they demoed to each other at 3PM.
  - Sharif built a desktop app that looked like ChatGPT but with Discord-style channels.
  - Farza built a Discord bot where two people prompt the model together and do real work alongside it. They wrote the title of his YouTube video this way, with Claude.
  - The bot was "quite bad" at first, but "the vibe was there", and they found it fun to generate ideas together with AI.

## Aligning on a vision: one-liners and launch tweets

- They merged the two prototypes (channels plus a multiplayer interface for models) by drafting many one-liners and potential launch tweets.
- Test for being on the same page: if the idea can be said in one line, or sounds exciting as a tweet, they agree. Then they "worked backwards from the one-liner" and built.
- The product became Tidbit, described as "like ChatGPT, but, with channels."

## Daily operating rhythm and working-together hygiene

- Every morning around 9AM they spent 30-60 minutes deciding what to finish by 9PM, then tried to do exactly that.
- The same check-in covered how they were working together. They raised and resolved any friction immediately. Since they had never worked together, Farza calls these check-ins "extremely important."
- By about Day 5 the trip's main goal was clear: build a product they love using. If they returned to SF with something they used every day, that was "a massive win."
- Most of the code was written by Claude Code, and both of them coded a lot. Farza says the cost showed up back in SF: "Claude just stacks technical debt."

## Demo-driven development and shameless public shipping

- They agreed to post daily demos right away and not care about polish, "optimizing for learnings. Not a user number, or a graph, or a money number."
- Demos forced them to get their thoughts in order. They didn't want to put out "absolute shit", but a demo was always fine.
- Day 5: the first demo went on Sharif's Twitter and did well. A few friends showed interest, which gave them a push when they still didn't know if the idea mattered.
- A day later they shipped a second demo on Farza's Twitter, recorded around midnight while jet-lagged. This one focused on the channel aspect, and "momentum just came."
- Result: about 1000+ DMs combined. Interest came from founders using it with teams or co-founders, college students collaborating, people planning trips with family or friends, and ChatGPT/Claude power-users tired of re-explaining themselves to Claude.
- Many of these use cases were ones they had never thought of: "people were coming up with the product for us."
- Some people just DM'd "Access", and he gave them access anyway.

## Turning attention into learning: user calls and a fast loop

- Once there was clear interest, Farza booked about 20 user calls while Sharif improved the product "every 2-hours." They sat next to each other 24/7.
- The goal of the calls was to learn why people were excited. Showing the product and talking to people "prompts my brain in new ways and gives me ideas."
- He ran a mix of experiments:
  - Gave some people free access and tracked them closely in PostHog.
  - Sent others a Stripe link in the DMs before the call.
- He describes this as random, with the aim of learning rather than any single metric.
- Findings: users wanted longer-context conversations and were tired of re-explaining themselves to Claude/GPT. Teams wanted to collaborate with each other and the model instead of passing GPT and Claude links around Slack.
- Outcome after 14 days: paying customers, users, a lot of attention, a product they were excited about, and clarity. Next steps were applying the feedback and running about 25 more user calls on the same loop.
