## Farza on shutting down Buildspace: timing, selling, and the team

- Farza thought about shutting down Buildspace for about a year before doing it. He talked it over with a friend a year before S5 IRL and gave the company 6 to 12 months to see if the feeling held: "you can't just shut down four years of work in like a couple of days."
- The decision point was after the S5 kickoff. His diary says S5 reached about 130,000 people, and the kickoff stream had about 20,000 live. He thought: "this is like the best it's ever going to be technically. Great team, great environment, everything's working", and he still didn't want to do it.
- His reasoning: "It's hard to run a company, but it's even harder to fake running a company." A good team sees through a founder who is no longer in it, so staying would have been a disservice to the team, fans and product.
- He ran S5 IRL fully anyway. His feeling in the moment was "this must all get done properly" and "we must not let the people down". The emotional crash came after the final post went out.
- He says he cares about the reputation of the roughly 20 people who worked at the company over 5 years more than his own. He wanted "worked at Buildspace" to stay a good line on a CV, not a stain.
- Public reaction was far better than he feared. Almost no one turned on him, and fans he met while traveling said versions of "I'm not happy about it but I get why you did it."

## Farza on why he didn't sell Buildspace, and what he would change

- Farza says the main reason he didn't sell was his own mindset: "I just thought the thing was worthless... why would anyone want this pile of garbage?" He admits this was wrong in hindsight, given the company's roughly $100M valuation.
- He says founders are "lost in the sauce" and can't see what the company is worth. He was always trying to push it forward, so he saw the flaws.
- He did look at selling and at hiring a CEO but didn't pursue them. There was no obvious acquirer or buyer for the IP. The ideas he had were non-obvious, such as Microsoft for Startups picking the company up for something like $25M.
- With no obvious option and a belief that the company had no value, he chose to close it out.
- The one thing he'd do differently: put much more effort into placing the team. He assumed no one on the team would want a job and felt it would be disrespectful to bring it up, so people had to fend for themselves. He says "I could have done a lot better there." Several of them ended up at Founders Inc., which he credits to luck, not his effort.
- He also thinks the company could have found a different home, with some financial moves done differently. He had "no regrets" overall.

## Farza on the 6 months of doing nothing after Buildspace

- After closing Buildspace, Farza took 6 months fully offline and traveled. A friend in Amsterdam told him to go to a different city every week and ask locals where to go next. He bounced between countries, including Berlin, Morocco and Pakistan.
- The first 3 months were bad. After 10 years on a laptop he felt dazed and didn't know what to do with himself.
- He wanted to prove to himself he had no compulsive "itch" to build. He did: he could sit on a couch all day and be happy.
- He found he didn't know whether he was happy, because he'd been wired only to make the company work. He asked an AI to scan his diary entries for the last time he was happy, and the answer was close to "pretty much nothing."
- His fix was a notebook where he rated each day out of 10 for happiness. For the first weeks he wrote "? out of 10." Eventually he logged days as a 6 and kept auditing what made him unhappy.
- He questioned habits that were really performance fuel, not enjoyment: running 10 km a day, watching anime, sauna and cold plunge. He found he liked simpler things, like grocery shopping and cooking, which he'd never done because he'd used DoorDash for 6 years.
- He spent hours a day in quiet churches and mosques as a way to be calm.
- He came back to San Francisco when he felt he could "reliably wake up and just be happy." His reason was only a gut feeling that something good was waiting there.
- On "optimize for impact, not happiness": he won't prescribe it for others, but he doesn't think he'll build anything good by being consistently unhappy. He says it isn't a playground, and "there are weeks or months that are hard," but he avoids being unhappy daily.

## Farza on rebuilding after a public failure: shots, collaboration and the comeback frame

- At his lowest, Farza believed no one would ever want to work with him or hear his name again. He says kind words from friends didn't change his mental state; only doing things did.
- His comeback frame came from people he admires, such as Steve Jobs with NeXT, Elon, Porter Robinson and LeBron. All had a stretch of public embarrassment or a lost championship before the next win. He compares himself to a good player who got injured in the playoffs, not a bad player.
- He defines "winning the championship" as doing the height of his work every day, consistently, while happy and energized. In business terms it means building something that wouldn't exist without him and his team together, like an iPhone or a Tesla.
- His 2025 approach was to take shots like a basketball player: sit on the court and shoot daily. His rule was "I have to make something and show at least one person" every week. He made something new almost daily, including a Slack interface for AI agents, a VR game about battling gorillas, a journaling tool called Free Write, and an AI tutorial channel.
- On bad ideas: "I don't care if it's horrible." He treats output as a muscle, and says nobody can name a single thing he did in 2025, but he needed that year to get where he is.
- He found he was happiest making things with others and unhappiest alone. His early collaboration was low-pressure, such as a 2-week trip to Japan with a friend (Sharief) just to make things, which he called the most healing creative activity of the previous two years.
- When a side project with a friend faded after 3 months, they both simply said they didn't want to keep going, so it never became a company. Timing and mindset have to match for both people.

## Farza on hiring and picking collaborators

- His top filter is whether he enjoys talking to the person: "that's like 95%." The last 5% is that they are really good at their craft.
- He tests skill through conversation. A real expert on a topic shows it within about 30 seconds, from the questions asked and the opening thoughts on the subject.
- For AI hires he asks how quickly they try a new model when it comes out. He once worked with someone who always did it within 5 minutes, and who dropped everything to tell him what had changed.
- He has interviewed somewhere between 1,000 and 2,000 people. To hire one person he figures he interviews around 80 to 100.
- He prefers to work with candidates for 2 to 3 months before hiring, ideally with them in person. If the company would fall apart when they leave after 3 months, that was the right person: "I'll do everything to keep that person."
- He prefers people he has history with, because he knows how they react when things go badly. His new team includes Nish, who won Buildspace Nights and Weekends season one and joined about two weeks before this recording.

## Farza on how HeyClicky started and the Clicky product path

- Farza's 2026 shift was to pick one thing: AI. He had trained models since 2016. While traveling he noticed he knew far more about AI than the people around him, and he taught strangers (for example a surf-camp owner in Morocco, who built his first website with him in a day).
- His thesis was to "democratize the models." He compares models to the craziest chips in the world with no laptop built around them yet.
- The first step was the dumbest, most direct one: stream on YouTube and teach people AI, the way he would teach someone on the street. With Julian, he helped about 10,000 people in a single month, and about 5,000 of them made their first thing with AI. He didn't want to be a YouTuber long term and did it to learn.
- In the community he saw the sticking point: beginners don't know what to type in the first box of tools like Claude Code, Codex or Replit, or what to do after the first website. So he built a tool that watched the learner's screen and guided them step by step, which was the first version, called Learning Buddy.
- Clicky came from a moment while he was learning DaVinci Resolve (to color grade his own videos). He kept screenshotting it into Claude 20 times in a row, and realized his existing code could let a model see his screen and teach him in real time. If it could teach DaVinci, it could teach almost anything.
- He sat on the idea for two weeks, thinking it was "pretty nonsense." His YC partner Aaron told him to just post it. His mentality: "I don't care how dog the idea is. I will happily just post it because I have no care about my reputation."
- About 8 weeks after posting, it was a company with a team, a "beautiful product" and paying users.
- He joined YC after Aaron pushed him for a couple of weeks. The line that landed was "you're already building your house alone." He joined with his YouTube channel and was working on a different idea for about the first month.

## Farza on fundraising for HeyClicky and how HeyClicky differs from Buildspace

- Farza says he fell into raising money, and each round came from relationships, not pitching:
  - About $300,000 from Founders Inc. (Ken) and Ryan Hoover, after he came back to SF and was nearly broke. He texted them to say he didn't know what he was doing but would build a company. His alternative was taking a job for a year at a large salary.
  - YC's $500,000.
  - $3 million from Ivan at Excel, whom he knew from his one job at 22 (training models at Mayhem, an AI gaming coach). Ivan offered it while Farza was still working on the YouTube channel.
- After YC demo day, his inbox was full of investors, and he says his content was getting millions of views. He lands at a $12 million total raise.
- He chose $12M on purpose: Buildspace raised $12M across its history (including Zip School), so he knew how to use that amount of capital. He liked putting himself in the same position again with a different company and team.
- He likes having more money "to an extent", since more capital lets him get more creative on the product and company.
- Biggest difference from Buildspace: he is now very focused on the business. He knows daily whether the company makes money and how much it loses per user, whereas at Buildspace the attitude was "we made a million there, we lost two million there, okay, next." He thinks he's a good business person but has never shown it, because he works in viral consumer products where building a great business is classically hard.
- The other difference is not "losing my head": setting up his own life so the earlier burnout doesn't recur.
- On success: he judges each day by whether it ended with a smiley face and good work shipped, and he aims to feel inspired at least once a day, because inspiration usually leads to good work. He says being "aloof" is partly a defense mechanism: if he took it too seriously, he'd psych himself out.
