## Farza's path into building: eBay, film school, and learning to "just do the work"

- At 13 he took a gym sign-spinner job. His dad (a car-sales/finance guy) told him he could be more than that, and Farza says his dad always "train[ed] me to think entrepreneurially."
- He then started selling donated clothes on eBay, and later a very specific type of blank DVD from one supplier.
- He says he was "the main guy on the market" for that product because he offered discount codes and did things other eBay sellers wouldn't, since at 15-16 he didn't know the rules.
- His margins were about 5%, he describes the money as good, and the business (Clothing House) passed $100K in sales before he was 18.
- He only went to college (UCF) because his mom wanted him to. His view: "the only valid reason" to finish school is a family promise like that.
- He argues the main bad reason to go is wanting "a place to figure it out", and he asks whether you can't gather 10 people like you and work together instead.
- At 18 his alternative plan was a house in South Florida with about 10 people who were also selling on eBay and making things online.
- He started in the film program (he wanted to be like Christopher Nolan). He left after seeing that film students watched movies while computer science students were "doing stuff from day one", so he switched to CS to make things immediately.
- His mindset: do everything as well as he can, and treat existing narratives as challenges to route around.

## Why he thinks college is "broken": expectations, not the education

- Farza says college in its purest form is good: "you go there and you learn something new and you leave", you learn to learn.
- What's broken is the promise that a degree gets you a job or a guaranteed income. He blames "whoever created that narrative", not the student.
- He frames Buildspace as "just a different path from college", a space to figure out your own idea, and not only for 18-year-olds. A 50-year-old with an idea can do it too.
- He says going to a weaker school (UCF, ranked around 180 at the time) helped him: he was surrounded by "normal people", not "killers", which let him build confidence.
- He suspects MIT or Berkeley would have drained that confidence. He arrived in San Francisco extremely confident.
- He says digital relationships can go far: his best friend is someone he has met in person about six times, from a Battlefield 3 ranking. Online can come first, and people over-index on physical proximity.
- Founders Inc began digitally as Furqan's weekly Discord chat during Covid, where he gave away one Ethereum (about $500 then) each week. The physical space came later.

## How Nights & Weekends emerged from tutorials

- Buildspace started as web3 tutorials (the Dowel course is cited as a hit). The tutorials "only got so big" and then fizzled, and he had no clear next step.
- He talked to users, who all said they wanted to keep working on their projects. People were customizing the tutorial projects (full DAOs came from a simple tutorial), which showed him demand for more depth.
- So he created a six-week program as a side project inside a roughly 5-6 person company. It wasn't an accelerator, just six weeks to take your project further.
- He pitched it to Stefan, who helped name it Nights & Weekends and design the brand. They shipped it fast.
- Season 1 numbers: 450 people started, about 60 graduated, and 40 bought tickets to come to San Francisco. The in-person event was only planned a week or two before.
- Seasons grew to about 1,200 (season 2), 7,500 (season 3) and 17,000 (season 4). He thought season 2 would break at 1,200, but it worked the same way at larger numbers.
- Early positioning was literally "a place for the hackers and the painters" (a Paul Graham line).
- Season 1 and 2 focused on web3 and AI. By season 3 he "opened up the gates" to artists, painters and other makers.
- His habit: every season he asks the team for "10 reasons we don't kill Nights & Weekends." He gets bored fast, but doesn't quit when bored. He asks "what needs to change here?" and kills the product only if nothing does.

## Building a one-to-many story, not a "community"

- Farza grew up watching Twitch streams and was struck by how close he felt to a streamer despite thousands in chat. That is why Buildspace is one-to-many.
- His premise: tell a great story and people will want to be part of it and complete their arc in it.
- He says "I even hate the word community". In his view Buildspace has a story that people attach to, more than to him personally.
- His comparison: "Harry Potter is a story." Readers feel like Ron or Harry. The team's job is to keep writing the next chapters and not let anyone else write the book, while leaving room for people to insert themselves.
- Concrete tactics: thousands watching together, funny videos, colorful image drops people become attached to, and "shenanigans".
- Buildspace never called itself a course or a cohort. The pitch was "come join us and work on your idea" with no expectation.
- He defines cringe from his gamer and film background: ugly shots, bad audio, and forcing 12 people into a group call. He is shy himself, so he optimizes for introverts (sit alone eight hours a day and share updates), assuming everyone is shy like him.
- He argues the world builds for people already confident and ambitious. Accelerators ignore you if you aren't building something big. Buildspace welcomes a small album or a to-do list app the same as a billion-dollar company.
- Word of mouth ("that Farza guy" / "that Buildspace thing") is the main way people find it, plus him being public about the journey on Twitter.
- Cringe for him changes over time. They called it Hogwarts for a while, then moved away from that branding.
- He controls the narrative, which is why he avoids magazines and business articles.

## Moving from online to a physical school

- Users who finished Nights & Weekends wanted more, and Farza had "nothing for them", yet they were his most motivated audience.
- His reasoning: the best version of Buildspace puts great people in front of you helping and teaching in person, not over a call. He made it for himself ("I would have never gone to a virtual school").
- About six months before this interview he decided to open a physical campus, spend as little as possible, and ship a Lo-Fi MVP.
- They spent about three months on it. He sketched the designs in his notebook and found two designers (Issa and Kyle) from Twitter.
- He threw a pizza party at the Founders Inc space and invited anyone to come look and give ideas. About 50 people showed up, and he found the two designers by talking to people for a few minutes.
- He wants a school to feel like McDonald's: Stanford's best parts delivered at McDonald's-style scale and consistency. He says that if they can keep quality at scale "we'll be number one", and otherwise "we'll probably flop."
- He doesn't run the campus day to day (Ahmed does). He says the brand can last as long as he does, and he compares it to Steve Jobs fronting Apple for 40 years.
- Ten-year picture: hundreds of thousands of people online through Nights & Weekends worldwide, thousands graduating through physical campuses, ideally about 25 campuses.
- Campus locations come from places the team likes to visit (for example Dubai "because we went to Dubai and it was fun"). He wants to pick by following the team's hearts but being logical about it and not getting emotionally attached.

## Funding, monetization and the business model

- He raised $10M from a16z while the product was still the web3 tutorials. His pitch to every investor: "you're not giving the idea $10 million, you're giving me $10 million", so vet him as much as you like.
- His original idea for the tutorials was teaching many people, helping them start companies or get jobs, and possibly tokenizing it.
- On monetization he says education has few models that get big. The one they're testing: charge governments a subscription to run a campus in their city.
- Example given: Abu Dhabi paying about $2M a year for a campus expected to produce a set amount of new revenue, companies and jobs.
- His angle is that most people in the program (musicians, YouTubers, small business owners) are essentially small companies, and governments want more innovators in their towns.
- He says this path could make Buildspace the biggest education company, and he was heading to Dubai within a couple of weeks of the interview.

## Running the team: solo, high-trust, little control

- He chose to be a solo founder, partly because he knew he was hard to work with when younger. He pushes back on the "you need co-founders" narrative.
- He credits discipline and range: "I can code for an hour, do sales for two hours, and raise money for three hours", and says people are "so much more skilled than you think."
- He doesn't do outbound hiring. People find him through Buildspace, Twitter or email because he's public about the journey.
- His hiring filter is a short conversation: he asks who they are, not their skill set, and says he is a good judge of who he wants to be around.
- Example: Stefan taught him what design is on a whiteboard for about 10 minutes. What mattered to him was that Stefan treated him as an equal and could handle new information without ego.
- He keeps people through pivots by letting them shape the future. Roughly a year before, when unsure what came next, he had everyone stop for a week to build a pitch deck for the next thing. He then took all the ideas and made a path (Stefan's "telescope for finding the best people" idea fed into Nights & Weekends scaling).
- His management style: "I don't really have a vision." There's no task board. People tell him what they're working on in a sentence on Monday, and if it's bad, they tell each other. He creates open space and has little control but a lot of trust, while the final call is his.
- His schedule: about four hours of solo (IC) work in the morning, then afternoons helping teammates and running Nights & Weekends, parts of the campus, IRL, and a government function.
- Biggest mistake: pivoting Zip School from one-to-many live classes (thousands watching one teacher) to small live classes of about ten kids. He calls it a purely emotional decision. Furqan told him he was being emotional and he didn't see it. It wasted about six months.
- On the differentiator for success in Buildspace: people with no preconceived notion of right or wrong. The ones who quit say things like "I couldn't figure out marketing", and he asks whether marketing is even the problem. In his words, "the less you know the better in this industry."

## Identity, fame and perspective

- He made YouTube videos from sixth grade (first shown only to family) and later posted them publicly so classmates would talk to him. "Farza" was an alter ego he made up. He was heavily bullied and says that is why he made the videos.
- His view on identity: "your identity is who you are, it's not what your work is." He says he's a son, brother and boyfriend and doesn't wrap his identity in Buildspace.
- On recognition: he's recognized a few times a week, treats it as proof the work means something, and takes it as motivation not to disappoint people. He never wanted to be famous.
- He distances himself from startup culture, which he finds cringe. He has very few founder friends, goes to no startup events and does almost no podcasts or talks.
- He mostly wants people to know "you're doing great", and says that expectations and narratives are stupid.
- He says he has no filter online, though he tries not to say "unhinged" things.
- His reason for doing this kind of work: to have fun and meet cool people, and he distrusts people who want more than that.
