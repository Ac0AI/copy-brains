## Building the Hogwarts for builders: Farza's path to buildspace

**Background and early companies**
- Farza started his first business at 13 after his dad refused to let him take a job spinning a sign outside a gym.
- He found a bag of his parents' donation clothes, sold a Polo Ralph Lauren shirt on eBay for about $9, and lost about $10 to fees and shipping.
- He kept going. Within two years it was a business doing roughly $120-130k a year in revenue at about 70% margin, which he describes as shipping up to 40 packages a day.
- He made almost two YouTube videos a week for four years (500+ videos), and credits that volume for his speaking and writing ability.
- He did a computer science degree at a "bottom two" university (UCF) because it was easy and left time to build. He did it mainly to satisfy his mom.

**What buildspace is and how it works**
- Farza describes buildspace as "a school for people who don't want to get a job."
- It has two majors, Web3 and machine learning. To finish a piece of content you must build and ship something to real people, not just study the math.
- Next comes "nights and weekends", a six-week sprint where people take what they learned and ship their own idea. The idea can be venture scale, for their neighborhood, or just for fun.
- Examples he gives: people going from zero to a job or a $2M raise in six weeks, and a musician trying to grow from 400 to 4,000 Spotify monthly listeners.
- The goal is not a job or a fundraise. It is to help people figure out what excites them and make progress on it.
- He contrasts this with bootcamps: "I'm not sitting here trying to teach you JavaScript... I don't give a [ __ ] about JavaScript." Skills are a means to an end.
- It is free for everyone. He says charging $10,000 would shut out "that random Pakistani kid" while meaning nothing to a Google PM.
- He says the content is "nothing special", about 12 pieces on a website. The differentiator is culture, the way Nike or Stanford sell culture more than product.

**Building culture from day zero**
- Every decision gets the question: "why is it different, why does it feel different?"
- He kicked off each cohort with a live Twitch show to rally people, "like Goku bringing the Spirit Bomb together."
- At the start he ran these three times a week (Saturday and two on Sunday), and says buildspace's growth came largely from him standing in front of thousands of people each week.
- He is introverted, so being on camera suits him better than meeting people in person.
- He says skills come from reps. He got good at speaking and writing by making things he enjoyed, not by trying to get good at speaking, and you need to stick with a skill for over a year before you can judge it.

**Brand, voice and growth tactics**
- The first 1,000 users came from "infiltrating" CS clubs at about 20 schools. The team built each a landing page (buildspace.so/<school>) and sent it to students.
- Pitch: "we're bringing the builders together at Berkeley, are you in?" It was backed by YC and a16z, so students wanted in. Schools gave about 20 users on average and some gave 200.
- He insists on being hyper-specific about the audience, e.g. 18-22 year olds at US colleges who want internships. If you can't describe your user three levels deep, you can't market to them. You can assume the details at the start.
- He is very selective about where buildspace is mentioned. He does almost no interviews, magazines or blogs, because any mention of the name should be somewhere really cool and he doesn't want his message mangled.
- Early on he delivered the message himself on Twitch and Twitter, which he calls very targeted.
- He polices voice in detail: lowercase, friendly support replies, not corporate ("why did you reply as if you work at Google?"). Tweets are never more than two lines in a row, and he checks how a tweet looks on mobile versus desktop.
- He admits the balance: spend some time on polish, but the growth engine matters too.

**Pivoting and choosing what to build**
- The company started in December 2019 as Zip Homeschool, software that registered a homeschool per state's laws. He built it in about two days.
- By the time of YC, with about 50 customers and then 40 in month four, he pivoted. He had also seen users abusing the homeschool system.
- Next came Zip School, a live TV channel for kids. It reached roughly 30,000 monthly users and millions of streamed minutes a week with a small team and about five teachers. It raised $2.2M from a16z around demo day in summer 2020.
- Over the following year he pivoted repeatedly: virtual classes with real teachers (about 50 part-time teachers), a mobile game, a "MasterClass for kids" and async recorded courses. He counts about 10 pivots total.
- By August 2021 he was out of ideas. Parents only paid for math and reading, which he didn't care about. He decided on a market pivot to developers.
- He wrote four content ideas (self-driving cars, machine learning, Web3, gaming), posted on Twitter, and got about 100 signups. Web3 had the most, so that is what he built.
- He tells his team: "trust me for two weeks." Results showed in two weeks and kept showing every two weeks after.
- He is wary of market pivots; the Zip Homeschool to Zip School move was only a product pivot with the same K-5 audience for about a year and a half.
- Idea advice: follow what you find interesting and ask "wouldn't it be sick though?" Don't let "what problem does it solve?" dictate the start, because it can't fire you up for 10 years. Time-box it to about six weeks, then check whether it solves a real problem. Don't let startup media or essays act as absolute laws. Airbnb or Stripe doing something doesn't mean you must.
- After six weeks, thousands of people had gone through buildspace. They said they hated college or their job and that it showed them a different path, which became the problem it solved.

**Metrics, mindset and sustaining the long game**
- Keep the graph in front of you, but if it's down, don't feel bad. Analyze what happened and change what you're doing: "if it's not growing, it doesn't mean you suck, it just means what you're doing sucks."
- Blaming himself sped up his learning, but he says it isn't sustainable over years.
- He aims to be about 90% logical and 10% emotionally attached, and if he drifts to 70/30 he takes a day.
- Don't emulate people. He copied Jeff Bezos as a teen and says it leaves you a "hodgepodge" of others. He warns against chasing a billion-dollar company as a goal, since that exhausts you.
- Expect it to take three to six years to get good at this, and accept that family and friends may doubt you.

**Fundraising: $10M at a $100M valuation**
- He says he has never sent a cold email to raise money. His approach is to create value, tell the world, and let investors come.
- a16z reached out when he had about 800-1,000 Twitter followers, which led to the $2.2M round about three months later.
- For the $10M round, he says winning a16z first meant the rest followed. Investors also included Founders Inc, Gary Vee and others.
- Having thousands of people going through the product each week made it far easier. He called it an intense month and a half.
- His advice: "build your network before you need it." The best network is a product people love, so ask how to get the right people to come to you.

**Context he gives on the team and current stage**
- At the time of the interview he describes buildspace as a small team that is profitable via sponsorships, and says he is serious about building a big company.
- He says his current "mountain" is balancing his drive to build with relationships, and that being a "degenerate" grinder works at 20-25 but not at 27.
