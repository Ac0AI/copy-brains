## Founder loneliness and getting personal validation when building solo

- Farza says being solo is "so difficult" emotionally. When Clicky went viral, he shipped late on a Monday night with no one to share it with.
- With a co-founder you high-five and get pizza. Solo, you "pat myself on the back, close my laptop, celebrate by drinking a glass of water," and go to bed.
- Solo, he says, "you have to really dig deep for personal validation." That is hard for anyone and harder when building.
- He says his worst emotional weeks come when the internet is most excited about what he's doing. Praise and inbound investor attention left him lethargic and flat, not happy.
- Loneliness is the emotion he is most prone to. His fix is to remember he is prone to it and recall that he has a partner, family and friends who support him.
- Not having a co-founder directly on the thing does not mean being alone.
- At 23 he was a "blind dog" sprinting forward and did not need anyone. At 30 he wants to collaborate.
- He credits his partner with keeping him sane and says he would never have returned to starting companies without her.

## Drive forward yourself and let people join

- His belief: "the best way to find people and to work with people is by just driving forward yourself. And as you drive forward, hopefully other people hop in your car."
- He admits it is easier said than done. He lets himself be down for a day or three, then finds the will to keep going almost every time.
- He asked two friends he calls geniuses to start a company together. Both declined and stayed in their jobs, which saddened him.
- His read: it is not that something is wrong with him. Co-founder search takes time, and "timing is such a big thing."

## Forming a 30-day hack team in three days

- After the users' feedback, he invited a few friends to SF for 30 days to hack on Clicky, new interfaces for agents, and education. They are not co-founders, just people working together in person.
- Four people agreed within about 3 days and bought tickets to arrive the following week:
  - A filmmaker flying from Bangalore to make videos.
  - An engineer he had worked with before, coming from Bali.
  - A neighbor who won a past Buildspace season.
  - A non-technical biomedical student from Houston who had just dropped out. He built the best custom Clicky implementation Farza saw, out of about 80 posted. His video had only about 80 views.
- Farza DM'd the Houston student, FaceTimed him, and the next day he agreed to fly out. Farza is paying for the flight.
- Clicky was open-sourced, which is how people made custom versions. Farza plans to keep the current version open source and make future code closed source.

## Take shelved ideas off the shelf and ship them

- Farza says he hates an idea sitting stale: "every idea should be just taken off the shelf and just thrown out into the world. And you just see what happens."
- Clicky had reached about 10 users the week before. He judged it a "shitty idea" and was letting it die.
- On Monday night he recorded a demo in about 10 minutes at the YC office, posted it on Twitter, closed his laptop and went to dinner. By the time he got back it was going viral, reaching roughly 1 to 2 million views over 2 to 3 days.
- Views mean little to him: "virality means absolutely nothing to me." He says a video of a celebrity dancing with AI would get a million views and a video of himself dancing maybe a thousand. Usage is what matters.
- That night he found the Mac app had API keys hard-coded, so anyone could steal them. He spent about 2 hours rebuilding it with a back end and launched around 10:30pm.
- By the next morning there were thousands of downloads.

## Talking to your top users instead of guessing

- When he doubted whether to continue, he did "the most simple thing possible": he looked at PostHog analytics, picked 10 of the top 25 users by usage, and emailed them to book calls the next day. He spoke with about four or five on Friday.
- Numbers about 4 days after launch: about 25,000 messages sent, about 4,000 unique downloads, and almost 600 users retained at day 4 (using it every day).
- Use cases he heard:
  - A founder writing all his LinkedIn content with it.
  - A video editor moving from After Effects to DaVinci.
  - A pro Xcode developer getting feedback on his iPhone app's animations.
  - A guy watching anime with it and discussing characters and story.
- His takeaway: "As a founder, you have no freaking idea who's going to use your product and why they're going to use it until you put it out there." He had launched Clicky as an AI teacher, and users found their own uses.
- Those uses excited him more than the teaching angle did. He wasn't excited about teaching as a business or long-term product.
- Every call ended the same way. They asked what was next, he said he probably wouldn't keep working on it, and each user urged him to continue. He notes that usually the founder is the one pitching users, and here it was reversed.

## How Farza treats user feedback

- The founder's job is "not to come up with the product out of my brain." It is to see how people use the product, what they find useful, and guide it.
- He disagrees with "build what people tell me to." If a user says "build this," he won't build exactly that.
- He treats feedback as inspiration: he listens, processes it, and builds it into the product in some way, not one-to-one. He jokes it is like being an LLM with users prompting him: tokens in, his own output out.

## Using a group office hours one-liner to find an idea

- At YC group office hours on Monday, founders explain what they're working on to about 25 people. Farza calls this "very underrated" and used to make Buildspace students do it.
- He found it hard to explain a project when he did not know what he was working on, but said it is a good exercise because it forces a one-liner.
- His one-liner that day: "a Pinterest but for companies," where a team collects things that inspire them as they browse (tweets, research papers, articles) into one shared knowledge base. He liked it more after saying it aloud and built a simple version that day.
- He showed it to his YC partner Aaron, who gave him more ideas and a comparison to workflows at another big-company CEO.
