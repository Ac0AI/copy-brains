## Early life: eBay, a $100K/year business at 15, and why he never got a job

- Farza was born in Pakistan in 1995 and raised in Florida. His dad started as a New York taxi driver, then a Miami pizza maker, then a car salesman at Nissan from 1999 until he resigned in 2026.
- At 13, Farza got a job offer spinning a sign outside a gym ($12/hour). His dad refused: "You're smarter than that. You're more creative than that. You're not allowed to get a job."
- He noticed his family donating clothes once a year, asked to sell a $40 Calvin Klein shirt on eBay, and started a six-year online business selling blank media, clothes and other goods.
- The business existed to fund a YouTube channel: "I wanted a job so I can make cooler videos." Money went back into cameras, props and costumes, sometimes thousands of dollars a day.
- By 15 he was making about $100,000 a year, more than his teachers. That broke the "go to college to get a job that pays six figures" logic and showed him "you can live life however you want to live life and make your value however you want to make your value."
- His dad quizzed him at dinner every night ("How much did you sell this week? Why did it go down?"), which Farza describes as daily training, not pressure. Family rules were only three: no drugs or alcohol, no girls, straight A's.

## How he got into Y Combinator

- In 2016-2017 he trained AI models for video games. One, which analyzed League of Legends gameplay, went viral, and "basically every technology company" (Google and most SF startups) tried to hire him.
- His plan was to graduate, then move to SF or New York to start his next company.
- A founder named Ivan, whose company was associated with Y Combinator (which Farza had never heard of), invited him to SF. He flew out within weeks, at age 22.
- At 24 he got into YC with Zip Homeschool, an online learning platform for homeschooled kids. It later pivoted into Buildspace.

## Lessons from Buildspace shutting down

- Buildspace's shutdown was public and, he says, "fully me": he couldn't take it anymore and broke down "as if you're a fighter."
- He was obsessed with what people said about Buildspace; one bad comment would stay in his head. He was also constantly asked about competitors and the next milestone.
- He now refuses those questions: "A great company is built by a great product and that's all we should care about. Someone should grow the product and someone should build the product."
- His key realization: "Companies only close for one reason because the founder didn't want to do it anymore." You could have kept working at Starbucks and spent an hour a week on it.
- His resulting rule: only stop if you give up, and arrange life around not giving up. The game is "who remains standing, not who was the best."
- He says that had he kept Buildspace alive, "it'd be doing so much better just cuz I stayed alive," because work compounds when you keep building on it.
- Practical mindset: after 16 weeks of no growth you can be upset or just ask "what are we doing next?" He compares it to staying calm rather than losing sleep.

## Building HeyClicky: demo, team, and tools

- Clicky began as a solo demo that went viral. He then asked friends and colleagues to fly to San Francisco and turn it into a company in six weeks.
- The OG team came from Poland, India and Peru. Jose, from Peru, built an open source fork of Clicky that had about 12 views on Twitter. Farza was impressed, FaceTimed him while Jose sat in his car in Texas, and asked if he'd fly to SF the next day. Jose said yes.
- He built the team because "if I worked on stuff alone... I wouldn't have the same energy."
- He built Clicky because "nothing else existed for me": "everyone's circle jerking themselves about AI and then I didn't find a single product I enjoyed using."
- Tech approach: he doesn't know Swift, so the product is generated code plus all-day prompting (the landing page was handmade). He uses whichever model is best at the moment (Claude now; GPT-6 or Grok's coding tool when they're better).
- Favorite product-ideation flow: take something he loves from the product, put it in Claude Code or another strong model, and ask it to brainstorm why he likes it and how to build a fresh version of it into Clicky.
- Internal tool "Black Hole": all company data (meeting transcripts, messages, everything) is dumped in and organized by an agent like a Wikipedia (successor to his earlier Farzipedia). The team asks it things like "five really cool hooks for videos based on what users are actually telling us."
- Product goal: "make the simple things magical for you."

## Making content: write first, post, then move on

- Posting policy for himself and the company: hit post on X, don't look at the results, and immediately ask about the next video. Results take 24-48 hours, so checking is useless. If everyone loves it, "who cares? They were meant to love it if we thought it was cool."
- Reason: praise and criticism both distort judgment, so the team asks what they deeply believe about themselves ("are we actually innovating?") instead of what the world says.
- He writes before he creates anything. Daily practice: 15 minutes of nonstop free writing on his own free app, Freewrites (freewrites.io), which earns him nothing. He angles his mind toward work and story, and hooks and good sentences appear.
- Advice he gave his YC batch: open Instagram Reels, record 30 unedited seconds on what you're doing, post, and repeat for 30 days. Nobody did it, and instead founders hired agencies for $10M-announcement videos "nobody cares [about] in two days."
- His reasoning: across 30 videos, one will probably hit about 1,000 views while others get about 50. Ask why, then make the next 30 better. "First make a thousand bad videos, or at least 30 bad videos over 30 days." His own instinct comes from having made thousands of bad ones.
- On people wanting the "big flashy DSLR presence": "Everybody wants to make the Odyssey before they've even made a shitty iPhone movie."

## Public persona and what he shares

- He says he doesn't think about comparisons to Steve Jobs and is "so far from being that level."
- He struggles to name living founders who inspire them the way Patrick Collison and Brian Chesky did in 2018; much of the industry conversation today is hate toward figures like Sam Altman or the Anthropic founders.
- His philosophy: share only what you're building and your take on what the world needs. Nobody knows his politics or personal life, only his products. He thinks that is the best form of communication, preferable to "random CEO pulling up to CNN talking about his take of the future."
- He has kept a Pakistani flag on his Twitter profile for about a decade, because growing up he never saw anyone who looked like him building technology. He wanted to be someone his younger self, "a Pakistani kid from Florida," could have looked up to.
