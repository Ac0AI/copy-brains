## Why Farza ran a live stream instead of a launch for Tidbit

Farza and his co-founder Sharif ran a live stream to force Tidbit out the door. Farza states two reasons.

- He is fatigued by "launches" and rarely converts on them. He says he has not watched a company's launch video in months.
- He "actually despise[s] launch culture in 2025" and misses "the 2016 vibes of people just putting stuff out they were jamming on."
- He and Sharif like deadlines that can't be pushed, because missing one means embarrassing themselves. "Forcing functions are good."

## Goal for the stream: 10 paying teams and a good demo

- The target was to find 10 legit teams willing to pay $99 a month, plus give a good demo.
- He admits they could have reached 10 teams by messaging friends or emailing potential users. He found that boring: "if we're not into it, we'll likely do bad work."
- Result: 14 customers, and a demo he called fun.
- Next step: make those 14 "really, really happy." Then "14 can turn into 28, and 28 to 56."

## Splitting the work between co-founders

- Sharif went all in on preparing the product for the stream.
- Farza went all in on making noise, working out why the stream would be interesting, and getting people to show up.
- Each trusted the other to deliver.
- As a former solo founder, he had to hold everything in his head at once, which "riddled me with anxiety." This split let him focus on what he loved doing.

## Deciding the narrative first

- Farza treats streams, launches, tweets, shorts and every touchpoint as "the narrative."
- He decides the narrative before anything else. If it isn't clear and compelling, the audience gets confused and loses interest.
- He wants narratives that are easy to latch onto and easy to tell friends.
- "I never make up narratives." The narrative is usually already in front of him, and he just puts it into words.
- The obvious question was why anyone would attend a 45-minute midweek stream for a barely working product.
- The answer was the real story: two guys, a few weeks in, still unsure, trying to make some magic.
- He wrote one clarifying sentence, then derived everything from it: the landing page, invites, tweets, stream look, and how the product would be presented.

## Low-key promotion and the audience Farza wanted

- He built a simple landing page for the stream.
- He made a few tweets and posts on Instagram and LinkedIn, emailed people who downloaded Freewrite, and dropped messages in a few WhatsApp groups. It was mostly word of mouth.
- He knew a tweet like "come to our livestream" would flop because of audience fatigue, so he stuck to the narrative instead.
- About 1,000 people RSVPed.
- He prefers 25 attendees who care over 2,500 who are barely listening.
- He criticizes founders chasing virality and numbers instead of "people that can help them build a better product + be a part of their true audience."
- He would rather find 10 people who "genuinely give a fuck," believe in the core idea and will iterate with him than get 1,000 likes on a tweet or 1M views on a video.
- His gut call was that if 100 people thought the product was cool, they would drive 1,000 people to the recording.

## Stream production: roughly 8 hours of setup

- He cut a simple waiting room from old footage of himself and Sharif in Japan.
- A friend, Josh, helped fix his camera setup (his settings had been poor for months) and set basic lighting and framing.
- He spent a few hours on the stream narrative and flow: what to show, what order, and what keeps people excited.
- He put the plan in an Apple Note and rehearsed it with Sharif the morning of the stream.

## Writing the conversion page: headlines and copy

- The question was how to turn stream interest into leads and sign-ups, and which headline would convert teams.
- Candidate headlines were "Claude for your team," "Claude, but, multiplayer" and "Your third co-founder." None felt strong, and he says he wouldn't convert on any of them.
- Farza and Sharif had been discussing an ideal where Tidbit is the only place a team chats and works alongside AI models, so Slack is no longer needed. Farza loved that it felt "new, different, direct."
- He took the landing page copy from 2015 Discord's landing page, which felt punchy and direct. Discord's enemy was TeamSpeak, and Tidbit paints Slack as the enemy.
- He may not keep that messaging long term, but it lets them test teams messaging aggressively.
- He keeps a diary of phrases he loves, such as "In Tidbit, Claude sits in your teams conversations." and "We want it to feel like you have this genius third co-worker you never even knew."

## Using the product itself to write the letter

- Farza and Sharif keep a #user-convos channel in Tidbit with every user call transcript, interesting quote and interesting DM.
- He gave Tidbit a screenshot of the old hey.com launch letter and asked it to write a similar letter about what they were building.
- The draft drew on real examples from users that he had forgotten about.
- One generated line: "When your cofounder talks about new pricing models, Claude might chime in with an insight."
- His lesson: "When AI has deep context, it performs really well." When the founders are impressed by their own tool, he treats that as a good sign.
- He generated several letters, picked his favorite pieces, ran them by Sharif, and wrote the final section.
- The conversion section gave two options: pay $99, or tell us you're broke. He likes giving users funny choices.
- His test: "it felt like a landing page I'd convert on for $99 a month. If I wouldn't convert on it no one else would."

## Results and why 14,000 views was enough

- The stream got 13,000 views on Twitter and 1,800 on YouTube Live, about 14,000 total.
- He says he has gone viral a bunch, but cares about "the 'right' views" at this stage.
- "I've made stuff that gets 1M views and 10 sign ups. It sucks."
- Views alone don't equal success. Success is a true audience, "a true group of people that give a shit."
- The viewers were mostly people curious about the product, founders with teams, ambitious people curious how AI models change teamwork, and friends.
- "Our DMs were full of the people we wanted to be hearing from."
- The stream forced "a lot of clarity" out of him and Sharif in a short time. Their goal is to keep clarifying the idea in a focused way and fast: "Every single day counts."
