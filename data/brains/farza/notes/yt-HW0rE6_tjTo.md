## Start with something repeatable, and run the supplier playbook online

- Farza started companies at 13 and says he had one near $100K in revenue by 15.
- His first sales were his dad's donated clothes on eBay, such as Calvin Klein jeans at about $10.
- That became a garage-sale flipping business, where he had to "make magic every single weekend" to find stock.
- He then sold blank DVDs to indie game developers in Latin America (around 2009), who needed discs to burn and sell their games.
- The idea came from his uncle's DVD shop. A local supplier sold blank discs and sleeves, so he did the same thing on the internet.
- His lesson is that a product he could recreate and repeat beat the one-off hustle of garage sales.
- He ran the DVD business for about seven years. It later pivoted into a t-shirt business and other things.

## Commit to one thing so learnings compound

- He rejects the "new product every month" approach: "nothing will teach you more than just grinding on the same thing every day for like three years."
- A founder who launched six unrelated products probably "didn't learn anything," because learning comes from launching and then sitting on it.
- Pivoting is fine when the new thing is close to the old one, so the lessons carry over. He calls pivoting every two to three months into something unrelated "crazy."
- He sets basic numeric goals early and tracks them:
  - eBay: $100 in revenue.
  - Self-driving car: a target model accuracy.
  - Buildspace: a number by the end of the month or year.
- He warns against working for 10 years on something only five people use, unless that is genuinely what you want.
- He still gives himself this advice at 26 and frames the goal as being a happy builder.

## Build the core loop before writing code

- Consumer products grow from a simple loop that you build once and then repeat.
- Buildspace's loop: sign up, enroll in a project, complete it, get an NFT, post it on Twitter, repeat. He credits this loop for essentially all of the early growth.
- He says to "draw that circle on a piece of paper before you write any code" and check that it makes sense.
- Making a React app is easy now. The impressive part is people using it the day after launch.
- Other growth levers he names:
  - Shipping something new every month, such as a new project each month, which got Buildspace onto Solana's homepage.
  - Partnering with blockchains and college blockchain clubs, which supplied the earliest users.
  - Being honest with the community.
- The first step was distribution: free web3 learning content that captured emails and an audience, which grew to about 55,000 Discord members.

## Talk to users and pivot early

- Buildspace pivoted into web3 about two weeks after starting, because users said they wanted to learn web3.
- He already liked the tech, which made the pivot easy.
- Zip School (homeschooling) began as a Webflow form plus Typeform, built in a day or two at a hackathon.
- The first customer paid $10 a month, and he walked her through the process manually.
- About three months later it had roughly 50 customers and around $500 MRR, and he applied to YC.
- During YC he pivoted again, to "Twitch for kids" with about 30 teachers streaming, and raised from a16z on that.
- He values having a few people ahead of him to ask dumb questions. One was a friend who is now Thirdweb's CEO and gave him $25K early. He calls YC a school for founders who ask dumb questions.
- For him YC was an easy call: he was a solo founder with no money, and his YC partners helped through real problems.

## Turn a content audience into a network with ownership

- Content was the way in. The long-term goal is a network, closer to LinkedIn, where professionals connect.
- Claimed metrics at the time:
  - About 20,000 profiles, growing around 5,000 every couple of days.
  - Hundreds of companies using the recruiting tool.
  - Inbound hiring requests from companies like Disney, who "just want to be where the most activity is."
  - About $1M in revenue.
- His view of the moat in big social networks is that people stay only because the network is there. Buildspace can undercut that by rewarding core actions with ownership:
  - Learning something, referring five people, or connecting with ten people each earns an NFT.
  - Part of revenue goes into a public treasury governed by NFT holders.
  - Holders can fund sub-groups such as Buildspace India, Women, Africa and Founders.
- On tokenized communities, he rejects a liquid, speculative coin because it fills the community with people trying to make money.
- He prefers non-transferable, non-liquid NFTs given for good contributions, which give people a seat at the table on what comes next.

## Monetization and planning horizon

- Normal users stay free. He doesn't plan to charge regular end users.
- Protocols such as Solana, Polygon, Ethereum and Avalanche pay Buildspace for access to its community.
- He sees a possible premium model for larger companies, built around the recruiting tool, but says the exact model isn't set.
- He plans on three horizons: one month, one year and ten years. The 10-year vision can stay vague ("a product used by billions") because the details will change.
- He thinks planning month by month for years is pointless.

## Treat technology as a tool

- He tinkers with emerging tech (AR, ML, web3, brain hardware) and does biweekly "hack days" with friends, shipping something in a day.
- His advice: "Don't get too captured by technologies." Machine learning or web3 has no value on its own, only through the problem it solves.
- He compares the rush into web3 to the rush into ML in 2016, and says people who chase hype should ask what problem they push forward.
- He sees body and brain hardware as the next shift: EEG headsets, finger-tracking gloves and eye-tracking cameras.
- He thinks starting with this hardware costs little. Buildspace's treasury funded a 16-year-old for about $300 for a disability-related project.

## Team and hiring

- Buildspace is mostly remote, at seven people and heading to 12 or 13.
- He says to meet teammates in person early and keep meeting every quarter or so, because going from in-person to remote is easy and the reverse is hard.
- He doesn't care about age and hired a 16-year-old.
- He says he'd rather pay someone to build something they pitch than run an "internship."
- On college he says "useless 99 percent of the time." He attended only to make his mom happy, and thinks you can build the same social experience for far less.
- A smaller, tight community of 10,000 who care beats 50,000 in a Discord, since closeness dilutes with scale.
