## Starting a company without a vision: trust and consistency over theatre

Farza raised $12M for his company (buildspace) and hired a strong team while openly saying "I have no idea" when asked for his 10-year plan. He argues a founder does not need a vision to start.

- For years he performed as a "visionary" because books and role models (great CEOs, directors, writers) suggested a god-given vision was required.
- He says that act was painful and "That wasn't me." He is not a natural storyteller or persuasive speaker; he is a "quiet grinder" who finds answers by working toward them.
- His rule: "don't be someone you're not." Improve yourself and fix your flaws, but never pretend to be someone else.

## What changed in 2019: admitting he had no vision

In 2019, at 23, Farza stopped faking confidence and started telling the truth: he did not know exactly where he was going, only that he was going toward "something".

- He could not describe the next 365 days, let alone 10 years.
- His standard line to hires and investors: "Down to figure it out together?"
- Many high-value hires and investors looked at him like a fool and never came back. Many people thought he was an idiot.
- The cost was real, but he accepted it.

## Honesty as a filter for hires and investors

Being upfront about not having a vision changed who joined and who invested.

- It attracted people willing to "navigate the unknown", who did not need a weekly vision or pep talk from him and who trusted the team would work it out together.
- These people make up the buildspace team today.
- Investors worked the same way: they knew he had no polished vision of the future but trusted him to figure out the long-term magic.

## Why people trusted him: results, not persuasion

Farza says trust came from a track record, not from being a persuasive visionary.

- Most people say they will do something and then you never hear from them for two years.
- His pattern: say he will do something, do it, then update people on the results, whether he failed or succeeded.
- He held this pattern for many years.
- His summary: "Just doing the work vs playing theatrics." Trust was "built on results".

## How a long-term vision actually forms

He is clear that a long-term view is eventually necessary: fantastic things are not built on 1-2 year timelines and often take 10-20 years. But the vision is a skill built over time, not a flash of genius.

- You cannot force a vision. It comes "bit by bit, over years and years" from dedicating yourself to a single craft.
- His movie example: Year 1 make a movie, Year 2 make another. By Year 3 you have learned enough to have a better vision for Year 3. Years 3-4 you make two more and learn what scripts to write, how to advertise, who to hire for the set. Year 5's vision gets clearer.
- By Year 10 you have a better 10-20 year vision and are "100X more equipped" to execute it.
- Swap movies for a generational company, the world's best sushi, or anything else.

## The fog of war and trusting the daily work

Farza describes the required mindset as boring: trust that doing the daily work to the best of your ability, while dedicated to the craft, will make things figure themselves out.

- Trust in yourself is "everything".
- Many give up because "the fog of war" is intense. He says he has thought about giving up hundreds of times.
- His own timeline: only now, nearly 5 years, thousands of work days, millions in revenue and a full team later, is he starting to have a long-term vision for the work and the company's future. He stresses "5. Damn. Years. Later."
- He now feels clear and excited about the long-term work, with good people alongside him.

## Takeaways

- You do not need a vision to start; a vision is something you work toward.
- Trust that doing the work gets you there.
- Do not play a role. Be yourself, admit what you do not know, and let honesty filter in people who want to figure it out with you.
- Build credibility by repeatedly saying what you will do, doing it, and reporting results.
