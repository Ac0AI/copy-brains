## How Farza approached raising from a16z as a solo, first-time founder

- Farza was a solo founder who had never raised money. He had built things since age 13, including products with 1M+ users, but never a startup.
- In 2019 he saw pitching as "cringe" and didn't want to learn it.
- His philosophy: build something really cool, talk about it online, and people will reach out and invest.
- He was building ZipHomeschool alone at his Mom's house. It was software that helps parents create a legal homeschool for their child, which he compared to "Stripe Atlas, but for homeschools."
- He shipped the MVP and got his first customer in the first week.
- Four months in he had about 50 customers and solid momentum.
- The more he talked about progress online, the more good people reached out to help.

## How the a16z conversation started and went

- In May 2020 a16z sent him a cold email, and the first call went well.
- On the call he talked about customers, growth, and his vision for an operating system for the next 10M homeschoolers. He did not run a polished pitch.
- When a16z asked how much he was raising, he was honest and told them he was a noob. He says he is "honest to a fault" and says exactly what he is thinking.
- Being upfront did not turn them off. Anne Lee Skates and Connie Chan agreed to a next step.

## Weekly updates instead of a competitive process

- For the next two months he spoke with a16z once a week for 30 minutes. Each call was a progress update plus brainstorming new ideas.
- Many people told him not to do this and to run a "competitive" fundraising process. He thought that was a dumb game and didn't do it.
- Why the cadence worked for both sides:
  - a16z got more data on him every week.
  - He kept building. If the company blew up, they would probably invest. If not, he would "just end up profitable."

## Letting traction drive the outcome

- During those two months he made a big pivot from legal software to "Twitch for teachers," where teachers streamed live classes to thousands of kids.
- It went viral, with 100,000 class joins a week and millions of minutes streamed a week. The company was still just him.
- After the two months a16z invested $2M. He says he got the valuation he wanted.
- Papers were signed and money wired about a month after the call that confirmed the investment.

## His takeaway on fundraising

- He sees many people waste time "playing the game of finding investors," or use it as an excuse for lack of progress.
- His position: "you simply need to put out really good work to an audience that grows over a long horizon. That is it."
- Public progress attracts inbound interest, so he built in public instead of chasing investors.
- Leaning on a friend (Furqan) through a hard period, with a weekly talk about the company's problems, gave him strength.

## The follow-on raise

- About two years later, with the same team, company and cap table, he pivoted away from K-5 to a new idea that became buildspace.
- buildspace grew big and raised $10M, with half coming from a16z, who doubled down.
- His closing advice: "Keep your head up. Stay shipping."
