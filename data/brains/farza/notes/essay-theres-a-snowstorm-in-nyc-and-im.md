## Selling a 3-day "first dollar" program instead of an AI chatbot app

- Farza and Julian time-boxed the project to 5 days. They first assumed a mobile app, then dropped it: a standalone AI chatbot ("use my AI chat bot to help you work through your mental barriers") was something they felt they would never use themselves. Farza says he only puts out things he himself would convert on or use.
- Instead they sold a simple, easy-to-understand 3-day program, announced in a LinkedIn post on Sunday night. They made the product on Monday and ran the first session on Tuesday.
- Within a few hours the post brought in over $1,400 and the first group. They charged money partly because Julian wanted Fred Again tickets in NYC.
- The final group was 35 people, including a teacher making worksheets for other teachers, a 65-year-old journalist working on AI for reporters, and a photographer who wanted to help others become photographers. Most had no clear idea at the start.
- They deliberately left AI out of the pitch: "No one wakes up and wants to use AI for mental breakthroughs. So, why sell it when people don't even know they want it? I rather package it differently."
- Takeaway: "No one signs up for 'builder therapy.' But tell them the goal is their first dollar in 3 days and they'll work through whatever's in the way."

## Program structure: lecture plus a shared product session

- Three sessions of 1.5 hours each, all on Zoom, with about 70% lecture from Farza and Julian and about 30% solo work in the product.
- Daily goals:
  - Day 1: come up with an idea.
  - Day 2: make a small version and show someone.
  - Day 3: make your first $.
- After lecturing, they put on music and had everyone use the product together. Making the AI part a group activity made it feel more magical, and let them prime people on how to have an effective conversation with the bot.
- His principle: "With bots, the quality of the conversation is the product. So anything that can make that feel better will make everything feel better."
- He notes none of the 35 participants got a 1-on-1 call.

## The product: free-write, then a bot that digs deeper

- Julian built it in a few hours. Flow:
  1. Click the day you're on.
  2. Write freely for 15 minutes on that day's topic.
  3. Have a 15-minute conversation with a chatbot using a special prompt that takes the entry and digs deeper, on the idea or on the person's mindset.
- Participants were told the founders would read all messages and notes, but to write openly anyway.
- On Day 1 people wrote and chatted deeply. Farza says the entries went far beyond "I wanna make a project," touching fear of judgement and family relationships (one person described an elder brother blocking them from building).
- This confirmed his view that participants didn't need more motivation, knowledge or "another startup school." They needed to work through their mental blockers.
- Afterward he had Claude Code read everyone's bot chats, pull out the "breakthrough moments" and quote users directly.

## Why people don't start: psychology, not money or knowledge

- Farza's thesis: the main barrier isn't money, knowledge or motivation. People don't understand their own psychology and carry hundreds of mental blockers about why they "can't" or aren't "ready."
- Example: a friend of his cousin wanted to become a content creator. He pointed out she already had an IG account and a phone camera, and suggested filming a 15-second Reel on the spot. She got nervous and refused out of fear of judgement. His point: giving her more knowledge wouldn't fix that.
- He estimates this is where about 90% of people are: they have the ability but "shoot themselves in the foot before they even begin."
- Common blockers he lists:
  - "I'm not smart enough to do this idea yet."
  - "If I make something, and it isn't perfect, and it fails, what then."
  - "All my ideas are small and useless compared to everyone else."
  - "It's not the right time, I'm busy with school."
  - "This idea will never make money."
- His framing of the opportunity: unlock more people to believe in themselves, teach them the skills, and have them go for it. He says productizing that would be one of the most important initiatives.
- His own method in 1-on-1 chats: question the core belief instead of arguing with it, e.g. "Why do you believe something must be good to post it? How can you know what good or bad is if you've never even posted anything? Who are you trying to impress?"

## Charging money was the deepest blocker

- Day 3 (make money day) surfaced the deepest limiting beliefs. Many participants wouldn't charge because they felt what they had wasn't good enough.
- Farza treats this as diagnostic: Why does something have to be "good" to charge? Are you more afraid of people saying no, or of being rejected outright? Why do you care so much?
- The 30 minutes of AI-assisted introspection led many participants to breakthroughs or reframed beliefs, and some went from "idk what I'm doing" to first paying customers in 3 days.

## Using models to push humans, and the buildspace lesson

- Farza and Julian both use Claude Opus to talk through their own mental barriers, and see it as a strong outside observer of one's actions and beliefs. Farza has long thought models should be used "to push humans."
- Buildspace context: Nights & Weekends season 5 had over 120,000 people. He wished he could have given every participant a 1.5-hour 1-on-1, but human mentors don't scale (tiny supply, huge demand). He believes thousands were lost to the stories they told themselves.
- In 2024 buildspace had no way to help people with this because the models weren't good enough. Now they are.
- Earlier history he cites: TeamMood at 19 (37 participants, 5 mentors, each person assigned a public repo so progress was visible; 10 people started their first projects after 8 weeks), then zipschool and buildspace.

## Takeaways Farza draws

- Live format matters: the product alone as a standalone app likely wouldn't have been as effective. "Live remains extremely under explored in tech."
- People won't opt into AI introspection, but in a room with others, with a prompt and music, "suddenly they're writing things they've never said out loud."
- "30 minutes of the right questions > 30 hours of tutorials."
- He frames his work as "building stages where people give themselves permission to try." It was a roughly 4-day project and he is unsure where it goes next, but plans to carry the learnings forward.
