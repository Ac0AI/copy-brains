## Start with the one-line hook before writing code

- Farza says he does not write a line of code until he has a headline-style line for the product. For Hey Clicky it was "An AI that can see your screen and teach you anything." His test: would he stop scrolling if he read it as an Instagram, TikTok or Twitter headline?
- He thinks of the Instagram caption while building, and compares it to writing a song where melody and lyrics come together. Before shipping a new feature he already asks how he will announce it the next day.
- He borrows from Miyazaki, who won't start a film until he has the first frame. Everyone then works around that frame, which sets the art style, story and character. For software, Farza says the "first frame" is often the first 5 seconds of the demo or the one-line caption.
- He keeps a notes app full of candidate lines and picks the ones that excite him. He did not know Clicky would be the winner when he wrote its line.
- He is baffled by founders who ask how to get their first 1,000 customers after they have built the product. Distribution "should be the first thing you think about."
- He says he doesn't care about marketing or acquisition as concepts. He needs the messaging to be good so that he stays a fan of his own product, and he calls himself his own worst critic on it.
- Current example: users rarely use Clicky's agent, but those who do like it. That led to the idea of running the agent without being asked, and the line "The world's first proactive agent."

## Building in public with a tiny audience: consistency over followers

- Farza rejects the claim that his products only work because he has a big following. If the product is bad, a huge audience won't help: a famous director or a celebrity with a poor product still gets ignored.
- He says today's algorithms are better than ever, and few people create content consistently. His claim: starting from zero, you are maybe 30 to 60 posts from 10,000 followers, then 50,000.
- His 2019 story: he was building an online school for kids with about 25 LinkedIn connections and about 50 Twitter followers. He posted progress on Twitter twice a day and on Substack weekly. After about 6 months a partner at A16Z, who had followed the progress, invested $2 million while Farza had around 600 followers.
- He says he is "a nobody from Florida" and an outsider. His view is that outsiders are "200 days of consistency away" from changing their situation, but most people quit around day 45 with five views.
- He quotes Mr. Beast's idea of not talking until you've made 2,000 videos. He applies the same logic to software: you probably need 1,000 to 2,000 launches before something hits.

## Measure inputs, not outputs

- His rule is to focus on inputs. A made video counts, and views don't matter to him: "I made the video. Full stop."
- He uses a basketball analogy. Judging yourself on every missed shot is crazy when you take hundreds a day, yet makes still matter, so you measure on a cadence.
- Company-level check: every 2 weeks he steps back and looks at the numbers analytically, not emotionally. For example, going from zero views one week to 10 the next counts as progress.
- Elite players, he says, review their stats and replays roughly monthly and track how often they practice. He sees the same pattern in building: show up, ship, review periodically.
- He admits people prefer results (the shot going in, the championship, money) because inputs are a harder thing to be motivated by. Unless you hit the lottery, those results take a long time.

## Getting your first 10 customers: talk to real people, skip social media

- When people ask how to talk about their product online, he first asks why they need to. His first instinct is to call friends and talk to 10 people in real life.
- For a hypothetical crypto wallet for teenagers, he would ask teens and parents directly. He lists places to find them: high school PTA meetings, grocery stores, farmers markets.
- He thinks a founder in 2026 might be best off deleting social accounts and not posting until they have their first 10 customers. Many people make 10 TikToks or 50 Reels, get about 20 downloads, burn out and quit. He says social media is distracting and defeating for founders because it makes you feel behind. He has said he would not use it until about 50 to 100 customers.
- His first Hey Clicky conversation was with his friend Joshua, a video editor with 10 to 20 years of experience. Joshua asked if it could do a specific color-grading technique. It could, and that led to new ideas.
- For the 2020 YC interview for his online elementary school, Zip School, he had his mom and dad interview him about the idea for about 6 minutes a day. They knew nothing about YC. When they said they didn't understand, that signaled the pitch was too complicated.
- When picking an idea in a category (like the Gemini X Prize categories), he asks what he can build in a day and give to someone. He builds a small version, gives it to his mom or the friend across the street, and sees whether they love or hate it.

## When and how to post online

- Once you are past roughly 10 customers and heading toward 100, he would start making videos online, about two to three a day.
- If you want to start right now, his lowest-effort, highest-reward advice is to pull out your phone and make three talking-to-camera videos today and post them.
- Example script: "My name is Farza. I'm working on this crypto wallet for teenagers. If you're a parent of a teenager, drop a comment, I'd love to talk to you." He says that might get him two parents to talk to.
- Posting daily is "like playing the lottery" for free. Don't expect virality by video 10; it is more likely to be around the 200th.
- If a founder's mental state suffers, he says skip it. He thinks filming 30 seconds a day is less daunting than building the product.
- Faceless formats work. A friend's workout app reached $3 million a year in revenue on a $10 a month plan, and his entire TikTok was AI-generated anime characters working out with the app, with no face or voice. He later got in trouble over using the characters, but Farza says it no longer mattered. His take: "I ask for forgiveness, not permission."

## Charge money from day one

- Farza's view is that the best way to learn whether people will pay is to charge. He says most people building today should charge from day one and skip the free plan.
- Early on he sees a free plan as useless and noisy. He thinks a free plan only becomes interesting once you pass about 10,000 users.
- He advises pre-orders even when the product isn't ready, and refunding if you don't deliver. He gave this advice to a founder with a waitlist for a device that helps people sleep better.
- The pitch should force a clear answer: "Cost 20 bucks a month. Are you in or are you out?" A "no" is valuable because the person tells you why.
- Hey Clicky has a $20 a month plan and a $100 a month plan. He added a free plan only as it grew. For the first 100 users he says paying users are far higher signal.

## Reading signals and learning from early users

- His rule for building: make the thing and give it to at least one person, who must use it or pay for it. That is when it becomes real.
- In 2025 he started a new idea roughly every 3 days, probably 20 or more, and ran out of people who cared. He calls this exhausting and says it is not the romantic version people imagine.
- His signal for committing to an idea is whether it inspires more ideas on the same idea, so that you build "like a skyscraper" and it compounds.
- Clicky data points: after going viral it got about 8,000 downloads, and about 400 users stuck around for 5 straight days. He says virality alone means nothing; retention is what blew his mind.
- He called those retained users one by one: kids, teachers, students and programmers. One was a 55-year-old woman using it to get copywriting feedback on her Shopify cookie store. She is not a native English speaker and found Shopify's AI features poor.
- He says the point of talking to customers is to spark your own ideas. If one user loves the agent and another never uses it, he asks how to make it useful to the second user using the first user's pattern. Find the people who get it and take what excites them to others.
- Example: one user loved it for DaVinci Resolve, so he built a marketing routine and Instagram Reels around video editors.
- He initially open-sourced Hey Clicky, expecting no one to care, then closed future work after 2 days when interest was high. The original code stays public.
- He argues you never "hit the lottery" with a company. Like a rapper who can't release the same song weekly, you must keep shipping the next thing. He sees every day and week as a new launch, and tries to put things out as quickly as possible.
