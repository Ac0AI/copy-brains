## Make the video a mini demo, not a polished launch

- Farza treats "launching" as a continuous channel, not a one-time event. In his words: "We're not launching to like get a bunch of clout on the internet." The goal is users, so he builds a repeatable channel.
- He calls these "demos" rather than launches and ships them constantly. The team asks "what are we launching this week?" and works backwards from the launch.
- For each feature, the team decides the Instagram caption, the hook and the video angle before building it. As he puts it, "how we communicate to other people is just as important as what we're building."
- He says a video of him saying hi and describing the product can outperform a $20,000 produced video, because old media no longer matters.
- Polished launches happen only about once a quarter. Otherwise the cadence is near-daily raw demos.
- The raw "yo, check this out" feeling between co-founders is, in his view, exactly how to present yourself online.

## Judge success by retained users, not views

- The first Clicky video got nearly 3 million views on Twitter. Farza said he does not care about the traffic and asks how many people actually downloaded and stayed.
- About 7,000 people downloaded Clicky after that video. Roughly 100 of them used it every day for 5 days straight.
- He contacted those ~100 users individually. They were using it in ways he never imagined, and that is what made him commit to building the company.
- He recorded that video in the office 5 minutes before dinner and almost didn't post it because he thought it wasn't good enough. It started as a side project he had open sourced.
- His rule: he is happy to post even if it gets zero views, and he forgets it by the time he clicks post, checking back about a week later.

## Script every video and write the hook for yourself

- Farza scripts every video, including ones that look off the cuff, writing the script about 3 days ahead. He rehearses lines in his head (shower, commute), so by the time the camera is on he has "visualized it like 10 times."
- Test for a script: "Do I enjoy reading this? Would I read this if someone texted this to me?"
- Test for a tweet: paste your text into a random tweet and ask whether your own eyes stop on it. If you wouldn't stop, don't post it. He applies this to Twitter, Instagram and YouTube.
- Structure: treat a 1-minute video like a movie, with a setup of only about 10-20%. The first 10 seconds should say what you're doing and why it matters.
- Formula he repeats: "This is who I am. This is what's going on. This is what I'm working on. Let me show you."
- Build for yourself. Frame the problem as his own ("when I'm writing, I often don't know what I want to say"), not "everyone has this problem." If he finds it cool, he assumes at least one other person will.
- Show your face clearly and be proud of who you are.
- Example hook: "Last week I went super viral for something I built that I thought was kind of ridiculous... and now I'm in Y Combinator." It targets the YC curiosity niche.

## Design the first frame and keep the story moving

- The first frame of the Clicky video was deliberately Xcode, zoomed in on a tiny triangle, aimed at curious developers rather than video editors. Most people only see the first 3 seconds while scrolling.
- Constantly ask "Am I bouncing? When?" while watching content. Farza studies Instagram Reels to take notes on templates, such as a dark background and a caption like "2 months ago..." that creates curiosity.
- He uses "beats" in the voice-controlled computer demo: "No hands," the song plays, volume lowered by voice, then the Stripe dashboard opens so viewers wonder how much money he made. That video had no cuts.
- Result: 3.8M views on Twitter and 4.6M on Instagram.
- He aims to keep the story moving ("Where's this guy going? Come on") rather than trying to hold attention.
- Some videos are built for people with no attention span, such as "It has been 9 weeks since I built and launched my idea to the world." He mentally checks "Am I still watching?" at each beat.
- Everyone wants a story, so a demo should be a mini story. Viewers see him as a character, with the "Farza" in the video being a version of himself.

## Show your process: the weekly "everything we built this week" video

- The team films a short update, made in about 10 minutes, where each person says what they shipped. These began as a way to stay accountable and now run every two weeks. One got nearly 1M views on Instagram.
- It has no hook or story, only the team telling what they did.
- His reasoning: what happens day to day inside a company is far from what outsiders imagine, and an inside look excites early adopters who wonder who the small team behind an AI product is.
- He is "anti" content about being a founder ("my day as a YC founder"). He shows what the team is doing and why, not his life.
- The tone is joyful, with music and the team clapping for each other.
- Practical tip: ask "what's something I did today that made me smile or felt new?", talk about it, show it, post, and go back to work.

## Pick channels by conversion, and target niches on every platform

- Farza says users of any product are on algorithmic platforms (X, LinkedIn, TikTok, Instagram, YouTube), whether the product is drones, AI or a dating app.
- Targeted example: Clicky found three design agencies naturally, so he made TikToks addressed to them ("Are you the founder of a design agency? Stop right there"). That produced about 50 design agency leads. The videos averaged about 4,000 views each, all targeted. His goal is the right audience, not virality.
- Conversion by channel:
  - Twitter: worst conversion. The original Clicky video had 3M views and 7,000 downloads (under 1%). The 2.9M-view screen-aware dictation video converted horribly.
  - YouTube: a 20,000-view video converted about 20% to app downloads. The channel is brand new with 872 subscribers, and a video keeps working for years ("a library that will last for decades"). Twitter is "your moment once and you're gone."
  - Instagram: about 70% of Clicky's traffic, and by far the best channel for his audience.
- He tracks it with a bio link of "/ig", which lets him measure the conversion rate of Instagram visitors who download the app (including a button to send the link to themselves by email).
- Format: Instagram is vertical and Twitter prefers 16:9, so he makes videos for one platform and cross-posts. A drawing-on-screen demo made for Twitter performed even better on Instagram.
- One Instagram video has 21M views and still drives traffic. He now runs it as a Facebook ad.

## Volume, early reps and the team and gear behind it

- His old ZipSchool launch video got 23 likes, one bookmark and one comment, and he still counts it as a launch. The day-one launch was just a landing page with no copy and a Canva video, to about 80 followers.
- Over years he kept posting, like basketball: "If you don't take a thousand shots, you're not going to make the 5,000th shot." In his words, "In order to not be cringe, you must first be cringe."
- First hires: a filmmaker and an engineer. Videos are made by Vikrant, who helps with shots, hooks, sets and editing. Farza brings the script, the song and the vibe.
- At one point Clicky had three people making videos and one engineer. Code is easy to clone now, but the audience, community and energy around a product are not.
- Stack:
  - Screen Studio (about $30) for screen demos.
  - iPhone selfie camera, edited in Instagram's Edits app.
  - Sony FX3 with a DJI mic for more cinematic videos.
  - DaVinci for laptop editing.
  - Freewrite (freewrite.io, his free open source app) for writing the script.
- Audio is the most important thing: "If your audio is bad, I'm bouncing." Use any mic that isn't the MacBook or AirPods mic; a cheap used DJI mic is enough.
- Solo founders should start with the phone.

## The 30-day challenge

- Spend 10 minutes a day recording yourself on your phone talking about what you did that day. Post it on Instagram or TikTok, on a new account if you want.
- Do this for 30 days straight. He promises at least one will pass 1,000 views, and you then base the next video on that one.
- Only about 1-2% of people he advises to do this actually do it, because they fear looking stupid. He says it is never easier to get your message out.
