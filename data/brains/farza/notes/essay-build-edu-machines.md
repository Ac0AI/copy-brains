## Education as human-capital machines, not study tools

- Farza thinks AI + education is stuck on "helps students pass exams" products: upload a PDF for flashcards, upload a lecture for notes, AI teacher lesson builders, language apps, AI tutors over video courses. He calls these "nothing that fresh" compared with how AI is reshaping robotics, self-driving cars, drones, coding and even memes.
- His core claim: "Learning, education, upskilling, studying" describe the act, not the outcome. Very few people learn just to learn; they learn to do something (close a first deal, start a YouTube channel, go freelance and quit a job).
- Examples of act-over-outcome products he lists: language apps with a 500-day streak where you still can't speak Spanish; platforms with thousands of hours of content nobody watches; certifications that look good on LinkedIn but don't change capability; AI tools that summarize lectures and generate quizzes for an exam you forget in a week. He says none of this is bad, but it is optimized for learning rather than doing.

## Why "learning" alone is a weak business

- "You can only monetize straight up 'learning' so much." People won't pay much or for long for learning without a clear result in sight.
- This makes the market look like an ocean of students with terrible retention, jobless people without money to spend, or consumers who pay for a few years as "lifelong learners".
- His chain of consequences: education companies look "small", so investors don't invest, so founders don't start them, so founders move to more lucrative things like "verticalized B2B voice agents for customer service".
- He says course businesses such as Coursera, Udacity, Duolingo, Skillshare and Masterclass can be good businesses but are weak "machines" (a typical buyer buys a course or two, never finishes, and moves from maybe 2/10 to 3/10).

## Reframe: build "human capital factories"

- Farza argues these aren't really education companies; they are "companies that build new human capital". A company that does its job produces inventors, designers, musicians, engineers, doctors and founders, the people who change reality.
- His mental model is the "machine": input a person at X/10 in their ability to impact the world and pursue their goals in some domain, output them at a higher number.
  - Stanford: an 18-year-old who is a 3/10 goes in; a 6/10 on average comes out after four years.
  - YC: founders enter around 5-6/10 and leave around 8/10, because money, mentorship and customers accelerate them.
  - The Knowledge Society (TKS), an after-school accelerator for 13-17 year-olds, where a kid at 1/10 in self-belief, ambition and ability to tackle hard problems can leave at 5/10 or better through projects, tight mentorship, internships, research and startups.
  - buildspace: designed so someone enters a 2/10 and exits a 6/10 on average in their ability to start a business, reach financial goals and work on their own ideas. He notes it ended up quite valuable and, 1.5 years after the company closed, he still gets near-daily messages about past participants raising rounds, landing dream jobs or starting side hustles.
- He cautions you can't guarantee outcomes ("do this and you'll make $100,000"), but you can improve someone's chances of reaching them.

## What actually transforms people (and the scaling problem)

- "It sure as hell isn't content." Content is as free and diverse as ever and we're not all geniuses. Transformation comes from "the right balance of learning, accountability, and structure. All while properly maintaining your motivation." The best human mentors do this naturally.
- Machines with strong guaranteed outcomes are high-touch and hard to scale: YC takes roughly 1,000 companies a year, MIT roughly 2,000 undergraduates, TKS supports about 1,000 kids a year. Top bootcamps that guarantee jobs and elite apprenticeships are tough to access because humans must lead them, so a tiny percent of humanity gets the best machines.
- He argues these succeed arguably because they don't scale, and says he is not telling people to only build things that scale ("I rather live in a world with MIT vs without").
- buildspace took the opposite approach, scaling to 100,000+ people per year as "low touch, but high access", designed as an open game anyone could join.
- He rejects the lazy version of the idea: "Okay lets build AI Steve Jobs and we can scale mentorship to 100M people" ("If that was the solution, it'd be done already").

## The AI opportunity: end-to-end machines per goal

- His thesis: the biggest AI + education opportunity is not helping people study random skills, but building end-to-end machines that output human capital, taking someone anywhere at 2/10 to 6/10 in pursuing a specific goal.
- Imagine a separate machine for each goal: becoming a YouTuber, an author, a coder who gets a job, a salesperson, a filmmaker.
- He expects "education companies that democratize the ability for people to actually become something." Tools alone won't create mass change.
- The models "haven't been pushed to push humans yet", which he calls very under-explored but worthwhile. It is "not exactly a 'learning' problem": it is human psychology and retaining motivation and drive.

## Pakistan case study: a human acting like an AI

- A year before writing, in Pakistan, Farza helped a 21-year-old learn to build a Shopify store, source clothes locally and sell them on Facebook Marketplace.
- His involvement: two check-in meetings a week, mostly setting weekly goals and reframing "I can't do this" thoughts.
- Result: the kid reached $250 a month, which is what his father earned per month as a driver. In Farza's view he went from 1/10 to 5/10, learning basic web design, marketing and customer service, and built a basic business.
- Farza says he "may as well have been an AI model he was talking to". The questions he poses for an AI version:
  - Could a "magical AI cursor" have taught him Shopify?
  - Could the AI hold a weekly call to get him to his first $10?
  - Could it tell him where he was messing up?
  - Could it keep his mental state in check?
  - Could it connect him with similar people locally?
- He thinks all of this is within what models can do and can be generalized, to a would-be designer in Morocco, an author in Berlin, or a genetics student in India aiming to solve epilepsy.
