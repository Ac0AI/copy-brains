## Why Farza says ideas are overrated

- He argues there is "no such thing as a good or bad idea" (his opinion, not fact). An idea is only "where you start your journey". You can't know where it ends up.
- Examples he cites: Angry Birds was Rovio's 51st game. Star Wars began as a 10-page story George Lucas wrote for friends and iterated on for years. The Dyson vacuum started as a drawing. Nike started as Phil Knight and Bill Bowerman wanting to make shoes for 50 people on a track team.
- "Great ideas are forged, they are not found." He compares it to taking a hammer to a rock until it starts to look like something good.
- Work is better with momentum than without: he would rather spend eight weeks working on any idea and learning than eight weeks looking for the perfect one.
- The idea should change in small ways every week, "kind of like a Pokemon".
- He says you never fully figure it out. Once an idea works, your goals, interests and the idea itself change (10 dollars becomes 100 dollars, 10 followers becomes 100). So don't feel everyone else has it figured out.
- Don't try to impress people with how important your idea is. You don't need to be "solving cancer".

## Farza's own path: Farza School to Zip School to buildspace

- At 23 he spent about three months at his parents' house trying to find a "good idea". He wrote words from product websites on index cards, shuffled them and drew three at a time, 9am to 6pm daily. He says he could not stay on any idea longer than three days.
- He broke the loop by asking what he would work on if it were his last year on Earth. The answer was a school for kids teaching fun things like Minecraft servers, websites and Discord bots.
- In October 2019 he made "Farza School" cards and put them in mailboxes in rich parts of town. Nobody called. The only student was his cousin Samir, who said he'd rather do this every day than go to his actual school.
- That led him into homeschooling Facebook groups, where he ran polls. He then made a fake Facebook account saying "I have this app that helps people start a homeschool", and many parents said they'd want it. He built Zip Homeschool from a Webflow template.
- First paying customer: 90 dollars, from a parent named Lucy. The service grew from setup help to lesson plans and worksheets to him teaching kids on Zoom (for example about rockets), then hiring teachers.
- Zip School went somewhat viral among kids, then got funded by Y Combinator. He says the same core people and company became buildspace.
- His summary: "I literally could not have predicted any of this." He says he'd rather tell this story than Steve Jobs or Bill Gates stories, because he wants to show how real people make things happen.

## How to choose what to work on

- Follow excitement and curiosity, however big or small. The number one reason people give up is that they stop being excited and things get hard.
- Don't pick something because it's trending or "sounds like a good idea". His example is B2B SaaS, which people pick for being good but then find boring. Do things because they're fun.
- Stuck? Pick something you're a big fan of and do something related. He loved League of Legends so he built an app around it. He loved Key & Peele so he made funny YouTube videos.
- If your idea already exists, that is validation: "you're not crazy". He cites Christopher Nolan making The Dark Knight after about 20 Batman movies. There is never a truly unique idea.
- Pick one idea and commit to it for the program rather than splitting across two or three. Let it evolve naturally, but don't bounce between unrelated things (movies, music, AI apps) every week.
- Do you have to solve a problem? He says not necessarily at the start. He says that to this day he doesn't know what problem buildspace solves, though he adds that to build a business you do need to solve a problem. His counterexamples: Justin Bieber and Mr Beast.
- Fear of getting copied: if someone copies you and wins, it "wasn't your destiny". Your idea works because you make it work, not because it is amazing.

## The one-liner test: make the idea clear, not good

- Goal: a clear idea, not a "good" one. Be able to describe it in one sentence, ideally by the end of the session and at worst within a week.
- Vague: "an AI app", "a marketing agency", "a short film". Clearer: "an AI app that helps teachers make flashcards", "a marketing agency for seed-stage companies", "a short film about a robot that falls in love with a toaster".
- Once it's a sentence, the next step becomes obvious (for the toaster film: buy a toaster, record, write the script).
- Test: tell your mom, dad or a friend; they should instantly say "I get it". Don't over-complicate with problem/solution/market frameworks.
- Format: a one-liner (what it is) plus one or two sentences on how it works. Keep it under two lines, three at most. Expect it to improve over the six weeks. The buildspace one-liner itself changed for years.
- Examples he rated acceptable: "I'm writing a song a day until one goes viral" (Joe Yang); a personalized workout and meal plan generator; an AI-powered SQL editor with charts and dashboards; a YouTube channel explaining math and technology.
- Bad: buzzword copy like "an innovative AI-powered platform that revolutionizes how you discover people". He rates that a zero. Don't use buzzwords to impress.
- Live critiques: cut filler words like "platform" ("a mental health platform for teenagers" is unclear; "an iOS app where teenagers get daily breathing exercises" is clear). An app for ordering food before you get to the restaurant, with the food and table ready on arrival, was a 7 out of 10. "A short film about a bench in Central Park" was an 8 to 8.5.
- He deliberately avoids showing a "perfect 10" so as not to bias people. He says everyone already knows whether they understand an idea when it is told to them at a party.

## Getting the idea out and getting feedback

- After writing the one-liner on a template (an "idea slide" with project name, one-liner and how it works), post it publicly the same day: Twitter, LinkedIn, Instagram, TikTok or a group chat. He says it is about having the courage to put it out there and using the internet to push the idea forward.
- Use a burner account if you're scared, but his view is "ship it to production". Keep the post simple: the slide, a line on why you're working on it, no hashtag spam.
- Give feedback to three other people's ideas in return. His line: to get feedback on your idea, start by giving feedback on others'. Last season this produced over 100,000 pieces of feedback.
- No excuses if nobody replies. He suggests showing a printed slide to 10 people in person and asking "do you get it?". Getting it out of your head makes you feel lighter and makes the idea public to friends.
- A short video where you explain your idea over a green-screen slide is a low-cost extra step. He argues "what do you have to lose": worst case you get better at talking about your work on camera, best case it pops.
- Weekly cadence: the number of participants drops each week (17,000 at kickoff, about 8,400 here). He treats that as a forcing function and says to network more seriously around week six with those who stay.

## Don't wait for permission

- He highlights people who started immediately without being told: building a robot friend, a new guitar tool, taking online kinematics classes, editing videos, drawing schematics, making their first content.
- His message: "Don't sit here and wait for me to give you permission to start doing stuff." Do what feels right, but do something.
- Lock the idea in week one so you can commit for the remaining five weeks rather than wanting to switch in week four.
- The goal for the week: pick, clarify and finalize one idea. If you already have one, start building. Even people far along (revenue, subscribers) benefit, because a single idea needs hundreds of smaller ideas (video, product piece, marketing) and the same clarify-and-iterate process applies to each.
