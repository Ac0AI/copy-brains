## A rational framework for supplementation: the layered stack

- Andrew dislikes the word "supplements" because it implies food replacements. Many compounds (for sleep, hormones, focus) are potent and not found in food in meaningful amounts.
- He stacks mental health, physical health and performance tools in four layers:
  1. Behaviors (do's and don'ts), the bedrock: morning sunlight, exercise, avoiding bright light 10 pm to 4 am, no late caffeine.
  2. Nutrition.
  3. Supplementation.
  4. Prescription drugs (via a board-certified MD).
- No pill replaces sunlight, exercise, social connection or sleep. He quotes a physician friend: "Better Living Through Chemistry still requires Better Living."
- The right dose of a given supplement for you may be zero. If you sleep deeply and feel rested, there is no sleep-supplement discussion to have.
- The key question set: How well do you sleep? How is your nutrition? What is your budget?
- He is not a clinician and does not prescribe. He says to consult a trusted board-certified physician before adding or removing protocols.

## Foundational supplements: the one category where multi-ingredient formulas make sense

- Foundational supplements act as insurance against dietary gaps. They can combine:
  - Vitamins and minerals.
  - Digestive enzymes (papain, lipases; anything ending in "-ase").
  - Adaptogens (ashwagandha, non-psychedelic mushrooms). He notes "adaptogen" has no agreed operational definition.
  - Prebiotics and probiotics.
- Skeptics say vitamins just give you "expensive urine". He replies that many people don't get enough water-soluble vitamins, and that moderate doses of fat-soluble vitamins are unlikely to build up to problem levels. The caveat is to avoid exceedingly high doses.
- Cost usually scales with dose, and he knows of no quality difference between cheap and expensive vitamin/mineral products. Ask whether you can afford it and whether you can reliably get variety from food.
- A vitamin/mineral supplement most likely makes sense for very physically or mentally active people and for intermittent fasters with restricted eating windows.
- Take vitamin/mineral supplements with food, ideally early in the day. B vitamins can upset the stomach on empty (he tolerates them fine). Zinc and CoQ10 should also be taken with food.
- Food first: aim for roughly 75 to 80% or more of intake from unprocessed or minimally processed foods. Avoid long ingredient lists and long shelf lives (chips, pastries, canned soups).
- If you can spend about $100 or more per month and can only take one thing, he recommends a broad foundational product (he has taken Athletic Greens daily since 2012, and it's a sponsor) or a DIY equivalent of the same ingredient categories. He says other brands are fine.

## Gut microbiome support

- Higher microbiome diversity supports immune function, hormones and the gut-brain axis (mood, motivation, neurotransmitter production).
- Per work from Justin Sonnenberg's lab at Stanford, about 4 servings per day of low-sugar fermented foods improves immune function and reduces inflammation. Examples: sauerkraut, kimchi, low-sugar Greek yogurt, kombucha, kefir, natto.
- Pickles and sauerkraut must be refrigerated and include the brine to support the microbiome.
- Fiber helped some people in those studies, did nothing for others, and raised inflammatory markers in a few. He still says fiber has other benefits.
- Most people don't get 4 servings a day, so a probiotic/prebiotic is a sensible foundational item. The best ones are refrigerated and expensive.
- Excess prebiotic/probiotic capsules taken ongoing can cause brain fog, so choose lower amounts.

## Sleep supplementation: isolate variables

- Before supplementing, cut caffeine after 2 pm (or earlier, even noon) and avoid food in the 2 hours before bed, without going to bed hungry.
- Match the supplement to the problem:
  - Waking at 2 or 3 am and struggling to get back to sleep: myo-inositol, typically 900 mg.
  - Trouble falling asleep: magnesium threonate or magnesium bisglycinate (interchangeable), which cross the blood-brain barrier and cause mild drowsiness. Apigenin (a chamomile derivative) also helps by calming rumination.
  - Theanine: 100 to 400 mg depending on body weight and experience. Skip it if you wake from very vivid dreams.
- His testing method: take one single-ingredient supplement for about one week, change nothing else, then swap or add a second. Stop immediately if there are negative effects. If neither works alone, try them together.
- His own pre-sleep stack: magnesium threonate, theanine, apigenin and inositol.
- He is not a fan of melatonin:
  - It induces sleepiness but doesn't keep you asleep.
  - Supplement doses far exceed normal endogenous levels.
  - It affects reproductive hormone axes.
  - Studies found bottles ranging from about 15% to many times the labeled dose.
  - He sees it as occasional use only (e.g. jet lag), and is cautious about it for children.
- On dependency: he says there is no dependency like sleeping pills, though placebo or belief effects can occur. Skip your stack one night every two weeks to a month (e.g. a Friday) to check. Sleep is usually still fine, but he finds depth and duration are lower over multiple nights without it. He attributes the carryover to neural plasticity.

## Hormone support: get behaviors and nutrition right first

- Prerequisites:
  - Adequate calories from quality sources.
  - Morning sunlight (raises early-day cortisol).
  - Intense resistance training a few times per week, in sessions of an hour or less (referencing Duncan French).
- Insulin lowers sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG). Fasting, very low carb, metformin or berberine can raise SHBG and lower free testosterone.
- Growth hormone: the best levers are deep sleep (released in the first 3 to 4 hours), no food in the 2 hours before bed, and no alcohol or cannabis. Extended fasting for GH is not logical. Arginine before bed has weak evidence. Large increases mostly require prescription peptides or GH itself.
- Broad-spectrum hormone supports:
  - Shilajit (fulvic acid) raises FSH and indirectly testosterone, and is pro-fertility and pro-libido.
  - Ashwagandha lowers cortisol and indirectly raises testosterone. Do not take it for more than two weeks at a time at high doses (see the Master Stress episode).
  - Maca root can help libido, including SSRI-related loss, probably through dopamine pathways. Evidence for raising testosterone is weak.
  - L-carnitine improves sperm and egg quality.
- Fadogia agrestis:
  - Dose: 600 mg per day, raising luteinizing hormone (LH), testosterone and sometimes estrogen. Responders are unpredictable.
  - Do not exceed recommended doses; it can be toxic to testicular cells.
  - Cycle it 8 weeks on and 2 weeks off, or 12 weeks on and a month off.
- Tongkat ali:
  - Dose: 200 to 600 mg per day, scaled to body size. Start low (e.g. 400 mg for 4 weeks, then 600 mg if no effect, but no higher).
  - It lowers SHBG and may build libido through neural pathways. No cycling needed.
  - Check blood work at 8 to 12 weeks.
- Do blood work before starting and again after about 4 to 8 weeks.
- Floor and ceiling effects: people starting very low (hypogonadal) see the biggest gains. One acquaintance with very low testosterone took fadogia and tongkat together and saw a rise of about 600 ng/dL in total testosterone, nearly tripling it.
- For women, effects of some supplements (shilajit, tongkat ali, maca) may differ or reverse across menstrual phases, so single-ingredient dose control matters.
- Avoid hormone-targeting supplements before puberty is complete (into the late teens and early 20s) unless a physician advises otherwise.

## Cognitive enhancement and focus: stimulants vs neuromodulator support

- The best cognitive enhancer is enough good sleep. Avoid being overly hungry or overfed, since "rest and digest" makes you sleepy.
- Caffeine:
  - Dose: 1 to 3 mg/kg body weight, taken about 30 minutes before the task.
  - Taking 2 days off beforehand makes the effect stronger.
  - Caffeine pills (100 to 300 mg) are much more potent and longer lasting than coffee or tea. Be careful if you are prone to anxiety or panic.
  - Avoid smoked yerba mate (carcinogenic).
  - Caffeine within about 8 to 12 hours of bedtime degrades sleep architecture.
- Alpha-yohimbine (rauwolscine) is a potent, "precarious" adrenergic stimulant. It can cause anxiety, especially if fasted or on a low-calorie diet.
- Non-stimulant neuromodulator support:
  - Alpha-GPC: 300 to 600 mg, half-life about 4 to 6 hours. Boosts focus via acetylcholine without jitters. He personally uses 300 mg before cognitive work or workouts, often with coffee or yerba mate.
  - L-tyrosine: 500 to 1,000 mg, a dopamine precursor. He finds he crashes afterward, so he avoids it. He uses phenylethylamine instead (shorter lasting).
- His distinction: stimulants mostly raise alertness; Alpha-GPC and tyrosine mostly tighten focus. Test them separately before stacking.
- Focus supplements don't create dependence that prevents you from focusing without them.

## Omega-3s, protein and other practical notes

- With a limited budget, fish oil is a cost-effective option for mood, depression (can reduce the antidepressant dose needed), cardiovascular and metabolic health, and focus.
- Get above 1 g of EPA per day, up to about 3 g. Check the EPA content on the label, not just the total of 1,500 to 1,600 mg of omega-3s. Rhonda Patrick takes about 3 to 4 g or more.
- Protein: about 1 g per pound of body weight per day for most people (Lane Norton episode). Whey or casein powders help hit the target. Potato protein was a plant option Norton mentioned.
- Powders and food-mimic supplements don't replace whole foods (fiber, satiety, micronutrients).
- Age: older adults may consider more cognitive-support supplements with a physician, because age-related cognitive decline is universal.
- Core principle: outside the foundational category, use single-ingredient formulas. They let you find the minimum effective dose, spot what doesn't work or causes side effects, keep costs down, and ease in "one thing at a time" over a year or more.
