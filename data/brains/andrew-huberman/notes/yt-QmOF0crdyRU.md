## Dopamine basics: baseline, peaks and what it does

- Andrew calls "dopamine hits" a misleading idea. The body keeps a baseline (tonic) level of dopamine circulating all the time, plus peaks (phasic) above it.
- How good something feels depends on the height of the peak relative to baseline and on recent dopamine history, not on the peak alone.
- Dopamine is a neuromodulator, not just a neurotransmitter. It coordinates many circuits at once rather than one neuron-to-neuron message.
- It drives motivation, drive, craving, time perception and movement, and it co-releases excitatory glutamate. In short, it makes you look outside yourself and pursue things.
- Low dopamine looks like lethargy and no drive. High dopamine looks like excitement and motivation, even when scared (skydiving, public speaking).
- Two main pathways: mesocorticolimbic (motivation, reward, craving; ventral tegmentum to nucleus accumbens and prefrontal cortex) and nigrostriatal (movement; substantia nigra to dorsal striatum).
- Dopamine acts through slow G protein-coupled receptors and can affect gene expression. It is released locally at synapses or volumetrically across many cells.
- Epinephrine and norepinephrine are synthesized from dopamine (L-DOPA to dopamine to norepinephrine to epinephrine). Epinephrine alone can mean fear; with dopamine it becomes excitement.
- Andrew's own example: thorazine given in the ER for a stomach bug blocked his dopamine receptors and made him profoundly sad. L-DOPA reversed it within minutes.

## Why peaks push baseline below where it started

- Evolutionary logic: dopamine drives foraging and seeking. After a reward it must fall below prior baseline, or you would never go looking again.
- The drop below baseline is proportional to the peak. Crossing a finish line ecstatic means a bigger crash a day or two later. He ties this to post-partum or post-win lows.
- The mechanism is depletion of the "readily releasable pool" of dopamine in vesicles. Andrew links this to the pleasure-pain balance from Dr. Anna Lembke's *Dopamine Nation*.
- Chasing the same activity to restore the peak lowers baseline further. Addiction is "a progressive narrowing of the things that bring you pleasure," often ending in depression.
- Stacking many moderate spikes also erodes baseline: food, alcohol, intense workouts and dancing across a week. The decline can be imperceptible until nothing works.
- Typical peaks above baseline (from microdialysis and blood studies): chocolate 1.5x, sex 2x, nicotine 2.5x, cocaine 2.5x, amphetamine 10x. Exercise is about 2x if you enjoy it, little or none if you don't.
- Caffeine raises dopamine only modestly but upregulates D2/D3 receptors with regular intake (Volkow 2015), so released dopamine works better.

## Recovering a depleted baseline

- To replenish the releasable pool, stop the dopamine-seeking behavior, either cold turkey or by tapering.
- Example: the child of a friend, hooked on video games and thought to have ADHD, did a 30-day fast from phone, games and social media. By day 29 his concentration and mood were up, and the psychiatrist wondered if he needed ADHD treatment at all.
- Andrew says the first 14 days are the hardest. He thinks some ADHD is misdiagnosed because of this depletion, while acknowledging genuine clinical cases.
- Highly palatable food works the same way. After about two days without it, plain broccoli with seasoning tastes good again.
- Long-term spiking of dopamine (amphetamine, cocaine, and likely Adderall, Ritalin, modafinil and armodafinil at similar dopamine levels) can limit later neuroplasticity. He cites Kolb et al. 2003 (PNAS) for amphetamine and cocaine and calls the effect long-lasting but probably not permanent.
- Pornography: heavy, intense use makes real-world romantic interactions harder to reach the same dopamine level. He frames the ethics as an individual question and the mechanism as clear.

## Intermittent reward schedules for lasting motivation

- Core rule: don't chase a high dopamine peak every time you do an activity you want to keep enjoying, whether school, exercise, relationships or work.
- Use random, unpredictable variation in how much dopamine an activity gives you, like a casino's variable reward schedule. A fixed pattern (for example every third time) wouldn't be intermittent.
- Don't always stack extras: coffee, pre-workout, energy drink, favorite music, a workout partner. Sometimes do it with all of them, sometimes with none.
- Simple tool: flip a coin before a workout. Heads, bring the music; tails, don't.
- Andrew's own change: his workouts had become less satisfying with phone, music, podcasts and texting layered in. For this calendar year, no phone in workouts (no music, no texting, often not even bringing it). The first week was tough, then the enjoyment came back.
- He does not think people "can't be alone" now. He thinks layered dopamine sources strip the pleasure from the activities themselves.
- Stimulants used every time you study or train will eventually reduce the satisfaction of those activities. Occasional use is fine. He sometimes uses a pre-workout on hard days.
- Caffeine is the exception because it upregulates D2/D3 receptors. But it increases MDMA toxicity, and he says that combination is dangerous.
- Yerba mate is his main caffeine source. It has antioxidants and GLP-1, and a couple of preliminary studies suggest it protects dopamine neurons in the movement and motivation pathways. He stresses this is early evidence.

## Building dopamine from effort instead of rewards

- Stanford nursery-school drawing experiment: kids who liked drawing were given gold stars, then the stars stopped. Afterward they drew less on their own (extrinsic reward undermining intrinsic motivation).
- Dopamine marks time. Working hard only for an end reward (the trophy, grade, or "hot fudge sundae" afterward) stretches the time bin and moves the dopamine to the end. The effort itself gets more painful and less efficient.
- It also makes you need more layered stimulation next time ("twice as much coffee and three times as much music").
- The alternative is growth mindset (Carol Dweck): attach reward to the effort and friction. Use the prefrontal part of the mesocorticolimbic circuit.
- Tactic: in the most painful moment, tell yourself "this is painful and because it's painful it will evoke an increase in dopamine later," and that you are doing it by choice because you love it. Andrew admits it is "lying to yourself" but within a truth: you want it to feel better.
- Repeated, this becomes reflexive for all kinds of effort. He cites David Goggins as an example.
- Don't spike dopamine before an effort or reward yourself after it. Learn to get it from the effort itself.
- Intermittent fasting as an example: some people start enjoying the deprivation and clarity of fasting, not just the meal. Andrew skips one meal a day. Believing the health benefits (autophagy, insulin sensitivity, lipids; he notes human evidence is still emerging) reinforces the reward.
- A 2020s *Neuron* study found that hearing something that confirms an existing belief itself raises dopamine, so subjective interpretation really does shape reward.

## Cold exposure for a sustained dopamine rise

- Study: European Journal of Applied Physiology. Humans were immersed up to the neck for up to an hour in water at 32°C, 20°C or 14°C.
- Cold produced immediate, large rises in epinephrine and norepinephrine. Dopamine rose slowly, kicking in around 10-15 minutes, and reached about 2.5x (250%) above baseline.
- Unlike drugs, the rise was sustained rather than a spike and crash, and stayed elevated for up to about 3 hours. Cortisol also rose transiently, most at the coldest temperature.
- Andrew says the dopamine release happens regardless of whether you stay calm (slow breathing, panoramic gaze), lean into the arousal, or distract yourself (reciting the alphabet).
- Safety: very cold water near 30-40°F can cause cold water shock. About 50-60°F is manageable for most, and colder only if acclimated. Don't stay in for an hour to the point of hypothermia (the study monitored core temperature and reheated subjects).
- Timing: best earlier in the day, not right before sleep. Frequency varies: daily, three days a week, or occasionally. Once you are fully adapted it may stop producing the same effect.
- Reported effect: a calm, focused, highly alert state for hours afterward. He calls it a zero-cost way to raise dopamine with no pharmacology.

## Supplements and compounds that change dopamine

- Mucuna pruriens contains L-DOPA, the direct precursor to dopamine. It reduces Parkinson's symptoms (at least five studies), lowers prolactin, and increases sperm count, quality and motility (four studies). Expect a large transient rise followed by a crash. Andrew doesn't use it: "too intense and too much of a crash."
- L-tyrosine (500-1,000 mg, as capsule or powder): dopamine peaks about 30-45 minutes after ingestion and fades by about 30 minutes later per a 1983 study (J Clin Endocrinol Metab; tryptophan raised serotonin instead). Andrew uses it only occasionally, once or twice a week at most, and doesn't want to rely on outside substances to drive dopamine.
- Phenylethylamine (PEA): found in chocolate. Andrew takes 500 mg PEA with 300 mg alpha-GPC about once a week or every two weeks for intense mental work. He describes a sharp, transient rise lasting 30-45 minutes, more even than tyrosine and much gentler than Mucuna.
- Huperzine A raises acetylcholine and reportedly dopamine in the medial prefrontal cortex and hippocampus. He hasn't tried it and says to research it first.
- Things that lower dopamine: melatonin (a 2001 Nishiyama study found a drop in dopamine 60 minutes after dosing), and bright light between 10 p.m. and 4 a.m. (work of Samer Hattar and David Berson), which can reduce dopamine for several days. He advises dimming lights at night and avoiding melatonin when possible.
- Caution: people with schizophrenia, psychosis, bipolar disorder or anxiety shouldn't use dopamine-boosting supplements. All of this is "check with your doctor."
- Wellbutrin (bupropion) raises dopamine and norepinephrine and is a prescription alternative for depression, with fewer sexual side effects than SSRIs. It can blunt appetite and raise anxiety, so dosing is a decision for a clinician.
- Indirect supports like maca (lowers cortisol) and gut microbiome influence dopamine only subtly compared to the direct approaches.

## Social connection as a dopamine driver

- A 2017 *Science* paper from Rob Malenka's lab at Stanford ("Gating of social reward by oxytocin in the ventral tegmental area") showed that oxytocin and social bonding trigger dopamine release.
- Andrew had assumed oxytocin sat mainly in the serotonin/opioid "enjoy what you have" systems. He now sees it as part of the seeking system: it is evolutionarily sensible to seek out connection.
- This holds for romantic, parent-child and friend relationships. Physical contact isn't required, so friends at a distance count.
- His takeaway: pursue quality, healthy social interactions to support the dopamine pathways.
