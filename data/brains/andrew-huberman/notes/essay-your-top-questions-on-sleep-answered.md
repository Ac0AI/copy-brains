## Sleep onset, rumination and middle-of-the-night waking

- For racing thoughts or anxiety-related sleep-onset insomnia, Andrew lists: guided breathing or guided relaxation meditation, body scans, long-exhale breathing, and a "mental walk" (visualizing a familiar route in great detail).
- Body scans move focus progressively from one body part to another. Non-sleep deep rest (NSDR) typically includes a body scan and can be very effective for falling asleep. He points to 10-minute and 20-minute NSDR scripts.
- If you wake in the night, don't try too hard to fall back asleep, since effort is often counterproductive. Embrace the idea of rest instead; this lowers stress and naturally helps sleep return. NSDR scripts help both for falling asleep initially and for falling back asleep.

## Why you wake at the same time every night (e.g., 3:30 a.m.)

- Fixed-time waking is likely the end of a sleep cycle and/or a learned habit.
- During REM sleep the body is paralyzed. On exiting REM it needs to move and reposition, which briefly awakens you. Usually these awakenings are too brief to remember, but some wake you fully.
- Checking the clock when you wake can train the brain to keep waking at that time, because it strengthens the memory association with that time.
- Fix: remove visible clocks from the bedroom.

## Sleep position, snoring and apnea

- Avoid sleeping on your back. It raises the likelihood of snoring or airway collapse, causing a "hypoxic event."
- Be curious about whether you snore. Apps like SnoreLab monitor breathing overnight and show a snoring distribution in the morning; Eight Sleep's Pod Covers can also detect snoring.
- If snoring is confirmed, see a doctor. 80% of people with sleep apnea are undiagnosed. Apnea harms health and lifespan, and treating it can be transformational for mental and physical health.
- Alcohol increases snoring.

## Banking sleep, sleep debt and learning

- You can't build up sleep debt and repay it on weekends. The body can only recoup about 25% of lost hours.
- Regularly sleeping less than the recommended 7 to 8 hours a night means permanently running a sleep debt, which predicts ill health and early mortality. Individual needs vary: some need 6 hours, others 9 or even 10.
- If you anticipate sleep loss, you can proactively "bank" extra sleep beforehand to mitigate the impact. This is useful for irregular-hours professions like doctors and emergency responders, but it doesn't fully remove the effect of sleep loss.
- After learning something important, sleep as well as possible that first night. Without good sleep you lose much of the chance to consolidate the information into memory ("if you don't snooze, you lose"). Naps can offset this somewhat.

## Sleep changes with age and menopause

- Older adults see a significant decline in deep sleep and sleep quality. Sleep becomes more fragmented and less efficient, and circadian changes make the second half of the night more fragile, so early waking is more likely.
- Remedy for early waking: gradually delay bedtime. If you sleep at 10 pm and wake naturally at 4 am, try 11 pm to push waking later.
- Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBTI) is effective for older adults. Discuss problems with a physician; options such as doxepin, trazodone, or dual orexin receptor antagonists (DORAs) can be prescribed for trouble falling or staying asleep.
- Menopause, premenopause and perimenopause disrupt sleep. Hot flashes are a frequent culprit because the body needs to stay cool to stay asleep.
- For hot flashes: keep the bedroom cool and consider a cooling pad, mattress cover, or mattress.
- Various forms of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) may help by normalizing some hormonal changes and thereby improving sleep.

## Dreams and dream recall

- Most people don't recall most dreams. Not remembering dreams does not indicate poor sleep quality or REM problems, and doesn't affect the quality of your waking day.
- Lucid dreaming may not be conducive to quality, restorative sleep, based on the data.
- Dr. Matt Walker proposed that forgotten dreams could still implicitly influence behavior without conscious awareness.

## Sleep supplements: stack and doses

Supplements should never be the first-line approach. Fix behaviors first: quality, quantity, regularity and timing (QQRT), plus sunlight/darkness and temperature. These are far more impactful than any supplement. When fine-tuning, take supplements 30-60 minutes before bed.

- **Magnesium** (threonate, bisglycinate or chloride): induces mild drowsiness; in older adults it increased total deep sleep. Especially helpful with magnesium deficiency, which is fairly common. "SlowMag" is a coated form that is easier on the stomach.
- **Apigenin** 50 mg (from chamomile): reduces anxiety to ease the transition to sleep.
- **Theanine** 100-400 mg: helps relaxation and falling asleep. It can worsen vivid, disruptive dreams.
- **Inositol** 900 mg: helpful if you wake at night and struggle to fall back asleep, reducing time to return to sleep.
- **Glycine** 1.5-2 g: reliable benefits in randomized controlled trials.
- **Phosphatidylserine** 100 mg: lowers the cortisol response, which can help people with insomnia.
- Consult a healthcare provider first. Start slowly and use one supplement at a time before considering combinations.

## The single most important tip: regularity and chronotype

- Andrew's top tip is sleep regularity and timing. Keep sleep and wake times consistent, even on weekends. This anchors the body's circadian clock and improves overall sleep quality.
- For timing, determine your chronotype and sleep in sync with it as best you can.
