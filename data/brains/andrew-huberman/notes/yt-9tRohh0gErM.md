## Time-restricted eating: what it is and why timing matters

- Andrew uses "time-restricted feeding" as the umbrella term. Fasting and eating are two sides of one coin, and everyone already fasts during sleep.
- His core idea: what and when you eat "set conditions" in the brain and body. These conditions are either fed or unfed (fasted), and the body cares about the condition, not about whether you are currently chewing.
- He says "when you eat is as important as what you eat" for liver health and mental health. In his framing, eating is a timing cue for the body's 24-hour clock, second only to light.
- Calories in versus calories out is the foundation for weight change. A 12-month Stanford study (Gardner et al., JAMA 2018) found no significant weight difference between a healthy low-fat and a healthy low-carb diet. Hormones, exercise and context still shape the "calories out" side.
- He flags that mouse and human results can diverge. Higher fasting blood glucose predicts mortality in humans and non-human primates, but the reverse held in mice.

## The mouse study behind the eight-hour window

- Satchin Panda's 2012 mouse study compared four groups: normal chow ad libitum, normal chow time-restricted, high-fat diet ad libitum, and high-fat diet time-restricted.
- Mice on the same high-fat diet and same calories, but restricted to about eight hours of their active phase, maintained or lost weight and improved health markers. Mice with round-the-clock access gained weight and became obese and sick.
- Time-restricted feeding also reversed some existing damage, including fatty liver.
- Andrew notes the eight-hour window was set by lab logistics (a grad student's schedule), not biology. "The eight hour feeding windows are not holy."
- Eating around the clock raises inflammatory markers (TNF alpha, IL-6, IL-1). Restricting the window lets them fall.
- About 80% of genes in the brain and body run on a 24-hour schedule. Regular feeding times tighten the rhythm of clock genes (PER, BMAL, CRY1). Chaotic feeding scrambles them.
- Light is the primary entrainer of these clocks and food is the second most powerful.

## Foundational rules for setting up a feeding window

- No food or calories for at least the first 60 minutes after waking. Longer is better.
- No food or liquid calories for 2 to 3 hours before bed.
- The sleep-related fast is especially valuable. Sleep fasting goes with autophagy and with clearing debris from the brain and organs. Andrew says to build the eating window around protecting it.
- Ideal for pure fasting benefit: eat in the middle of the day, for example 10 AM to 6 PM. He calls it ideal but impractical for most social schedules.
- His pragmatic version is roughly noon to 8 PM, with the last bite or sip of calories by 8 PM and bed between 10 PM and 1 AM. He says this allows lunch and dinner with others and still captures the sleep fast.
- A window pushed to 2 or 3 PM through 10 or 11 PM means you are still digesting for roughly the first six hours of sleep. That loses much of the sleep-fast benefit.
- Digestion takes about 5 to 6 hours. Stopping eating is not the same as being fasted.
- How fast you reach the fasted state depends on three things:
  - Meal size: bigger takes longer.
  - Fat content: more fat slows gastric emptying.
  - Liquid versus solid: liquid calories clear faster.
- Fibre and fat blunt and prolong glucose rises. Pure sugars spike them.

## How long the window should be

- Eight hours is Andrew's anchor. Humans studies, including Panda and Krista Varady's trial in obese adults, show that an 8-hour window produced mild caloric restriction and weight loss without calorie counting. It also lowered blood pressure. Studies exist in obese adults, non-obese adults and some children.
- People underestimate their real window. Data from Panda's My Circadian Clock app (food photo logs from thousands of people) shows actual windows are usually one to two hours longer than people think. So aim for a shorter window than you want: for a 10-hour target, plan 8 to 9 hours; for 6 hours, plan 7 to 8.
- Short windows of 4 to 6 hours improve insulin sensitivity, beta cell function, blood pressure, oxidative stress and evening appetite. But they either do nothing for body weight or increase it, apparently because people overeat inside the window.
- One meal per day has very few studies. People tend to maintain or lose weight and may under-eat, which matters for training, hormones and fertility. Andrew says it is fine if it works for you, as long as you do not under-eat.
- Practical shifting: move the window by about an hour per day over 1 to 10 days, then hold the new schedule for at least 30 days, ideally indefinitely. Going straight from three meals over 16 hours to 8 hours can leave you hungry and irritable while leptin and the hypocretin/orexin system adjust.
- Consistency matters. Window drift on weekends, say 2 hours later, acts like jet lag for the clock genes. The effects of that can take two to three days to recover from. "We don't want to be overly neurotic," but regularity matters as much as length.
- Andrew personally uses roughly an 8 to 10 hour window and does not fuss over it. He likes it because he does not negotiate with food: "I don't eat half the croissant." A time rule removes the portion-control decisions (the basal ganglia go/no-go circuitry is metabolically demanding).

## Muscle, hormones and who should not squeeze the window

- Aoyama et al. (Cell Reports, 2021, mice and humans): protein early in the day supports muscle maintenance and hypertrophy, via protein synthesis pathways regulated by the clock gene BMAL. Andrew's rough cutoff is to get some protein before about 10 AM if muscle is the main goal.
- This held regardless of when the resistance training was done. It does not mean later protein is bad.
- If muscle is your priority, a late-shifted window is less ideal. He mentions this cuts against his own habit of not eating until noon to 2 PM.
- Elite cyclist study (Mauro et al.): about 4,800 calories per day eaten in an 8-hour window versus a longer window, same calories. Free testosterone dropped significantly, but cortisol fell more than in controls, which may offset the testosterone effect. Inflammatory markers did not rise.
- Baseline testosterone matters: a 10 to 20% drop is more consequential if you start low. Andrew recommends blood work.
- For hard training, high psychological stress, or heavy workloads, he would not go shorter than 8 hours. The body does not distinguish physical from mental stress.
- Reproduction: leptin from body fat signals the brain to maintain ovulation and fertility. Cutting calories or window length too far can lower sperm counts and disrupt cycles. Women trying to ovulate regularly or conceive should avoid very short windows.
- Mouse data suggest male-female differences in response, with human data still awaited. Some people do not do well on time-restricted eating, and for them smaller meals spread through the day may be better.

## Speeding the shift from fed to fasted

- Glucose clearing:
  - A 20 to 30 minute light walk after eating speeds gastric emptying and glucose uptake.
  - Air squats or pushups also help.
  - High-intensity interval training lowers blood glucose when done in the second half of the day and raises it when done early. The early rise reflects nutrient shuttling to muscle and is not bad. Andrew prefers training early. Late HIIT is only worthwhile if caffeine or stimulation do not wreck sleep.
- Glucose disposal agents (use with caution):
  - Metformin (prescription) and berberine (over the counter, with effects "almost identical" to Metformin).
  - Berberine without carbohydrates gave Andrew a splitting headache from hypoglycemia. Response varies by person and time of day. Some people take it before high-carb meals.
  - Cinnamon is a mild disposal agent. Lemon or lime juice can lower blood glucose through acidity. Half a lime in water helps if a sweet food is overwhelming.
  - A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) is the best way to see the effects.
- Cell signaling logic: eating or having high glucose raises mTOR (cell growth). Fasting raises AMPK, sirtuins, FOX-O, ATF and ketones (repair, autophagy). This is about conditions, not about which food. "Anytime you eat any food, doesn't matter if it's plant-based, animal-based... you are biasing your system towards... cell growth."

## Health effects and fat loss specifics

- Gut: in time-restricted eating, the mucosal lining and microbiome improve. Lactobacillus (high levels correlate with metabolic disorders) falls and Acetobacter rises. Helps some people with IBS and colitis.
- Liver: non-alcoholic fatty liver disease is now common in adults and children. A recent Cell Reports Medicine study found low brown fat, not the gut microbiome, correlates with it. Time-restricted eating and cold exposure (1 to 3 minutes, 2 to 4 times per week, or about 10 minutes) both appear to raise brown fat. Andrew says time-restricted eating may help prevent and "potentially" reverse fatty liver.
- Fat versus lean loss: with 60+ days of time-restricted eating and a caloric deficit, mouse and human data point to more of the loss coming from fat. Hepatic lipase rises (promoting lipolysis) and CIDEC (a lipolysis inhibitor) falls. "You're removing the brake and you're pressing on the accelerator of fat loss." He says no feeding schedule is holy, since a calorie deficit still drives weight loss.
- Longer fasts: alternate-day fasting and 5:2 regimens appear safe, with no bone loss, and can cause rapid weight loss and lower blood glucose. But adherence is hard, and weight and glucose rebound after stopping are unstudied. No quality peer-reviewed studies yet on long fasts for dementia.

## What breaks a fast, and fasting tactics

- Answer: it depends on context, because the real readout is blood glucose and metabolic state, not the clock. He suggests a CGM if you want a definitive answer.
- Fine during a fast: water, tea, black coffee, caffeine pills.
- A Panda-lab finding he cites: even 1 gram of sugar after dinner can disrupt expression of circadian genes tied to the sleep fast. A half glass of wine a couple hours after dinner breaks the fast.
- Context examples: one peanut after a big carb meal an hour ago can break a fast, while a handful deep in a fast may not. A soda breaks it unless you just ran an ultramarathon. Pizza breaks it. Pure fat probably does not after five or more hours of strict fasting (so-called fat fasting, MCTs).
- Sweeteners: evidence is mixed. Artificial sweeteners can cause a brief glucose rise and then a dip, which may drive hunger. Stevia and monk fruit have little data. He considers moderate use unlikely to "take you out of the fast." Heavy use may harm the microbiome, and some people get appetite spikes from sweet taste.
- Toothpaste does not break a fast unless swallowed.
- Salt for fasting symptoms: if shaky, lightheaded or unable to concentrate during a fast, try about a half teaspoon of sea salt or a small pinch in water, optionally with lemon or lime. Neurons run on sodium, potassium and magnesium. Caffeine increases water and sodium excretion. Skip heavy salt if you have hypertension, and talk to a doctor.
- Sauna: with a CGM, Andrew found sauna spikes blood glucose as high as a meal and then drops back. This is because dehydration concentrates blood sugar. He still uses the sauna 3 to 4 times per week before sleep and is not worried.
- Tools he mentions: the free My Circadian Clock site (Panda lab) for logging food, and the Zero app for tracking feeding and fasting windows. He has no affiliation with either.
- For anyone diabetic or making major dietary changes, he says to consult a physician.
