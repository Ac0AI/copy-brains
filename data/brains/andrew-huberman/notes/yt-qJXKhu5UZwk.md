## Why Andrew frames hormone optimization around ratios and salutogenesis

- Andrew separates the pathogenic model (exercise and eat well to avoid heart disease, dementia, bad body composition) from the salutogenic model (do behaviors to move toward energy, endocrine and metabolic well-being). He argues the best mindset holds both.
- He cites Alia Crum's Stanford work: hotel cleaners told their work counted as exercise showed greater health benefits than uninformed colleagues. His takeaway is that knowing why a behavior is good for you changes physiology.
- Estrogen and testosterone are the "sex steroids" and are present in everybody. Their ratio, not the absolute level of either, drives effects on mood, anxiety, reproduction and libido.
- Estradiol is the active form of estrogen in both sexes. Testosterone is made mainly by the testes (some by the adrenals); estrogen mainly by the ovaries.
- Aromatase converts testosterone to estrogen. It is made by body fat and, at low levels, by the testes themselves.
- Levels vary hugely between people and across life. Estrogen rises sharply at puberty and falls at menopause (roughly age 45-60). Testosterone is said to fall about 1% per year, but some men in their 90s still have pubertal levels.
- He gives no target numbers and recommends blood work with a physician, ideally an endocrinologist.

## How testosterone, competition, sex and parenting interact

- Testosterone lowers the amygdala's threshold for stress and anxiety. Andrew says it makes "effort feel good" and promotes novelty-seeking, foraging and competitiveness.
- Competing raises testosterone whether you win or lose. Winning adds dopamine, and dopamine drives pituitary signals that lead to more testosterone later.
- Testosterone drives sex-seeking in all sexes. In females, estrogen drives receptivity, and testosterone peaks just before ovulation.
- Men need estrogen for libido. If estrogen is pushed too low, libido is lost, even with high testosterone.
- Sex itself raises testosterone and dopamine. Prolactin then rises after ejaculation and sets the refractory period.
- Escasa et al. (2011): watching pornography raised testosterone about 10%, while real sex raised it about 70%.
- Ejaculation does not lower testosterone. Abstinence or sex without ejaculation for a week or more raised it up to 400%.
- Expecting fathers show about a 50% drop in testosterone, a roughly three-fold drop in cortisol and doubled estradiol, which Andrew attributes to prolactin. Extra body fat is laid down in both parents. Ongoing contact with the baby's smell influences how long this lasts.
- Illness and the inflammatory cytokine IL-6 lower libido, testosterone and estrogen, and also interfere with sex steroid receptors. IL-10 is the anti-inflammatory counterweight. Keeping IL-6 low supports these hormones.
- DHEA raises both testosterone and estrogen. Where it goes depends on your starting ratio and how much aromatase you have. With high aromatase or low testosterone, it may mostly become estrogen.

## Pheromones: what is established in animals and what is not in humans

- A pheromone is a chemical released by one member of a species that affects others. He says human pheromones remain controversial because no clear vomeronasal organ has been found.
- Animal examples he lists:
  - Lee-Boot effect: females housed together without males develop longer estrus cycles.
  - Whitten effect: male presence changes female cycling.
  - Bruce effect: a pregnant female aborts or reabsorbs her litter if the sire is replaced by a novel male for about 48 hours.
  - Vandenbergh effect: a novel male accelerates female puberty, and older females delay it.
- Human evidence he points to: Stern and McClintock (1998). Armpit sweat from donor women was diluted in alcohol so it had no detectable odor, then swabbed under other women's noses. It changed the timing and pattern of their menstrual cycles, though not clean synchronization.
- Partners can pick out their boyfriend's washed t-shirt among hundreds at far above chance levels.
- Andrew notes that animals use the flehmen response to capture pheromones with the mouth. He calls the perfume industry's pheromone claims "well-established and well-documented" as a business.
- Whether human miscarriage relates to the Bruce effect is an open question.

## Breathing, apnea and sleep as the foundation for sex hormones

- Apnea (breath-holding or under-breathing, mostly in sleep) is consistently associated with lower estrogen in people with ovaries and lower testosterone in people with testes. Andrew says the direction of causation is unclear.
- Mechanism he describes: CO2 buildup raises cortisol. Cholesterol is shunted toward cortisol rather than toward testosterone or estrogen, and deep sleep also supports gonadal cell turnover.
- He found at least four quality studies showing that reducing or eliminating apnea raised testosterone in males and improved estrogen-to-testosterone ratios in females.
- Tools he lists:
  - Nasal breathing during the day, unless eating, speaking or at maximum effort.
  - Nasal breathing during cardio below maximum effort. The sinuses dilate over time, so it gets easier. Swimmers, fighters and martial artists have reasons to breathe differently.
  - Taping the mouth shut at night (as James Nestor describes), using tape you can still open through if needed.
  - CPAP for severe sleep apnea.
- He cites "Jaws" (Sandra Kahn and Paul Ehrlich) on chronic mouth breathing changing the jaw and face. He also notes that being very overweight worsens apnea.
- The physiological sigh (a double inhale followed by a long exhale) is what the body does spontaneously after an apnea episode, and it can also be done deliberately to offload CO2.

## Light viewing, dopamine and seasonal biology

- In seasonal breeders such as foxes, rabbits and ferrets, coat color and breeding status track day length. Tyrosine is the shared precursor for dopamine and melanin pigmentation.
- Light to the eyes raises dopamine. Dopamine drives GnRH, then LH and FSH, then testosterone and estrogen.
- Humans are not seasonal breeders, but mood and hormones tend to run higher in spring and summer. Susceptibility to winter lows has a strong genetic component.
- Protocol:
  - View bright light, ideally sunlight, in the first hour after waking, for about 2 to 10 minutes.
  - Sunglasses do not substitute for it, though prescription glasses and contacts are fine. If sunlight is unavailable, use bright artificial light.
  - Never look at anything painfully bright.
  - Avoid bright light in the middle of the night. He cites two papers (in "Cell" and "Neuron") showing it suppresses dopamine. He suspects much of sleep deprivation's hormone effect comes from night light, which he says no study has tested yet.
- Light raises both hormones in parallel and keeps your existing ratio. He says you cannot raise "the wrong one".
- Order of priorities: breathing, light and sleep come before supplements.

## Cold, heat and the gonads

- Cold exposure may raise testosterone and estrogen, but he says the effect is likely not the cold itself. His best guess is rebound vasodilation after vasoconstriction.
- Cooling the neurons that control blood vessels reduces blood flow. The re-warming then delivers more blood and more GnRH, LH and carrier proteins to the gonads. He calls this his most plausible explanation, not a proven mechanism.
- Examples he mentions: 1 to 10 minute ice baths, cold showers, cold-water swims, and ice-pack underwear sold online (he did not buy any).
- No studies test the gonad-cooling products directly. He says there is no firm evidence behind the folk claims about room temperature and a child's sex.
- Too much heat is bad for sperm. Sperm proteins are heat sensitive, which harms fertility independent of any effect on testosterone or estrogen.

## Exercise protocols for testosterone

- Heavy resistance training raises testosterone in males and females for about a day, sometimes up to 48 hours.
  - Use heavy loads, roughly 70-95% of maximum, in sets of about 1 to 6-8 reps.
  - Stop short of failure. Training to failure was not the best for testosterone in the studies he cites.
- Studies he names: Ratamess et al. (2005), on androgen receptor content after heavy resistance exercise, and Izquierdo et al. (2006), on training to failure versus not to failure. Duncan French is an author on several.
- He thinks the signal comes from recruiting high-threshold motor units, and he admits the mechanism is not well understood.
- Combined sessions: lift heavy first, then do cardio. Endurance first lowered testosterone during the lifting. On separate days there was no interaction.
- High-intensity interval training and sprinting increase testosterone.
- Endurance work beyond about 75 minutes starts to lower testosterone, presumably via cortisol. Intensity matters, so he says a 3-hour hike is unlikely to hurt but 3 hours of hard running probably would.
- He says cyclists caught doping with testosterone may use it partly to avoid cortisol-driven drops in testosterone, and he is not recommending it.

## Menopause options and supplements for estrogen

- Standard care is hormone therapy (oral, patch or pellet), with variable success. He flags caution when there is a breast cancer background, since many such cancers are estrogen-dependent. Tamoxifen and anastrozole were developed for that reason.
- Alternatives with human trials, from examine.com:
  - Black cohosh: 13 peer-reviewed studies, mostly 6+ months and double-blind, in women aged 45-64. It gave consistent but modest improvements in estrogen and symptoms, above placebo.
  - Panax ginseng: reduced some symptoms, mostly libido-related, with unreliable effects otherwise.
  - Maca (increases dopamine): minor effects.
  - Valeriana officinalis: some improvement in hot flashes and insomnia in 100+ subject trials.
  - Pueraria mirifica (Kwao Krua Kao): four studies, each comparable to estrogen replacement for symptoms. He calls it "particularly interesting" and cautions about hormone-related compounds generally.
- He is not a physician and says to talk to your doctor first.

## Nutrients and supplements for testosterone and the HPG axis

- Housekeeping nutrients: vitamin D3, zinc and magnesium. Wrzosek et al. (2020) links deficiencies to impaired HPA/sex steroid function. He takes all three himself, but says to check doses on examine.com and get blood work.
- Opioids sharply lower testosterone and estrogen by disrupting receptors on hypothalamic GnRH neurons, and can cause gynecomastia in men and ovarian disruption in women.
- Creatine raises DHT, possibly via 5-alpha reductase. People prone to hair loss may notice it.
- Total testosterone is typically cited as about 300-900, with supraphysiological levels around 1,200-1,600. Most testosterone is bound to SHBG or albumin, which shuttle it to tissues, and he argues against demonizing them. Albumin carries it across the blood-brain barrier.
- Free-testosterone supplements he mentions:
  - Tongkat ali (Eurycoma longifolia Jack): 400-800 mg per day, with possible side effects of alertness and insomnia if taken late.
  - Boron: a couple of grams per day, with no known side effects.
  - Stinging nettle: possible effects on prostate and liver.
- Ecdysteroids such as turkesterone (Isenmann et al., 2019, "Archives of Toxicology"): in a 10-week study with strength training, they gave above-placebo gains in muscle mass and strength. The authors called for banning them in sport; Andrew presents that as their view.
- Fadogia agrestis can raise LH and so testosterone and estrogen, in the existing ratio. Its side-effect profile is undocumented, so he stresses blood work.
- hCG is a prescription LH mimic, originally extracted from pregnant women's urine. It stimulates the gonads and is used for fertility or pituitary issues.
- He did not cover Bulbine natalensis because its evidence is mostly rat or sponsored studies and it shows liver toxicity concerns.
- The tissues with the most cell turnover (breast, prostate, testes) are cancer-prone and androgen- or estrogen-sensitive. He says "more is definitely not better".
- The menstrual cycle's feedback loop is delicate, so hormone manipulation can disrupt it. He advises blood tests and cycle monitoring.
