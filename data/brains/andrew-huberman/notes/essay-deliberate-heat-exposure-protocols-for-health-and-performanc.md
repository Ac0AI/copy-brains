## Safety limits and who should skip sauna

- Andrew says heat exposure is only beneficial when it isn't too hot. Too-high temperatures are "extremely dangerous."
- Pregnant women and children younger than 16 should not use sauna.
- He advises starting slow, with cooler temperatures that don't significantly raise heart rate. Hyperthermia and dehydration are always possible.
- Men trying to conceive should know that repeated deliberate heat exposure can reduce sperm count. Counts rebound after stopping sauna or hot baths, but this takes about 45-60 days.
- Men who want to avoid this can put a cool or cold pack on while in the sauna. That isn't possible in a hot bath.

## Ways to get deliberate heat and the infrared caveat

- Options include dry saunas, steam saunas, hot tubs, hot showers, or wearing warm layers on a brief jog to raise body temperature.
- He recommends picking whatever method fits your schedule and budget and can be done routinely.
- Infrared saunas: there is currently insufficient evidence that they have effects beyond a standard sauna. Most also don't reach the heat range (80-100 °C) he lists for positive health effects.

## Cardiovascular health and Protocol #1

- Regular sauna use is linked to a lower risk of fatal cardiovascular events and stroke (Laukkanen et al.). Longer and more frequent sessions were associated with lower long-term cardiovascular disease risk. Other clinical groups have linked sauna to lower "all-cause mortality."
- Heat triggers some of the same responses as cardio: heart rate and blood flow rise, and blood vessels vasodilate as the body works to cool down.
- **Protocol #1 (cardiovascular):**
  - Temperature: 80-100 °C (176-212 °F), adjusted to personal tolerance.
  - Duration: 5-20 minutes per session.
  - Frequency: 2-3x per week, or up to 7x per week. More often appears better for cardiovascular health.

## Mood, stress response and Protocol #2

- Heat drives release of dynorphins and endorphins. Dynorphins first cause discomfort and agitation but set the stage for endorphins to have enhanced effects on mood and body. This explains the mild, happy euphoria after sauna.
- Heat is a form of hormesis, a mild tolerable stress that helps the body adapt. Regular sauna use has been found to:
  - decrease cortisol levels
  - enhance activation of DNA repair/longevity pathways
  - activate heat shock proteins, which help monitor and possibly repair protein structure within cells
- **Protocol #2 (general health, mood, stress, hormetic pathways):**
  - Total of 1 hour per week, split into 2-3 sessions rather than done all at once.
  - Temperature: 80-100 °C (176-212 °F).

## Growth hormone and Protocol #3

- Growth hormone supports muscle growth, bone strength, tissue repair and metabolism. It is released at night during sleep and declines with age.
- Specific sauna protocols have been shown to boost growth hormone release dramatically, up to 16-fold in one study.
- **Protocol #3 (growth hormone):**
  - Use sauna infrequently: once per week or less.
  - On that day, do 30 minutes in the sauna, cool off outside for 5 minutes, then 30 minutes more, then cool off.
  - A few hours or more later, repeat, for a total of four 30-minute sauna sessions in one day. Andrew notes "that's a lot!"
  - Do it in a semi-fasted state, with no food for 2-3 hours beforehand. Lower blood glucose encourages growth hormone release, as it does for daily release during sleep.
  - More frequent sauna has other effects but blunts the growth hormone increase.
  - To add metabolic gains, alternate sauna with deliberate cold exposure during the rest periods.

## Hydration and timing

- Hydrate well before and after sauna, since sweat contains water and electrolytes.
- He recommends drinking at least 16 ounces of water for every 10 minutes spent in the sauna.
- Body temperature fluctuates with circadian rhythm. Using sauna in the afternoon or evening lets the body's natural cooling align with the "post-cooling sauna effect," which helps with falling asleep at night.
