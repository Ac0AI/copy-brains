## Fermented foods are Andrew's first lever for gut microbiome diversity

- Andrew cites a human study by Dr. Justin Sonnenburg and Dr. Chris Gardner (Stanford). It tested diets high in plant fiber versus diets high in fermented foods.
- Fermented foods increased overall gut microbiota diversity and reduced key markers of inflammation (the "inflammatome").
- Participants ate six servings per day. Higher total amounts did not give further benefit, so Andrew stresses consistently including fermented foods daily over eating large quantities.
- Low-sugar examples he lists: sauerkraut, plain yogurt, kimchi, kombucha, natto, kefir, and even drinking brine.
- Buy them from the refrigerated section so they contain live active cultures. Shelf-stable fermented foods are pasteurized and do not give the same boost.
- Making your own kombucha or sauerkraut at home is a cost-effective option.

## What the fermented-food vs. fiber study found about fiber

- A high-fiber diet did not increase microbiota diversity in the study.
- It did increase carbohydrate active enzymes, which digest fiber and may improve the microbiome's ability to break down other complex carbohydrates.
- Some participants in the high-fiber group showed reduced inflammation markers.
- Andrew still recommends plant-based, high-fiber foods (vegetables, legumes, whole grains). They offer broad health benefits and supply key nutrients for microbiota already established in the gut.

## Prebiotics and probiotics: dosing and cautions

- Definitions: prebiotics are fermentable dietary fiber or microbiota-accessible carbohydrates that feed existing gut microbiota. Probiotics are live bacteria or yeasts that can colonize the gut. Synbiotics are mixtures of the two.
- Low levels of prebiotics and/or probiotics, alongside whole quality foods, improve gut microbiome health.
- Higher levels can help recovery and replenishment in dysbiosis, for example after antibiotics, during high stress, when traveling, or when changing your diet.
- Excessive probiotic intake has been linked to brain fog, gas and bloating. If you notice these, reduce the amount you take.
- Supplements are not FDA-regulated, so Andrew advises choosing an independently validated product.
- The gut microbiome is highly individualized, so supplementation affects people differently.

## Sleep protects the gut microbiome through stress

- The gut microbiome is closely tied to your stress level, through direct links to immune system cells.
- Andrew says getting deep, good-quality sleep for generally 6-9 hours per night is essential for managing stress, and therefore for gut microbiome health.
- He points to his Toolkit for Sleep newsletter and Episode 2 of the Huberman Lab Podcast, "Master Your Sleep & Be More Alert When Awake," for sleep tactics.

## Limit processed foods and favor fiber that feeds short-chain fatty acids

- Emulsifiers are detergent-like additives common in processed foods. They can disrupt the mucus layer of the GI tract.
- In animal models, emulsifiers reduced microbial diversity, caused low-grade inflammation, and increased body fat, blood sugar and insulin resistance, which are markers of metabolic syndrome.
- The typical Western diet (high fat, low fiber, more processed food) lacks many nutrients microbiota need.
- When microbiota ferment complex plant fiber, they produce short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate. These reduce inflammation, maintain the gut's mucosal barrier, regulate the immune system and modulate metabolism along the GI tract.
- His summary: prioritize whole foods, plant-based fiber and fermented foods.

## Artificial sweeteners: possible disruption, test it yourself

- Clinical studies have not fully separated out the effect of artificial sweeteners on the gut microbiome.
- Animal models show evidence that they can disrupt it.
- A recent study found that gut neuropod cells distinguish natural from artificial sweeteners. They send the brain a different pattern of signals depending on whether the sugar is nutritive (has calories) or non-caloric.
- Because the Human Microbiome Project showed how individualized the microbiome is, Andrew suggests trying removing some artificial sweeteners and seeing whether you notice an effect.
- Non-caloric plant-based sweeteners like stevia are "probably fine," but few studies have examined stevia and the microbiome.

## Don't over-sanitize your environment

- Andrew quotes Dr. Sonnenburg: "Exposure to microbes from the environment is likely an important part of educating our immune systems and keeping everything in the proper balance."
- The gut microbiome is also seeded by social contact (shaking hands, hugging, kissing), pets, dirt and grass.
- Over-sanitizing environments or overusing antibiotics can remove sources of beneficial microbiota.
- He still says to avoid disease-causing pathogens and harmful environmental chemicals such as pesticides, but notes many environmental microbes help establish and maintain a healthy gut microbiome.
- Low microbial diversity is a marker of dysbiosis, associated with autoimmune diseases, obesity and cardiometabolic conditions.
