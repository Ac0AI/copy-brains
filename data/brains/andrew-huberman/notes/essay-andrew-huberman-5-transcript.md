## Does the conversation hold up as protocol material? Mostly anecdotal, so the notes below stay narrow

## Morning exercise, caffeine timing and energy
- Andrew says delaying caffeine 60-90 minutes after waking can offset an afternoon crash for people who get one. He regrets over-emphasizing it: a too-big lunch or poor sleep the night before will still cause a crash.
- He sometimes goes straight to hydration and caffeine, especially before a workout.
- His personal observation: exercising before 8:30 AM, even starting a bit tired, gives him energy "that lasts all day". Waiting until his mid-morning peak (10-11 AM) leaves him exhausted in the afternoon. He says he doesn't know why, and that it depends on workout intensity.
- He aims to be done, showered and heading to work by 9:00 AM.
- Entrainment: forcing yourself to exercise, eat, socialize or view bright light at the same time of day for 3-7 days in a row builds an anticipatory circuit. He says this is why most people can become morning people to some degree, and why you wake before your alarm.

## Daily structure for deep work
- Wake up; if he feels under-slept, he does a non-sleep deep rest (NSDR/yoga nidra) session of 10 or 20 minutes. He says he and Matt Walker are submitting an IRB to study what NSDR does to the brain.
- Hydrate, then caffeine (Mateína yerba mate, coffee). He discloses he's a partial owner of Mateína.
- Training: resistance training 3 days a week, running 3 days a week, 1 day off.
- Deep work immediately after: 90 minutes to 3 hours when in a groove. He keeps a sticky note on his computer reminding him how good it feels to accomplish real work, and fights "tooth and nail" to stay off social media, text, WhatsApp and YouTube.
- Lunch, more deep work, then around 2:30 PM an NSDR or short nap, a little more caffeine, and another bout.
- With two or three deep-work bouts, he says the workday is done by about 5-6 PM.
- Social time: he likes gathering people at a set time of day (sauna and cold plunge at Jocko's place, training at his home gym), and notes he has had to learn to be the one who calls the gathering despite being an introvert.

## Creativity: still body with active mind, or a creative outlet "15 degrees off center"
- Rick Rubin keeps his body very still while his mind stays active, in a meditative state. Karl Deisseroth forces himself to sit still late at night and think in complete sentences. Andrew compares this to REM sleep (body paralyzed, mind active). He notes these are anecdotes, "far from controlled studies".
- Many people also report ideas in the shower or while running, when the body is active and the mind is in default-mode wandering.
- Andrew tried Rick's still-body approach but found something else works better: the practice of his friend Tim Armstrong and Joni Mitchell, doing a creative activity "15 degrees off center" from your main one. For Andrew that is drawing anatomical and neuroscience diagrams (e.g. autonomic circuitry). His mind then starts churning on topics for the podcast.
- Jungian analyst James Hollis's point, which Andrew endorsed: spend 10-15 minutes a day in quiet (walking, sitting or lying down, no stimulus-response) so unconscious material surfaces. Balance it with "get up, suit up, shut up and get to work."

## Nicotine and cognitive enhancers
- Andrew's use is small and occasional: about half a piece of Nicorette (a couple of milligrams) now and then, with a personal cap of two pieces of Nicorette total per week.
- He says it sharpens the mind, especially on an empty stomach. Mechanism as he explains it: the nucleus basalis releases acetylcholine to the neocortex when you focus, and nicotine artificially heightens that nicotinic-receptor signaling.
- Cautions he gave:
  - It raises blood pressure and is probably not safe for everyone.
  - He doesn't recommend it frequently or at all, and not for anyone 25 or younger (plastic brain).
  - Pouches like Zyn run about 6-8 mg, and people drift from one a day to a canister a day.
  - It is a vasoconstrictor, the opposite of Cialis-type vasodilators.
  - A day after use, throat spasm or an urge to cough can occur, which matters for singers and podcasters.
  - Smoking, vaping, dipping and snuff are different and have their own problems.

## Peptides and how he trials supplements
- He adds one thing at a time and notices quickly if it doesn't feel good. He typically decides in about 4-5 days, then moves on, and he does blood work.
- BPC-157: his concern is that one known effect in animal models is angiogenesis, which could vascularize a tumor if one existed. Human data is "one study and it's a lousy one".
- Growth hormone secretagogues (ipamorelin, tesamorelin, sermorelin): he tried them and found they "nuked" his REM sleep while boosting deep sleep, which didn't feel good to him. He avoids peptides that tap major hormone pathways.
- Pinealon: taken twice a week before sleep, it seemed to affect circadian timing, because he got very tired at that time on non-dose days. He experiments for a couple of weeks, then stops.
- His view on supplements generally: "food supplements" like protein powder differ from vitamins and compounds, some compounds have real benefit, and some are vetted for potency and safety more rigorously than others.
- On GLP-1 drugs (Ozempic, Mounjaro): he is "middle-of-the-road" and criticizes the wellness community for dismissing them. Muscle loss can be offset with resistance training, and for people carrying a lot of weight, running can cause injury.

## Light, time perception and sleep
- Circadian signal: short- and long-wavelength cones subtract to signal morning versus evening. Low solar angle gives blue-plus-yellow/orange light at sunrise and red-orange-plus-blue at sunset, which entrains the suprachiasmatic nucleus.
- His standing advice: get sunlight in your eyes in the morning and evening. On overcast days, outdoors is still far brighter than night, so it still counts.
- Sleep deprivation is followed by REM rebound; people coming off cannabis often get insomnia, then very vivid dreams.
- Time-window point: the brain can deliberately widen or narrow its time window. When things are hard, zoom out and see it as one element of your whole lifespan.

## Mouth health and correcting errors
- His oral health research: Streptococcus that causes cavities can enter the body via the mouth, and he links oral health to heart and brain health. Teeth can remineralize if a cavity isn't too deep and saliva has the right substrates. Alcohol-based mouthwashes can kill off helpful things. He consulted at least four dental experts.
- His stance on fluoride and sunscreen: he is not against fluoridation, and he wears mineral-based sunscreen on his face.
- How he handles mistakes (the cumulative probability gaffe): he corrected it publicly with the formula and graph, and corrects errors in show-note captions and audio. His approach to topics: find the "center of mass" of expert opinion, report it, and accept that some of it will draw criticism.
