## Cortisol is an energy-deployment hormone, not just a stress hormone

Andrew argues cortisol's main job is to deploy energy: it releases glucose into the bloodstream (from liver glycogen and muscle) and directs much of it to the brain. Stress is only one trigger; waking up, hard work tasks, exercise and focus all need it.

- It is made in the adrenal glands (middle layer), is lipophilic, and crosses the blood-brain barrier. The hippocampus is dense with cortisol receptors.
- Adrenaline and norepinephrine act in milliseconds to seconds. Cortisol must be synthesized first, takes about 10 minutes, and lasts longer. That is why stress lingers after the stressor is gone.
- His core rule: "You want your cortisol high early in the day, shortly after waking, and you want your cortisol low in the hours right before sleep and in the first hours of sleep."
- Getting this rhythm right is what he credits for energy, mood, sleep and immune health. Getting it wrong underlies most burnout.

## The 24-hour cortisol rhythm and how it regulates itself

Andrew cites a classic study ("24-hour pattern of the episodic secretion of cortisol in normal subjects") describing four phases:

1. **Minimal secretion:** from 4 hours before lights out until 2 hours after. Cortisol should be very low and falling.
2. **Preliminary nocturnal episode:** hours 3-5 of sleep, a slight rise.
3. **Main secretory phase:** hours 6-8+ of sleep, a fast rise. A 6-hour sleeper gets only part of it. This coincides with REM, which needs lots of brain energy.
4. **After waking:** the cortisol awakening response (CAR). The first hour (up to about 90 minutes) is when you can amplify it. After that the window closes until the next morning.

Mechanism:
- Hypothalamus (paraventricular nucleus) releases CRH, which makes the anterior pituitary release ACTH, which acts on the adrenals via the melanocortin receptor to release cortisol. This is the HPA axis.
- Negative feedback: high blood cortisol shuts off CRH and ACTH. Low cortisol restarts them, which is why it rises again late in sleep.
- A second, parallel pathway runs from the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) via the splanchnic nerve and can boost cortisol for hours after waking. Melanopsin retinal ganglion cells send bright-light and luminance-change signals directly to the SCN.
- Practical consequence: a bigger morning peak triggers the feedback loop, so cortisol falls more cleanly through the afternoon and stays low at night.

## Boosting morning cortisol: light, water, caffeine, exercise, cold

Andrew says bright light is the foundation, and the other tools stack on top of it.

- **Light:** get bright light into the eyes within the first hour of waking, ideally the first 30 minutes. Outside, sunglasses off (glasses and contacts are fine), look toward the sun, blink as needed. Do it on cloudy days too, when it matters more. If no sun, use a 10,000 lux artificial light (about $100). Indoor lights and phones are not enough. He says it can raise cortisol by up to 50% and is the same protocol used for seasonal affective disorder.
- **Hydration:** 16 to 32 ounces of water first thing, with electrolytes in his case. Mild dehydration is common after waking and hydration modestly raises adrenaline and cortisol pulses.
- **Caffeine:** for habitual users (caffeine each of the last 5 days), it does not raise cortisol much but extends how long cortisol lasts. Non-habitual users get a large spike. If you crash in the late morning or afternoon, delay caffeine 30 to 90 minutes after waking to flatten the decline. If you love caffeine right away, he says do it anyway and stack other tools. He does not suggest starting caffeine just to spike cortisol.
- **Exercise:** familiar exercise at a familiar time raises cortisol little. Novel types, intensities or timings raise it more and for longer. Regular timing acts as an entrainment cue to the SCN. Train within the same 2 to 3 hour window, ideally 4 to 6 days a week with at least one full rest day, and you get an anticipatory cortisol and energy rise in the hour before.
- **Deliberate cold:** novel cold exposure raises cortisol, but regular exposure (more than about twice a week) does not meaningfully. It still gives lasting dopamine, epinephrine and norepinephrine increases. He says there is no evidence that cold is bad for women's cortisol specifically.

## Foods and compounds that extend cortisol: grapefruit and black licorice

Andrew uses these when he can't get light or exercise.

- **Grapefruit** (whole pink or yellow, or about 6 to 8 oz juice): compounds inhibit CYP3A4 (and maybe A5), a major cortisol-degrading enzyme. Studies show it extends cortisol's life in the bloodstream by roughly 25 to 50%. It also prolongs caffeine's effect. He eats one pink grapefruit in late morning before his first meal (around 10:30 to 11:00) and notes more energy into the afternoon. People on medication that affects cortisol, or with Cushing's, are told to avoid it.
- **Black licorice** (not red): glycyrrhizin inhibits 11-beta-HSD, the enzyme converting cortisol to cortisone, raising active cortisol. It is potent, so start with the lowest dose, avoid it later in the day, and do not use it if you have hypertension or are pregnant or breastfeeding.
- He also mentions yohimbine and eleuthero as modest cortisol raisers but does not emphasize them.

## Lowering cortisol in the evening: light, caffeine, breathing, carbs

The 4 hours before bed through 2 hours after is when cortisol should be lowest, and it is easy to spike then because the baseline is so low.

- **Light:** about 2 hours after sundown, even modest short-wavelength light (white/blue LEDs, screens) can spike cortisol and suppress melatonin. Dim lights, favor floor and table lamps over overhead lights (the light-sensing cells sit in the lower two-thirds of the retina, viewing the upper field), and use red or amber (incandescent) bulbs. On iPhone, set a triple-click shortcut for a red screen. He worked with Roka on short-wavelength-blocking glasses.
- **Sunset:** viewing sunset light can make the retina less sensitive to evening artificial light. A sunset walk is good.
- **Caffeine cutoff:** he uses about 2 PM. Noon for sensitive people, up to 4 PM for others, and generally 2:30 to 3 PM at the latest.
- **Real-time stress control:** the physiological sigh: a deep nasal inhale, a second sharp nasal inhale to fully inflate the lungs, then a long mouth exhale until empty. Do 1 to 3 when stressed, or just emphasized exhales. Calmer autonomic state gives better control of behavior and thoughts.
- **Starchy carbs at the last meal:** rice, potatoes, yams, pasta. Elevated blood glucose makes cortisol less readily released, which is why carbs are "comfort foods." Ideally stop eating 2 to 3 hours before bed, but he allows a small snack if hunger prevents sleep (it also impairs growth hormone in early sleep if too close).
- **Low-carb diets:** below roughly 30% of calories from carbs, cortisol rises in the first ~3 weeks and then normalizes. If you sleep fine, he wouldn't worry. If you feel more stressed, give it past 3 weeks, or add starch at dinner. Insulin resistance can independently raise cortisol.

## Late-day exercise and cortisol-lowering supplements

- **Late workouts:** moderate-to-high intensity exercise late in the day can triple or quadruple cortisol when baseline is low. Early afternoon or around sundown is usually fine. If you train in the evening: get protein plus starchy carbs and fruit, do 2 to 3 minutes of long-exhale breathing, take a warm (not cold) shower or sauna, dim lights, avoid screens, and skip caffeine beforehand. A spike that lingers into sleep suppresses the next morning's cortisol and causes a sluggish, foggy start.
- **If you slipped:** after a stressful or short night, boost morning cortisol harder (bright light, hydration, caffeine, cold shower, maybe skip a second workout) to reset the rhythm.
- **Behavior first, supplements second.**
- **Ashwagandha:** studies show cortisol reductions of about 11 to 29% at roughly 300 to 900 mg. He starts at 300 mg and takes it with or after dinner. Keep it in the late afternoon and evening, since doses of 50 to 100 mg or less (like in blends such as AG1) won't matter early in the day.
- **Apigenin (chamomile):** 50 mg before bed, a smaller cortisol effect. He has taken it for 8+ years as part of his sleep stack: apigenin, magnesium threonate (3-in-8), and theanine, 30 to 60 minutes before bed. Magnesium threonate may help indirectly lower cortisol.
- Phosphatidylserine and rhodiola are also purported, but he does not highlight them. Under-18s should be cautious with supplements.

## Burnout: two patterns and how to fix each

Andrew says burnout is real but is usually not adrenal failure. True disorders are Cushing's (excess cortisol: rounded face, belly fat, blotchy pigmentation) and Addison's (adrenal insufficiency). See an endocrinologist for those (e.g., dexamethasone suppression test). Most burnout is a mistimed cortisol rhythm.

**Pattern 1: stressed in the morning, exhausted later** (early waking with anxiety, afternoon crash, fog). Cortisol rises too fast or too early.
- Do 10 to 30 minutes of NSDR (non-sleep deep rest/yoga nidra) on waking or in the night, with a long-exhale breath. He cites a study using 11 minutes that reduced cortisol. Falling back asleep is fine (set an alarm).
- Hydrate, then get bright light to shift the peak (acrophase) later.
- Delay caffeine 60 to 90 minutes to flatten the afternoon decline and make the peak less sharp.

**Pattern 2: sluggish in the morning, wired at night.** Evening cortisol is too high and the morning one is blunted.
- Focus on the 6 hours before bed and 2 hours after: no caffeine or stress, dim lights, sunset walk, and 3 to 5 minutes of physiological sighs (his Stanford trial with David Spiegel found improved calm, heart rate variability, mood and sleep).
- Fix the night first so the morning cortisol can rise again.

His advice: start where the pain point is on the 24-hour curve. True night owls have the same rhythm shifted later and are fine if it fits their life.

## Aging, flattened curves, and cognition

- Men and women look similar until about 40. Then the morning peak rounds off and the afternoon decline becomes flatter. This also happens at menopause, in chronic illness, and in cancer.
- A flatter curve is associated with lower longevity and poorer survival. In cancer patients, a bigger peak, faster afternoon decline and low night levels predicted longer survival.
- Sleep targets: 6 to 8 hours for most people (maybe 9 for kids or illness). He says few people truly thrive on 4 to 5.
- Cognition: chronically mistimed or high cortisol degrades hippocampal neurons, hurting memory and also the brain's ability to tell exciting from stressful, which worsens stress regulation.
- He says that if you get the rhythm right for 3 to 4 days, you should notice improvements in energy, focus, sleep and mood.
