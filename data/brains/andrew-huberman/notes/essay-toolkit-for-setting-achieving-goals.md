## Setting a goal: the six-step framework

Andrew's six steps for defining a goal before pursuing it:

1. **Choose one priority goal.** Overhauling many habits at once (diet, workout, sleep, meditation, all starting January 1st) often causes all of them to fail. He asks: "What is the one goal I can give the necessary time and energy to pursue right now?"
2. **Make it lofty.** Simple goals don't create enough neural arousal and stress to stimulate real growth and learning, so the goal should be ambitious.
3. **Define specific actions.** Macro-goals like "get fit," "write a book" or "run more" must be broken into action steps, e.g. run three times per week. This significantly improves the odds of achieving them.
4. **Make it measurable on a finite timeline.** He suggests a 12-week period for most goals. Within it, define total hours per week, specific days, the length of each work session, and a time window (e.g. 8 a.m. to 9 a.m.) or constraint (e.g. before 9 a.m.).
5. **Quantify it.** Some goals are easily quantified (running a marathon under a set time); subjective ones (developing a meditation practice) need extra care and clearly outlined actions. For any goal, designate smaller outcomes along the 12 weeks to keep motivation up.
6. **Don't tell the world.** Positive feedback on a newly set goal can activate the brain's reward mechanisms and reduce motivation to pursue it, because you got the reward without the work. Keep it to yourself at first; sharing is more fun after the first milestone.

## Reminders and visualization during goal pursuit

**Post-it note fallacy**
- A note saying "floss!" or "run today!" stops working after a few days because the brain monitors for what's new and stops "seeing" static reminders.
- A static visual reminder fails to trigger action. Write a new note every few days and post it in a different location.

**Visualize success and failure**
- If motivated before a work session, spend 1-3 minutes visualizing the positive outcomes of goal success as well as goal failure, to sustain motivation through the work bout.
- On unmotivated days, spend 1-3 minutes visualizing the failure of not achieving the goal.
- Humans work hard to avoid feelings of failure, and this exercise recruits neurochemicals like adrenaline to boost arousal and focus.

## Visual targets and removing the phone

**Visual target strategy**
- Mental focus is strongly anchored to the visual system. Narrow the "cone" of visual attention to a specific object or point in front of you and hold it for 30-90 seconds.
- Dr. Emily Balcetis and colleagues have shown this increases alertness and autonomic arousal, and helps build and sustain motivation.
- When finished working, relax your gaze and look off to the horizon. This "turns off" the release of chemicals associated with alertness and arousal and aids relaxation.

**Phone**
- During work bouts, put the phone out of reach or turn on airplane mode.
- Two costs to avoid: task-switching (checking the phone between bouts of work) and procrastinating (checking it right before starting).

## Intermittent rewards to sustain dopamine-driven motivation

- Consistently receiving rewards diminishes the reward's power and reduces overall motivation. Rewarding yourself only at the finish line (~12 weeks) also reduces motivation.
- The solution is random, intermittent rewards, like a slot machine. After goal work, flip a coin: heads = reward, tails = no reward.
- On a reward day, use positive self-talk for 30-60 seconds to reinforce the neural circuits associated with internal motivation.
- Treat yourself to a physical reward from time to time, such as watching a movie or eating a food you enjoy.

## The middle problem

- People tend to be highly motivated to start and finish a goal, but motivation often declines in the middle.
- To overcome it:
  1. Acknowledge this is a normal phenomenon.
  2. Use time chunking: break the middle portion into 3-4 smaller time segments.

## Timing goal work to circadian rhythms

- Andrew cites Dr. Pablo Valdez's description of three peak times each day when attention and focus are highest.
- As a rough guide, 30 minutes, 3 hours and 11 hours after waking are great times for hard work.
- This arises from the body's circadian rhythm (sleep-wake cycle) and the rhythmicity of body temperature and associated neurochemicals.
