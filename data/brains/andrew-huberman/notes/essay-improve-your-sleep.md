## Sleep fundamentals: the QQRT framework

Andrew frames good sleep around four factors: Quantity, Quality, Regularity, Timing.

- **Quantity:** the typical adult needs **7 to 9 hours** per night, enough time for both deep sleep and REM sleep. Babies, teens and people fighting illness or infection need more. Some need less.
- **Quality:** continuity and structure. Frequent awakenings (even ones you don't remember) mean fragmented sleep. Wearable trackers score this as sleep efficiency, and **85% or higher** is a good goal. Without a tracker, poor quality shows up as excessive daytime sleepiness despite enough hours.
- **Regularity:** keep bed and wake times within **±30 minutes**, weekends included. This anchors the circadian clock. Andrew suggests adding a "bedtime alarm" alongside the morning alarm. He cites a study linking regular sleep patterns to lower all-cause mortality and possibly lower cancer and cardiovascular risk, more so than duration alone.
- **Timing:** align sleep with your chronotype (morning person vs. night owl), which is mostly genetic and shifts a bit across life. The Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ) can find it. Sleeping out of sync with it lowers sleep quality, though life's demands still apply.

## Sleep trackers and orthosomnia

- Trackers can cause anxiety about sleep quality, termed "**orthosomnia**", which can paradoxically disrupt sleep.
- Andrew's fix: review scores weekly, not daily, to cut constant monitoring.
- A simple self-check: do you wake refreshed, or feel you could sleep past your alarm? If the latter, apply the hygiene protocols.

## Light and dark for sleep

- Melatonin signals sleep onset. Darkness allows its release, and bright light at night potently inhibits it, making you less sleepy and worsening sleep.
- **Evening:** dim home lights, use **low-positioned lights** rather than overhead, and prefer yellow or, better, **deep orange/red bulbs**.
- Minimize screens at night, dim them heavily, use warm-color modes or a free app like f.lux.
- Use **blackout curtains** or an **eye mask**.
- **Morning:** get **10-15 minutes of sunlight** after waking. It suppresses melatonin, resets the circadian rhythm and triggers a cortisol release that boosts daytime alertness.
- On overcast days it matters even more; plenty of photons penetrate cloud cover, and you don't need to see the sun directly.
- If natural light isn't possible, SAD lamps are a decent but less effective substitute.

## Temperature, a warm bath, and meal timing

- Falling asleep requires core body temperature to drop about **2-3°F (1°C)**.
- Keep the bedroom cool, around **67°F (19.4°C)**, or use a temperature-controlled mattress cover. Sticking hands or feet out of the covers helps cooling.
- A **warm bath or shower before bed** is science-supported: it first raises core temperature, and the subsequent cooldown helps you fall asleep and sleep deeply.
- **Food:** for most people, eating about **2 hours before bed** doesn't affect sleep. Closer to bedtime can increase reflux (heartburn) for some. If eating late, make it small, but don't go to bed overly hungry. Experiment to see what works for you.
- Avoid excessive fluids before bed to reduce nighttime waking.

## Caffeine and alcohol

- Caffeine doesn't reduce sleep need; it masks sleepiness. When it wears off, built-up **adenosine** causes a "caffeine crash", usually early afternoon.
- If you have trouble sleeping, consume caffeine **early in the day** and not within **8-10 hours of bedtime**.
- Some people sleep fine after afternoon caffeine, likely due to tolerance or metabolism differences, but it can still harm sleep structure, including REM duration.
- Alcohol is a sedative, **not a sleep aid**. A nightcap may speed sleep onset but impairs natural sleep structure and fragments sleep.

## Wind-down routine

- Andrew compares it to slowing a car before stopping: "deceleration" from the day's activity.
- Do relaxing things: meditation, music, podcasts, sleep stories, reading.
- Avoid stimulating things before bed: TV, news, strenuous exercise, social media.

## Trouble falling asleep: four tactics

- **Walk it out:** if sleep or getting back to sleep takes longer than about **20-25 minutes**, leave the bed and do something relaxing elsewhere. This prevents associating the bed with wakefulness. Return only when truly sleepy and don't fall asleep elsewhere.
- **Do nothing after a bad night:** avoid compensating by sleeping later, going to bed early, adding caffeine, or taking long or late naps. These disrupt circadian rhythms and make it harder to return to your usual schedule.
- **Mental walk:** visualize walking a familiar route to pull attention from ruminating thoughts. A cited study found it cut time to fall asleep significantly, outperforming counting sheep.
- **Limit naps:** keep them to **20-30 minutes or less**, no later than early to mid-afternoon.
- If protocols don't help, Andrew advises seeking professional help for possible **sleep apnea** or true **insomnia**.
