## Stress as a generic mobilization system, and why it matters

Andrew defines stress as a generic system that mobilizes other systems in the brain and body. It was not built for tigers specifically. It treats physical stressors (cold, famine, infection) and psychological ones (exams, worry, loss) the same way.
- The acute response runs through the sympathetic chain ganglia, a chain of neurons from the neck to about the navel. They release acetylcholine, and epinephrine (adrenaline) is then released at target organs.
- It is two-pronged: a yes for the heart, leg muscles and cognition, and a no for digestion, reproduction and salivation (hence the dry throat).
- It creates agitation that biases you to move or speak. Suppressed movement shows up as tremor.
- Telling yourself or someone else to "calm down" tends to worsen stress. He says the most useful tools go through the body's hardwired calming system (the parasympathetic nervous system), which exists in everyone and needs no neural plasticity.

## Real-time tool: the physiological sigh and exhale-emphasized breathing

Andrew calls the physiological sigh the fastest hardwired way he knows to lower stress in real time.
- **Mechanism:** Inhaling moves the diaphragm down, enlarges the heart and slows blood flow through it. The brain then speeds the heart up. Exhaling shrinks the heart, blood flows faster, and the parasympathetic system slows the heart.
- **Rules of thumb:** Longer or more vigorous inhales than exhales speed the heart. Longer or more vigorous exhales than inhales slow it.
- **The sigh:** A double inhale followed by a long exhale. The second inhale re-inflates the collapsed alveoli (lung sacs, "as big as a tennis court" in surface area). The long exhale then clears more carbon dioxide, which relaxes you quickly.
- **Dose:** One to three sigh cycles (double inhale, long exhale) bring stress down fast. Expect 20 to 30 seconds for heart rate to settle, and repeat a few times if needed. Don't expect an instant drop; too fast a drop is how vasovagal fainting happens.
- **Nose or mouth:** Ideally double inhale through the nose and exhale through the mouth, but any version works.
- **Sleep:** Ten to fifteen cycles can help some people fall asleep.
- **Other effects:** It engages the parafacial nucleus, which tends to relax the jaw and face. His lab and David Spiegel's lab at Stanford are studying breathing patterns and stress; he notes those studies are not finished.

## Short-term stress is useful: cyclic hyperventilation, cold, and immunity

Andrew stresses that acute stress is good for the immune system and cognition. Adrenaline mobilizes killer cells from the spleen, and short-term stress sharpens and narrows focus. He calls stress "the most powerful nootropic": deadlines and fear of failure drive performance (and explain procrastination).
- **Immune tool:** Deliberate cyclic hyperventilation (Wim Hof / Tummo style) or cold exposure (ice bath, cold shower) releases adrenaline.
- **Study he cites (PNAS):** People who did this breathing pattern and were then injected with E. coli endotoxin had essentially no symptoms (no fever, nausea or diarrhea).
- **His own protocol when run down:** About 25 to 30 breaths, exhale and hold about 15 seconds; repeat, with a final big inhale and hold until the urge to breathe. He says he has cleared it with his doctor.
- **Safety:** "Never ever ever do this anywhere near water" (shallow water blackout). Get doctor clearance. The breath holds should not be done by anyone with glaucoma or eye pressure concerns. Anxious people may feel anxious doing it.
- **The "let-down":** He notes people often get sick right after a long stretch of stress, when adrenaline and immune function crash. The skill is turning the stress response off when you're done.

## Medium-term stress: raising stress threshold with dilated gaze

For weeks-long stress (a hard semester or quarter), Andrew focuses on raising stress capacity by learning to keep the mind calm while the body is highly activated. He describes this as dissociating mental response from bodily state, not unifying mind and body.
- **Protocol:** Get heart rate up (hard bike or sprint to about 80 to 90% of max, an ice bath or cold shower, or cyclic breathing), with physician approval. Then calm the mind while the body is activated.
- **Dilated gaze:** Stress dilates the pupils and creates tunnel vision, tied to a brainstem alertness circuit. Deliberately dilating your gaze to see the whole visual panorama at once (without moving the head and eyes around) releases that circuit and calms the mind. He mentions he's "not blinking" only as a demonstration; blinking is fine.
- **Frequency:** Even about once a week, what once felt overwhelming becomes manageable. Related reading: he says limited research exists on this.
- He also points to NSDR (non-sleep deep rest) protocols, covered in earlier episodes, for replenishing capacity.

## Long-term stress: sleep, social connection, and what is bad

Andrew says chronic stress is genuinely bad (heart disease, hypertension, hippocampal and other brain effects, relapse risk in addiction).
- **Rule of thumb for acute vs chronic:** When you can no longer get good sleep, you have moved from acute to chronic stress. His own off nights run about three or four per month.
- **Basics:** Exercise, good sleep and real-time tools. Heart rate variability (breathing-driven sympathetic/parasympathetic interplay) is good; chronically high or low heart rate is not.
- **Social connection is the key lever:** It can be human, pets or anything you truly delight in. He stresses it takes effort and flexibility (eating on others' schedules, staying up a little later).
- **Chemistry:** Serotonin is released with trusted connection, delight and play. Oxytocin is released only in narrow contexts (post-orgasm, lactation, intense pair bonding), not casual hugs.
- **Tachykinin:** The "sinister" molecule that rises with prolonged isolation, causing fear, paranoia and impaired immunity. He keeps a Post-it above his desk reading "Tachykinin" to remind himself to stay connected.
- Writing down things you're grateful for appears to support the serotonin system.
- He cites Robert Sapolsky on humans interacting with unprecedented numbers of strangers at a distance.

## Supplements for stress (non-prescription), and cautions

Andrew says he uses these only during stressful periods, and tells people to check examine.com (its human effect matrix) and their doctor.
- **Ashwagandha:** Six studies show cortisol reductions of 14.5 to 27.9 percent in healthy but stressed adults, and five more show reduced stress, fatigue and cognitive impairment. It can lower total cholesterol (up to about 10%), which may or may not be desirable. He does not take it year-round.
- **L-theanine:** 100 to 200 mg, 30 to 60 minutes before sleep, can aid sleep onset and depth. It raises GABA, quiets the thinking/ruminating forebrain, and has studies showing reduced stress and anxiety. He may take some in the late afternoon during chronic stress.
- **Melatonin:** He doesn't recommend supplementing. Typical 1 to 3 mg doses are "outrageously high" versus natural levels. Concerns include effects on the reproductive axis and puberty (especially in kids), and chronic high use may suppress adrenal output.
- **Taurine:** He avoids taurine-containing energy drinks after it caused bursting of microvasculature in his sclera (eyes). He frames this as personal.
- **Adrenal burnout:** He says it doesn't exist; the adrenals hold enough adrenaline for "200 years of stress." Adrenal insufficiency syndrome is a real but different condition.
- He prefers Thorne for supplement quality control.

## A framework for emotions: matching internal state to demands

Andrew frames emotions (following Lisa Feldman Barrett, though he looks through a physiology lens) as context-dependent, and in part as whether your internal state of alertness or calm matches the demands on you.
- Being tired when you need to sleep feels good; being wired when you need to sleep feels bad. Being alert when working is good; being anxious or exhausted when you need to perform is bad.
- The autonomic nervous system is a seesaw from sleepy to alert to panic. Learning to move along it deliberately lets you lean into life.
- **Use case:** Before hard feedback or a tense conversation, lower the stress response a little so you can hear. Closing your eyes can help you focus on what is being said.
- He argues "be responsive, not reactive" only works if you have tools to modulate reactivity in real time, since you can't use the mind to control the mind when highly activated. He says constant self-observation ("mindfulness") can hinder performance for him.
