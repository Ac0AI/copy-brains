## Core principle: study to offset forgetting, not to "learn"

- Andrew argues that most of what people believe about the best way to study is wrong, and the best learning practices "are not intuitive."
- He says preferred learning styles (auditory vs visual, reading vs slides, group vs solo) mostly melt away in the research. What matters is whether a method accesses memory in a way that offsets forgetting.
- His reframe: don't think "study to retain," think "study to inoculate against forgetting."
- Most information the brain meets each day is discarded. Learning is about making the right things stick.
- He says he wishes he had known this in elementary school, high school and college.

## How learning works: neuroplasticity in two steps

- Neuroplasticity is the nervous system's ability to change with experience. It happens in three ways:
  - Strengthening existing synaptic connections.
  - Weakening existing connections.
  - Neurogenesis (new neurons), which is rare in adult humans and only a trivial share of learning. It occurs mainly in the olfactory bulb and the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus.
- Weakening or removing connections is not "forgetting" or "getting worse." Much motor-skill refinement from infancy to adulthood is removal of connections, and the remaining ones become more robust and reliable.
- Step 1 is alert, focused attention during exposure. Focus and attention cue the nervous system that something is important and must change.
- Step 2 is the actual rewiring, which happens during deep sleep and sleep-like states, especially REM sleep, which predominates in the latter half of the night.
- He describes a "first night effect": material is mostly consolidated in the first night's sleep after learning.
  - A poor first night doesn't doom the material forever.
  - But late-night caffeine and all-nighters work against learning.

## Getting alert and focused

- Sleep well the night before. He calls sleep "without question the best nootropic."
- Use a silent script such as "I need to learn this" to ramp up focus. Don't passively wait for the material to pull attention out of you.
- Effortful focus is a skill. Constantly pulling your mind back is meant to feel difficult.
  - The strain reflects release of neuromodulators like epinephrine.
  - He treats that strain as a cue to the nervous system that plasticity is needed.
  - If learning felt seamless, circuits wouldn't need to change.
- Behavioral tools to improve focus:
  - 5-10 minutes of daily mindfulness meditation, citing Wendy Suzuki's lab at NYU. He says it improves focus and memory and recall.
    - Sit or lie down, close your eyes, focus on your breath, and bring attention back each time it drifts.
    - Missing a day is no big deal. Timing doesn't matter, though late-night sessions disrupt sleep for some people.
  - An eyes-open version: stare at a visual target 1-3 ft away for 1-3 minutes (up to 10), blinking as needed, and keep bringing attention back.
- Supporting basics: good hydration and an amount of caffeine that makes you alert but not shaky.
- People with clinically diagnosed ADHD should talk to their doctor about prescription options. Great sleep matters especially for them.
- He recommends NSDR (non-sleep deep rest) or yoga nidra for 10-20 minutes to restore mental and physical vigor and enhance plasticity.
  - Use it in the morning, the afternoon, or in the night if you can't sleep.
  - It's especially useful if you haven't slept enough.

## Habits of the most effective students

- He cites a survey of close to 700 medical students (self-report, correlational) that identified about 10 habits of highly effective students.
- The top habits he highlights:
  - They schedule set times to study.
  - They remove distractions: phone away, and they tell friends and family they are unreachable.
  - They study alone.
  - They study about 3-4 hours a day, split into 2-3 sessions rather than one block, at least 5 days a week.
  - They teach other students the material.
- On teaching peers, he says to ignore the worry that you're giving competitors a free advantage. Teaching tests and builds your own mastery.
  - He cites the lab saying "watch one, do one, teach one," for anything that is safe to do that way.
- Scheduling:
  - Study at regular times if you can.
  - Allow about 2-3 days for the brain to entrain to a consistent study schedule.
  - Attention is limited but renewable. Adenosine builds the longer you're awake, which is why sleep restores focus.
- The best students also had a long-term, abstract, aspirational motivation, such as how success would change their lives and families.
  - He says that pull helps when you don't love the material.
- He says material you struggle with is often what you later remember most fondly. Effortful study is the most effective study.

## Testing is the best way to study

- The central claim: testing is "one of the best ways to build our knowledge, to retain our knowledge, and again to offset forgetting."
  - It is not just for evaluating what you know.
- A 1917 study had children read biographies.
  - One group reread them repeatedly.
  - Another group read them once and then recalled them from memory.
  - The recall group vastly outperformed the rereading group, in both the amount and the accuracy of what they remembered.
- A more modern set of experiments compared four groups:
  - Study-study-study-study.
  - Study-study-study-test.
  - Study-test-test-test.
  - Each group was then given a final test days, weeks or a year later.
  - Performance on the final test rose with the number of earlier tests taken.
- The confidence trap:
  - The study-four-times group felt most confident.
  - The study-once, test-three-times group felt least confident.
  - The actual results were the reverse.
  - Re-exposure builds a feeling of familiarity, not mastery.
- You can get quiz answers wrong and still benefit, as long as you see the correct answers afterward. Even scoring 40-50% or less still beats rereading.
- Why it works: getting things wrong, noticing the gap and correcting it cues the nervous system to lock in what you have right and fix what you have wrong. That differs from passive re-exposure.
- He uses the distinction between familiarity (recognition) and recollection (being able to recall and use the material).
  - He points to a review in the journal Hippocampus on this.
- His own example: in a hard undergraduate neuroanatomy lab course, he lay on his bed with his eyes closed and mentally "flew through" the nervous system.
  - He entered through the ear, the eye and the dorsal brain surface, and traced tracts.
  - When he hit a gap he looked it up in the textbook, then resumed.
  - He says this is why he still has strong mastery of neuroanatomy.
  - Today, after reading a paper, he often takes a walk and mentally rehearses its key points and graphs.
- Self-testing can take whatever form suits you best. The principle is recalling the material and finding your gaps.

## When and how to test yourself

- Test soon after first exposure, the same day or the next day. This beats waiting and then testing right before the exam.
  - The best group: expose, test soon after, then a long delay, then the final test.
  - The middle group: expose, delay, test, delay, test.
  - The worst group: expose, long delay, then two tests back to back.
- A prerequisite to testing is a first exposure to the material.
- One self-test on new material roughly halves forgetting.
  - He says it improves retention by about 50%.
  - He cites studies across music, math, language and motor learning, with benefits measured out to a year later.
- Don't walk out of class and straight onto your phone. Take a few seconds to a few minutes right after exposure to test yourself on what you just heard.
  - Finding that you're confused is useful. It tells you what to review.
- Format:
  - Open-ended, short-answer or long-answer questions with minimal prompts are the best, because they require recall.
  - Multiple choice is weaker because it lets you rely on recognition.
  - The exception is multiple-choice questions with plausible trick answers, as on the GRE, LSAT or MCAT.
- Testing for learning should be separate from grading.
  - He notes students dislike pop quizzes and penalize teachers for them in evaluations.
  - The better approach is to announce at the outset that regular quizzes are part of the method, or to test yourself every day.
- He points to the literature on testing: "Testing Enhances Learning: A Review of the Literature" and "Test-Enhanced Learning."

## Gap effects, emotion and interleaving

- Gap effects:
  - Take pauses of about 5-10 seconds, up to 30 seconds, during learning.
  - During those pauses the hippocampus replays the material at roughly 20-30 times the normal rate, as it does in REM sleep.
  - Use them in learning of any kind, including music and motor skills.
- Emotion and neuromodulators:
  - Emotionally charged material is remembered more durably, because epinephrine and norepinephrine (and others) wire experiences into circuits.
  - Extreme stress produces one-trial learning, which is the basis of PTSD.
  - With a dull teacher or source, deliberately raise your own internal intensity and interest.
  - He cites James McGaugh's review of a medieval practice of throwing people into cold water after teaching them, to boost adrenaline and consolidate memory.
  - He doesn't say you must do cold exposure after learning, but a cold shower or plunge while mentally testing yourself is an option. He points to his deliberate cold exposure newsletter for best practices.
  - Caffeine modestly raises epinephrine and probably helps a little.
- Interleaving:
  - Mix in related but undemanding anecdotes or bits of information, which act as more than a pause.
  - They give the hippocampus more repetitions.
  - They let the brain link new knowledge with existing or distantly related knowledge.
  - He doesn't mean mixing two hard subjects like math and history in one session, which would be overwhelming.

## Levels of skill

- Andrew distinguishes four levels:
  - Unskilled: limited understanding or ability to use information.
  - Skilled: can recognize and use information in basic or advanced ways.
  - Mastery: close to the full depth of knowledge, usable flexibly.
  - Virtuosity: mastery so deep that you can invite uncertainty and spontaneity, as great musicians and athletes do.
- He says most people would be content with skill and mastery.
