## Core principle: bookend the day with cortisol

Andrew's single highest-leverage health rule: "Make the first hour of your day high cortisol and the last hour of your day low cortisol."

- High morning cortisol sets up mood, focus and alertness all day, buffers stress, and keeps nighttime cortisol low.
- If morning cortisol isn't spiked enough in the first 1-3 hours, the result is a "high flat cortisol curve": less stress resilience, brain fog, poorer learning.
- Morning spikes: bright light (sunlight, or 10,000 lux artificial light, especially in the first 30 minutes after waking), hydration, caffeine if you like it, exercise in the first 3 hours if possible. Eating also spikes cortisol, since anything proarousal does. He personally fasts for the first 3 hours.
- Evening reversal: dim lights (especially overhead), dim screens, red or candlelight, limit caffeine, exercise in the final hour only if it's the only time available.
- Chain: high morning cortisol, low night cortisol, high night melatonin, great sleep, better days.
- Understanding the principle lets you improvise when you miss a day or are on a plane: sunlight and exercise both work, and a day or two missed is fine.

## Top-down vs. bottom-up control and training willpower

- Bottom-up influences are impulses from deep brain structures (eat the cookie, keep scrolling). Top-down is prefrontal cortex suppressing impulse. Being effective means building the top-down circuits.
- Poor sleep cripples this circuit so bottom-up wins; he says people become "slightly psychotic" after 3-4 bad nights.
- Two ways to build it: big hard efforts (ass to chair writing, the workout you don't want to do) and small daily friction ("micro sucks"), such as picking up trash or making your bed. Andy Stumpf's rule: when choosing between easier and harder, do the harder.
- Andrew's view: do both. The small reps are like "grease the groove" pull-ups; the circuit gets trained, not the specific task. He finds this makes overcoming friction elsewhere easier.
- Lean into curiosity and the fear point: learn what you avoid for fear of poor performance.

## Focus, brain fog and learning protocol

- Brain fog is an inability to focus. Even severe ADHD people focus on things they like, so the catecholamine systems (dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine) work but the access threshold is too high.
- A Washington University study found Ritalin's attention benefit roughly equal to a great night's sleep. Stimulants can reduce sleep and stunt growth. Lower doses plus better sleep may be an alternative.
- Learning steps:
  1. Be alert (sleep, exercise).
  2. Exercise: 45-75 minutes of hard training, not to exhaustion, raises catecholamines for 4-6 hours afterward.
  3. Focus on making errors through genuine attempts. The gap between current and target performance is the signal for change, and the agitation reflects epinephrine and norepinephrine tagging synapses.
  4. Sleep (or a day or two) lets synapses rearrange.
  5. Self-test mentally (not visualization): ask what you remember, look it up when stuck. Retesting fights forgetting.
- Do the hard version: if crosswords are easy they won't keep the brain young; play go if you like chess. Exercise is necessary but not sufficient to make you smarter.
- Optimal learning state is "alert and calm", like having your hands on top in the hot hands game: ready anticipation. Too much stress causes false positives. Attention tires after about 90 minutes.

## Stress management and breathing

- Fastest way to calm down: the physiological sigh. Two big inhales, then a long deliberate exhale to empty lungs. It slows heart rate via respiratory sinus arrhythmia (vagus pathway to the sinoatrial node).
- Ten deliberate exhales through the day can raise HRV noticeably that night.
- Exhale-emphasized breathing calms; inhale-emphasized breathing alerts.
- Resonance breathing: he endorses the mhealth lamp (vibrating stone, paced to your personal resonance rate). Chris noted it works best at over 12 minutes.
- Non-sleep deep rest (NSDR): long exhales, eyes closed, shifting from thinking to pure sensation. It recovers some lost sleep, lowers cortisol, aids learning and helps sleep at night.
- Morning sunlight is the other core stress protocol.

## Being present

- A wandering mind is an unhappy mind (Harvard study). Presence means present-tethered awareness.
- Method: ask trivial questions about your immediate physical environment (how many cans on the table, what does it smell like, how loud are the kids). This moves the brain from past/future thinking to perception.
- He argues interoception (focusing on your breath or heartbeat) is a weaker route to presence.
- Step two: from that awareness, decide what you want to do, say or think next. Thoughts "seed" each other, so choosing the seed matters.
- Elevated default mode network activity is associated with depression.

## Getting over trauma: four steps

1. Acknowledge the bad thing, no suppression.
2. Feel the feelings completely, in a safe setting.
3. Recall accurate detail with less emotional load, uncoupling emotion from memory.
4. Transmute it into something of beauty or contribution.

- Pennebaker writing: 15 minutes a day for 4 days about the event; he says this moves people through steps 2-3 even if the writing is discarded.
- Don't stay at step 2 only.
- He describes combining MDMA and psilocybin in a legal clinical setting as helpful, while noting little data exists. No endorsement beyond his own experience.

## Exercise and mobility routine

- Weekly cardio: one long slow session of 45-60 minutes (upper zone 2/zone 3, a jog or weighted ruck); one midweek 25-30 minute run or swim in zone 4; one all-out session, e.g. Airdyne bike 20 seconds on, 10 off, 8 rounds, reaching 85-90% of max HR (220 minus age, estimated from pulse). Mile time check every 2-3 months.
- Resistance: 3 weight days per week, one leg day, one torso/push day, one arms/calves/neck day.
- Core and back: hanging knee raises progressing to L-sits, 100 back hyperextensions a week (progressing to single leg), standing small stabilizer work (face pulls, glute medius). He argues the small movements keep big lifts going into your 50s.
- Daily mobility minimum: knee behind glutes (hip flexor stretch, e.g. 10 seconds, 3 times a side), deep squat with one elbow inside the knee while looking up at the ceiling, reverse tabletop for shoulder extension, and the founder's pose. Use full range of motion and hold tension in the stretched position.
- Why: strength and endurance "so I can sprint for the plane."

## Caffeine, nicotine, microplastics and peptides

- Nicotine: alert and calm, useful for learning, but raises blood pressure and is highly addictive. 3-6 mg pouches multiple times daily just rescue withdrawal; use occasionally (about twice a week maximum) if as a performance aid.
- Microplastics: glass over plastic; sweating (sauna, heat) helps; sulforaphane might bind some. He noted a second analysis suggesting the "credit card of plastic in the brain" figure was overestimated because of measurement problems.
- His peptide experience, anecdotal and unproven: BPC-157 (two injections, 100-200 micrograms) resolved shoulder pain; pinealon every third or fourth night (about a tenth of an IU) raised REM to roughly 2.5-3 hours but caused next-day sleep inertia. Growth hormone secretagogues (tesamorelin, ipamorelin, CJC) nuked his REM and spiked his PSA. He flags the placebo possibility.
- Sleep tip: the "wacky eye movement" trick to fall back asleep, which confuses body awareness.
