## Two time-of-day frameworks for habit work: the Phase 1/2/3 day structure

Andrew's habit program starts from the idea that changing habits requires overcoming "limbic friction" (the energy needed to push past anxiety, procrastination and/or fatigue). He says to use the natural rhythms of the brain and body so that engaging in or maintaining a habit is more likely. He splits each 24-hour day-night cycle into three phases counted from the time you wake up. He says the Phase framework and the alternating-blocks framework are aligned with biology and neurochemistry and "work best when combined".

- Phase 1: 0-8 hours after waking
- Phase 2: 9-15 hours after waking
- Phase 3: 16-24 hours after waking

## Phase 1 (0-8 hours after waking): schedule effortful, focus-heavy habits

- Brain and body are more action and focus oriented in Phase 1, due to elevated dopamine, adrenaline and cortisol levels. This makes it easier to overcome limbic friction.
- Caveat: people are also more prone to distraction and reflexive multitasking at this time. He says "Don't succumb to that."
- Set 1-4 habits for completion in Phase 1. These should be the habits that require energy and focus (for example exercise, writing, studying, focused work).
- Use a window for completion rather than a precise start and stop time. Example: 45 minutes of focused reading or work somewhere in Phase 1. He suggests exercising, writing or studying "after waking but before noon", so it can happen at 8 a.m., 10 a.m. or 11 a.m. but definitely in Phase 1.
- Doing it at the same time each day is fine if you can, but a broader window helps with busy lives.
- He calls Phase 1 terrific for habits where precise execution is needed.

## Phase 2 (9-15 hours after waking): looser, creative and low-friction habits

- Naturally higher serotonin and lower adrenaline make this the time for habits that don't require overcoming much limbic friction.
- Good fits are behaviors and thinking that can be completed with less focus, especially creative exploration:
  - writing fiction or rough drafts
  - writing music
  - play of any kind
  - experimentation
  - lower-focus physical pursuits such as Zone 2 cardio
- Best for looser things: trying a new recipe, brainstorming, exploring a new approach to some aspect of work, a physical pursuit, relationships or learning.
- He quotes from Episode 53: "A lot of habit formation has to do with being in the right state of mind and being able to control your body and mind."

## Phase 3 (16-24 hours after waking): reset limbic friction through rest and sleep

- Phase 3 is when the ability to overcome limbic friction is reset, by resting and sleeping.
- In short, to master it:
  - Avoid bright lights.
  - Sleep in a cool, dark room.
  - Explore supplementation if needed, but not melatonin.
- He points to his Toolkit for Sleep (Neural Network Newsletter #3) and Episode 2, "Master Your Sleep & Be More Alert When Awake", for details.

## Forming habits: the 21-day block with 4-6 of 6 habits per day

- Based on his read of the literature, Andrew suggests picking six new habits to incorporate per day for 21 days.
- Write them down, aim to complete four to six of the six each day, and mark them off on a calendar.
- Do not fret about completing only four out of six on a given day.
- "Never compensate by doing more than six per day." He says a no-compensation system is best.
- Merge this with the Phase 1, 2, 3 structure to be in the best position to succeed.

## Testing whether habits stuck: alternating 21-day blocks

- To know if you are succeeding, spend the next 21 days tracking your behavior without incorporating any new habits.
- The cycle is 21 days of habit formation, then 21 days of testing to see which habits actually became habits.
- Then return to 21 days of habit formation, using the progress in the previous block to decide whether to add more to the four to six per day list.
- How to judge whether a habit has formed: the strength of a habit is dictated by (1) how much limbic friction you need to overcome to perform the behavior, and (2) how much context-dependence there is.
- Context-dependence means whether you perform the habit no matter what, or only when calm, rested, in the presence of others, caffeinated, and so on. A strong habit happens regardless of context.

## Breaking habits: awareness, then an immediate positive behavior

- Andrew calls the method "simple (alas, not always easy)".
- Step 1: bring conscious awareness to the fact that you participated in the habit you are trying to break.
- Step 2: when you realize you did it, engage in a positive behavior immediately afterward.
- The specific behavior matters less than the fact that it comes immediately after the habit and is not a negative behavior.
- Examples he gives: ten jumping jacks or pushups, as do-anywhere positive behaviors. They count as positive because they are good for you, not because you necessarily enjoy them.
- He cites "task bracketing" and review articles in peer-reviewed journals as the science behind these approaches (listed in the caption of Episode 53, "The Science of Making & Breaking Habits").
