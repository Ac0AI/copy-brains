## Hydration baseline and timing

- Water makes up roughly 60-80% of human cells and tissues. It supports cellular function, acts as a solvent, protein structure and chemical reactions, and can act as an antioxidant against free radicals.
- Even mild dehydration hurts physical performance, alertness and focus, and can cause "brain fog."
- Andrew's baseline: about 8 oz (237 mL) of fluid per hour for the first 10 hours of the day, roughly 80 oz total. It is an average, not a schedule. Example: 16 oz on waking, 8 oz a few hours later, 32 oz later, and so on.
- Why the first 10 hours: the circadian clock regulates kidney and gut cells via the hormone vasopressin. In the first ~10 hours awake the kidney filters fluid efficiently, then output drops overnight.
- He says drinking more by day means more bathroom trips, "but that is not a bad thing." Most people simply don't drink enough during waking hours.

## Reducing nighttime urination

Waking 1-2 times a night to urinate is not a sign of pathology. If it happens more often, Andrew suggests:
1. Hydrate sufficiently during the day.
2. Cut fluid intake in the evening and in the 2-3 hours before sleep.
3. If you must drink at night, sip slowly. Both the rate and the volume of intake affect the urge to urinate.

## Exercise, heat, sauna and caffeine

- **Galpin Equation** (additional fluid during exercise, an average, not something to obsess over):
  - Body weight in lbs ÷ 30 = ounces to drink every 15-20 minutes.
  - Body weight in kg × 2 = mL to drink every 15-20 minutes.
- In hot temperatures or heavy sweating, increase those amounts by an additional 50-100%.
- Sauna: drink an extra 8-16 oz for every 20-30 minutes.
- Caffeine is a diuretic. Offset it at 2:1: an 8 oz caffeinated coffee calls for 16 oz of water, ideally with low- or no-sugar electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium) such as LMNT, or simply a pinch of salt.
- Dry environments also raise needs.
- Thirst is a useful signal, but "your perception of thirst lags behind the body's true hydration status." Not being thirsty doesn't mean you don't need fluid, so plan hydration ahead in extreme weather or high-intensity activity.

## Tap water contaminants and how to check yours

- Standard municipal filtration at scale leaves contaminants that can harm health at high concentrations:
  - **Disinfection byproducts (DBPs)**, left over from municipal disinfection. Some are endocrine disruptors that can impair male and female fertility.
  - **Fluoride**: at ≥0.5 mg/L it can disrupt thyroid function, per the study he cites.
  - **Lead** from pipes into the home or building. Rarer in developed countries but still present in places.
- To find out what is in your water: search your zip code for a local water quality report, or contact the EPA Safe Drinking Water Hotline. CDC has further resources.
- In the U.S. these reports should be free from the city; you shouldn't pay.
- Optional testing: home test kits cost about $25; independent lab analysis about $100+.

## Filtering tap water by budget

Andrew says it is healthier for most people to filter tap water. Pitcher filters like Brita catch some disinfection byproducts but not smaller particles, notably fluoride.
- Under $100: pitcher filter that includes fluoride filtration (e.g., ClearlyFiltered Pitcher).
- About $400: countertop filter for larger volumes (e.g., Berkey).
- $800+: whole-house filter (e.g., Aquasana).
- Zero cost: let 1-5 gallons sit uncapped at room temperature for about a day. Sediment settles, so pour off the top two-thirds to drink.
- Don't boil tap water to filter it: high heat can make contaminants worse.
- He states he has no affiliation with or compensation from these brands.

## Water types and minerals

- Water with higher magnesium (ideally 8.3-19.4 mg/L) is more alkaline and absorbs better. He stresses this "has nothing to do with adjusting the pH of your body" and calls brand claims to that effect "pure falsehood," since tissue pH is tightly regulated.
- **Hard water** (more magnesium and calcium): higher pH. Some evidence that more alkaline water can reduce inflammation and blood pressure and lower cardiovascular risk.
- **Distilled / double-distilled**: strips calcium and magnesium. Don't drink it regularly because of those ions' health benefits.
- **Reverse osmosis**: removes many contaminants and is safe to drink, but is expensive and can lack key minerals.
- **Hydrogen-enriched, electrolyzed-reduced, deuterium-depleted**: higher pH than tap. Not studied extensively, but one study showed reduced inflammation and improved health metrics, likely from better cell hydration. A DIY version: dissolve a molecular hydrogen tablet (contains a specific form of magnesium) in a glass of water for 5-15 minutes. If your tap water is already high in magnesium, it is already sufficiently alkaline.
- **Structured water**: no scientific evidence of health benefits currently. Some researchers theorize water is structured within cells with added benefits. He calls this controversial with a growing body of science.
