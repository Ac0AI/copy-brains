## Muscle as a nervous system output: neural control and energy

- Andrew frames muscle around the nervous system: "the whole reason why you have a brain is so that you can move." Muscle matters for movement, metabolism, posture (breathing, even alertness) and aesthetics.
- He says muscle size and quality raise metabolism. The ability to jump and to get up off the floor quickly is one of the most predictive markers of biological aging.
- Control runs through three nodes:
  - Upper motor neurons in the motor cortex drive deliberate movement.
  - Lower motor neurons in the spinal cord send axons to muscle and release acetylcholine, the only way muscle contracts.
  - Central pattern generators run reflexive, rhythmic movement such as walking.
- Flexors and extensors are reciprocally inhibited (bicep/tricep, hamstring/quadriceps, abs/lower back). Activating one inhibits its antagonist.
- He argues hypertrophy is driven by the nerve-to-muscle connection, not by something that happens only inside the muscle.

## Lactate and "the burn": a hormonal signal for heart, liver and brain

- Glucose or glycogen breaks down to pyruvate, which yields a little ATP. With oxygen, pyruvate goes to the mitochondria and yields 28 to 30 ATP. Muscle is the most metabolically expensive tissue after brain.
- Without enough oxygen in the muscle, pyruvate picks up hydrogen and becomes lactate. Andrew stresses humans make lactate, not lactic acid, and that the common belief that lactate causes the burn is backwards.
- Lactate has three roles:
  - It buffers acidity, so it suppresses the burn rather than causing it.
  - It is a fuel that lets contraction continue.
  - It is a hormonal signal to the heart, liver and brain, with positive effects. In the brain it acts partly through astrocytes.
- Protocol: take about 10% of your effort in a workout (any modality: weights, running, cycling, swimming) to the point of the burn, if you can do it safely. Don't spend the whole session there; it would impair recovery.
- Keep breathing through the burn. Holding your breath makes it feel worse and blunts lactate's buffering and fuel role.
- He says exercise-driven neurogenesis in humans has little evidence (the data are strong in mice). The brain benefit more likely comes from blood-borne signals such as IGF-1 and lactate, which improve connections between neurons.

## Henneman size principle, strength vs. hypertrophy, and the mind-muscle connection

- Motor units are recruited from low to high threshold, which conserves energy. More units are recruited as effort continues.
- Andrew says it is a misreading that you need the heaviest weights to reach high-threshold units. Loads from 30% to 80% of one-rep max (1RM) can drive both strength and hypertrophy when sets are taken close to failure.
- Hypertrophy happens when stress, tension and/or damage trigger protein synthesis that thickens myosin. His analogy: the balloons on a bouquet of strings get bigger.
- Strength means distributing work across many muscles and the whole nervous system, moving progressively greater loads. Hypertrophy means isolating a muscle and making it do the work, with hard, almost cramping local contractions.
- Self-test: try to contract a muscle hard with no weight (for example the calf, or the lat by pulling the elbow behind the body). If you can nearly cramp it, you have a high potential to grow and strengthen it, and you need fewer sets. Poor control means more sets.
- For a pure strength or performance goal, avoid isolating single muscles.

## Training volume, intensity, rest and session design

- Biasing: 75% to 90% of 1RM biases toward strength. About 30% to 50% with many more reps biases toward hypertrophy and muscle endurance. In between, he says it doesn't matter much.
- Take sets to failure or near it, but only about 10% of sets or workouts should go to true failure. Most sets stop short of failure, so you can do more volume without fatiguing the nervous system.
- Weekly sets per muscle (they can be split across sessions):
  - About 5 hard sets per week maintains muscle.
  - 5 to 15 sets per week suits most untrained and moderately trained people (he cites a 2 to 20 range for beginners).
  - Experienced lifters can benefit from up to about 25 to 30 sets per week, but some people who generate a lot of force respond to as few as 4 to 8.
- Keep sessions to 45 to 60 minutes; beyond roughly 60 minutes cortisol and inflammatory pathways rise.
- Rep speed for hypertrophy and strength: anywhere from 0.5 to 8 seconds per rep appears to work.
- Rest between sets for strength and hypertrophy: 2 to 6 minutes can work. Walking or standing still helps clear lactate.
- Pre-exhaustion: doing an isolation exercise first (for example leg extensions before squats) makes the compound lift target that muscle more, at the cost of weight lifted and possibly form.
- Flexing the target muscle hard for about 30 seconds between sets can improve hypertrophy and the nerve-to-muscle connection. It lowers performance on the next set, so skip it when the goal is strength or moving weight.
- Explosiveness and speed: use roughly 60% to 75% of 1RM, move the weight as fast as safely possible, and stop before failure (as sets near failure the bar slows). Gains here are mostly neural.
- Andrew suggests periodizing training over 1 to 6 months and adjusting, since neuromuscular gains are fastest early on.
- Cooling the palms between sets (palmer cooling) lets you hold reps and add sets at a given weight.

## Testosterone-boosting resistance protocol (Duncan French)

- Per Andrew, work by Duncan French (UFC Performance Center) found a clear testosterone rise with 6 sets of 10 reps, 120 seconds rest, on big compound movements (squats, deadlifts, chin-ups).
- Lighten the weight between sets if needed.
- 10 sets of 10 did not raise testosterone and tilted toward catabolic, cortisol-like effects.
- Do it as a stand-alone session, at most twice per week.
- Hypertrophy or explosive training under 60 minutes also raises testosterone. Sessions over about 75 minutes at high intensity tend to lower testosterone and raise cortisol.

## Measuring recovery, and what blunts adaptation

- Andrew says the adaptation happens during recovery, not training, and gives three morning checks of nervous-system recovery:
  - HRV (needs a device; higher variability is good).
  - Grip strength: squeeze a hand tool or a floor scale and compare with your rested baseline. A 10% to 20% drop is a warning.
  - CO2 tolerance test:
    - Do 4 deep nose inhales with full exhales.
    - On the 5th, inhale as deeply as you can through the nose and fill the belly.
    - Exhale through the mouth as slowly as possible (like through a tiny straw) and time it until you truly can't exhale more.
- CO2 exhale-time zones:
  - 20 to 25 seconds or less: probably not recovered.
  - About 30 to 60 seconds: green zone.
  - 65 to 120 seconds: nervous system recovered.
  - A drop of 15% to 20% from your own norm means you are veering toward not recovering.
- To start recovery, finish training with about 5 minutes of deliberate parasympathetic engagement, such as non-sleep deep rest (NSDR) or 10 physiological sighs (double inhale through the nose, long exhale). Some athletes also use physiological sighs between sets to refocus.
- Cold exposure (whole-body or neck-down) within about 4 hours after resistance training lowers inflammation and soreness but can interfere with pathways like mTOR that drive repair and growth. It is fine if the goal is quick recovery or endurance.
- Antihistamines and NSAIDs can blunt gains from endurance and resistance training, so be cautious within 4 hours before or after exercise. NSAIDs also mask pain, which is a useful warning signal.
- The aim is inflammation during and just after training as the stimulus, then lower inflammation later.
- Baseline anti-inflammatory trio per Galpin: above 1,000 mg EPA per day (omega-3), vitamin D, and magnesium malate (for delayed onset muscle soreness).
- Soreness isn't required for gains. If a muscle is still sore, Andrew's takeaway is that it isn't recovered.

## Supplements, protein and leucine, and training around cognitive work

- Electrolytes: sodium, potassium and magnesium are essential for neurons to fire and for nerve-to-muscle communication. Needs rise with sweat, caffeine, heat and diuretics. Water alone won't do it.
- Creatine:
  - He cites examine.com for the supporting studies.
  - Dose, by body weight: about 5 g/day around 180 lb, 10 to 15 g if 220 to 230 lb, and 3 to 5 g (or 1 to 3 g) if lighter.
  - Loading isn't required.
  - Benefits include more power output, less fatigue, more cell hydration and possible cognitive benefit.
  - It can raise DHT, which may worsen hair loss in some men.
- Beta-alanine: 2 to 5 g per day, for efforts of about 60 to 240 seconds (sets of 8 to 15 reps, rowing, intervals). Andrew hasn't tried it himself.
- Arginine and citrulline: may help long-duration endurance via vasodilation, but can trigger cold sore (HSV-1) outbreaks.
- Protein: aim for 700 to 3,000 mg leucine per meal, preferably from whole foods. Animal proteins are denser in essential amino acids per calorie; vegans may want to supplement leucine. Two to four meals per day is enough, not six or seven.
- Hard exercise (roughly 30 to 60 minutes near failure or with intervals) can reduce brain oxygenation afterwards, so manage intensity and duration if you need to think that day.
- Train at a consistent time. Your body then anticipates focused effort at that time, so on non-training days that same clock time may be good for deep cognitive work.
- Training time of day (morning vs. afternoon) doesn't matter much for strength or hypertrophy. The afternoon may be slightly better for performance.
