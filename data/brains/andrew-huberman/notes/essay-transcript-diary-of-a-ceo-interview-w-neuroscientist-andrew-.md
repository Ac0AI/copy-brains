## Daily cortisol curve: why a high morning peak matters

- Andrew says the healthy cortisol curve peaks in the first 60 minutes after waking (the cortisol awakening response). It then falls to a low at night.
- He says a high morning peak is what gives elevated mood, focus, attention and ability to learn. It also sets up low cortisol in the afternoon and at night, which sleep requires: "this peak sets up this trough."
- A flat or low morning cortisol curve is linked to shorter lifespan, poorer recovery from illness, and longer stress bouts because stress rides on a higher baseline. It also impairs melatonin's function and disrupts sleep.
- He argues most people's morning cortisol is too low and their afternoon and night cortisol is too high. Cushing syndrome (massive cortisol overproduction) is very rare.
- He defines cortisol as a hormone "released during stress," not a stress hormone. Its job is to mobilize energy (glucose and other fuels). He predicts the "cortisol is bad" idea will be closed out soon.
- Melatonin is the sleepiness hormone. Adenosine builds with time awake and thought, which makes you sleepy.
- The first protocols are meant to build the morning peak. Everything done early in the day should be done less in the evening.

## Morning protocols: hydrate, sunlight, exercise

**Hydrate**
- Drink 16-32 oz (about 16-18 oz) of clean water on waking. Electrolytes are optional.
- He says this helps boost morning cortisol, via a vagus nerve pathway that wakes the brain after the bladder empties and you hydrate.
- The daily target is about 80 oz of water.

**Sunlight or 10,000 lux light**
- Get bright light in the eyes in the first 30-60 minutes of the day.
- Low-sun light has the optimal wavelengths for melanopsin ganglion cells, which signal the circadian clock (suprachiasmatic nucleus) that the day has begun.
- After about an hour awake, the clock can trigger an extra pathway through the spinal column that makes the adrenals release more cortisol.
- An overcast day still works. He suggests going out on a balcony or rolling down a car window. Don't do it through a window or windshield.
- If there is no daylight, use a 10,000 lux artificial light. These lights are shown to offset seasonal affective disorder symptoms.
- If you miss a day, get outside twice as long the next day (the effect is additive).
- He cites UK Biobank data: you want bright days and dark nights, and the effects are additive. Bright nights raise the risk of dying early.
- Low-angle sun has much lower UV because of Rayleigh scattering, so the sunburn and eye-damage risk is lower.
- Phone screens are not bright enough to trigger the morning cortisol rise, but they can raise cortisol at night.

**Exercise early**
- Do cardio or resistance training in the first 3-4 hours of the day if possible. Afternoon is fine too.
- Exercising at a consistent time entrains the clock, so you wake more willing to exercise.
- Deliberate cold exposure raises adrenaline, norepinephrine and dopamine hugely but cortisol only trivially, in women and men. Exercise is the cortisol stressor you want.
- If you exercise later, bring cortisol down with long-exhale breathing and a hot shower. The shower warms the surface, so the brain's thermostat cools the core, which helps sleep.

## Light on skin, long-wavelength light and evening light

- Glen Jeffery's lab (University College London) shone red light on people's backs during a glucose tolerance test. Peak glucose fell by more than 25% and the rise was slower. It works on bare skin or through a t-shirt.
- Long-wavelength light penetrates up to about 8 cm into the body and improves mitochondrial function. Cellular melatonin acts as an antioxidant and is liberated by long-wavelength light.
- In Jeffery's work, people over 40 who looked at long-wavelength light for 1-3 minutes a couple of times a week saw vision improvements, but only when done in the first 3 hours of the day.
- He says no red-light device is needed. Sunlight has the full spectrum, and he says to get sun on as much skin as is socially appropriate 2-3 times per week, ideally when the UV index is low (early or late in the day).
- UVB exposure raises testosterone and estrogen and supports libido, but avoid sunburn.
- Greenery reflects infrared light, so being outside among plants adds long-wavelength light.
- Take off a brimmed hat so sun reaches the face.
- He says indoor LED lighting may damage mitochondria, per Jeffery's lab and others. He cites a Swedish study in which more sunlight went with longer life.
- **Evening light ("Netflix inoculation"):** get some daylight in your eyes, without sunglasses, in the last third of the day. This buffers the eye's cells against artificial light at night. You can then use screens at 9 or 10 pm with less melatonin and glucose disruption.
- This does not buffer adolescents against screen light at night.
- The main harm of screens at night is a cortisol bump, plus blood glucose effects and a lower next-morning cortisol peak. Falling asleep fine is not the full test.
- He includes tools for night shift workers and jet lag in the book. He argues anyone on devices after 6 pm counts as a "night shift worker" in these studies.

## Nutrition and cortisol

- He says to eat enough calories, neither too many nor too few.
- Choose proteins with a high "complete-protein-to-calorie ratio," a concept he coined. For 30 g of complete protein, beans and rice cost about 4-5 times the calories of chicken breast. Good options: eggs, lean meat, fish.
- Eat vegetables and fruit for fiber and micronutrients. He notes some people's autoimmune issues resolve when they cut starches or vegetables, and that too much fiber can be pro-inflammatory for some.
- Low-sugar fermented foods (sauerkraut, kimchi, beet kvass) lower the "inflammatome," citing Justin Sonnenburg's Stanford work.
- Use olive oil and small amounts of butter. He calls the seed oil debate poorly done science (studies compare mismatched diets).
- Starches fuel muscle, glycogen and brain. People overdo them mostly by pairing starches with lots of fat or sugar (for example, sourdough with equal butter).
- Foods that raise glucose lower cortisol, so starches help you fall and stay asleep. People who eat too few starches often have sleep issues. He suggests a bowl of rice or pasta in the last meal.
- Don't eat too close to bedtime, and don't go to bed with gnawing hunger.
- Low starch intake can show up as shakiness, and buffering cortisol with glucose lessens it.

## Stress control: physiological sigh, cold exposure and resetting

**Physiological sigh**
- Do a double inhale through the nose followed by a long exhale. Repeat 1-3 times to calm in real time.
- In a study from his lab (Melis Yilmaz Balban, published in Cell Reports Medicine), about 100 subjects were split into four groups for 5 minutes a day: meditation, box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation, and physiological sighs. All groups benefited, but sighing benefited most.
- Mechanism: the double inhale re-inflates collapsed lung sacs (alveoli), which maximizes CO2 offload on the exhale. Long exhales slow the heart via respiratory sinus arrhythmia (the calming arm of the vagus nerve).
- He says to use it when waiting for a diagnosis, before going on stage, or when you feel too alert for any reason.
- To raise heart rate variability (HRV): do deliberate long exhales through the day, plus VO2 max, max heart rate and HIIT training.
- If you wake at 2-3 am and ruminate, do 10 long deliberate exhales.

**Cold exposure and stress tolerance**
- Cold plunging trains the ability to stay calm while flooded with adrenaline and norepinephrine. He says that carries over to other stressors.
- Doing it safely means not too cold and no hyperventilating beforehand.
- For the first 20-30 seconds of any stressful event, prefrontal cortex activity is suppressed. His advice: don't react or send the text, wait until it comes back online.
- Tired, stressed or drunk states also suppress the prefrontal cortex. "Don't do things tired. Don't say things tired."
- He suggests not texting upset at night because stress feels bigger then. He used long exhale breathing instead.

**Anterior midcingulate cortex (aMCC)**
- Joe Parvizi at Stanford found that stimulating it makes people feel a challenge is coming and they will lean in. Andrew calls it the brain area of tenacity.
- It grows with doing things you don't want to do, such as dieting successfully, adding exercise (his example: mobility work), or learning a language.
- Any task with internal resistance builds it: cognitive, emotional or physical.
- He cautions against trading all friction for convenience, but is fine with trading for it knowingly (his example: ordering DoorDash). He supports optimization that lets you engage life with more vigor.

## Focus, learning and neuroplasticity

- Do a 90-minute to 2-hour bout of effortful learning or hard mental work every day.
- Remove the phone from the room. A study found that people focus equally well with the phone off, upside down, or in a bag. But if it is anywhere in the room, cognitive flexibility is reduced, because the brain runs a subscript anticipating it.
- Friction when learning is adrenaline. Propranolol blocks adrenaline and learning.
- Plasticity needs alertness, then focus, then errors, then rest. He debunks the claim that every experience changes your brain.
- The error is the plasticity signal, relayed to the cerebellum. He says computational models suggest an appropriate error rate of about 15%.
- Two kinds of practice: drilling what you already know (aim for near 100%) and learning something new, where you make real attempts and fail some of the time.
- Return a day or two later and repeat.
- Intense weightlifting or HIIT primes you for learning for about 4 hours after, because of stimulatory molecules in the body. He says long slow cardio is not that great for the brain.
- Performance follows an inverted U: too little or too much alertness hurts. Cortisol or adrenaline before, during or after an event (McGaugh and Cahill studies) enhances learning.
- **Self-testing:** use an LLM to generate quizzes at a chosen difficulty, for example "I'm a professor of neuroscience, and I want a 15% error rate on average." Studies found reading once with no notes or highlighting, then self-testing a week later and looking up errors, gave far more stable learning at 6 months and 2 years than rereading, notes or highlighting.
- **Dementia:** mental, physical and social activity matter. Masud Husain described long-lived people with plaques and tangles but no dementia, who exercise and stay engaged in learning. He notes people can focus intensely on interests (video games), and life is partly about forcing interest in things you don't want to do.

## Sleep: environment, falling back asleep, glymphatic clearance, meditation

- Keep the bedroom as dark as possible (eye mask, earplugs if you like). Dim lights in the evening because bright light raises cortisol as well as suppressing melatonin.
- No screens for the last 30 minutes before sleep, and dim the phone or triple-click for a red screen. The first 30 minutes after waking go to sun, exercise and hydration instead.
- Cooling the bed helps if you run warm.
- **Eye-movement trick for falling back asleep:** with eyes closed, move them slowly to one side, then the other, then up, then down, then counterclockwise and clockwise in a circle. Then point them toward the nostrils (without crossing) and do a long exhale. Repeat 2-3 times.
- The idea is that it shuts down the cerebellum's proprioceptive circuits, so you lose awareness of your limbs. He notes it has "indirect scientific support" and may not work if very stressed.
- He mentions a sleep mask based on work by two MIT scientists that stimulates eye muscles via an electrode behind the ear, and he says it put him to sleep fast. He predicts devices within 12-14 months that facilitate chosen amounts of REM and deep sleep.
- **Glymphatic clearance:** during sleep, astrocytes open space in the brain and waste flows out, including the puffiness under the eyes after bad sleep. Sleeping on your side with the head slightly elevated seems to improve it (he says it used to be thought slightly inverted).
- On waking, move to shift lymph: shaking, jump rope, an inversion table.
- A PNAS study he details in the book found short meditation increases glymphatic clearance in the daytime. His speculation is that regular meditators, at about 20 minutes a day, might shave an hour off sleep.
- His morning routine: 10 breaths of meditation in bed, then the bathroom, water, sunlight, a dog walk, caffeine, then exercise.

## Supplements and peptides

**Supplement stack he describes**
- Magnesium threonate or bisglycinate (interchangeable) for sleep, only if you're not sleeping terrifically well.
- At least 1 g/day of EPA omega-3. He prefers prescription Lovaza EPA (high concentration, free of heavy metals, cheap) and says he doesn't eat fatty fish.
- Vitamin D, 5,000-10,000 IU/day. He says most people underdose, and his blood work improves at 5,000 versus 3,000 IU.
- Alpha-GPC, 600 or 900 mg, as a focus stimulant, particularly later in the day or with caffeine. He says it gives more REM sleep. Caveat: it can raise TMAO and cardiovascular risk, which he mitigates with a 600 mg garlic capsule.
- Creatine, 5-10 g/day (taken since age 17-18). Cognitive benefit is greatest when sleep-deprived or stressed.
- Caffeine: 200-400 mg/day correlates with offsetting dementia (correlative, not causal). He drinks yerba mate and coffee.
- Also in the book: L-tyrosine, theanine with low-dose caffeine for task switching, tart cherry, apigenin, Quviviq.

**Peptides and GLP-1 drugs (not in the book)**
- He says the book leaves out hormone therapies, peptides and psychedelics, and he experiments on himself. Behavioral tools are the foundation.
- Pinealon: a tiny amount every third night for about a month gave him lots of REM that night and on following nights. He now takes it only sporadically.
- Growth hormone secretagogues (tesamorelin, ipamorelin, CJC-1295): they gave him a lot of deep sleep but "nuke my REM sleep," and he doesn't like them.
- BPC-157: he thinks the healing claims are accurate based on animal data, but notes a concern about vascularizing tumors. He wouldn't take it without an injury.
- GLP-1 drugs: he hasn't tried them but is curious about very low-dose tirzepatide or retatrutide. He relays a friend's anecdote of low-dose tirzepatide every 10 days helping chronic fatigue. They are anecdotes, not trial data.
- Concerns he names: vision loss in people with certain optic nerve head abnormalities, and apathy at high doses. He recommends an eye exam and eye pressure test.

## Walking, movement and metabolism

- Aim for 7,000-8,000 steps per day rather than a strict 10,000.
- A 5-10 minute walk after a meal buffers the glucose peak, which means fewer energy crashes and possibly better metabolic health.
- The soleus muscle (about 1% of musculature) uses outsized amounts of glucose. Non-weighted seated calf raises ("soleus push-ups") regulated blood glucose strongly in one study, though he calls it impractical.
- Rhonda Patrick pointed to a study where 10 air squats about every 45 minutes was comparable to 30 minutes of walking after meals.
- Non-exercise movement is a large calorie burn, up to about 1,000 calories per day in some people.
- He cites a Science paper saying the age-related decline in metabolism isn't real if muscle mass is maintained. His read is that people just move less as they age.
- He says to avoid mouth breathing at rest and in sleep (though it is fine during exercise).
- Calorie excess overloads mitochondria and makes the cell's energy system sick.

## Personal development and faith

- His last chapter uses James Hollis's rule for each day: "shut up, suit up, show up."
- "Shut up" means gratitude (giving and receiving it), since someone is always suffering more.
- "Suit up" means preparing: health, hygiene, homework.
- "Show up" means fulfilling outward roles (father, teacher, podcaster).
- Once a day, exit role fulfillment and stimulus-response mode: sit quietly, draw, listen to music, go camping. The aim is to let the unconscious feed you your "deepest heart's desires" without trying to accomplish anything.
- You find your calling "while working your butt off doing something else."
- He thinks in five-year chunks.
- Faith: he prays every night (not missed in nearly 3 years), gets up to pray if he wakes in the night, and prays before every podcast to move his ego out of the room. He says it removed the pressure to control everything and made life better.
- He frames faith as "the infrared goggles of human understanding." He calls militant atheism a kind of "spiritual anorexia."
- He has done clinical MDMA and psilocybin with therapy, finds them beneficial, and says he is done with them. He doesn't discuss psychedelics publicly.
