## Safety and finding the right temperature

- Andrew says never enter a dangerous body of water, and never do deliberate hyperventilation before or during cold (or any) water immersion.
- He advises starting slow, warmer rather than colder, because cold shock is possible. Like lifting weights, the right temperature is individual, but safety comes first.
- Temperature is impossible to prescribe because tolerance varies. The target is a temperature that evokes the thought "This is really cold (!), and I want to get out, _BUT_ I can safely stay in."
- That might be 60°F for some people and 45°F for others.
- With repeated exposure people get more comfortable in the cold and can use colder temperatures with more confidence, as with exercise.

## Colder means shorter: temperature and duration trade-off

- The colder the stimulus, the less time is needed.
- He cites one study showing significant, prolonged dopamine increases from about an hour in cool (60°F) water up to the neck, head above water.
- He cites other studies showing significant epinephrine increases from just 20 seconds in very cold water (~40°F).
- Most studies use ice baths or cold water immersion to the neck. These are best, but cold showers can work too and are more accessible.
- He leaves out cryo chambers: expensive, hard to access, and with little protocol variation.

## Basic protocol: 11 minutes per week total

- Andrew's baseline is **11 minutes per week TOTAL**, not per session.
- Spread it over 2-4 sessions of 1-5 minutes each.
- The water should be uncomfortably cold yet safe to stay in for a few minutes.
- He calls this the minimum to get the benefits and says you can do more.
- The figure comes from a recent study covering a range of effects. Very cold, very brief exposures can also be used for adrenaline release.

## Energy, focus, mood and dopamine

- Deliberate cold causes a significant release of epinephrine (adrenaline) and norepinephrine (noradrenaline) in brain and body. These make you alert, and can make you feel agitated or want to move or vocalize during the exposure.
- Levels stay elevated for some time afterward, increasing energy and focus that can be applied to mental or physical tasks.
- Cold also causes prolonged dopamine release, which he says elevates mood and enhances focus, attention and goal-directed behavior.
- He says even short bouts can cause a lasting rise in dopamine and sustained elevation of mood, energy and focus. He notes this is not true of every stress.

## Resilience and the "counting walls" approach

- Embracing cold as a self-directed challenge builds "top-down control" by the prefrontal cortex over deeper brain centers that regulate reflexive states.
- He says this is the basis of "resilience and grit", and that the skill carries over to real-world stressors, helping you stay calm and clear. In short, "deliberate cold exposure is great training for the mind."
- Counting walls:
  - Before or during cold, your mind pushes back ("I really don't want to do this," "Get me out of here"). Each of these is a "wall".
  - The walls are adrenaline pulses, and they trigger the adaptive response. Without difficulty there is no stimulus to adapt to.
  - Staying in despite the urge to exit means you've traversed the wall.
  - Set a goal of walls to traverse (e.g., 3-5 per round), or go by time.
  - He prefers walls because it transfers better to life stressors, which can't simply be timed, and it strengthens mind-body connection.
- To make cold a stronger resilience stimulus without lowering the temperature, keep moving your limbs while hands and feet stay in the water. Staying still lets a thermal layer insulate you, and moving breaks it up so the water feels much colder. He compares it to slowing a weight lift to remove momentum.

## Metabolism, shivering and the Søeberg principle

- Cold increases metabolism short-term because the body burns calories to raise core temperature, but he says the total calories burned are not significant.
- The bigger benefit is the conversion of white fat (energy storage) to beige or brown fat, which are highly metabolically active. This helps cold adaptation (feeling more comfortable in cold) and triggers further, sustained metabolic increases.
- He stresses calories in versus calories out still governs weight: "There is no escaping the laws of thermodynamics."
- The Søeberg Principle (from Dr. Susanna Søeberg) is to force your body to reheat on its own, or "End With Cold."
- Shivering may enhance the metabolic effect: it releases succinate from muscles and further activates brown fat thermogenesis.
- To increase shivering, during or right after cold:
  - Don't huddle or cross your arms.
  - Don't towel off.
  - Let your body reheat and dry naturally.
- He admits this is tough. Unless it's a hot sunny day, he takes a hot shower and towels off after cold, knowing he is likely limiting the metabolic effect.

## Recovery and timing around training

- He cites a meta-analysis finding cold water immersion can be a highly effective recovery tool after high-intensity or endurance training.
- Short immersions (under 5 minutes) improved muscle power and perceived recovery and reduced muscle soreness, partly via lower circulating creatine kinases.
- Cold water immersion (not cold showers) can blunt hypertrophy, strength or endurance gains if done within roughly 4 hours after training.
- He suggests waiting 6-8 or more hours after training, or doing cold before training.
- The exception is when you only want recovery without adaptation, such as in competition mode.

## Time of day

- Cold is followed by the body heating up, and body temperature increases tend to wake you while decreases push you toward sleepiness.
- He suggests using deliberate cold early in the day, not close to bedtime.
- Late is better than never, but not if it disturbs sleep. If it affects sleep, move it earlier or skip it.
