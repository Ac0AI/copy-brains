## Sleep is governed by two forces: adenosine and the circadian clock

- Andrew says sleep and wakefulness are two sides of one system. What you do awake sets when you fall asleep, whether you stay asleep, and how you feel the next day.
- Force 1 is adenosine, a molecule that builds the longer you are awake. It creates "sleep pressure", like hunger after fasting.
- Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, so the sleep signal is not sent. When it wears off, adenosine binds and you feel a crash.
- Caffeine tolerance varies genetically. Andrew can drink it at 4-5 pm and still sleep well; others can't have any after 11 am. His advice: test it yourself and stick with what works.
- Force 2 is the circadian clock, which explains why you get alert again in the morning after an all-nighter even though adenosine kept building.
- Sleep is best treated as an average of many behaviors, like nutrition, not one fix.

## Morning cortisol, evening melatonin, and the clock

- On waking, the adrenal glands release a pulse of cortisol (and epinephrine) that raises heart rate and gets you moving. Andrew says it should come early in the waking period.
- Waking starts a timer: roughly 12-14 hours later (he also says 12-16) the pineal gland releases melatonin, the sleepiness signal.
- Light is the strongest signal for these rhythms. The pathway is: light enters the eye, special retinal ganglion cells (melanopsin-containing) signal the suprachiasmatic nucleus (the central clock above the roof of the mouth), which sets cells and organs throughout the body.
- He says a late cortisol rise (around 8-9 pm) is a hallmark of anxiety and depression, though cause and effect are unclear.
- Without light cues (a cave or constant light), the rhythms free-run and drift later each day.
- The pineal is a "darkness gland": sunlight suppresses melatonin release, and darkness permits it.

## Morning sunlight protocol

- Get outdoor light in your eyes soon after waking, ideally at sunrise. If you wake later, still go out within an hour or two of sunrise, while the sun is low.
- Why low sun: the cells respond to the yellow-blue contrast present when the sun is low. Once it is overhead, that quality changes and you miss the window.
- Duration:
  - Bright clear day: about 30-60 seconds can be enough in a very bright setting (his example: a snowy field in Colorado).
  - Typical guidance: 2-10 minutes.
  - Overcast: longer; still roughly 10,000-50,000 lux outdoors.
- Window glass or a car windshield is about 50 times less effective than being outside.
- Don't wear sunglasses if it is safe. Prescription glasses and contacts are fine. Never compromise safety.
- Never stare at the sun or any light that hurts; blink and look away.
- Indoor light is weak by comparison: the Light Meter app showed him roughly 500-1,000 lux indoors vs 10,000-20,000 outdoors.
- If you can't get sun (e.g. Scandinavian winter), use bright artificial light that is rich in blue or mimics sunlight, such as sunrise simulators or ring lights.
- Phones are not enough in the morning, because the retina is less sensitive early in the day.
- He says the benefits are cumulative: the clock averages your light exposure, and it takes about 2-3 days to see rhythms settle. Missing a day is fine.
- Blind or low-vision people usually keep these cells (provided the eyes are intact), and the effect doesn't depend on conscious sight.
- Caution: people with retinal degeneration, retinitis pigmentosa, macular degeneration or glaucoma should avoid very bright light and may need longer, dimmer exposure.

## Evening and night light: what to avoid

- The retina gets more light-sensitive the longer you've been awake, so a small amount of light at night has a big effect.
- Keep light low after about 8 pm, and avoid bright light between roughly 11 pm and 4 am.
- Night light can suppress dopamine (via the habenula, which he calls the "nucleus of disappointment"), hurting mood and learning. This is from work by David Berson and Samer Hattar.
- Light at night can phase-delay your clock, pushing sleep and wake later.
- Placement: the relevant cells sit mostly in the lower retina, so they see the upper visual field. Use low lamps at desk or floor level, not overhead lights. Overhead fluorescents are the worst case.
- Candlelight, fireplaces and campfires don't activate these cells (mind fire safety). Red light doesn't either.
- If you wake at night, keep lights dim, use blue-blocking glasses if you must use a screen, and dim the screen.
- Blue-blocking glasses are for the evening only; he says to take them off during the day.
- A sunset view (about an hour around sunset, outdoors, no sunglasses if safe, 2-10 minutes) helps buffer the effect of later light and tells the clock the day is ending.
- He says there is no photoreception through the skin or knee in humans; a study claiming this was retracted.

## Shifting your sleep schedule

- Light before waking: Jamie Zeitzer's study turned lights on 45-60 minutes before waking, with eyelids closed and not under covers. In teens who stay up late, after a few days it increased total sleep and made them sleepy earlier.
- Early light advances the clock; light late in the evening or at night delays it.
- To become an earlier riser: get bright light early, and avoid bright light late at night.
- Early-day exercise also helps shift the clock, but he says sunlight is roughly 1,000-10,000 times more effective than exercise in the dark.
- Eating times also act as cues; he points to Satchin Panda's *The Circadian Code* and restricting food to a daily window.
- He says many self-described night owls simply aren't getting enough morning light.

## Naps, NSDR and falling asleep

- Naps under one ultradian cycle (20-60 minutes) can help many people with the mid-afternoon dip. Waking groggy may signal poor night sleep. Test it yourself.
- He proposes "non-sleep deep rest" (NSDR): yoga nidra, guided meditation or hypnosis scripts, 10-30 (up to 60) minutes. He says it shifts the nervous system toward the parasympathetic (calming) side and can help you fall asleep.
- A Danish study he cites suggests yoga nidra helps reset dopamine in the striatum.
- He says to use the body to control the mind: attention to exhale-focused breathing, body scans and posture, since it is hard to control the mind with the mind.
- Doing NSDR at any time, including on waking in the night or after morning light, trains the shift from high alertness to calm.
- Resources named: Headspace; Reveri (reverie health) hypnosis scripts from David Spiegel, including a free sleep script; yoga nidra scripts.

## Sleep aids and supplements (Andrew's views)

- He stresses behavior first (light, food, exercise, environment), supplements last, and checking with a doctor. He says he is not promoting any of them.
- **Melatonin:** he generally avoids it. He says it helps you fall asleep but not stay asleep. It's an unregulated hormone supplement; label doses can be wildly off (he cites Matt Walker: from about 15% to 400% of the stated amount). It also suppresses puberty onset and may affect other hormones.
- **Magnesium threonate:** he takes 300-400 mg, 30-60 minutes before bed.
- **Theanine:** he takes 100-200 mg, 30-60 minutes before bed. It can make dreams more vivid, so sleepwalkers should be careful.
- **Apigenin (from chamomile):** 50 mg. It is an estrogen suppressor, so women trying to keep estrogen up should avoid it, and men should consider it.
- Magnesium plus theanine can make some people so sleepy that waking is hard, so adjust the dose.
- He mentions stimulants (modafinil, Adderall, cocaine) only to say they raise epinephrine and dopamine, carry downsides, and need a doctor.
- Energy drinks: he stopped them after his eyes' whites turned bright red, and an eye doctor friend suggested taurine-related microvessel damage.
- Source for supplement research: examine.com.
