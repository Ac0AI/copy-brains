## Morning light: the core daily practice

Andrew's single most important tool is getting bright light, ideally sunlight, into the eyes soon after waking.

- **Timing:** within 30 minutes to an hour of waking, and sooner is better. Most days is enough, not every day.
- **Duration:** on a clear day, 5 to 10 minutes is "very important". On an overcast day, stay out longer. He spent about 30 minutes outside on a cloudy day and took his work outdoors.
- **Overcast days:** outdoor light is still far better than indoor lights, which are not bright enough to do the job.
- **Missed a day:** no problem. The next day, spend roughly twice as long outside.
- **Eyewear:** contact lenses and prescription glasses are fine, because they only focus light on the retina. Sunglasses can be a problem. Never stare at the sun, and never look at any light bright enough to hurt.
- **Windows and cars:** don't count on glass. Windows and windshields filter wavelengths, and he says it takes about "50 times longer", so you'd spend all day waiting for the clock-setting to happen.
- **Before sunrise:** turn on as many bright artificial lights as possible, then go outside once the sun is up.
- **Low-cost lamp:** a SAD lamp is expensive. A cheap ring light like the ones used for Instagram or YouTube, placed on the breakfast table or desk in the morning, can do the job. He has no financial ties to any of these companies.
- **Blue-blocking glasses:** he says not to wear them in the morning or during the day. Blue wavelengths are among the main signals that set the body clock, so blocking them cancels the wake-up and clock-setting signal. If you use them, do it late in the day.

## Why morning light works (clock, cortisol, melatonin)

- The retina is a piece of brain that sits outside the skull. Light reaching it affects energy, immune function, sleep timing and depth, and appetite.
- Intrinsically photosensitive retinal neurons (found by David Berson at Brown and others) send signals to the hypothalamus. The suprachiasmatic nucleus there is the master clock, which coordinates the 24-hour clock in every cell. Andrew compares it to a clock shop where the SCN sets every clock to the right time.
- This system is a slow integrator that effectively counts photons over time. That is why a dim day needs more minutes. It is separate from the parts of the visual system used for seeing edges, faces and motion.
- **Cortisol:** a cortisol pulse happens at least once every 24 hours whatever you do. Early light places it early, giving energy and focus for roughly 10 to 12, maybe 14, hours.
- **Late pulse:** without early light for several days, the pulse drifts later toward the afternoon. A late cortisol peak is closely linked to many forms of chronic depression, evening anxiety and sleep problems.
- **Melatonin:** morning light starts a countdown of about 16 hours to melatonin release. Melatonin helps you get into sleep (it doesn't keep you asleep), so morning light improves sleep onset and quality later that night.
- **Speed:** two University of Colorado studies show that morning light plus avoiding artificial light at night corrects melatonin and cortisol rhythms within about two days.
- **Seasons:** day length across the year also shifts neuromodulators. Longer days raise dopamine and serotonin. Shorter days bring lower mood and energy and cravings for cortisol-lowering comfort carbohydrates.
- **Other clues:** disrupted sleep schedules almost always precede depressive episodes and anxiety. Waking at 2 to 4 a.m. and being unable to fall back asleep is typical of depression, though it doesn't mean you have it. Clock disruption is tied to digestive problems, poor focus, depression, anxiety, OCD, ADHD and Alzheimer's.

## Evening light: dim down between 10 p.m. and 4 a.m.

- Bright light at night suppresses melatonin from the pineal gland. Retinal sensitivity changes, so it takes little light late at night to disrupt the clock.
- **Window:** avoid bright light between about 10 p.m. and 4 a.m. This includes Netflix and screens.
- **How:** dim lights to what you need to move around safely. Use table lamps or lights near the floor. The cells that signal the hypothalamus are in the lower retina, so they see the upper visual field.
- **Fine sources:** firelight, candlelight and moonlight. Even a very bright moon carries little photon energy.
- **Don't panic:** a trip to the bathroom, the store or a hospital in the night is fine. These are slow-adapting systems and he calls the target "80%" correct, like nutrition or exercise.
- **Shift workers:** a separate problem, covered in a full episode of his podcast.
- **Kids:** if evening screen work is unavoidable, dim the screen and the room. Keep phones and tablets out of the bedroom at night. Evening screens delay melatonin and leave kids more tired, more reactive and worse at regulating emotion the next day.

## Daytime outdoors, distance viewing and panoramic vision

- **Myopia:** large trials with thousands of participants found that children (and adults) who spent about two hours a day outdoors, even when doing schoolwork on a computer, had far less myopia. Prolonged near viewing lengthens the eyeball.
- **Look far:** several times a day, look at distant things, ideally the horizon, or far down the street. Constant near viewing strains the lens and eye muscles and can cause headaches and eye strain.
- **Mood and metabolism:** daytime sunlight acts through a path that is separate from the clock. It runs via the habenula, linked to dopamine, mood and feeding rhythms (work by Samer Hattar at NIMH).
- **Walk without the phone:** moving forward through the world creates optic flow and "compensatory eye movements". When self-generated, this suppresses the threat-response circuitry, including the amygdala, and reduces anxiety.
- **Where:** walking, running or cycling outside works. A treadmill does not, and a stationary bike is probably better than nothing.
- **Panoramic vision:** widen your visual field without moving your head or eyes, so you take in the ceiling, the space in front of you and, ideally, your own body in the periphery.
  - It releases the link between the eyes and the brainstem alertness circuits. He compares it to easing your foot off the gas, a slowing rather than a hard brake.
  - It is invisible to others, unlike a breathing tool.
  - It also increases alertness and situational awareness. Magnocellular neurons carry peripheral and motion information faster, and reaction speed improves roughly fourfold. Special forces personnel he works with use it.
  - Use it before a hard conversation, public speaking, or a doctor's visit. Use it when walking down a hallway or stairs instead of looking at your phone.

## Visual focus drills and attention breaks

- **Focus drill:** mental focus follows visual focus. If you can't concentrate on work, put a small mark on a sheet of paper at screen distance. Fix your gaze on it with eyes converged, blinking as little as possible, for about 60 seconds. It narrows your attention and helps exclude distractions.
- **His morning routine (about 8 years, daily):**
  1. Eyes closed, attention on internal state (interoception), three breaths.
  2. Eyes open, focus on a hand or object about arm's length away, three breaths.
  3. Look at a distant point, three breaths.
  4. Shift to panoramic vision, even indoors, three breaths.
  5. Close the eyes and return inward, then focus on a small cross mark and begin work.
- **Why it works:** it trains moving attention across the visual "space-time" and between near and far. This makes transitions between tasks and conversations easier.
- **Phones:** social media is like watching 50 movies in two minutes, changing context over and over. Much of what looks like ADHD is self-inflicted. Andrew accepts real clinical ADHD exists.
- **Short breaks:** pauses of even 10 seconds during focused work or learning let the brain consolidate. Focus is also better when you return. He compares attention to dropping small coins all day.

## Stress control: the physiological sigh and the nervous system

- **Stress basics:** short-term stress is not bad for the immune system. Adrenaline signals the immune system to recruit killer cells and cytokines. Problems come from chronic adrenaline with no off-switch (too much coffee, too little sleep).
- **Adrenaline tools:** 3 minutes of cold exposure (cold shower) every day or two, or cyclic hyperventilation, both raise adrenaline. Per peer-reviewed studies this improves resistance to bacteria, viruses and fungi.
- **Anxiety:** at the biological level, anxiety, stress, trauma, fear and PTSD are all uncontrolled adrenaline release. The goal is learning to turn it up, ease it down, or brake it hard.
- **The physiological sigh:** it is the fastest tool he knows for reducing stress, and it works in real time.
  1. Inhale deeply through the nose.
  2. Take a second, short sharp inhale through the nose on top of the first (only a bit more air).
  3. Exhale long and fully through the mouth.
- **Why it works:** a small cluster of brainstem neurons detects CO2 buildup. Stress or poor breathing collapses the alveoli, and the double inhale re-inflates them and lets you offload CO2.
- **How often:** often one is enough, sometimes two or three. People do it spontaneously about every five minutes, and also after crying and before sleep.
- **Body first, then mind:** "trying to control thoughts with thoughts is like trying to catch fog". Use the body to shift state, then think the problem through from a calmer vantage point. Journaling, even badly, helps too.
- **Seesaw model:** the autonomic nervous system works like a seesaw between alertness and calm. Healthy means moving easily between the two. He favors physical, action-first tools because they are shared, concrete and available to everyone. He also strongly endorses therapy, CBT and talk therapy for deeper work.
- **Neuroplasticity:** the nervous system changes in response to whatever you do. Regular use of these tools makes relaxing and focusing easier by default. Benefits compound over about six months, and missing a day or two is fine.

## Sleep and supplement stack

Andrew puts behavioral tools first and supplements second. He doesn't sell supplements, and his podcast's tie to a supplement company is not something he plugs. He advises shopping on price, adding one thing at a time and checking with a doctor.

**Sleep tools**
- **NSDR / yoga nidra:** a free 30-minute script for deep rest without sleep, usable before bed or after waking in the night.
- **Reveri.com:** a free self-hypnosis app (iOS and Android) from a Stanford colleague, for sleep, anxiety and focus.
- **Magnesium:** threonate or bisglycinate, either one. About 5% of people get stomach upset with threonate. He also says there is early evidence threonate may reduce some cognitive-decline symptoms.
- **Apigenin:** 50 mg (chamomile extract). Together with magnesium, these have helped his sleep. He judges sleep by subjective feel, and wearable users report more deep sleep.

**Other things he takes**
- A multivitamin, ginger, and garlic (allicin, strong data on cardiovascular health).
- 1 to 3 g EPA a day, because he doesn't eat fatty fish. He says 1 to 2 g of EPA was as effective as SSRIs in double-blind placebo-controlled trials without the side effects, and can lower the SSRI dose needed.
- Vitamin D3.
- No probiotics. Instead, 1 to 4 servings of fermented foods a day, based on Justin Sonnenburg's work at Stanford showing improved inflammation-related pathways.
