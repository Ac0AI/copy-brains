## Morning light and the cortisol peak

- Andrew says a cortisol peak should land early in the day, and bright light viewed within 30-60 minutes of waking is the main way to place it there.
- Intrinsically photosensitive melanopsin cells in the eye signal the suprachiasmatic nucleus. That triggers the cortisol rise and the wake-up signal, suppresses leftover melatonin, and starts a timer for sleep roughly 16 hours later.
- Exposure guide:
  - Clear day: about 5 minutes (3-7 minutes).
  - Some cloud cover: about 10 minutes.
  - Dense overcast or rain: 20-30 minutes.
- Practical rules:
  - Go outside. A windshield or window is far too weak.
  - Don't wear sunglasses for this. Corrective lenses and contacts are fine and help focus light on the retina.
  - Never stare at a sun so bright it hurts. Look toward it and blink freely.
  - If you wake before sunrise, turn on bright artificial lights, then still get outside once the sun is up.
- Indoor light is roughly 1,000 lux. Outdoor light is 5,000 to 90,000 lux even on overcast days. A free light meter app shows the difference.
- Do it at least 80% of days. If you miss a day, double the duration the next day.
- For dark climates, a daylight simulator, a ring light or an LED drawing tablet on the desk works as a cheaper substitute.

## Waking up: temperature, movement, caffeine and food

- Cold exposure: 1-3 minutes under a cold shower or in an ice bath releases adrenaline and dopamine. It also raises core temperature, because the hypothalamic thermostat compensates for a cold body surface.
- Movement also raises core temperature: a walk, light jog or skipping rope.
  - Andrew skips rope 10-20 minutes while looking at the sun, then takes a cold shower.
- Caffeine:
  - Wait 90-120 minutes after waking, so leftover adenosine clears and the afternoon crash is avoided.
  - Caffeine is an adenosine antagonist.
  - Keep late intake to 100 mg or less after 4:00 p.m., ideally stopping at 2-3 p.m.
  - Caffeine late in the day disrupts sleep architecture even if you feel you sleep fine.
  - He likes coffee, espresso and non-smoked yerba mate. Smoked varieties carry a cancer risk.
  - Anyone prone to panic or anxiety can skip caffeine entirely.
- Food:
  - Eating early supports a food-entrained clock and raises metabolism and alertness.
  - Andrew usually doesn't eat until about 11 a.m. or noon.
  - A large meal diverts blood from the brain and causes sleepiness at any time of day.

## Mid-day and afternoon: naps, exercise and evening sunlight

- Naps are fine if they stay under 90 minutes and aren't late enough to hurt night sleep. Nobody has to nap.
- Andrew does NSDR or Reverie hypnosis most days in the early afternoon. It replenishes him and has improved his ability to fall and stay asleep.
- Afternoon or evening intense exercise raises body temperature and delays the circadian clock, so you want to sleep later. Exercise early in the day, within 0-4 hours of waking, doesn't shift the clock much.
- He cites research suggesting exercise right after waking, 3 hours after waking, or 11 hours after waking, but says it's hard to be prescriptive.
- Get low-angle sunlight in the eyes in the late afternoon or around sunset, for 5-30 minutes depending on cloud cover.
  - It acts on the evening oscillators and sets a second anchor point for the clock.
  - It partly (not fully) inoculates against the harm of artificial light at night.
  - The brain distinguishes morning from evening light by the yellow-blue-orange ratio.
- During the day, bright overhead lights help alertness.

## Evening and night: light, temperature and sleeping environment

- Light at night:
  - Between 10 p.m. and 4 a.m., avoid bright artificial light of any color, especially overhead light.
  - Very little light is enough at night to disrupt the clock and suppress melatonin, the reverse of the morning, when you need a lot.
  - Photosensitive cells sit in the lower retina, which views the upper visual field, so overhead lights mimic daytime sun. Use low lamps and floor lights.
  - Dim screens. Candlelight is about 3-10 lux and moonlight is low. Desk or overhead lights are 100-1,000 lux. Supermarket and gas-station fluorescents are the worst.
  - Andrew has worn sunglasses at night in those places.
  - Blue blockers aren't necessary, because bright light of any color wakes you.
- Temperature:
  - Core temperature must drop 1-3 degrees for sleep onset, and a rise of the same size wakes you.
  - Avoid cold showers at night.
  - A hot bath, hot tub or sauna for no more than 20-30 minutes, followed by getting out, triggers a compensatory drop that helps sleep. It can also help if you're wired from caffeine or late exercise.
  - Keep the bedroom cool, lowering it at least 3 degrees. An Eight Sleep or a fan also works.
  - Heat leaves through glabrous skin: palms, soles and the upper face. Putting a hand or foot out from the blankets cools you.
  - Andrew doesn't wear socks to bed, though they help people who wake with cold feet.
- Alcohol and THC can help people fall asleep, but they degrade sleep architecture. Andrew doesn't drink, since alcohol just knocks him out and the sleep is disrupted.
- CBD and THC edibles may lower anxiety for some people.

## Sleep supplements and dosages

- Andrew's order of operations: behavior first, then nutrition, then supplements, then prescription drugs. Check with a physician before adding or removing a supplement.
- The base stack, taken 30-60 minutes before bed, especially if you haven't eaten for about 3 hours:
  - Magnesium threonate: 145 mg.
  - Apigenin: 50 mg.
  - Theanine: 100-400 mg.
- You need not take all three, and some people need none.
  - About 5% report that magnesium threonate upsets their gut. If it does, skip it.
  - Some find theanine causes vivid or anxious dreams, so they leave it out.
- Single-ingredient products let you titrate and add or remove one component at a time.
- Extras Andrew adds:
  - Every third or fourth night: 2 g glycine plus 100 mg GABA. More often and the stack seems to work less well.
  - Every other night, on the nights without glycine and GABA: 900 mg myo-inositol with the base stack.
- Inositol helps him fall asleep and fall back asleep after waking in the night. It also seems to give a lasting anxiety-lowering effect, so he feels "buffered" against stress. He notes it may suit people who are fasting or low-carb.
- He prefers this stack to melatonin, because commercial doses are far above physiological levels and melatonin interacts with the testosterone, estrogen and puberty systems. Occasional use for jet lag is probably fine; chronic use, especially in kids, is potentially problematic.

## Falling back asleep, NSDR and sleep-environment tools

- NSDR (non-sleep deep rest) scripts and yoga nidra are free on YouTube. They help with falling asleep, staying asleep and falling back asleep.
- Reverie (self-hypnosis from Stanford's David Spiegel):
  - It has a fall-back-asleep session. Andrew says that 99 times out of 100 he's asleep within minutes.
  - It costs $14.99/month, $99.99/year, or $249 once, with a 7-day free trial. It is currently Apple-only.
  - This is clinically supported self-hypnosis, not stage hypnosis.
- If he wakes at night, he goes to the bathroom with dim lights and returns to bed, then uses Reverie if needed.
- Eye masks help if not too tight and the room is cool enough. The upper face is a heat-exchange area, so a warm room plus a mask wakes you.
- Ear plugs: some people love them; Andrew hears his own heartbeat and finds them unhelpful.
- Raise the foot of the bed 3-5 degrees (or the feet with a pillow) for deeper sleep via lymphatic washout. If you have acid reflux, do the opposite and raise the head side by 3-5 degrees.
- Sleep apnea is dangerous and common in heavy-set people, including very muscular ones. It is linked to cardiovascular, sexual and cognitive problems.
- Nose-breathing in sleep helps:
  - Tape your mouth shut with medical tape. This also reduces snoring.
  - Practice nasal breathing during low-intensity cardio (mouth closed, or a held sip of water). It gradually dilates the nasal passages.
  - He cites James Nestor's book *Breath* on this.

## Consistency, compensatory sleep and shifting your clock

- Keep wake times within plus or minus one hour on weekends. If you sleep in, do it by at most an hour, then use an afternoon nap instead of sleeping until 11 or noon.
- After a bad night:
  - Still delay caffeine 90-120 minutes. Caffeine especially disrupts compensatory sleep.
  - Use NSDR or Reverie to partly offset the loss. He says nothing fully compensates for lost sleep.
- Temperature minimum is about 2 hours before your usual wake time. For a 7:00 a.m. riser it is 5:00 a.m.
  - Bright light, exercise or caffeine in the 2-4 hours before it delays the clock, so you want to sleep and wake later.
  - Bright light, exercise, socializing or food in the hours after it advances the clock, so you want to sleep and wake earlier.
- To become an early riser:
  - Move bedtime and wake time earlier by 30-60 minutes each night over about a week.
  - On waking, get light (artificial if the sun's not up), exercise and breakfast even without hunger.
  - Eating on local time is one of the fastest ways to adapt to jet lag or shift work.
- Use the same logic to pre-shift before travel.
- Shift work:
  - Stay on one shift for about 2 weeks at a time and avoid swing shifts.
  - If you must be awake in the middle of the night (feeding a baby, caring for someone), use red light, which hasn't been shown to disrupt the cortisol rhythm.
- Jet lag can also come from being woken at night.
- Andrew says early risers show cognitive and grip-strength benefits, especially night owls who shift deliberately.
