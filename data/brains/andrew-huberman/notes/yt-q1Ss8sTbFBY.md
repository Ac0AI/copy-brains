## Weekly template: how Andrew structures his baseline fitness protocol

Andrew describes a baseline template he has used for over three decades (he is 47). It is a framework to adapt, not a rigid prescription. Day-by-day layout:

- **Sunday:** long endurance (Zone 2)
- **Monday:** legs (resistance)
- **Tuesday:** heat/cold contrast
- **Wednesday:** torso + neck (resistance)
- **Thursday:** ~35 min moderate-high cardio
- **Friday:** high-intensity interval training (HIIT)
- **Saturday:** arms, calves, neck, indirect torso

Core principles:
- Each day emphasizes one fitness quality, but the week trains all of them: endurance, strength, hypertrophy, speed, power and flexibility.
- Start days are arbitrary. He says the exact day matters less than spacing between sessions.
- Do one long endurance session, one moderate-high cardio session, one all-out cardiovascular session, and train every muscle group with resistance at least once a week.
- He quotes Andy Galpin: "few concepts, many methods". The key concept is manipulating adjustable variables with progressive overload (more weight, a steeper hill, faster or farther).

## Sunday long endurance and the Zone 2 target

- Goal is 60-75 minutes of running in Zone 2, or substitutes: rowing, cycling, swimming.
- Zone 2 means breathing and heart rate are elevated but you can still hold a conversation. Push a bit harder and you can't finish sentences.
- He doesn't wear a heart rate monitor for this and doesn't obsess over staying exactly in zone. Stopping briefly for breathlessness doesn't ruin the session.
- Some Sundays he does a 2.5-5 hour hike instead, often as a social outing.
- Weighted vest (10-50 lbs) raises effort, and lets him match a slower hiking partner's pace without leaving them behind. A weighted backpack works as well (a former Navy SEAL friend once handed him a ~40 lb pack).
- Beginners: start shorter on flat ground at a pace that reaches Zone 2 without being brutal.
- Progression: increase time, then weight or speed. As fitness improves, Zone 2 stays the same but you need to move faster or carry more to reach it.
- He cites Peter Attia's target of 180-200 minutes per week of Zone 2 for longevity and health. Sunday's session covers a big chunk and acts as a safety net if the rest of the week derails.

## Resistance training: legs (Monday) and structure rules

- Why legs first: largest muscle group, systemic metabolic and hormonal effects (he cites growth hormone and testosterone, plus other anabolic hormones), and support for tendon/ligament strength. Doing it early also leaves recovery days afterward.
- Session length: ~10 min warm-up, then 50-60 minutes of work. He says cortisol rises beyond ~60 minutes and impairs his recovery. He sometimes reaches 75 minutes when waiting for equipment.
- He trains legs directly once a week (Monday). Friday's HIIT works legs indirectly.
- Exercise selection: two exercises per muscle group (three at most). Pick one that loads the muscle in its shortened/contracted position and one that loads it in a stretched or long-range position.
  - Quads: leg extension + hack squat.
  - Hamstrings: leg curl + glute-ham raise (as he describes it).
  - Calves: standing and seated calf raises.
- He doesn't free-bar squat or deadlift for safety reasons and has still hit his goals. Pick movements you can do safely.
- Nutrition tip: the night before leg day he eats carbs such as rice or pasta so the muscle has enough glycogen.

## Sets, reps, rest and periodization

- Cycle about a month at a time, 3-4 weeks per block.
- **Heavy block:** 4-8 reps, 3-5 sets per exercise (fewer reps means more volume in sets), rest 2-4 minutes between sets for heavy leg work.
- **Moderate block:** 8-12 (up to ~15) reps, 2-3 sets, rest about 60-90 seconds up to 2-2.5 minutes.
- Galpin's point: you can build muscle across roughly 5-30 reps, but vary rep ranges.
- His experience: staying in the heavy block longer than ~4 weeks stalls progress; alternating lets him progress through the year. During moderate-rep months his endurance sessions feel easier.
- Intensity: roughly 30% of his sets go to failure. Others are hard but not to failure. When sick he drops that to ~10%.

## Wednesday torso and neck, Saturday arms

- Wednesday trains chest, shoulders and back (push/pull) in 50-60 minutes after a warm-up. He sometimes alternates push and pull sets, but isn't rigid, and says don't hog multiple stations in a crowded gym.
- Same logic as legs: two exercises per muscle, one for peak contraction and one for stretch.
  - Chest: cable crossover (contraction), incline press with full range (stretch).
  - Back: seated/bent-over/dumbbell row (contraction), chin-up/pull-up (stretch).
- **Neck training:** he trains the neck several times a week for spinal stability, posture (counteracting forward-chin posture from screens), and injury resilience. He credits it for walking away from a rooftop fall and a rear-end car collision without injury. Method: weight plate wrapped in a towel, moving the neck side to side and front to back, light-to-moderate load. He warns against neck bridges (disc damage can come suddenly). He points to Jeff Cavaliere's videos for form.
- **Saturday arms/calves/neck:** two exercises per muscle again.
  - Biceps: dumbbell curls, incline-bench curls (stretch), then chin-ups (2-3 sets), which also hits the lats.
  - Triceps: kickbacks or overhead extension, plus dips (which also load chest and shoulders).
  - Calves and neck as on other days.
  - Total ~45-60 min of work. Keeps indirect torso stimulation spaced from Wednesday.

## Thursday moderate-high cardio and Friday HIIT

- **Thursday:** 5-10 min warm-up, then 30-35 minutes at about "75 to 80 percent" of maximal effort. He defines 100% as sprinting away from someone chasing him with a syringe of poison. Breathing is hard, so not nose-only. It works multiple energy systems and endurance categories. Substitutes: rowing, cycling, stair running in hotels, jumping jacks (full range) or burpees, jump rope. Rope can be too easy for high heart rates unless you add double-unders.
- **Friday HIIT:** goal is reaching near-max heart rate at least once a week.
  - His favorite is an assault/air bike: 20-30 seconds all-out, 10 seconds rest, repeat for 8-12 rounds. By round 5 or 6 he's near max even if starting a bit easier.
  - Alternative: sprint 20-30 seconds then jog 10 seconds.
  - Max heart rate estimate (Galpin): 220 minus age. He doesn't use a monitor; he goes until he "feels like dying".
  - Also provides indirect leg stimulus, about 48-72 hours after Monday's leg session.
  - Safety: he runs sprints at about 95% rather than 100% to avoid hamstring, sciatic nerve or pelvic floor issues. Bike, rower or ski erg are safer than field sprints. Don't start this protocol if you lack cardiovascular health.

## Tuesday heat/cold contrast and cold-exposure timing

- Purpose: speed recovery from Monday's legs, support cardiovascular and brain health (vasoconstriction in cold, vasodilation in heat, driven by nerves supplying the vessels).
- Protocol: sauna ~20 min (15 sometimes), then cold plunge ~5 min at 45-50°F. Repeat 3-5 rounds. He builds heat tolerance gradually and says don't plunge so cold you go into shock.
- Rule of thumb: hot enough to sweat and want out but not unsafe; cold enough that you want out but safe. It varies by person.
- Travel substitutes: hot bath then cold shower; plastic garbage bag over the body while running; or just cold exposure.
- Heat note: he cites a Finnish study in which repeated sauna sessions in a single day raised growth hormone up to 16-fold, hence concentrating sessions once a week.
- Key timing point: ice-bath or deep cold immersion right after resistance or endurance training can blunt strength, hypertrophy and endurance adaptations, so keep contrast sessions on a separate day. If you shift it, keep cold exposure at least 4 (preferably 8) hours away from torso/resistance training. Cold showers differ from full immersion and he knows of no data that they interfere.

## Real-world adjustments: sleep, illness, fasting and scheduling

- **Bad night of sleep:** usually skips the next day's workout and pushes the schedule back a day. Reason: training after poor sleep or high stress makes him prone to getting sick. Exception: if he got a few hours but can do NSDR (10, 30 or even 60 minutes), he often can train well afterward.
- **Illness:** with a mild "might be getting sick" feeling he cuts the session about 15 minutes, reduces sets to failure, or trims endurance by 10-20%, then focuses on sleep, warmth, nutrition and recovery. With real cold/flu symptoms he stops entirely until fully recovered. After time off, he ramps back over 1-2 weeks instead of jumping to full intensity.
- **Flexibility with scheduling:** any session can move a day earlier or later. Think big picture first: one long, one moderate-high and one max cardio session, plus each muscle group trained at least once.
- **Fasted vs fed:** he prefers training fasted. His first meal is typically around 11:00 AM with the last bite around 8 PM, not strict. He eats carbs and protein soon after resistance training. If hungry or socially booked, he'll eat 60-90 minutes before a workout rather than skip it.
- **Mind-muscle connection:** deliberately contract the working muscle (focus on the muscle favors hypertrophy; focus on moving the weight favors strength).

## Nervous system tools: breathing, grip and flexibility

- Between sets he uses the **physiological sigh** (two inhales through the nose, then a long full exhale through the mouth) to lower heart rate and relax, then ramps focus up for the next set.
- Grip and irradiation (Pavel Tsatsouline's principle): squeezing the handles hard (both hands) and tensing the core increases force output; relaxing the grip and exhaling relaxes the body during stretching and rest.
- **Flexibility** (from his flexibility episode): static stretches held 30-60 seconds, moving to about 60% of end range rather than max, relaxing and exhaling, repeated 2-3 times a week per muscle group (he gets about three sessions a week).
- **Post-session:** 3-5 minutes of slow, deliberate breathing to shift toward recovery (based on Galpin-related research). Also, hang from a bar as long as possible as a longevity marker (Peter Attia).
- **Standing/sitting tool: soleus push-up.** From a University of Houston study in iScience: soleus is ~1% of body muscle, mostly slow-twitch. Repeated seated heel raises (10-15 degrees, knees at ~90 degrees) for up to 270 minutes cut the post-meal glucose spike by 52% and hyperinsulinemia by 60%. Benefits lasted at least 2 hours after a meal. Not yet replicated, so he suggests it as a free supplement to normal exercise, and notes that shorter durations likely help too.
