## Omega-3s, phosphatidylserine and choline as the structural nutrients for neurons

- Andrew's framing: much of the brain (beyond water) is fat, and neuron membranes are built from structural fat, not belly storage fat. The foods you eat supply the building blocks of the neurons that let you think.
- Two "modulatory" foundations come first: regular, ample sleep, and 150-180 minutes per week of cardiovascular exercise (heart health feeds the brain's oxygen and glucose supply). Resistance training is a bonus on top.
- **Omega-3 (EPA):** most people get enough omega-6 but not enough omega-3. He says to aim for at least 1.5 g of EPA per day, ideally 2-3 g. He says 1-3 g/day of EPA has mood benefits on par with some antidepressant treatments, and can allow lower antidepressant doses for people already on them.
- Sources: mackerel, salmon, herring, oysters, sardines, anchovies, and caviar ("the heavyweight champion of EPAs per unit volume"). Plant sources include chia seeds, walnuts and soybeans. Alternatives are liquid or capsule fish oil, krill oil, or algae-based EPA.
- **Phosphatidylserine:** found in meat, fish and, to some extent, cabbage. In at least three studies, 300 mg/day modestly improved cognition, and in at least five it reduced cognitive decline. He calls the effects "modest" but real.
- **Choline** (for acetylcholine, the "electrical highlighter pen" for focus and alertness): the main food source is egg yolks, with smaller amounts in fish, potatoes, nuts, seeds, grains and fruit. He suggests 500 mg to 1 g per day from food and supplements combined.
- Hydration plus sodium, magnesium and potassium are also required for neurons to fire.

## What Andrew takes himself

- Liquid fish oil (1-2 tablespoons per day, often lemon-flavored), switching to capsules when traveling. The target is 2-3 g of EPA per day, not just 2-3 g of fish oil. He takes less if he has eaten fish.
- Alpha-GPC at 300 mg, about 2-3 times per week, early in the day because it can be mildly stimulating (far less than a double espresso). Studies on offsetting cognitive decline used 600-1,200 mg/day. He considers 900 mg sounds very high for himself.
- Creatine monohydrate at 5 g per day, for a long time, as a "baseline insurance policy." He has no phosphatidylserine habit yet. He eats limited meat and does not seek out meat for creatine.
- Glutamine in small amounts regularly, since college, originally for immune support.
- He tells people to check with their doctor first, and notes supplement quality varies (he points to Thorne).

## Creatine, anthocyanins and glutamine for cognition

- **Creatine:** can fuel the brain and may support frontal circuits tied to mood and motivation (emerging evidence for mild depression). Benefit appears at 5 g/day or more and is especially clear in people who eat no meat, with some evidence for meat-eaters too. Caveats: water retention varies by person, and creatine may raise DHT, which could matter for people prone to hair loss. He cites a 2021 review (Roschel).
- **Anthocyanins** (blueberries, blackberries, black currants, thin-skinned dark berries): one study in people aged 65+ used about 428-598 mg of anthocyanins daily for 12 weeks and found improved verbal learning and memory. Effects on DNA damage and LDL oxidation are also reported (review: Afzal, 2019). Supplement range: about 5.5-11 g of blueberry extract, with the higher end seeming more beneficial. Food range: 60-120 g of fresh blueberries per day.
- **Glutamine:** found in cottage cheese, beef, chicken, fish, dairy, eggs, beans, cabbage, spinach and parsley. Supplemental doses typically run 1-10 g/day. It may reduce sugar cravings by engaging glutamine-sensing gut neurons that signal satiety.
- Glutamine may offset cognitive effects of low oxygen (altitude, and likely sleep apnea) by reducing inflammation. He flags apnea as a serious health issue linked to cognitive decline and amyloid burden (Sharma 2018; Quaresma review).

## Three signals that drive food preference

- Andrew describes the brain's "yum, yuck or meh" analysis as three channels:
  1. Taste in the mouth: five tastes (bitter, sweet, umami, salty, sour) are converted into electrical signals that travel to the brainstem and then the insular cortex.
  2. Subconscious gut signals about nutrient content.
  3. Learned association between a taste and its metabolic payoff, which is where belief and conditioning come in.
- **Taste is central:** Charles Zuker's lab at Columbia showed that sweet and bitter are represented by separate neuron populations. Silencing the sweet neurons removes the preference for sweet. Activating them makes animals prefer bitter or plain water. Preference is a central brain phenomenon, not just a tongue phenomenon.
- Normally these systems are correlated, but they can be uncoupled and re-paired.
- The insula handles interoception, meaning the internal sense of how you feel (fullness, anxiety, alertness).

## Gut neuropod cells, hidden sugars and gut health

- Diego Bohorquez (Duke) found neuropod cells in the gut lining. They sense amino acids, sugars and fatty acids and signal via the nodose ganglion to trigger dopamine release, driving you to seek more of that food. This happens subconsciously.
- Robert Lustig's "hidden sugars" in processed foods are described as engaging these mechanisms so you want more, even without an obviously sweet taste.
- Gut health: rather than relying on supplemental probiotics or prebiotics, he favors 2-4 servings per day of low-sugar fermented foods (for example sauerkraut, natto). He cites Stanford work from Justin Sonnenburg and Chris Gardner published in Cell. A healthy gut environment lets these neurons signal appropriately for healthy food seeking.
- Time-restricted feeding recap: begin the eating window at least 1 hour after waking, end it at least 2-3 hours before bed, and keep it at roughly the same time each day because it affects clock genes.

## What the brain is actually seeking, and the artificial sweetener warning

- Work from Ivan de Araujo (Mount Sinai) and Dana Small (Yale) showed the reward is not just taste, dopamine, or even a rise in blood glucose. Using 2-deoxyglucose, which stops neurons from metabolizing glucose, the reward disappeared. Andrew's summary: what you subconsciously seek is for your neurons to be metabolically active.
- Food preference is partly hardwired and partly "soft-wired," like language, so it can be changed.
- **Artificial sweeteners alone:** initially not rewarding (no dopamine rise), but with repeated use dopamine can eventually rise, so people may come to like or crave them.
- **Sweeteners paired with carbohydrate:** the Dalenberg/Small paper (Cell Metabolism, March 2020), "short-term consumption of sucralose with but not without carbohydrate impairs neural and metabolic sensitivity to sugar in humans," showed that taking sucralose along with carbs impairs metabolic sensitivity to sugar. After that pairing, drinking the sweetener alone triggered an outsized insulin release. In a study of children, insulin responses were large enough to make them pre-diabetic and the study was halted.
- Andrew's takeaway: he does not say artificial sweeteners are bad. He says to consume them away from foods that raise blood glucose. If you want a diet soda, "be my guest," but not with a meal.
- He also mentions a Nature paper suggesting some sweeteners can disrupt the gut microbiome, with mixed results depending on the sweetener.

## How to learn to like healthy foods (belief and pairing)

- The protocol: if you want to eat more of a food because it is good for you but you find it "meh" or mildly "yuck," pair it in the same meal with foods that fuel your brain metabolically. For a ketogenic eater, that means ketone-supporting foods. You do not need to hide the flavor.
- Timeline: based on the human sucralose conditioning data, the food should taste at least better within about 7 days, and certainly within about 14 days.
- Limits: this will not override truly noxious foods, which are hardwired avoidance. It works for neutral-to-mildly-unpleasant foods.
- Belief matters: Alia Crum's Stanford work found that people told a milkshake was high-calorie and nutrient-rich had larger insulin and blood glucose responses than those told it was low-calorie, even though it was the identical shake. He calls this a belief effect, distinct from placebo.
- Eating progressively sweeter, highly palatable food shifts the dopamine system toward needing it. Eating less sweet, less palatable food can reset what feels rewarding.
- Food preference circuits stay rewirable even in people with type 2 diabetes or metabolic disorders (de Araujo, Small, "Rethinking Food Reward," Annual Review of Psychology, 2019).
- He suggests that much "food war" certainty (carnivore versus plant-based) partly reflects reinforcement of whatever one eats regularly, though he does not take a side on that debate.
