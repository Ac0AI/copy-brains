## Dopamine is a wave pool, and the trough follows the peak

- Andrew uses an analogy from endocrinologist Kyle Gillette: dopamine is a reservoir, like a wave pool. If you generate big waves, they crash out of the pool and deplete it.
- After a big surge from cocaine, amphetamine, pre-workout stacks, excess caffeine or intense media, dopamine drops below baseline.
- In the trough, the same thing feels less good, and you need more of it for the same output.
- The trough's depth and duration scale with how high the peak was, not how long it lasted.
- People in the trough usually reach for something to re-trigger dopamine, which pushes them deeper. Andrew compares it to a glucose spike followed by a crash below baseline.
- He says dopamine is "the universal currency of motivation-seeking and reward." The brain isn't built to release it in huge amounts without preceding effort.
- Andrew cites Anna Lembke's *Dopamine Nation* seesaw model.
- He describes a "forward center of mass" state (term from a former Navy SEAL), versus flat-footed or back on your heels. His advice is to master the transitions between these states, because you can't be full tilt all the time.
- Burnout, he says, is "trying to be forward center of mass for too long."
- Most people do well to come off the gas pedal a little, especially when a peak comes from pharmacology or an extreme circumstance.

## Getting out of the dopamine trough: abstain, then return to baseline

- Andrew says most addiction and compulsive behavior can be reset with abstinence lasting roughly 30-60 days. To a person craving the thing, that sounds like horror.
- For burnout from work plus stimulants (he mentions tech and finance people on Adderall), the answer is to accept feeling low for about a week. Then you feel a bit less low and return to baseline, and only then can you go forward again.
- He sees a clinical use case for ADHD medication in people who need it. Others have driven themselves into a trough and are chasing anything to get out, when what they need is to stay away from all of it and wait.
- Rewards in smaller things, like time with dogs, help.
- The chocolate test (from Lembke): abstain from chocolate for a week, then taste your favorite piece. It tastes great, but the brain quickly shifts to wanting more rather than savoring.
- Healthy habits aren't a problem, but the same logic applies. Loud music, pre-workout and a hard workout are fine occasionally, but you should also be able to train without them.
- Stacking catecholamine drivers 7 days a week leaves you "cooked" in the afternoon.
- Know your own baseline. Andrew can work about 12 hours a day, five or six days a week, with one full day off (Sunday). Pushing to 15-hour days or 7 days a week makes him "run aground."
- He says you can deviate from your groove by about 15-20%, but anything more extreme leads to trouble.
- Andrew worries far less about energy drinks, loud music or workouts than about high-intensity pornography used regularly, or prescription stimulants taken without need. He says the latter is "a recipe for burnout, depression, or worse."

## Daily energy protocol: NSDR, hydration, sunlight, movement

- **Sleep:** Not everyone needs 8-9 hours. Andrew got about 6 hours 45 minutes the night before the interview. If you wake unrested, don't push through; do a non-sleep deep rest.
- **NSDR (non-sleep deep rest):** 10, 20 or 30 minutes lying down with eyes closed, long exhale breathing and a body scan (feet, then legs, then hands, and so on). It is similar to yoga nidra. Free scripts are on YouTube: Andrew's own, Kelly Boys (a female voice), and Kamini Desai.
  - The instruction is to stay awake, but falling asleep is fine if you set an alarm.
  - Andrew cites a study showing yoga nidra raised baseline dopamine in the basal ganglia by about 60%.
  - He also says there's some evidence it speeds learning, and that his lab is gearing up with Matt Walker's lab to study the mechanism.
  - He did a 10-minute one on the way to the interview, and uses it in the afternoon when depleted.
- **Hydration:** 16-32 oz of water on waking.
- **Morning light:** Get bright light, ideally sunlight, in your eyes first thing. Face east and take sunglasses off. Glasses and contacts are fine. Even through cloud cover it's far brighter than indoor light.
  - It triggers a healthy cortisol peak that supports alertness, immunity and mood, and improves sleep that night.
  - You don't need to see the sun itself, only the light it emits.
  - On very dark winter days (UK, Scandinavia), use bright artificial light in the morning and through the day. SAD lights are a special case at 10,000 lux or more.
  - If you wake before sunrise, you have to wait.
- **Exercise:** Some movement in the morning, even 5 minutes of skipping rope, jogging in place or a walk. Everyone should do at least 2-3 days a week of resistance training plus cardiovascular training. Timing is flexible (morning, lunch or evening), as long as caffeine or pre-workout doesn't wreck sleep.
  - Andrew finds that finishing his workout before 9 a.m. gives him more energy all day. A mid-to-late-morning workout leaves him sleepy in the afternoon.
- **Caffeine:** Some people get light and exercise before caffeine. Andrew fully supports anyone who wants coffee or tea first thing.

## Circadian entrainment, shift work and chronotype

- Body temperature rises in the morning, which makes you more alert. In the early afternoon it peaks, which is a common source of post-lunch sleepiness.
- To fall asleep, body temperature must drop about 1-3°. A cool room with warm blankets helps, as does sticking a foot out.
- Entrainment: if you hate mornings, force yourself up and exercising at 6 a.m. for 3 days in a row. By day four you naturally start waking around then.
- The suprachiasmatic nucleus is driven by sunlight to the eyes, physical activity, meal timing and social engagement. Light, exercise, caffeine, hydration and an early meal shift your clock earlier. By 10-11 p.m. you start feeling sleepy, then you need the discipline to put the phone in another room.
- Chronotype is genetically determined: morning people, typical (bed 10:30-midnight, up 6-8), and night owls. Andrew works best from 6 a.m. to noon and again from 6 p.m. to midnight, and is weak in the afternoon.
- Shift workers have worse health outcomes. The worst case is swing shifts.
  - What helps: regular meal and exercise times.
  - Red-shifted (long wavelength) light at work rather than bright fluorescents may reduce mistimed cortisol release.
  - Stay on the same schedule for at least 2 weeks before switching.
  - Morning light is still useful even at 11:30 a.m. if that is your morning.
- Best ideas often come right before sleep, so he keeps a notebook for capture.

## Cold, heat, food and stimulants

- **Cold plunge or shower:** it raises dopamine, epinephrine and norepinephrine for a long duration. Andrew thinks the value is the state shift (alertness, motivation, feeling great after), not a big metabolic effect ("probably not much").
  - Use the minimum dose. He mocks a friend who did 30 minutes naked and then got sick and felt low.
  - Andrew says to stay in until you want to get out, push through that barrier, then exit. That might be 30 seconds to 3 minutes. Don't fixate on time.
  - A cold shower works fine if you don't have a plunge.
- **Sauna:** He does sauna and cold at least once a week, and often finds himself cranking the sauna too hot. People who run hot and active can lean toward more sauna than cold plunge.
  - Heat is bad for sperm. Sauna, hot tub or hot bath lowers viable sperm count over the next 60-90 days. If trying to conceive, avoid it or use an insulated cold pack in the groin. It is not contraception.
- **Minimal effective dose** applies to training too. For him, a couple of warm-ups and 2-3 hard sets per exercise (low recovery quotient). He says 16-20 sets per week per muscle group depletes him. He trusts his own experience alongside the data.
- **Food:** Eat mostly non-processed or minimally processed food regardless of diet type, in amounts that give you mental energy. For him that means high-quality protein plus fibrous vegetables and fruit.
  - His own pattern: fasts until about 11 a.m. to noon, then eats meat and salad and maybe oatmeal or rice. Dinner is around 7-8 p.m., with more starches and less protein, which helps him sleep. Others do the opposite.
  - His vice is thin-crust pizza or pasta with melted Parmesan, especially with a diet Coke. He also likes carrot cake.
  - His friends lost 30-60 lb and kept it off by eating only meat, fish, eggs, chicken, fruit and vegetables, drinking water and caffeine, and avoiding calories in beverages, over a couple of months. He thinks a caloric deficit explains the loss. He also thinks it retrains the taste-calorie-nutrient association. Afterward a slice of birthday cake tasted disgusting or made them sick.
  - Healthy foods become more attractive the more you eat them and avoid junk.
  - Don't be too restrictive around holidays. GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic have taught that obesity comes from consuming too many calories relative to activity, and he says the average American eats about 3,500 calories a day.
- **Stimulants:** Caffeine and energy drinks are fine in moderation. He drank caffeine during the interview.
- **Alcohol and drugs:** Andrew has never done cocaine or amphetamine and never will.

## Neuroplasticity, stories and breaking habits

- Until about age 25, passive experience shapes the brain. After that, change requires a shift in the neurochemical environment: alertness and focus (the catecholamines dopamine, epinephrine and norepinephrine), followed by good sleep that night and the next.
- Rewiring happens during sleep and in meditative states and rest, not while you're learning.
- Fear is a strong plasticity stimulus because the brain changes to keep you safe. Love, excitement and appreciation also work. Passive exposure, like playing a lecture in the room, doesn't.
- Old dogs can learn: neuron addition persists even in 80s and 90s (Salk Institute study of terminal-cancer patients, who agreed to take a dye that gets incorporated into new neurons). Most learning, though, is rewiring of connections.
- Andrew credits the Hubel and Wiesel Nobel work and Mike Merzenich's finding that plasticity persists across the lifespan.
- Habits are stories with a beginning, a middle and an end. To change one, disrupt the story by challenging it with questions or running the counter-narrative (similar to Byron Katie's work).
  - His suggested exercise: write one page on what a tidy person you really are. You'll know it's partly a lie, but the writing interrupts the default fluency.
  - Sticky notes and rubber bands work for about a day because they lose novelty. The nervous system only changes for what's new and salient.
- Fear-setting (Emily Balcetis, NYU): spend about 5 minutes thinking about everything terrible that happens if you don't meet your goal. It's effective, though nobody likes it.
- Visualization: detailed visualization and writing goals helps, but the end goal isn't always known, so break it into milestones.
- Before writing, he spends 2-3 minutes closing his eyes and focusing on his breathing. He tells himself: if he can't focus on breathing for that long, he can't focus on writing for hours.
- Be careful who you tell your goals to. Supportive friends who say "you should" can give you the reward without the work (tip from a UCSF cardiologist friend). People told they can't often dig in.
- A mix of fear or competition and pure delight in the craft is probably needed. He cites Peter Thiel on competition as anti-creative.

## Creativity: capture and states of mind

- Mode of capture: his notebook holds ideas that surface from the unconscious, and he reviews it weekly or on planes for ideas that feel "sticky."
- The unconscious geysers up as the conscious prefrontal circuits tire at sleep onset. Ideas come in the shower, on walks, or when peeing behind a tree.
- Two states that help creative ideas surface:
  - Body still, mind active. Rick Rubin lies still with his mind active. Karl Deisseroth sits completely still after putting his five kids to sleep and forces himself to think in full sentences. Einstein reportedly stopped on walks to let the mind continue.
  - Body active, mind free-running. Andrew runs 60-90 minutes every Sunday at an easy pace, bored, with no audio. His best ideas surface.
- Meditation types:
  - Focused breathing for 10-13 minutes a day (Wendy Suzuki's lab at NYU) improves working memory and lowers stress.
  - Open monitoring meditation (attention spread to everything around you) is associated with better lab-measured creative capacity.

## Pornography, dopamine and the abstinence reset

- Andrew frames it biologically and says he isn't passing moral judgment. Dopamine rises with intensity and novelty. Anticipation of sex itself raises dopamine.
- Regular intense pornography leads to lower peaks, deeper and longer troughs, and escalating intensity needed for arousal. Easy, high-dopamine input without preceding effort is the concern, as with meth, gambling and food.
- The Coolidge effect: a novel mate shortens the refractory period because dopamine is tied to novelty. Prolactin after orgasm sets the refractory period and suppresses dopamine, which is part of pair bonding.
- He hears from thousands of young men struggling with porn addiction. The answer is simple but hard: a period of abstinence, maybe forever or reduced. He says it can impair real-world arousal and intimacy.
- He can't find a net benefit for young men, and says some people may consume it healthily.
- He says the practical route is to throttle back and build healthy dating and relationship behavior. It requires self-regulation, since legislation won't solve it.
- By contrast, he says he doesn't get messages about ribeye or coffee addiction wrecking lives.
- About 80% of college students have taken prescription stimulants without a prescription.

## Friendship as a protocol, and handling hard times

- **Good morning text:** Text one person each morning. It doesn't have to carry content. It makes you feel part of the tribe. Andrew does it with two friends religiously and starts to worry if he hasn't heard by noon. Extend it with "how'd you sleep?" and so on.
- For no connection at all: adopt a dog, or get a fish. Something that depends on you and gives you connection. Most people need one or two reliable friends.
- Be of service. Be the person who checks in. Walk the same neighborhood, say hello to the barista. Zero-cost community programs exist for addiction in most cities.
- Interests make people interesting. Show up when hard stuff isn't happening, and give full attention (put the phone away or just hang out).
- He keeps a list of about 30 people, with a core 10-15, and checks in on them. Richness of interaction matters more than frequency. He tells close friends he loves them.
- When things went badly, friends (including Lex Fridman, who showed up at his house) sat with him. Andrew says the support is often just "sit in the mud" with someone.
- He mentions his mentor Jim Thiebaud, who sat with him at 14 and gave him a poetry book, and later showed up again.
- Dealing with public criticism: you have three options: counter the narrative, say nothing, or agree. A real adult can say "some of it, yes" and "some of it, no."
  - A public forum isn't where healing happens.
  - Get a few trusted opinions, not 100. Rely on sleep, NSDR, friends and family.
  - He says fame takes away freedom.
- Emotional regulation (from Martha Beck): imagine feelings in one hand and thoughts in the other, in battle. Step into a third position, the "compassionate observer," and sit with the suffering. Ask what feels right for the next 5 minutes, then pick the next best choice.
- Paul Conti: the limbic system doesn't know the clock or calendar.
- Gratitude gives energy rather than complacency.
- In relationships: he admits he struggles to let go and has stayed in relationships too long. He says self-care and communication are most of the work, and he is working on both.
- His daily question: how rested am I, what must I do today, and what can I do to make my life and others' lives better today?

## How Andrew got from lockup to Stanford

- At 14-15 he was in a residential treatment program (teen years of depression, confusion and truancy after his parents' divorce). He realized the only way out was to do the work, so he talked and listened.
- He found skateboarding and punk culture. Training (resistance training and running at 16) taught him the link between effort and outcome.
- On July 4, 1994, after a fight, he wrote himself and his parents a letter forgiving them and saying he'd take control of his life. He took a leave of absence, worked as a busboy, did community college, and made learning his sole mission.
- He returned to university living a disciplined life: study, workout, run, with one party a month. He graduated with honors, then did a master's at UC Berkeley, a PhD, and a postdoc at Stanford. He later got a lab at UC San Diego and was recruited to Stanford with tenure.
- In graduate school he worked 80-100 hour weeks because of his interest-based attention. He parented himself.
- He started posting science on Instagram in 2019, did 20-30 podcasts in 2020, and launched the Huberman Lab podcast in January 2021.
- Advice: stay in school (especially if you're not doing well), because college tells you what to do. Know your own strengths and turn weaknesses into strengths.
- He says the purpose of his life is to make the best choices he can about what to seek out, learn and share, so people can be better versions of themselves.
- His book *Protocols* (operating manual for the human body) is due April 22, 2025.
