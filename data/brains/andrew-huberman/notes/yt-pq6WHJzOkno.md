## Temperature rhythm and where cold fits

- Core body temperature bottoms out roughly 2 hours before waking (about 4 AM if he wakes at 6 AM), rises with waking, keeps climbing into the afternoon, then falls 1-3 degrees toward sleep.
- That evening drop is "important if not essential" for getting into and staying in deep sleep.
- Deliberate cold raises core temperature (rebound thermogenesis), so Andrew treats it as an alertness tool.
- Timing rule: do it early in the day or any time you are prepared to be alert for the next 1-6 hours. Late evening cold can disrupt sleep by blunting the temperature drop.
- His own habit: mid-morning, sometimes as late as 3-4 PM, occasionally 5-6 PM in summer. During a 30-day daily challenge he did it at 10-11 PM and still slept fine, but he was exhausted on those days and says it could disrupt others.

## How cold, how long, which method

- Rule of thumb for temperature: "uncomfortably cold" so you want to get out, but you can stay in safely. This varies by person, by day and by time of day.
- Very cold water can cause cold shock and death. Consult a physician first and progress gradually.
- Ranking of methods:
  1. Cold water immersion up to the neck, hands and feet submerged (most effective).
  2. Cold shower (few studies, but practical).
  3. Going outside in minimal, culturally appropriate clothing until near shivering.
- Heat transfer into water is about 4x greater than into air.
- Ice vests, ice underwear and cryo chambers get little attention from him; he notes most people using cold packs do not know about glabrous skin cooling.
- Weekly dose: at least 11 minutes total per week, split into 2-4 sessions of roughly 2-3 minutes. He calls the 11 minutes a useful consistency target, not a strict threshold, and it comes from metabolism research.
- His own routine: 3 times a week, 2-6 minutes per session, about 11-15 minutes a week, using the "walls" method below to decide when to get out.
- Once 11 minutes a week feels easy, lower the temperature, extend the duration or add frequency (all safely).

## Building resilience: the "walls" method

- Cold reliably triggers norepinephrine and epinephrine, the generic chemical code for stress. Staying calm and in control through that surge trains top-down prefrontal control over the reflex to flee. Andrew's working definition: this is resilience, grit or mental toughness.
- Timed progression option: for example Monday 1 minute, Wednesday 50% longer, Friday double. Track it like sets and reps.
- His preferred method: count walls. A wall is a surge of "I want out." Getting in can be wall one (some people dread it beforehand). The next arrives when it arrives. Pick a number before you start (for example 3, then 5 another day) and get over each wall before leaving, if safe.
- Why walls beat pure time/temperature: you can keep varying the stimulus without endlessly lowering temperature, and it mirrors real life, where stress arrives as surprises like bad-news texts.
- Move your limbs. Staying still lets a warm thermal layer build around the body. Moving breaks it up and feels much colder, giving more stimulus at the same temperature. Keep hands and feet under water.
- Mental strategy: either calm yourself (double inhale through the nose, long exhale through the mouth) or lean in and grind. Like gravel-road driving, the best approach varies.
- Cold causes shortened breath and a 30-80% drop in frontal cortex function, with stress and panic areas ramping up. Doing a cognitive task (hard mental math, speaking in full sentences, recalling difficult information) trains your prefrontal cortex to stay online under stress. He compares this to chess boxing.

## Dopamine, norepinephrine and mood

- Study: Sramek et al., European Journal of Applied Physiology, 2000. Subjects sat up to the neck for 1 hour at 32°C (89°F), 20°C (68°F) or 14°C (57.2°F).
  - 32°C: no meaningful change in metabolism or catecholamines.
  - 20°C: metabolic rate up 93%.
  - 14°C: metabolic rate up 350%, plasma norepinephrine up 530%, dopamine up 250%.
  - Dopamine and norepinephrine stayed elevated for the 2 hours measured afterward.
  - No significant rise in cortisol, which Andrew links to Hans Selye's "eustress" (stress with positive outcomes).
- Andrew says this likely explains why people feel good and focused for hours after cold, even if they hate it in the moment. He notes the water was only moderately cold, so longer exposure drove the effect, and that shorter, colder exposures are not yet studied for dopamine.
- He mentions Dr. Anna Lembke's patient who used cold exposure to help stay sober.
- Mindset matters: deliberate exposure you chose and believe in produces different effects than stress imposed on you (distinct from placebo).
- Optional stack for dopamine:
  - Caffeine 60-120 minutes before cold (study used 300 mg, about 2-3 cups of coffee) raises striatal D2/D3 receptor availability, so more of the dopamine can act.
  - Fasted state already raises norepinephrine and epinephrine. Avoid cold on a full stomach.
  - He cautions against layering so many protocols that they take over your day, and says to consider cardiovascular health and blood pressure.

## Metabolism and brown fat

- Study: Søberg et al., young winter-swimming men, about 11 minutes of cold water immersion per week. Result: more brown fat thermogenesis, higher cold-induced thermogenesis, and more comfort in cold. (Men only, but he sees no reason it would not apply to women.)
- Mechanism: norepinephrine binds receptors on white fat cells, driving UCP1, PPAR-gamma and PGC1, which increase mitochondria and shift gene expression. White fat becomes beige or brown fat, which burns energy as heat.
- Cold-sensing neurons also release norepinephrine directly into fat, not just via blood.
- The acute calorie burn is modest. The longer-term shift in fat type is the main metabolic payoff, and it only helps weight loss within a caloric deficit.
- "Søberg principle" (his name for it): to maximize metabolic benefit, end on cold and let yourself rewarm naturally. Do not follow cold with a hot shower or sauna. He admits he often takes a hot shower afterward and loses some of this.
- Shivering matters: a 2018 Nature study found shivering muscles release succinate, which activates brown fat thermogenesis.
  - To induce shivering: after 1-3 minutes in cold, turn off the water and stand with arms extended (not hugging yourself), air-drying. If that fails, turn the cold back on, alternating 1-3 minutes each. He calls this brutal.
- Babies have lots of brown fat because they cannot shiver. Adults lose it, but white fat is plastic. His review pick: "Adipose tissue plasticity in health and disease" (Cell).

## Exercise performance and recovery

- Strength and hypertrophy: if size and strength are your main goal, avoid cold immersion and ice baths for about 4 hours after training, or move it to another day. Showers are less studied; he doubts a brief one erases gains but says to err on the side of waiting if you are neurotic about it.
- Endurance, sprint and interval work, skill training, or lifting where performance is the focus: no reason to avoid cold right after.
- A meta-analysis of 52 studies found cold water immersion after high-intensity exercise helps muscular power, soreness and perceived recovery at 24 hours, and lowers creatine kinase, compared with passive recovery.
  - It did not help after eccentric-only exercise.
  - Shorter, colder exposures (about 1-5 minutes) looked effective after high-intensity and endurance work.
  - Immersion data only; no good dose threshold exists yet.
- Cold reduces inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and raises anti-inflammatory ones like IL-10. Inflammation from hard training can show up hours to weeks later, so heavy trainers can add cold sessions on separate days.

## Cooling the body: glabrous skin and hyperthermia

- The brain's thermostat (medial preoptic area of the hypothalamus) reads a cooled body surface as "too cold" and raises core temperature. Cold towels on head, neck or torso can make overheating worse.
- Efficient heat exit points are the glabrous (hairless) skin: palms, soles and the upper half of the face. These have arteriovenous anastomoses, direct artery-to-vein shunts that dump heat.
- Hyperthermia study (Heller lab, chemical cold packs): glabrous skin cooling let subjects keep walking on a 9-17% incline at 40°C (104°F) longer and return to baseline faster than traditional neck/armpit/groin ice packs. Not for home replication.
- Hyperthermia is dangerous (heat stroke kills). If someone is overheating, get them out of the heat, stop exercise, and cool palms, soles and upper face (cool water on head or neck may help too).
- The cooling object must not be so cold it causes vasoconstriction, because collapsed veins stop the heat exchange. The commercial CoolMitt (Heller colleagues; Andrew has no financial tie) circulates water at a safe temperature. Cooling one palm alone drops core temperature substantially.

## Palm cooling for training volume

- Study: "Work volume and strength training responses to resistive exercise improve with periodic heat extraction from the palm."
  - 6 weeks of pull-up training with palm cooling between sets: volume up 144% (in experienced subjects).
  - Bench press 1RM up 22% over 10 weeks.
  - Strongest for breaking plateaus.
- The gain is mostly total work, not a bigger first set. Dips example: about 40 on set one, 40 on set two, 38 on set three, instead of a steady decline.
- Palm cooling also reduces or eliminates delayed onset muscle soreness (per Heller).
- Mechanism he proposes: pyruvate kinase, needed for muscle contraction, works only in a narrow temperature range. Heated muscle fails sooner, and heat signals may drive the quitting reflex in endurance work.
- Home protocol (from Heller): warm up first, then hold a cool (not ice-cold) object in the palms for about 1-2 minutes between sets. Examples: bottles of cold water with a few ice cubes, or a bag of frozen blueberries or broccoli. Feet work too, but most gyms will not allow shoes off. Less effective if you are overheated by heavy clothing or a hot room. He suggests using it to push through plateaus rather than every session.

## Testosterone claims

- Andrew found no controlled study showing that cold on the groin or testicles raises testosterone, and asks listeners to send references.
- Two plausible mechanisms he offers: rebound vasodilation and blood flow after cold, and the dopamine surge influencing luteinizing hormone (which can also raise estrogen).
- He thinks direct genital cooling is probably unnecessary (a shower or immersion exposes the area anyway) and could risk tissue damage, so be careful.

## Related energy and focus finding (pre-work exercise)

- Study (Legrand et al.): 101 students did either 15 minutes of moderate jogging (about zone 2: can talk with slight strain) or 15 minutes of relaxation/concentration. The joggers felt more energetic and finished the trail-making tests (A: circling scattered numbers; B: alternating numbers and letters) faster. The key variable was subjective energy.
- Takeaway: before focus-heavy cognitive work, 15 minutes of moderate exercise beats 15 minutes of mindfulness. Sequence: get energized, do the learning, then NSDR or meditation afterward to support plasticity.
- Andrew infers catecholamines drive this, the same theme as cold: anything that raises norepinephrine, epinephrine and dopamine sharpens acuity and mood for a while.
