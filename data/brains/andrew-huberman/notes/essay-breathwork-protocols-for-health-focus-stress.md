## Why breathing matters: the O2:CO2 balance

- Andrew describes breathing as balancing oxygen and carbon dioxide in the brain and body, not just taking in O2. Oxygen passes from lung alveoli into the blood and hemoglobin carries it to cells.
- Carbon dioxide is not just waste. He says it changes hemoglobin's shape to "liberate bound oxygen" into tissues. During exercise body pH drops slightly, which increases oxygen offloading to muscles.
- The brain is the most metabolically active tissue at rest, so it needs a lot of oxygen. Improper O2:CO2 ratios can create a hypoxic state that hurts cognition and physical function.
- Breathing is regulated by parallel mechanical and chemical pathways that monitor O2 and CO2 constantly.

## Overbreathing, nasal breathing and sleep apnea

- Healthy resting breathing moves about 6 liters of air per minute, roughly 12 shallow-to-moderate breaths per minute. Most people overbreathe at 15-18 breaths per minute.
- Overbreathing over-exhales CO2 (hypocapnia), which lowers oxygen delivery to the brain and tissues. The brain becomes hyper-excitable, causing difficulty focusing and anxiety.
- To reduce overbreathing outside exercise:
  - Take small pauses between breaths.
  - Breathe through the nose, because nostril resistance extends exhale duration.
- Nasal breathing at rest and during sleep:
  - Allows maximum lung inflation each breath, increasing oxygen delivery.
  - Warms incoming air, which is healthier for the lungs.
  - Produces nitric oxide in the nasal passages, relaxing smooth muscle and improving blood vessel dilation. This helps remove waste, deliver nutrients and relieve sinus congestion.
  - Is linked by Andrew to facial structure benefits (jaw, cheekbones, tooth and jaw structure).
- Mouth breathing has its place during exercise.
- Some people under-breathe at night. Sleep apnea means breaths are too shallow or infrequent, causing temporary hypoxia and waking. It is more common with excess weight, but lean people can have it too.
  - Symptoms: sleepiness, snoring, morning headache, irritability, inattention.
  - Risks: cardiovascular disease and stroke, worsened dementia, impaired cognition, decreased libido.
  - Options: a CPAP machine; medical tape over the mouth during sleep to force nasal breathing; practicing nasal breathing during exercise and the day.

## Test and train CO2 tolerance with box breathing

- To reduce overbreathing, practice diaphragm control: deliberately expand the stomach "out" on the inhale and let it return "in" on the exhale.
- Andrew uses the carbon dioxide tolerance test, developed by Brian Mackenzie:
  1. Breathe normally for 4-5 breaths.
  2. Inhale once deeply through the nose until the lungs are completely full.
  3. Start a timer and exhale as slowly as possible.
  4. Stop the timer when the lungs are empty and note the duration. He stresses: "it's not about measuring how long you can hold your breath!"
- Use the exhale time to set box breathing durations from a reference table.
- Box breathing is inhale-hold-exhale-hold, each side the same duration, like the sides of a box.
- Practice 2-3 minutes of box breathing, 1-2x per week. Retest after 1-2 weeks and see if each side can be longer.
- Progress means less frequent breaths and healthier breathing patterns. He says this should mean less overbreathing at rest, better exercise performance and reduced anxiety.

## Physiological sigh for fast stress relief

- Technique:
  1. Two consecutive inhales through the nose: one big inhale, then another with no exhale in between, to maximally inflate the lungs.
  2. Exhale fully through the mouth until the lungs are empty.
- He says it rapidly shifts the autonomic nervous system from arousal and agitation toward calm, and is, "to my knowledge," the fastest way to calm down in real time. Even 1-3 sighs can help.
- Daily practice: his lab, with Dr. David Spiegel of Stanford, published a clinical trial showing 5 minutes a day of cyclic physiological sighing reduces overall stress, promotes relaxation, improves sleep, lowers resting heart rate and enhances mood.

## Cyclic hyperventilation for alertness and focus

- Forceful, deep, repeated inhales followed by exhales release adrenaline in the brain and body, raising alertness and capacity for focus.
- Protocol:
  1. Deep inhale through the nose, immediately followed by a deep exhale (active or passive) through the mouth.
  2. Repeat 25 times.
  3. Fully exhale until the lungs are empty and hold for 15-30 seconds.
  4. Repeat the round, for up to 5 minutes total.
- Expect tingling or agitation right after. Over the next few minutes, adrenaline rises and focus and attention improve greatly.
- Repeated practice trains staying calm while agitated and leads to a higher stress threshold.
- Safety: use caution if prone to panic attacks or high anxiety. "Never, ever" do it near or in water or while driving, because of passing out, shallow water blackout, drowning or car accidents, especially with extended breath holds.

## Controlling heart rate with the breath

- Breathing and heart rate are directly linked through respiratory sinus arrhythmia, a normal hard-wired feature involving the vagus nerve, heart, lungs and brainstem.
- Rules:
  - To increase heart rate, inhale longer and more deeply or vigorously than you exhale.
  - To decrease heart rate, exhale longer and more vigorously than you inhale.
- How common practices map:
  - Physiological sighs emphasize exhale: calming.
  - Cyclic hyperventilation emphasizes inhale: activating.
  - Box breathing is balanced.
- Use cases: lower heart rate before sleep, an interview or public speaking; raise it before athletic performance.
- He notes that simply understanding this lets you shift your state anytime without dedicated breathwork.
