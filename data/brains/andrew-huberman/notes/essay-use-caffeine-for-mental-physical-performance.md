## Caffeine dosage: how Andrew sets a personal dose

- Andrew recommends starting at 1 to 3 mg of caffeine per kilogram of body weight, and lower if someone is sensitive.
- Example: he weighs about 100 kg (220 lb), so a tolerable dose for him is 100 to 300 mg. He often takes more because he is "quite caffeine-adapted."
- He says to check what is actually in the drinks you consume. Coffee and energy drinks can contain anywhere from 50 to 500 mg, and as much as 800 mg depending on size and source.
- He warns against extended consumption of high caffeine levels, which raises the risk of headaches, anxiety, irritability and blood vessel damage.

## Hydration, electrolytes and theanine alongside caffeine

- Caffeine is a diuretic, causing loss of fluid, sodium and other electrolytes.
- Andrew suggests drinking an equal volume of water with caffeine to avoid jitteriness, blurry vision or a low-blood-sugar feeling.
- Ideally add a small pinch of sea salt, table salt or an electrolyte powder to that water.
- Most people wake up slightly dehydrated, which can cause fatigue and reduce mental and physical performance, so hydrate with water when having morning caffeine.
- If even small doses cause anxiety or jitteriness, he suggests supplementing with theanine. He notes many energy drinks include 100 to 200 mg of theanine for this reason.

## Timing caffeine through the day

- If you tend to have an afternoon crash, he suggests delaying caffeine for 90 to 120 minutes after waking. Otherwise, caffeine first thing is "A-OK."
- Caffeine on an empty stomach lets a smaller dose produce the same stimulant and performance-enhancing effects.
- Peak focus and alertness occur about 30 minutes after consumption and last about 60 minutes.
- Cutoff: avoid caffeine within 10 to 12 hours of bedtime. For some people 8 hours is fine.

## Caffeine, adenosine and sleep

- Adenosine builds up during the day and creates sleepiness. Caffeine binds adenosine receptors and blocks its effects, so you feel less tired.
- For most people, caffeine has a quarter-life of about 12 hours, though genetics vary. Caffeine at noon means roughly 25% is still active at midnight.
- Even if you can fall asleep, late caffeine can disrupt deep (slow-wave) sleep and subsequent REM sleep.
- His bottom line: "To protect your sleep, it's typically best to avoid caffeine for at least 10-12 hours before bedtime."

## Mood and mental performance

- Caffeine raises catecholamines (dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine), which increase motivation, alertness and drive.
- Andrew cites large-scale analyses showing regular caffeine intake improves mood and well-being and is associated with a lower risk of depression.

## Physical performance and the abstinence protocol

- Research he cites shows caffeine improves reaction time, alertness, focus, coordination, power output and endurance. It also reduces fatigue, increases VO2 max, enhances strength, peak power and muscle contractility, and improves feelings of well-being during exercise.
- **Abstinence protocol:** abstain from caffeine for 3 to 20 days, then take caffeine about 30 minutes before the physical challenge (e.g., a marathon). Effects are more potent after abstinence.
- **Gentler variant:** if full abstinence is too painful, reduce daily caffeine for 1 to 3 weeks, or even a few days, then take your regular dose on the day of the challenge.
- He advises first exploring caffeine's effects during training, then deciding how to use abstinence and "caffeine-pulsing" for specific challenges.

## Dopamine stacking caution

- Dopamine stacking is combining multiple chemical and behavioral inputs to boost dopamine, e.g., loud music, a highly caffeinated energy drink and l-tyrosine before an intense workout.
- Occasional use is fine, but he says not to make it the norm, especially for people who struggle with motivation.
- A dopamine spike is followed by a dip below baseline before returning to baseline, leaving you unmotivated or melancholy.
- "The worst thing you can do in the below-baseline phase" is to spike dopamine again pharmacologically, since that drives baseline down further.

## Using caffeine as a reinforcer

- Caffeine is a strong reinforcer. People subconsciously prefer caffeinated foods and drinks, partly because of improved focus, mood and well-being. Caffeine can also increase the number of dopamine receptors in brain reward pathways.
- **To build a habit** (e.g., exercise): deliberately use caffeine with it. It raises dopamine for mood and motivation and makes the experience more pleasant and rewarding.
- **To break a habit** (e.g., sugar cravings): avoid caffeine. Don't pair coffee with dessert, or cravings are doubly reinforced through both sugar and caffeine's effects on dopamine.
