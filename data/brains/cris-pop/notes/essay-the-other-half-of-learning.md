## Active learning: extraction over consumption

Cris argues that most people consume the same information (books, podcasts, motivational reels) but only some change, and the difference is intentional engagement with their own growth. He is not talking about money or corporate ladders. He means becoming "someone with a solid internal compass", who can navigate life well whether things are going great or falling apart.

- He acknowledges growth is not always linear. What looks like going in circles can be deep processing, and circumstances like mental health or family responsibilities shape when someone can focus on development.
- Even so, he sees a widening gap between people who grow on purpose and people who "just float".
- His warning: "we'll wake up one day realizing we've been busy, but not becoming."
- His priority is maintaining intentionality "even in the mess", rather than obsessing over how fast you grow or how visible the progress is.

## The illusion of passive learning

Cris says most people consume information passively, hoping life will hand them a shortcut if they accumulate enough. He compares it to collecting seeds you never plant.

- "Just because you've consumed the information doesn't mean you've learned the lesson."
- Every book, conversation, or hard experience is raw material. The insights won't chase you down; you have to pull them out, sit with them, make them yours, and let them disrupt your comfort.
- A common failure he sees: people get exposed to powerful information, then file it away like a document they'll "get to later", and later never comes.
- Real learning takes effort and honesty. It means looking at things you'd rather avoid and letting go of stories you've outgrown.
- He points to Alex Hormozi on The Game podcast, who repeatedly says transformation requires active work. Cris's version: if you expect transformation to never challenge your identity, you'll be waiting forever.

## Two questions that separate growers from the stuck

Cris says two questions, which many people avoid, separate those who grow from those who stay stuck: "What's in it for me?" and "What do I learn from this?"

When something goes wrong (a failed business, or work that feels meaningless), growth-minded people switch into "extraction mode" and get "ruthlessly curious" about their own experience:

- How can I change this?
- What pattern am I missing here?
- How can I do better next time?
- What does this teach me, and how do I need to evolve?

He stresses these are asked from ownership and insight, not blame or self-pity. Most people instead treat setbacks like bad weather, something that happened to them, and move on without mining the lesson buried in the mess.

## Courses and paid learning: you pay for the chance to do the work

Cris criticizes people who pay for courses and then sit back expecting transformation to happen automatically.

- His framing: "you don't pay for transformation, you pay for the chance to do the work."
- That chance is only valuable if you engage with it fully. Otherwise "you've just paid for the privilege of owning unused potential."
- The same logic applies to any purchase of information, whether a course, book, or program: the value is in how much you apply, not in owning it.

## Habit: regular honest conversations with yourself

Cris says people who consistently evolve share one habit: they regularly sit down and have honest conversations with themselves about what they've learned and how they're applying it.

- Consuming and moving on only creates "the illusion of progress."
- They ask hard questions about how new information challenges their current way of thinking or operating.
- He says this should be done without "the burden of overthinking or self-criticism". The goal is intentionality, not rumination.

## Treat every experience as raw material for becoming

Cris's core framework is to treat every experience, good or bad, as input for who you become:

- Every book becomes a mirror.
- Every failure becomes a teacher.
- Every success becomes data for what actually works.

He ends on personal responsibility: information is everywhere and opportunities to learn are endless, so the only question is whether you take responsibility for extracting what you need or keep "expecting change to find you". As he puts it, "the human you become is entirely up to you."
