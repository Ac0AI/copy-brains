## Thunder's leadership book on legacy as the ripple people carry

Thunder's leadership book ("Leadership Before It Has a Name", 2025, written by Andreea Popa and coordinated by Cris Pop) closes with a chapter on legacy. It argues that people think they'll be remembered for "the systems we built, the frameworks we created, the decisions we made," but "roles fade. Titles get reassigned. Even the best systems get rewritten." What stays is human: "the silent stories people carry about you." The chapter asks what shows up when your name comes up: unity when things got hard, kindness at the cost of speed, listening and noticing, or drive and discipline. All matter, but the question is which one "created a chain reaction." Its claim is that "leadership leaks through your humanity" and that what a leader leaves behind is "the ripple," the way they changed how people think about what's possible.

## The book on Cris Pop: building Thunder from "we" instead of "me"

The chapter says Cris grew up watching his family give without expecting anything back; they "never counted the cost," and that generosity became his blueprint. When he built Thunder he started from the same place, believing success was "never a solo act" and that real leadership means lifting others up rather than stepping on them. The book says that when he stepped into leadership the world offered a different playbook, "Be loud. Put yourself first or get left behind," which "sounded like ambition" but "felt like betrayal." It argues that game is a trap: leaders who bet on themselves alone "build monuments to their own isolation." The book says Cris never bought it; he knew "the real game wasn't 'me', it was 'we,'" and that leadership is about the people you empower, the trust you build and the impact you leave. The chapter frames Thunder as built by inviting others in: "a team, a community, and anyone ready to believe in a better way to work together." Not everyone will join, and the book says that's fine, since this is "for the believers."

## Leadership as service without a title: Anda, Gabi and "leadership before it has a name"

Thunder's leadership book shows leadership appearing before any role exists. Of Gabi, it says he joined and "simply stepped in and did the work that others didn't have time for." Before he signed a contract or had an official role, "he had already become part of the team." The shelves got built and the desks got organized, and "no one invited him to lead. He simply started leading." The book defines this as leadership "before it has a name": owning what no one asked you to own, cleaning the workspace without being told, researching tools that make colleagues' work easier, showing up consistently. Its advice is to begin small: onboard the new person patiently, organize shared tools, share a resource, "notice what's missing, then fill it, quietly and repeatedly." Of Anda, who works across marketing, event strategy, legal documents and team experiences, it says she is "defined by her presence" and calls modern leadership "being human enough to lean in wherever you're needed," which in its purest form is "service without ego." Its summary line: leadership is "not waiting for permission to care about the work and the people around you."

## Sharing mastery and honest challenge: Adi and Horațiu

The book's portrait of Adi says that when Cris asked him to mentor, he said yes "without hesitation." Adi is described as demanding at first, with tough standards and tougher questions, but someone who "lives for the craft" and codes for the joy of it. He shifts roles as coach, challenger and mentor, and knows "when to push and when to support, when to teach and when to let you figure it out yourself." The principle drawn: "Your knowledge is a gift. Share it. Give it away." His goal is not to be the smartest in the room but to make sure no one is left behind. The book also profiles Horațiu, who questions rules "every time." It argues that raw, unfiltered honesty is "a service" to the team, the ideas and the work, because it sharpens ideas and makes space for better thinking, and that he doesn't pretend to agree to keep the peace. It adds that he delivers it with humor so the goal is "not conflict but progress," and says every team needs someone "brave enough to question the system yet grounded enough to be the glue." A disruptor "who doesn't divide but connects."

## Ownership to the last mile and hard work as commitment: Iuli, Florina and Manu

In the book's account of Iuli, a project near its end was not going to ship on time because of misjudged time estimates and unforeseen challenges. With her contract ending, she could have handed it off. Instead she worked late evenings, documented every blocker and edge case, insisted on a final sync with the tech lead, refined her deliverables and left detailed notes and clear next steps. The feature wasn't complete when she left, but the book says her leadership "wasn't measured by the number of tasks she ticked off, but by the level of care and responsibility she carried until the very last minute." Of Florina, it says hard work is not hours or burnout but "showing up fully, relentlessly, with a commitment to growth." That can mean fourteen-hour sprints or stepping back to recharge, and it comes down to ownership of wasted moments, of the gap between where she is and where she wants to be, and of the process. The book's line: "You don't learn to work smart until you've worked hard, really hard, for a long, long time." Of Manu, it describes quiet excellence: autonomy as her default, punctuality that respects everyone's time, preparedness, attention to detail, and no ego as a follower or leader.

## People first, and leading yourself: Cosmin, Vlad, Anda and Deni

The book says Cosmin's recurring message is "It's all about people," not from a self-development book but because that's how he believes the world should work. It says he would rather do work he doesn't love with people he cares about than the reverse, and it concludes that "the work will never be as important as the lives we touch while doing it," asking readers to show up as humans first and measure success by "the quality of care we bring to the people around us." The book also covers leading yourself. Vlad was "quietly stuck" and found discipline not in a breakthrough but in the people around him: "Their pace becomes your pace. Their standards raise your own." Anda, after leaving a safe accounting path and teaching herself UI/UX before joining Thunder, shows that "you don't need a title to lead." Deni left law school for a creative writing and photography internship with no portfolio, and the book says she succeeded because she "backed it up with grit," built her own proof, and was coachable, curious and committed. Its advice: "Start where you are. The path clears as you walk it."

## "Fight for it": the book's closing call against waiting for permission

The chapter ends with a personal story from author Andreea Popa, not Cris. She recounts playing football as a child in worn-out sneakers, then sandals her mother bought to stop the costs, and finally barefoot, because she was willing to invest in her passion whatever the discomfort. From this she argues adults have traded curiosity for comfort and boldness for safety, and she quotes the excuses that kill leadership before it starts: "If I don't get a raise, I won't lead." "If I don't have the right resources, I won't start." The book's conclusion is that it's time to take ownership, stop letting the world define what's possible, and fight for what you want, "because if we're not willing to fight for it, we'll never have it."
