## Hooks are bait, and they trade honesty for performance

- Cris used to see hooks as "harmless tools", a smart way to grab attention, "like offering someone a seat before you start the conversation."
- He now sees them as trapdoors. A hook "doesn't bring value on its own. It just pulls you in."
- His critique: creators invest disproportionate time in hooks and thumbnails, which is surface-level, even when the content itself has depth and quality.
- When packaging takes center stage, "the soul of the message starts to blur." He frames the cost as losing integrity, the part that trades performance for honesty and reach for relationship.
- The pattern he calls out: thumbnails and titles built on negativity, exaggeration or absolutism.
  - "if you do X, then you must do Y"
  - "here's why you're failing"
  - "everything you know is wrong"
- The irony he notes: creators preach "slow down" and screen-time breaks while making content that screams "run faster."

## Algorithms reward speed, so honest work waits

- Cris argues that "the louder things rise. The honest things wait." Meaningful work often goes unseen because it refuses to compete on the terms the platforms now reward.
- The tools reward speed, not depth, so creators adjust: they "optimize" and "title things more sharply, not because it's who we are, but because it's what we're told works."
- His charge against growth-chasing: people say they care about community and real connection, but in pursuit of faster growth and broader reach they design content for algorithms instead of humans.
- The question he asks of any growth tactic: "what are we really trying to build? And who are we really here for?"
- The costs he lists: "attention without depth. Reach without resonance. Growth without meaning."

## Who to serve: the people already listening

- Cris says to serve the people already listening, "not chase the ones who never stay."
- Long-time followers and loyal readers "don't need to be tricked" and don't need "a dopamine trap to show up." They are there for truth, stories and trust, the things hook-driven tactics put at risk.
- He is not saying to burn the internet down. He asks for "a shift in intention, in tone, and in the tactics we follow", and concedes "we can care about headlines, but not at any cost."
- His preference is to build slowly and honestly, accepting that it "wouldn't go viral, but it would go deep", and that depth is the kind of impact that lasts.

## Alternatives to manipulative hooks

Cris offers three concrete replacements:
- **Honest openings instead of traps.** Beginnings that invite rather than manipulate. Trade hype for curiosity and speak to understand.
- **"Anti-hooks."** A quiet question, a single sentence that feels like someone reaching out, or a moment of real reflection.
- **Clarity instead of confusion.** Summaries, topic lists and chapter-like markers that give a gentle frame of what the piece covers, rather than a dopamine trap.
- He wants content that feels "less like a panic attack and more like a conversation," and content that calms and grounds people.

## Creators he points to as proof

- **Ali Abdaal:** Cris cites him as one of the most algorithm-savvy creators, who shared that the most rewarding work he has done "didn't explode but it endured" because it was optimized for meaning, not attention.
- **Lex Fridman:** Cris says Lex doesn't chase the algorithm. He treats every episode as a moment worth preserving, and you always know what the conversation will explore. That "quiet confidence draws people in deeper than any viral hook ever could."
- His summary line: "the real hook? Not needing one."

## Write for the human, accept fewer eyes

- Cris's closing principle: when you stop writing for the algorithm and write for the actual person on the other side, you remember the point is connection (not views or virality): "the kinds of sentences that land and stay."
- He asks creators to trust that "a slower, quieter kind of growth can still take us where we need to go."
- His stance on the trade-off: "if that means fewer eyes, but deeper hearts, let that be enough."
- "The real work, the work that matters, just needs to speak."
