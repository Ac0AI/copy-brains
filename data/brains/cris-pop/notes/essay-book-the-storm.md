## Courage as the "actual storm" in Thunder's leadership book

"The Storm" chapter of Thunder's leadership book, "Leadership Before It Has a Name" (2025), written by Andreea Popa and coordinated by Cris Pop, argues that leaders watch the wrong storm. They track market crashes, rising competitors and product failures, and build bunkers for economic hurricanes. The chapter argues the storm that matters is internal, and that it is courage. It describes this as a daily, relentless choice, not a one-time lightning strike: speaking the truth when your voice shakes, making the unpopular call because it's right, holding steady when the people you lead doubt you, and looking fear (of failing, of being misunderstood, of not being enough) in the eye and moving toward it. The chapter says to be "the one who moves first, admits first, asks first," and that "real leadership lives" in facing the storm inside. Markets recover and competitors fade, but courage "keeps you standing no matter what changes."

## Courage is remembered, not built: "You weren't born ready. You were born willing."

Thunder's leadership book argues, in its chapter "The Storm," that courage is something people lose rather than find. It says we were born courageous (learning to walk by falling a thousand times) and later learned to hide, to wait and to perfect before presenting, collecting reasons why "not yet" feels safer than "right now." The chapter argues that people who challenge the world did not find more courage than anyone else; they remembered they never lost it and stopped believing they needed permission to be themselves. Stepping into a room where you don't belong yet, or speaking up when your voice shakes, is framed as returning to who you were before you learned to be afraid. The line the chapter lands on: "You weren't born ready. You were born willing."

## Keeping your heart open as an act of leadership courage

The book chapter "The Storm" argues that after early disappointments a small voice says "next time, don't let them get so close," and most people feed it, trading wonder for wariness and hope for realism. It describes the people who refuse: those who still get excited about small things, still believe in people despite the evidence, and choose curiosity over judgment. The chapter says the world calls them naive, but they have felt rejection and disappointment and refused to let that pain become their teacher. Staying soft "doesn't mean avoiding hurt. It means absorbing it without becoming it." The chapter's conclusion: "Your kindness is your rebellion. Keep your heart open anyway."

## Integrity over approval: standing alone when your values aren't popular

Thunder's leadership book argues, in "The Storm," that people are "addicted to approval": saying yes when they mean no, apologizing for boundaries they should be proud of, and twisting themselves to fit other people's comfort zones. The chapter argues the hard part is not learning to stand up for what you believe but learning to stand alone when it isn't popular, and to say no to the easy path so you can say yes to the right one. It urges readers to stop trying to be everyone's favorite person and to be "someone you can live with," someone who "knows the difference between being kind and being convenient." It adds that the people who matter will respect this, and the ones who don't may never have been on your side.

## Accountability in the dark: fingerprints on the fix

The chapter "The Storm" in Thunder's leadership book describes the "loneliest kind of courage": picking up the pieces when something breaks and everyone suddenly becomes busy. When responsibility sits "in the middle of the room like an unwanted guest," most people have perfected the vanishing act. The chapter argues the person who steps in is not doing it to be a hero or get a pat on the back, but because someone has to. It says this courage happens where no one will ever know, with only your own reflection as witness. Its line on credit: "We live in a world full of people who want their name on the success story. But the world runs on people who are willing to have their fingerprints on the fix."

## Being willing to be terrible long enough to be great

Thunder's leadership book argues, in the chapter "The Storm," that feeling like the most unqualified person in the room is "the doorway to everything you want." It challenges the conditioning that if something is hard you're doing it wrong, or that struggling is a signal to pivot rather than persist. The chapter argues the skill that will define you will humble you before it honors you, and the work that becomes your signature will feel like a foreign language at first. "Everyone wants to be good at something. Few people want to be bad at something long enough to become good at it." In its framing, courage is "not in the trying but in the staying": showing up tomorrow after a day that felt like failure, enduring the embarrassment of being a beginner when you're used to being competent, and trusting that discomfort is temporary while quitting is permanent.

## The two bold moves that built Thunder: "You don't wait to be chosen"

In the story that closes "The Storm," Thunder's leadership book tells how Thunder was born from "two men who refused to wait for permission." The chapter says Cris, fresh out of college and "completely clueless about programming," walked into a company where Adi was working. The system had given him theories and grades but left him unprepared for the real world. Instead of applying for entry-level jobs, Cris went straight to Adi and said: "Teach me. Mentor me. I'll work for free. I don't want money. I want to learn." Adi saw commitment and drive, said yes, and mentored and pushed him until Cris went from knowing nothing to being great.

The second bold move, per the book: Cris asked Adi to build a company together, with no roadmap or guarantees, only a vision and the belief that business can be different, a company meant to "disturb the status quo." That, the chapter says, is how Thunder began, with one bold move growing into a dream shared by thirteen people.

## The lesson the book draws from Thunder's origin: make yourself impossible not to choose

The book argues that what Cris understood was: "You don't wait to be chosen. You make it impossible not to choose you." According to the chapter, he created value from the day he walked in, showed up every day, put in the work, learned, improved and lifted others up, and when it was time to build something bigger "he didn't ask if he was ready. He simply stepped up." The chapter generalizes this into its view of leadership: it "is not about titles or permission," but about making yourself so valuable, reliable and committed to the mission that people naturally want to follow you. It also frames Thunder's founding as two complementary acts of courage: one person had the courage to ask for help, the other had the wisdom to give it. Its closing call: don't wait for permission, take the responsibility, own the challenge, and "the world is waiting for people bold enough to step up before they're ready."
