## Cris treats employees as partners and builds on values first

Cris co-founded Thunder with Adi, and they built it on the same partnership dynamic the two of them have with each other. He says they wanted to keep that dynamic as the team grew, rather than a classic employer-employee relationship where staff are fully subordinate and the founders hand down rigid directions.
- He argues that giving people power through partnership makes the dynamic clearer to build on.
- He says the founders did not want to be part of every process or be present from the start.
- Cris calls it a challenge, but says it has worked reasonably well so far, with the team embracing the same collaborative view.

## Delegate with real freedom, or you end up micromanaging and burned out

Cris says delegation means giving people at least partial freedom on certain aspects. Without it, you micromanage, and people become an extension of you instead of creating through their own unique strengths.
- He says the founder ends up "burned out" sooner or later in that setup.
- His main role as leader is to "create a context": logistics, resources, knowledge, ideas and inspiration, so each colleague can perform as well as possible.
- He sees himself as being "in service" of the team, facilitating the work process across programmers, marketing and others, rather than commanding it.
- The shared direction is a collective mission: changing work culture, starting with a healthy culture inside their own group.

## Support experts you hire and learn their field so you can talk to them

Cris wants colleagues, for example the content writer, to become better than him in their domain. He helps their growth so he is not their limit because of his own ego.
- To serve them he reads and learns about their fields, so there is a shared basis for communication. He is a programmer, and he says it would be hard to build a partnership with a content writer if he understood nothing of what she does.
- He expected entrepreneurship to be complex and to cost a lot of time, so the learning effort doesn't bother him.
- He says it is easier to learn together with the team than alone: once you have solved values and mutual trust, it works like mentorship from them. You can ask experts which books to read, how to shape the marketing context, and how to reach shared goals.
- It requires setting your ego aside: you own the company, but you don't have to be number one at everything.

## You can't be the expert at everything, and shouldn't pretend to be

Cris says he realized early that there are things he does better and things he doesn't, even after reading, developing himself and attending events.
- Time is limited. With five to eight main pillars in a company, an hour a week from a founder can't beat someone investing eight hours a day in one pillar.
- Being the cap of all knowledge limits the whole company. Building something big and strong, you can't be an expert in content, programming and sales at once.
- He finds it "liberating" not to present himself as the best at every technical thing.
- The founder should instead be the reference point for values, and carry them in everything he does.
- He contrasts this with a Romanian "jack of all trades" culture that was agile and resilient when resources were scarce, but doesn't scale.

## Define values from day zero and hire for them

Cris says Thunder was designed as an extension of who he and Adi are, so values existed from "day zero". His view is that it's better late than never, but earlier is better.
- They built the team on values from the start and chose people with principles close to theirs.
- They try to make the company vision leave room for people's own visions, adding individual goals to the collective ones.
- They support personal growth beyond the job, for instance backing a programmer colleague who chooses to run marathons. He argues the team benefits too, and that it sets a health example for others.
- On hiring, he admits it's hard to filter for values with only a CV, social accounts and a couple of conversations, and that you will sometimes pick wrong and have to admit it.
- Their approach has been little standard process and more good sense in how they feel the person in front of them.
- He says to resist being dazzled by "that one is a better programmer, so I can sell him better".

## Slow growth and saying no to clients that don't fit

Cris says Thunder said no to projects and growth opportunities where they couldn't bring people in on the basis of their values, for example hiring many people overnight just because revenue could rise.
- They chose "slow growth": less accelerated but more solid, and stronger as culture and values. He says they grew roots before the crown.
- They've also turned down client collaborations that wouldn't be a values-based partnership, even though financially it looks like a loss, and they communicate those decisions transparently to the team.
- His warning: "it's just a small step sideways" is how you drift. One step becomes two or three, and in two or three years you are somewhere else entirely and may lose control. Hiring processes get changed because a client without your values influenced you to add a person and raise revenue.
- If a project's extra income were to be used for something good, like a community event, he'd still say no to a client that doesn't fit.

## Why values matter commercially: consistency and trust

Asked how values affect results, Cris admits they are harder to measure than technical skills, and uses "humble" (smerit) as Thunder's core example.
- He says you can't write with a humble attitude if you aren't humble yourself, and putting the value on the wall doesn't help if people don't live it.
- If team members speak from different attitudes, clients get conflicting messages and the company's overall image gets confused.
- The same holds for content: an article about humility written in a tone that doesn't convey it doesn't build trust. Readers think it sounds good "but not really".
- He adds that people who hate their work end up carrying that into the rest of their life, because you can't be humble after work and not at work.

## Spreading the culture: community, partnerships, voting with money and time

Cris says Thunder wants a healthy work culture inside the company, and also pushes it outward through Thunder Events and Thunder Labs, because they will hit a ceiling alone as the market is made of many other companies.
- They try to open many doors so students, high schoolers, established entrepreneurs and early-stage founders can meet them halfway, and look for people who want to do business like them. Their channels include Instagram, LinkedIn, their site and mentorship with colleagues.
- He believes real change at societal level happens only together with many people, and that past a certain volume the change happens. His analogy: when enough companies give blue-collar workers decent conditions (protective gear, proper schedules, shelter from cold and rain), workers refuse jobs that lack the minimum.
- He says "we vote in two ways, with time and with money": money through what we buy, time through the work we do and the clients we serve.
- He applies this personally, for example no longer going to a restaurant where staff are mistreated, and found one where people are treated well and the food and service are better.
- His view is that consumers underestimate their power to shape the market, and that over one to three years their choices shift it.
