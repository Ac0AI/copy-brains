## Cris's definition of connection: values over transactions

- Cris says his relationships are not transactional. They start from values and principles, not from a financial or material interest.
- Over time he has built an intuition for recognizing values he shares in new people: generosity, honesty, and giving openly.
- He built every relationship at Thunder this way, with colleagues and with community members.
- Opportunities to collaborate and earn money do appear later, but he treats them as "an effect" of a base built on values.
- He describes himself as a "human developer". Early in his career he focused on soft skills in a more human direction. He wants to be a colleague and friend first, and only then the person doing code reviews.
- The interviewer suggested "human entrepreneur", and Cris accepted the idea.
- He initially stayed away from entrepreneurship because the version he saw was almost entirely about profit and very little about people.
- He changed his mind after meeting founders who build companies "with soul, not only for money". That led him to found Thunder to build a work culture around human principles.
- Thunder is three years old and has 13 people. Cris calls them "closer to friends than colleagues". He says his biggest success as a founder is the well-being of his colleagues and how they feel day to day.

## Where Cris's generosity comes from

- He traces it to his family. He saw his parents practice generosity, honesty and help for the community.
- His parents ran businesses in a village near Baia Mare. Those businesses earned money but started from community needs.
- Their question was "if we do something, what do we do?", for example buying a piece of equipment because it could serve people in the village who needed certain work done.
- They did not ask what was most profitable or "the coolest initiative". They asked how to put themselves in service of others.
- Cris says he only understood this influence in recent years. He never had an obsession with being an entrepreneur.

## How to start mentoring, and why it works

- Cris says the common mistake is not trusting that you have something valuable to offer. People think they must become experts first.
- His argument: advice from someone close to you in experience is often easier to digest and apply than an expert's.
- Mentoring someone with little experience takes real effort from him. He has to put himself back in an earlier context and work out what the person can actually understand. The goal is to be useful, not to say things that "sound good".
- You do not need years of experience in an area to start passing knowledge on.
- Steps he suggests:
  - Start with the people closest to you: friends, family, cousins, anyone who could use help.
  - Choose people who are open to change. If someone does not want change, you cannot add value.
  - Open the conversation and see how you can help.
- Scale can be tiny. He says you can start with one hour a week, one Saturday evening, meeting a young person who is at the beginning of their path.
- He treats each one-to-one as a chance for a deep connection. People arrive with their whole story and emotions. They may have no job, a toxic job, or many fears.
- He does not claim that everything he knows is perfectly true. He shares things he did, things he discussed with others, and things that tend to work.
- His reasoning: people need to connect deeply with you before they will actually apply your advice. Without that, the time is largely wasted.

## Thunder's community program structure

Cris describes a ladder of formats, all based on mentoring:

- **Thunder Mentorship:** one-to-one mentoring. Participants include people at the start of their career, and people with years of experience who want to pivot to another industry or another area of the same industry. Discussions focus on their concrete needs.
- **Thunder Book Club:** a small club that discusses books.
- **Thunder Lounge:** an open-doors format at the company. People drop in, can take books as a gift from Thunder, and talk about their ideas and challenges. The aim is to connect and get to know people.
- **Thunder Talks:** a more structured event on a set theme, such as organizational culture or leadership. The team arrives prepared. Attendance has reached about 50-60 people.
- **Thunder Lab:** a newer follow-up to Thunder Talks. Cris says they will pick up to 50 attendees (maximum) who want it, for personal discussions applied to their own situation. For example, a company owner who attended a talk on organizational culture can work through concrete solutions for their own company.

Cris says the formats serve two goals: spreading knowledge to more people, and creating a more visible movement with more collective power than one person would have.

On size, he says the goal of Thunder Talks was never to maximize headcount. The team will probably cap growth, because they want to connect deeply with each attendee and build something that goes beyond the company.

## The book-gifting initiative

- Cris has gifted books since early in his career. At first it was far fewer because he lacked the financial means. He gave more as he could afford it.
- The idea behind it is to make generosity a principle in life, not only in professional work.
- The Thunder team added a multiplier: a person receives a pack of books, for example 10 books made up of 5 titles in two copies each. They keep two for themselves and must give the remaining four packs on to others.
- Nobody is forced. Recipients can keep everything. In practice Cris says most chose to pass them on.
- Beyond the multiplier effect, he says people realize that with a small financial effort they can bring joy to others and also to themselves.
- He says the book "What We Owe Each Other: A New Social Contract for a Better World" by Minouche Shafik gave him something close to a revelation.
- Takeaway he drew: many things we treat as fixed in society exist because we all chose to believe in them, and by uniting we can change them toward more well-being, knowledge and relationships with meaning.

## Doing it inside a company and finding the time

- Thunder started giving back to the local community from the beginning, not after it had grown.
- The company has one core pillar, software (clients, programming, projects). Cris calls it the engine that provides the money to run community activities.
- He defines generosity broadly. Events count as generosity to him, because the team's knowledge and experience are valuable when people can come and extract and use them. He argues this can be more valuable than a financial gift, since someone early in their journey may not know what to do with money.
- How they find the time: they prioritize. The 2024 plan started from events, community and the initiatives for the community, which Cris calls "the foundation of the company" as vision and meaning. From there they built the plan for programming:
  - what kind of clients they need and how many,
  - how much time to invest,
  - how many colleagues work on external client projects versus internal projects.
- Condition he stresses: clients must not suffer because of community initiatives. Both have to work, which he calls complex and different from case to case. His view is that if you want it enough, you can find a solution.

## What the company gets back, and the hard parts

- **Fulfillment:** he says every colleague feels it. The team shares messages from people with each other, he gets most of them, and he forwards them to colleagues. He believes they feel their work has direction and a vision that is materializing.
- **Closer relationships:** organizing an event for 50-60 people is not trivial, and things go wrong. These experiences bring the team closer.
- **A community around the company:** people return from event to event and join all initiatives. Cris's reading is that the group's strength grows, and being united from the start gives any new initiative a better chance.
- **Hard part, clients:** Cris says finding partners is a challenge. He says they have a few partners they have worked with for two to three years, and they are grateful. But over time they had to refuse many potential clients because they could not offer the work context they want for their colleagues. Taking them would have meant damaging the work culture for profit, or for money that would at best cover salaries even with no profit left.
- He says this is still a challenge today. Because the community and the company's people come first, he has to turn some partners and clients away when there is no common ground.
- **Hard part, mentoring:** people often come in a difficult period, with no job, a toxic job or many fears. Cris says handling their emotions and helping them have trust in you and your ideas is a challenge, but he says relationships are worth it.

## Cris's closing principle

- His message: those who know him know he believes strongly in generosity. His conclusion is that generosity is how we gain meaning and a life experience that fulfills us.
- He asks everyone to apply that value in their own life.
