## Thunder's Substack on values that cost something

In a Thunder Substack essay, "We carry values, but do we let them carry weight?" (2025-06-27), Andreea Popa argues that people claim values such as honesty, empathy, loyalty and presence because they feel right, without always paying for them. She says that "real values show up when picking them feels like choosing against your ego." The essay is her own reflection, not Cris Pop's words.

## When a value becomes inconvenient, according to Andreea Popa

Thunder's Substack argues that values only mean something once they are tested. Andreea writes that honesty is easy until it means saying something someone doesn't want to hear, kindness is easy until we're angry, and loyalty is easy until it's no longer convenient. Her test is: "If it's always easy, always convenient, maybe it's not really a value yet. Maybe it's just a preference."

She lists what living a value often looks like: taking the longer route, keeping your mouth shut when you want to win the argument, showing up for someone when you're tired, not choosing revenge when the chance is there, and making the harder choice even if it hurts a little. She adds that the choices that matter most often happen in complete solitude. She admits, in her own voice, that she caught herself being kind only when it felt easy and patient only when she wasn't in a rush, and realized she was "chasing the feeling of being good, not the weight of actually living it."

## Expecting more from others than we give, in Thunder's Substack

In the essay, Andreea Popa points to how naturally we expect the best from people: friends who understand our silences, colleagues who read between the lines, family who know how to love us without being told. When they fall short we say "They should've known" or "If they really cared, they would have...". She asks readers to turn the question around: have I ever misunderstood or hurt someone without meaning to? She writes that most people are "doing the best we can with the emotional maps we've been handed," and those maps can be incomplete.

Her proposed shift is from "why did they fail to show up the way I needed?" to grounding questions: can I hold space for someone who is still growing, and can I accept the version of them that exists today rather than the one I wish they already were?

## Forgiveness as a lived value, per Andreea Popa

Thunder's Substack defines forgiveness not as pretending the hurt didn't happen, but as "choosing not to reduce someone to their worst moment." Andreea frames it as remembering that others, like us, are works in progress. She calls that "soft remembering" on some days "the most honest value we can live," placing forgiveness among the values that cost something.

## "This is water": noticing what is invisible

Andreea Popa retells the parable from David Foster Wallace's commencement speech *This Is Water*: two young fish meet an older fish who asks "Morning, boys. How's the water?" and one later asks the other, "What the hell is water?" Her reading is that the most obvious, important realities are the hardest to see and talk about.

She says real awareness is about learning how to think "in the deeply human sense": choosing what to pay attention to and building meaning from your own experience. She links this to Cris, noting that Cris explored "seeing what's invisible" in the previous Thunder newsletter. This is the only place the essay references Cris. Her warning: "if you don't choose your attention, the world will choose for you," and you drift through life as if the water is something happening to you rather than something you swim through.

## Where values actually live, according to Thunder's Substack

The essay closes by arguing that most days we forget: the traffic jam is not about us, the slow person at the pharmacy may carry a burden we'll never know, and the world is not a movie with us as the main character. Andreea writes that real life, "the true work of being human," is not achievements or goals ticked off, but "what we notice, what we choose to care about, over and over and over again."

Her conclusion is that values live "in the moments when being good costs us something and we choose it anyway." Surfacing, she says, means "not into a different life, but into a different way of living."
