## Why Cris defines himself as an "ordinary man" and what drives his leadership

- Cris founded Thunder, and says its approach rests on human leadership and civic entrepreneurship.
- For him, being an ordinary person means choosing every day to make a small effort for others: help, contribute, "leave something good behind you," even if nothing is spectacular.
- He believes real meaning sits in simple gestures: a word or an act that changes someone's day.
- He learned this from his parents, simple people who were always present for those around them. What mattered was how sincere the gesture was, not how big.
- His lesson: real impact comes from the consistency of choosing to be good every day, not from big things.
- Generosity is the value under everything he does, personally and at Thunder. He defines it as creating contexts where people can grow, collaborate and support each other, not just giving.
- He sees the world's challenges as chances to offer more and build together, not just obstacles.

## Self-leadership: where it came from and what it changes in teams

- He reached the conviction out of frustration. He worked in environments where people waited to be told what to do, waited for permission to decide, or complained about problems without acting. He realized he did the same in some areas of his own life.
- His core realization: "nobody comes to solve your life for you." No boss, partner or magical opportunity will. The only one who can change something is you.
- What changes when people adopt it:
  - They move from "I can't because..." to "what can I do with what I have?"
  - Motivation comes from inside; they stop waiting for validation or instructions at every step.
  - They take initiative, first small, then bigger.
- In a team, the difference is felt immediately. When people think like leaders and own their work and results, meaning stops being something to search for and is built every day. Work becomes part of their identity, and people become more involved and more grateful.
- He frames self-leadership as "taking your power back."
- He admits it is not easy: it takes courage to take responsibility for everything around you. But once you understand it, you can't go back; the power was always there and you forgot to use it.

## Thunder Talks: how a 4-person meetup grew organically into a movement

Cris's example of civic entrepreneurship starting small:

- **Start:** 4 people in Thunder's own office ("Thunder home"). No grand strategy; he wanted a space where people could talk openly about things that truly matter.
- **First editions:** about 4 editions, max 15 people, because the space allowed no more. People kept coming, asking questions and bringing friends.
- **Step up:** rented a room at ABC Incubator. 35 people the first time, then 60. Growth was organic, "without forcing anything."
- **Edition 7:** even more interest, with Alexandra Crăciun as speaker. Later speakers included Mădălina Hotca, Tünde Macarie, Cristina Frei and Tudor Ciuleanu.
- **Speaker principle:** always bring people with authentic stories, close to the community, who "live what they speak."
- **Today:** the event runs at Cinema Florin Piersic.
- **Lesson:** "We don't have to know the whole road, we only need the courage to take the first step." What began as a meeting of four became a movement that regularly brings together curious, involved people who want change, learning from each other and forming real connections.

## The problems that motivate him: authoritarian leadership and education

- The biggest challenge that pushes him to act is authoritarian, rigid leadership that puts control above people and limits their potential.
- His response is to build a different model: human leadership and civic entrepreneurship based on empathy, collaboration and real involvement of people in decisions that affect them.
- He is also concerned that the education system often fails to prepare young people for real life.
- Thunder's answer: events for students to help them build a solid foundation, professionally and personally. The Thunder Way offers 15 scholarships for students who want to grow and contribute actively to the community.
- He treats these challenges as opportunities for transformation, not barriers. The goal is a community of leaders defined by how they do things, not by titles, who put values, empathy and generosity at the center of their decisions.

## Who you are when you're not the leader: no mask, same person

- His answer: he is the same person regardless of role. His relationships with colleagues, friends and community members rest on respect, empathy and openness.
- He argues a leader should not put on a mask when moving into a leadership position. Too often leadership is understood as authority, when it should be "a space of support, listening and collaboration."
- Leadership doesn't change him; it only gives a bigger platform for what he does anyway every day: listen, encourage and build authentic relationships.
- Regardless of title, he stays a curious, involved person eager to learn.
- Core line: being a leader "does not mean rising above others, but staying close to them and to your values."

## Learning from ordinary people: his mother's village shop

- He names several ordinary people who inspire through their growth (Dragoș Pătraru, Andra Pintincan, Marius Balo, Alexandra Crăciun), but picks his mother as the single example.
- She runs the shop in the village with a healthy respect for work and responsibility. For her it isn't just a business but a service to the community: many people can't get to town, so she makes sure they find what they need, even if that means extra trips or stocking products that aren't profitable.
- Specific habits:
  - Sets aside bread for someone who comes late from work.
  - Buys certain products for just 2-3 people who need them, because it makes them happy.
  - Is attentive, helpful and always present with each person.
- Result: the shop becomes a meeting and connection place; people come to talk and be listened to, not only to buy.
- His takeaway: real impact doesn't come from big actions but from "small, consistent gestures done with care every day." The differences that truly matter happen daily, "not just once a year."
