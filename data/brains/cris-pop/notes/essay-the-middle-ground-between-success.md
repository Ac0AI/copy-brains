## Strategic quitting beats the success-or-failure binary

- Cris rejects "manifest your dreams" absolutism: "everything I want will happen if I just want it badly enough" does "more damage than good."
- He argues people are taught only two outcomes, success or failure, with no in-between. When you aren't "winning," you assume you're "failing," so you keep fighting battles that no longer matter and force puzzle pieces that don't fit.
- His reframe: "I don't believe in failure. I believe there is only information." Stopping something that isn't working makes space for something more aligned with your needs.

## When to stop: the signals and the capital logic

- Sometimes what you want doesn't happen. Sometimes it isn't supposed to. If you try different approaches, push harder, and still hit the same wall every time, Cris says there are "no better signals to stop than those."
- He treats time, energy and attention as capital: "why spend capital (time, energy, attention) on assets that don't grow?"
- Pushing calibration:
  - Push too little: nothing moves.
  - Push too much: things break.
  - Push wisely: life opens.

## Strategic quitting as an edge

- Cris says the real edge is strategic quitting: "deliberately saying no to 99% of opportunities so you can say yes to the 1% that could change everything."
- It requires patience, and progress may look slow. In his words, "what looks slow is often leverage others can't see."
- He frames abandoning a fight as a gift to yourself: "Every fight you abandon is a gift to yourself."

## The battle traffic light: a decision tool for which fights to take

Cris uses a three-color filter for deciding which challenges are worth pursuing:

- **Red**: not worth it. Drop it.
- **Yellow**: maybe worth it. Weigh cost against benefit.
- **Green**: go for it, because the battle has real impact.

## Cost-benefit table for yellow situations

For anything yellow, he suggests a simple table with these columns:

| Battle | Potential Benefit | Cost (time, energy, relationships) | Worth It? |

- The cost side counts time, energy and relationships, not just money.
- The rule: if the cost outweighs the benefit, move on.
