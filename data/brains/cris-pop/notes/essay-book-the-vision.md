## Vision as something lived, not announced

Thunder's leadership book (chapter "The Vision") argues that most leaders try to speak their vision before they have lived it, treating it as a blueprint or a clean statement. People do not follow words or a nicely designed plan; they stay because of resonance, and "resonance can't be faked." The chapter frames vision as relational: you do not hold it over others, you invite them in. If it truly lives, it will outgrow the leader, so the job becomes holding it lightly and walking it humbly rather than controlling it. Vision, the book says, "has always been a frequency you learn to live at until others can hear it, too."

## The leader as messenger, not owner

The chapter argues that the visions that matter belong to no one. A leader is "a messenger," carrying something that was already trying to happen. Great movements may start with one person but only become real when others help shape them. This changes how a leader leads: you are not convincing people to follow your plan, you are inviting them to witness how moved you are by what you carry, and letting them contribute. The book asks leaders to hold the vision with integrity, make space for other voices, and walk it daily in their choices and priorities. The more a leader serves the vision rather than using it to serve themselves, the more faithfully it moves through them to others.

## Vision should show people their place in the story

Thunder's leadership book argues that vision is not just a picture of a destination to pump people up. Visions that work help people see themselves inside it, answering the quiet question "Do I matter here?" The chapter contrasts "We want to grow our business" with "You are the reason we're able to grow": one is a statement, the other tells people why they matter to the story. People want to be part of something bigger, but also to know their specific chapter is essential and that the story wouldn't work without what they uniquely bring. When they feel that, they stop working for something and start building it with you.

## Keep the vision an open canvas

The book argues that a vision should never be finished or polished. A vision that is too complete is hard to breathe in: it may be clear and impressive, but it leaves no room for new voices or for real life to unfold. A better vision is an open canvas, a beginning waiting for other hands. Its essence is not how much it defines but how much it can hold, above all people, with their change, creativity and courage to show up differently. The chapter's practical advice: "leave space. Write in pencil. Create margins where others can dream."

## The test of a vision: it keeps moving when the leader is absent

The chapter argues that a vision is working when it keeps moving even when the person who started it isn't in the room. That requires trust, clarity and shared ownership from the beginning. The leader doesn't need to obsess over details; they need people to understand what matters most, so they know how to move and feel confident doing so in real decisions. This takes presence: listening, sitting with people when they're stuck, asking meaningful questions, and letting them run with parts of the mission and feel the weight of it. If people aren't stepping up, the book says, that is not about them; it signals the vision isn't clear enough or hasn't reached their hands. A leader still steers the ship, but if only one person touches the wheel, no one else learns to navigate. If the vision lives on in others, "you led in a way that mattered."

## Make the vision touch the ground every day

Thunder's leadership book argues that "a vision dies in the clouds if it never touches the ground." Its prescription is to pick one daily act that embodies the vision so clearly it can't be ignored, and keep doing it until people know you mean it. Examples given: if the vision is connection, look someone in the eye every day and make them feel they belong; if it is transformation, take one daily step that terrifies the old you; if it is service, leave one person better each day. A vision you don't walk daily is "just a story you tell," and a leader who doesn't work for their vision won't make others believe in it.

## How Cris carries the vision at Thunder: hiring for resonance

In the chapter's closing story, Andreea Popa writes about watching how Cris Pop handles vision. Most leaders announce it once and hope it is remembered; she says Cris treats it as something living that needs to be nurtured and protected. Her example is hiring. She describes hearing that Cris spends far more time in hiring conversations asking who someone is as a human being than what they can do technically: what drives them, what they believe in, where they see themselves growing. Andreea says she first wondered whether the focus should be on whether a candidate can do the job, then realized Cris is looking for people who already resonate with what Thunder is building. Systems, tools and methods can be taught, she writes, but you cannot teach someone to care about building genuine community or to be excited about showing up to events and connecting with people.

## Protecting the vision means people who make it more itself

Andreea Popa writes that people who truly get what Thunder is about become "guardians of the vision" too: they suggest ideas that strengthen it, call out what doesn't fit, and bring energy to conversations. In her telling, Cris is not just hiring employees but finding people who were already looking for this story, even if they didn't know it. She concludes that protecting the vision is not keeping it safe from change but making sure every person who touches it makes it "more itself, not less." Cris carries the vision, sometimes through people and sometimes through conversations, and "always points out that this vision belongs to us, and we belong to this vision."
