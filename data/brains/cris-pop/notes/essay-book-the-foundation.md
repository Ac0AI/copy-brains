## Leadership is built, not born: the premise of Thunder's leadership book

Thunder's leadership book, "Leadership Before It Has a Name" (2025), opens its chapter "The Foundation" by telling readers to discard clichés about being born a leader, management-book bullet points, and the idea of a mystical leadership factor. The chapter argues that leadership is something you build. It is "shaped in your collaboration, sharpened in your humility, and strengthened in your quiet work of showing up again and again." It is born "in the small spaces where you decide to care enough to act."

The evidence it offers is ordinary: someone with no title and no "leadership gene" steps up when no one else will. The chapter concludes that leadership is available to anyone willing to do the work. It closes the same way, with a "Keep digging" image. Leadership accumulates "like dust, decision by decision." There is no exact day you become a leader. You dig each time you choose the harder conversation, admit you don't know but commit to finding out, or stay when others leave. What you uncover is not hidden talent but "your capacity to care about something bigger than yourself." Leadership is "something you do, again and again, until doing it becomes who you are."

## Self-leadership and purpose before frameworks, per Thunder's leadership book

The book argues that leadership starts with the question "Who's leading your life?" If you don't decide who you want to be, your past, your habits, fear or ego will decide for you. The chapter asks readers to reflect on:
- who they are at their core
- what shaped them
- what matters to them
- what they avoid
- what they stand for when the world isn't looking
- how they will pay it forward

It calls purpose the step people keep skipping, "overused... but strangely, still... underlived." The chapter says to ask why you are leading, beyond the company mission or team goal. It also asks what leadership gives shape to inside you, what you are trying to prove, protect or heal, and who you hope to become. People who chase execution before intention end up in roles that empty them. In the chapter's words, "Leadership without purpose is not quite leadership. It's more like performance," and performance burns you out before it fulfills you.

## Cris's formula for self-trust: Confidence = Certainty + Faith

The chapter argues against hovering over a process. Monitoring every step is not care, and the more you control a process, the more you lose what makes it powerful. It reframes "Do you trust the process?" as "Do you trust yourself enough to let the process work without your constant supervision?" The author, Andreea Popa, writes that self-trust rests on three things: what you know, what you can do, and what results you have achieved and can achieve again.

At one talk, Cris shared the formula "Confidence = Certainty + Faith."
- Certainty comes from looking back at accomplishments, the wins that prove you can trust yourself.
- Faith comes from looking forward, believing in your potential before there is proof.

The book names negative self-talk as what kills self-trust, because it erases your wins. Its fix is to catch the thought and add "BUT": "I'm not good at this, but I did X before, and I can learn." The word is the bridge "from doubt to possibility."

## Extracting growth from setbacks and living one idea deeply

Thunder's leadership book says people who eventually lead differ from those who stay stuck by asking two questions: "What's in it for me?" and "What can I learn from this?" After a failed project or meaningless work, future leaders "flip into extraction mode." They ask what pattern they are missing, how to do better next time, and what the experience is teaching them. Most people treat setbacks as bad weather. In the chapter's framing, every book becomes a mirror, every failure a teacher, and every success data on what works.

The chapter says Cris has a habit of sitting with an idea until it transforms him. He reads one book ten times and replays the same podcast episode again and again, instead of chasing the next big thing. The book turns this into a four-step practice:
1. Choose one powerful idea that fits your current leadership challenge.
2. Ask how it applies now, where you are resisting it, what would change if you acted on it today, and who you would become if you embodied it.
3. Take one small action daily based on it.
4. Teach it to someone else.

The chapter's line is: "Most people are drowning in insights but starving for integration."

## Break the old playbook and write your own rules

The chapter argues that the old management playbook has expired. It cites managers running meetings "like it's 2010," executives who think digital transformation means buying software, and leaders who measure hours instead of impact. It names remote work, AI and Gen Z's different expectations as changes already here. The authors recount Thunder's commitment when it launched its first tool: "We're going to build, fail, learn, and rebuild until we create something that changes lives. We won't wait for permission, perfect conditions, or complete information." Readers are asked which assumptions, methods and comfort zones no longer serve them.

The book also sorts the rules governing your life into three types:
- **Moral rules (what you must keep):** the ones that shape integrity and how you treat others.
- **Intentional rules (what you choose to keep):** the ones you create to move toward your vision.
- **Limiting rules (what you need to break):** unspoken expectations such as "Don't ask for more than you deserve," "Don't challenge people above you," "Don't start before you're ready," and "Don't show your flaws."

Limiting rules show up as hesitation: not speaking in meetings, not applying, not starting. The test is to ask "Which rule am I following right now?"

## Kindness without reason, and excellence beyond the minimum

The book argues for being good to people "for no reason at all." It says that if kindness is treated as currency or reciprocity, it is not leadership. It asks leaders to give kindness "like air," like a lighthouse that shines without asking who deserves it. In the chapter's words, "if your leadership requires a reason to be kind, it isn't leadership but rather performance."

In "The minimum is crowded," the chapter argues that in every project you choose between what is expected and what is right. Leaders care about the work because they care about the impact and the people behind the tasks. Half-hearted effort creates half-hearted results and then half-hearted teams. Mediocrity spreads, but so does excellence. Before submitting work, the chapter suggests asking: "What am I capable of that I'm not giving? What would make this matter more?"

## Noticing, and culture built by example at Thunder

The book says culture does not come from rules or posters but from people who lead by example. A leader's job is "not to have all the answers" but to hold the vision, protect the values and model what matters most. It gives Thunder examples of unassigned leadership:
- Anda noticed the Instagram links page needed clarity. She reorganized it into thoughtful categories without being asked.
- Deni started cooking lunches to bring people together, which grew into a quiet tradition.
- Colleagues give up their birthday gifts to support a cause.

The chapter says this happens constantly at Thunder: someone spots a problem early, improves a process unprompted or offers help before it is requested. "No one assigned them these roles... The rest of us followed."

It asks leaders to become "a detective of the human experience." That means noticing small changes, such as a quiet teammate who now offers three insights per meeting instead of one, or a collaborator whose replies slowed from hours to two days. It also suggests asking:
- what invisible behaviors shape culture more than words do
- who is quietly leading without a title and how to amplify them
- what moment of kindness or courage to acknowledge before the day ends

## Why Thunder was built, and the invisible backpack

The chapter says Thunder was built because its founders heard a call that "the game itself is broken." It names hierarchies that crush creativity, metrics that measure motion instead of meaning, and cultures that reward conformity over courage. Its stance is "We're not here to climb someone else's ladder. We're here to build bridges to places that don't exist yet." It gathers people who stop asking permission and start taking responsibility, and who understand that "true power comes from empowering others."

A section on gratitude argues that leaders get to prove "high performance and deep care multiply each other" and that command-and-control leadership is crumbling. Grateful leaders see broken systems as opportunities and frustrated colleagues as people "waiting to be unleashed."

The chapter ends with a story of a teacher and an "invisible backpack" of beliefs. A student asks whether things can be taken out, and the teacher answers, "Yes, but you have to notice what's inside first." The book's lesson is to take inventory, own your shadows and choose your burdens with intention. It says the leaders we need "are not perfect," but are brave enough to unpack, face themselves and keep walking.
