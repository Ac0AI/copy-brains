## Generosity as a default operating principle

Cris names generosity (dărnicie) as one of his core values, rooted in his family, where his parents ran businesses with people at the center. He avoids calling himself generous, since saying it undercuts the value, and aims for his actions to show it. At work it covers salary design and negotiation, office setup, and education spending (courses, books, a better computer). His working test: do more than what the other person would expect from him. The same applies to clients: deliver more than they expected from a day's work or a whole project. Thunder has a "library of generosity" at its Cluj office where visitors can take a book home, and it gives away content through events it runs in Cluj.

## Collective before individual

Cris's second principle is unity: the collective always comes before the individual, himself included. He applies it through meritocracy, so no one gets treatment based on personal preference or friendship. He and his co-founder Adi also agreed in hard periods (weak project pipeline, weak finances) that Thunder never lays off a colleague because the founders failed to bring in enough clients. Cris's reasoning: founders carry most of the responsibility for how things look, and they can't throw people out of the boat "at will". Joining a team is a vote of trust and loyalty, even after two or three months. A person given time may still be there 10 years later. He excludes people who aren't serious or who don't do the work.

## Growing leaders instead of delegating tasks

Cris wants Thunder to be a team of leaders rather than "one or two people who think everything and ten who execute, factory style". He compares two teams: 2 thinkers plus 10 executors versus 12 people each leading in their own area. He says the second one wins, because a group of 12 beats its smartest member and diversity of perspectives is lost when only one person thinks. His example is design: he can't write a ticket detailed enough for their designer, who has the artistic sense and strategy he lacks, so he lets her lead.

- Everyone is a leader on something and a follower elsewhere. The specialist leads, but is not an authoritarian leader who can't be questioned.
- Leaders get decision power through what they have learned and how they have grown, not because of a title.
- If people's input is never asked for or heard for years, they turn passive. Then the manager wonders why no feedback comes.
- He sees growing leaders as the highest form of leadership. If you fear it, he says you read it as "digging your own grave".
- Advice for founders who fear it: experiment on a small project. Give someone power in one area and see how you feel. He says it feels liberating, and what follows is a sense of fulfillment and meaning.

## Experiment to resolve disagreements

When he and a colleague hold opposite opinions, Thunder's default is to experiment. If the idea is too big or the resources aren't there, they scale it down to a small test and see what happens. Cris says better communication, understanding emotions in yourself and your colleagues, makes conflicts easier to handle. They become more constructive because people understand why someone says things a certain way and what the shared goals are.

## Personal development as an accelerator, run as a team practice

Cris sees business as an extension of who people are, and as a chance to be forced to grow. He doesn't treat development as a precondition for making money. He treats business as the context where you apply what you read.

- Baseline targets: one book a month plus courses, "obligatory" in the sense of a shared practice, not a forced one.
- Recent focus is leadership and ownership.
- "Personal development" includes sport, nutrition, rest and working through fears or small traumas, not only books.
- Colleagues support one another. If someone has low drive on a topic, others create context to help them find the motivation. Cris says he himself misses targets fairly often. He treats failure as a weekly constant for anyone who plans for more than they can carry, and he asks what he can really do this month versus the next.
- He measures progress by comparing himself now with himself three years ago, calling the difference "enormous". He says it comes partly from his own work and partly from colleagues' feedback and example.
- He argues the work/life wall is an illusion. Emotions don't stay at the door ("run like IT software in the background"), and they affect relationships and work quality either way. He rejects "close the door on emotions".

## Healthy closeness between boss and team

Asked what to do if employees get too close to you, Cris says people who worry about this likely don't have healthy friendships. In his view a healthy friendship, work or personal, keeps principles and standards, and a relationship in which you accept anything from the other is closer to manipulation. He says a manager can set limits clearly and firmly even in a close relationship, and Thunder applies this, though he admits "it's not a perfect process" and they are still learning. Asked about the downside of investing heavily in people (they become good enough to leave), he says he doesn't treat it as a risk. A mediocre team can lose people just as easily, and a strong team lets you build more interesting things. He states the real limit on a business is the founder: if you don't invest in your growth and don't let others grow beyond you, the ceiling is easy to calculate.

## Excellence, intentional discomfort and advice to start

Cris names excellence as another value, meaning curiosity about how good he can become rather than beating others. It is a moving target, because "the more you grow, the further it moves away". He says that early in life we are products of our environment, but from roughly 18-25 onward we can intervene consciously. He calls choosing to read for an hour instead of lounging on the couch "intentional suffering", friction against what the brain wants. His claim is that it prepares you for unavoidable hardships such as a bereavement or a business failure that returns you to zero.

On handling hard times, he says he too slips into victim mode briefly but doesn't stay. He tries to change the situation, or change his perception of it, and sees life as an opportunity to learn.

His advice for people starting out:
- Read anything and stay curious, because recommending a book you don't connect with won't work.
- Over time build filters for which authors and books match your values, since "everyone writes a book today and not everyone has something to say".
- Don't look for fast fixes. He says he spent a long time hoping for a "magic pill" course. Development is a continuous introspection process, a spectrum rather than switching from black to white.
- Don't wait to be convinced by others. He says if you need someone to persuade you, you have no chance, and experiencing the difference yourself is the only real proof.
- It's never too late. Experience from decades in business can make connections easier, though he declines to say it's harder or easier either way.
