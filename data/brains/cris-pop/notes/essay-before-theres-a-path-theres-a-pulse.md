## Trust the process, or process that trust? Control versus care

In a Thunder Substack essay, Andreea Popa writes that teams repeat "trust the process" like a mantra, but treat the process like a suspect: they audit it, timeline it, and call it inefficient or unscalable when it wanders. The essay argues that confusing control with care is the root problem. Care is "patient" and "spacious"; it listens when the process says it needs more time. Control, by contrast, is "the fear of uncertainty and failure," and the more teams rush, question and box in the process, the more they "choke the life out of it." Popa describes real process as "a living, breathing collaboration between trust, time, and the human heart," not a button that produces brilliance. These are the author's own views, not statements attributed to Cris Pop.

## Reframing the question: trust yourself enough to let the work breathe

Thunder's Substack essay by Andreea Popa proposes replacing "do you trust the process?" with "do you trust yourself enough to let the process work without your constant supervision?" She poses a set of tests for leaders and makers: Can you hold the tension of not knowing without rushing to fill it? Can you sit with silence without assuming something has gone wrong? Can you stop refreshing the timeline and start listening to what the work is trying to say? Her conclusion is that "the process doesn't need your trust. You do." The point is that supervision-heavy behavior is a symptom of low self-trust, not a sign of professionalism or maturity.

## Self-trust as something built: Zoltan Veres's three pillars

In a Thunder Substack post, Andreea Popa argues that self-trust is not only a feeling but something built "piece by piece, through experience and results." She cites Zoltan Veres, who says self-trust rests on three things: what you know (the information you hold), what you can do (your skills), and what results you have achieved and can achieve again. Her practical advice is to flip the doubt statement "I don't trust myself" into a sharper question: "Trust myself in what, exactly?" This turns vague self-doubt into specific, addressable gaps.

## Cris's formula at Thunder Talks 10: Confidence = Certainty + Faith

According to Andreea Popa's Thunder Substack essay, at Thunder Talks 10 Cris shared the formula "Confidence = Certainty + Faith." The essay explains it this way (the elaboration is the author's reading, not a direct Cris quote):
- **Certainty** comes from looking back at what you have already accomplished. The question is which wins prove you can trust yourself. No one is expected to believe they can do everything, only what matters most to them. A skills gap is simply the next thing to grow, bit by bit.
- **Faith** comes from looking forward: believing in your potential before there is proof, and trusting that even if the path is unclear you will find your way.

## Fighting negative self-talk with a "BUT"

Thunder's Substack essay by Andreea Popa names negative self-talk, the inner voice that erases your wins, as what kills self-trust. Her counter-practice is to catch the thought and add a "BUT" after it: "I'm not good at this, but I did X before, and I can learn." She pairs this with reminding yourself of consistent achievements, which feeds the "certainty" half of Cris's confidence formula. The essay frames neither the process nor your doubts as the enemy; the power lies in holding steady without seeing what comes next.

## The bird and its wings: faith without a guaranteed path

Andreea Popa writes in Thunder's Substack that a bird never senses whether a branch is weak or about to break, yet it trusts its wings regardless. People often say yes to a challenge while quietly bracing for the fall, which is why trust feels distant. The essay's closing message, "Before there's a path, there's a pulse," is to act with faith in your own ability rather than waiting for certainty about the route: "So fly anyway. Wings were made for this moment."
