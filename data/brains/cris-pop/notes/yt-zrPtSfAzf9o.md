## Thunder's mission: software for mission-driven founders
- Cris founded Thunder, a company that serves founders building mission-driven software products.
- He wants to be a partner to those founders rather than a vendor of services or coding lines. Thunder helps empower the mission through software, marketing, storytelling and whatever else it takes to launch and develop the product.
- His reason for the focus: it is easy to do work "just for the sake of doing it", which engineers call dev flexing or technical flexing. Software can have a big impact on people's lives, so he wants the work tied to a human-centric mission.
- Not every early project fits. His view is that this mission is a North Star. When you start small you take projects that are far from ideal, then steer the company toward the direction as soon as possible.

## Principles are red lines, everything else is flexible
- Cris sets values and principles that he never crosses, "no matter what happens". He would rather go bankrupt than cross that line.
- His example: if the company preaches trust, he has to be a trustworthy person.
- Beyond the red lines he asks for humility. Early on, especially when you are young, you will do projects that are not meaningful or not cool, and you build off them.
- He sees a generational shift from "from A to B" thinking toward asking "why am I going there, what's in it for me and my family", and he likes that shift.

## Strategy as guidelines, not rules
- He accepts that there is always more work and more opportunity than the team can do. A strategy is the best set of trade-offs the team picked, which is imperfect and guarantees nothing. It can take weeks to build.
- He keeps strategy as guidelines rather than strict rules. Strict boxes make people feel unproductive and bad whenever they miss them. Guidelines leave space to explore, fail and discover things that were not visible when the strategy was written.
- He adjusts the strategy or the actions after a few months, once the team has learned things it did not know at the start.
- His analogy is parenting: you create a space where the child can play and discover what is right, instead of forcing specific behaviors. He says he treats his team the same way. He adds that guidelines also move things from punishment toward something more positive.
- He optimizes for a meaningful day rather than a perfect day. His target is a solid 8 out of 10 input: not a 5, which would disrespect clients and partners, and not a forced 10. That gives the team and partners predictability while leaving a human in the loop who can rest and be creative.

## Choosing what to work on
- He often finds the work that moves the needle is also the work that grows him most, because that is where the discomfort and the things he does not yet know how to do are.
- When he has a pool of tasks that all look urgent, he asks which ones he is anxious about or avoiding, and starts from there.
- He does not expect every task to be meaningful. He dislikes legal and invoicing work, and so do his colleagues with their own chores. They accept it as a needed piece of the bigger picture.
- He warns against judging your whole job harshly because 10 hours a month of errands are boring. In his words, it is odd to keep pretending that work should be "magic" every day.

## Daily and weekly rhythm
- He plays paddle most days as a guideline, and skips it when a day off is the right call.
- He empowers the team to make decisions without waiting for him, and recommends this to every founder. He wants to be of service so he never blocks them.
- He uses the first hours of the day for productive creative work, including coding, which he treats as a creative activity. That work needs 3 to 4 hour blocks with no distractions.
- He groups meetings and tasks instead of alternating an hour of work with a half-hour meeting.
- His current struggle is finding time to write, since he needs 5 to 6 hours or a full day to get the quality he wants. His plan is to use Saturdays for it, and to take free time during the week to balance it. He frames this as an experiment that needs enough time to show whether it works.
- He encourages people who control their own schedule (solopreneurs, or anyone leaving a 9-to-5 culture) to design the week from scratch. His example is doing all hated errands on Monday, which makes the rest of the week feel great.

## Work from first principles and treat setbacks as learning
- He thinks people take themselves too seriously, partly because school trains you on fixed problems with one correct answer.
- His habit is to boil down a situation, especially a bad one, and reconstruct it from first principles, asking how it would look without inherited beliefs from family, country or history.
- He says he does this with his self-talk day to day, to reframe what happened in a helpful way.

## Human-centric culture with no double standards
- A near-rule for him is no double standards between himself and the team. His example: he would not buy himself a laptop five times as expensive as the team's just because he is the founder, unless the work truly needs that compute.
- He invites the team to organize their days so they can do the most valuable work, including sleeping when they are tired.
- When clients push for 9-to-5 presence, his response is that people do not have to be there. Forcing it by brute force does not produce good work, and "just because we are there, work happens" is a lie everyone knows.
- His reason for building this culture comes from experience. He worked in teams where the human side was never discussed, only a Jira board or a scrum process. He says it did not feel right, he did not look forward to the work, and people pretended to be engaged without raising ideas or questioning processes. A lot of potential never reached the client. He does not want that for himself, so he does not want it for his team.
