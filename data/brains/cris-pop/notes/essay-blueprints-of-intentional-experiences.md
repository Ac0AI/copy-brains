## Designing events by sitting in the empty chair

Cris starts event design from the attendee's seat, not from logistics or content. For Thunder Talks, the team imagines an empty chair and asks: "I've never been here before. I'm not sure if I belong. How do I want to be welcomed?"

- Attendees arrive from many contexts: introverts, extroverts, tech people, creatives, skeptics, community-seekers. Each carries different hopes, questions and hesitations.
- Planning begins with those people, not with the program.
- He frames it as welcoming "not as guests, but as friends stepping into our home": meet them, try to know them, make them comfortable, create "a human experience."

## Small gifts and personal welcome as part of the event

At each event, Cris's team prepares small gifts: a book and a message "that might land close to the soul."

- The gift is part of the hospitality, not a sponsor or promo item.
- The goal is that people feel understood before they realize they needed to be.
- Welcoming is treated as a design task with the same weight as content.

## Reverse engineering through empathy: designing for participation

Cris wants Thunder Talks to be collaborative spaces where people "don't just receive inspiration but create it together through questions, conversations, and genuine engagement."

- Tactic: make space through words and a quiet animation that signals "this is a safe place to ask, to share."
- The design target was the introvert in the back row who carries a question they won't ask out loud. He asks what would nudge them just enough, and what might quiet the noise of self-doubt.
- Principle: "One question can open a door, and your voice might give someone else permission to use theirs."
- He calls the method "reverse engineering through empathy." Instead of asking "Will they like what we've built?", ask what they actually need, then shape the experience from that answer.

## Giving feedback and hard truths so they land

Cris notes people often act in ways they would not accept from others: abrupt feedback that they would call cold if received, or fast, raw truth that frustrates them when teammates do the same.

- He cites Marshall Rosenberg (Nonviolent Communication): "The greatest gift we can offer is honest expression, delivered with compassion."
- Being right is not enough; you have to be effective. If your truth only hurts without helping, or shuts people down instead of opening them up, "what was the point?"
- He does not advocate diluting the truth. The aim is to give it a place to be received, "Not just heard, but actually felt."
- Before a difficult conversation, picture yourself on the receiving end:
  - How would you want to hear this?
  - What tone would make you feel respected, even if the message is tough?
  - What space would help you stay open rather than defensive?
- Honesty anchored in empathy is where strong communities and relationships start.

## The mirror test: how Cris stays grounded

Cris calls turning the mirror on yourself "something of a golden rule." He asks three questions from the other side of the table:

- If I were part of this community, what would I care about?
- If I were the customer, what would I expect, and what would make me trust or return?
- If I were attending this event, what would make me feel like I belonged here?

He admits this is not always easy: when you're close to the work or focused on efficiency, pausing to shift perspective feels unnatural. The habit of asking is where clarity begins, even when answers aren't immediate.

## Design for people, not personas

Starting from your own humanness strips away assumptions. In Cris's words, "You stop designing for personas and start designing for people."

- This reconnects the work to real human experience.
- The value is in the effort to see from the other side, even when the answers aren't obvious.
- The same principle applies across his work: community building, events (Thunder Talks), customer experience and team communication.
