## Voice as a place: why sounding smart fails and lived truth lasts

In a Thunder Substack post, Andreea Popa writes that she used to think the goal was to sound smart. She believed polished phrasing and vocabulary would make people nod and admire her. She now argues that "if you have to explain the brilliance, maybe it's not," and that words which do not change something (a mind, a belief, a habit, a decision) are doing nothing. Thunder's Substack argues that in a world saturated with information, people rarely remember a single sentence. Being understood, meaning helping someone take away something meaningful, matters more than looking clever. People may admire what they do not understand, but they will not follow it, remember it or change because of it.

## Words that touch versus words that perform

Andreea Popa writes that she catches herself reaching for clever paraphrasing, and that a "shiny inner voice" tells her to use a new word because it sounds smart. When she returns to the page a day or two later, a humbler inner voice shows her what matters. Thunder's Substack contrasts sounding smart with being felt, calling them "two different universes." It lists what stays with a person:

- A sentence that cuts through the noise and lands like truth.
- Honesty that makes you look away for a second, just to recover.
- Words that "don't perform, but recognize."
- The line they whisper to themselves when the room goes quiet.
- The simple phrase "you're not the only one."
- Something they always knew but needed permission to believe.
- Questions that remind them who they are.

The post's test for anyone writing, speaking or leading is a question: "is this performing, or is this touching something real?" Only the second one stays and makes someone feel a little less alone.

## Cris's rule: only teach what you have lived

Andreea Popa writes that Cris shared a rule in his last newsletter that stuck with her: "Only teach what you've lived. Only say it if it changed you first." The post treats this as the base for credible communication and leadership. Andreea adds her own extension, which is not Cris's: "If your words don't scare you just a little, they're probably not the truth yet." She also advises starting a piece with a sentence your past self needed to hear. Her advice is to speak for the version of you that felt small or confused, because "that's where resonance begins."

## Your voice is a place people walk into

Thunder's Substack argues that a voice works like a world, borrowing the world-building idea from fiction. It credits Tolkien with understanding that a world must follow its own internal logic or the magic collapses. The same applies to speaking and writing: if you break your own truth, your message loses its weight. Andreea Popa writes that every time you speak or write you invite someone into a feeling, a memory or a worldview. As with walking into a room, people know within seconds whether to stay. They ask whether it is grounded, true, safe and alive, or designed to impress rather than invite.

If the words offer nothing real to stand on, people leave. The reason is not rudeness or distraction. There are too many rooms to walk into, and too many voices say too much that does not mean enough. The post says the aim is not to decorate ideas but to build a world so honest that people believe it while inside, and where someone pauses and thinks "I need to sit with this."

## Clarity as an act of respect

Andreea Popa writes that the target is not brilliance, polish or even value, but something "alive. Real. Clear." She urges people to talk as if it matters and to speak as if someone's future hinges on the sentence they are about to say, because sometimes it does. Thunder's Substack argues that words should not be safe, and should be something that wakes people up.

The post also makes a case for knowledge distillation. In its words, "We don't simplify because people can't handle complexity. We simplify because clarity is an act of respect." The reasons it gives are that the world moves fast, attention is thin, and the brain remembers what it feels, not what it decodes. The practice is to take everything you know and have lived and strip it down to the sentence that hits "straight in the chest," a truth someone can carry into a hard day. The post concludes: "Smart words can impress. But simple words, spoken with lived depth, those connect. Those change people." Its closing line is "Say less. Mean more."
