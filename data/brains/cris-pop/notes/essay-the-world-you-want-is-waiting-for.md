## Andreea Popa on the cost of waiting and why you should lead yourself first

In a Thunder Substack post, Andreea Popa (writing for the Thunder team, not Cris Pop) argues that most people's careers stall because they stop acting, not because they lack options. She frames it as forgetting "we're the ones holding the pen": we can rewrite the rules and build a new world. The post is a personal essay about leadership of the self, published on The Thunder Way in July 2025, and its views are the author's, not Cris's.

## Popa's own story: three years of staying put

Andreea Popa writes that she started her career in environments that valued quick results over thoughtful process, where "leadership often meant control, not care." Those places taught her to doubt her gut and silence her voice. She spent years asking whether she fit and never acted, because the discomfort was familiar and felt safe. She says that waiting cost her three years doing work that didn't mean much to her, years she could have spent growing among people who "saw more in me than what I could deliver on a deadline." Her line: "Sometimes we don't see the price of waiting until it's already been paid." She says she now writes from the career she once thought she couldn't build. This is her anecdote, not Cris's.

## When patience becomes inaction

Thunder's Substack, through Popa, argues that patience is only a virtue sometimes. Holding out for a role that fits who you are can be a form of self-respect, especially if the alternative is another draining environment. But patience can become "the story we tell ourselves to justify staying still": waiting for the right moment while avoiding the truth that you aren't moving. Popa admits she told herself she'd start searching but wasn't doing the work, just hoping the right thing would find her. Her warning is that "eventually, waiting becomes a wall."

## Complaining is not the same as changing

Popa writes that complaining without action is "another form of procrastination." Venting about jobs, managers, missing mentorship and lack of support can feel productive, but it avoids the fact that change requires risk. She describes raising feedback with her manager and getting defensiveness or personal criticism instead of effort to fix the system, then staying because known frustration felt safer than the unknown. Her diagnosis: people build lives around pain they can predict and call it patience or realism, when "really, it's fear."

## Lead yourself before anyone else does

The post's core mindset shift, according to Popa, is moving from "Why is this happening to me?" to "What can I do about this?", focusing on what you control. She lists four areas to invest in:

- **Skills:** learn a tool, take a course, build something out of curiosity; "you don't need permission to grow."
- **Network:** connect with people in roles that interest you, ask questions, join a community, attend events.
- **Narrative:** reframe your experience around what you gained (resilience, clarity, direction) rather than what you endured.
- **Standards:** don't adjust yourself to fit every opportunity; look for the ones that fit you.

Her advice is to start with what you can carry today and let the first step be the proof you're ready.

## Your world is waiting: act on small steps

Popa closes by arguing the career you want won't arrive through perfect timing or wishing. It "will exist because you chose to create it." She says somewhere there is a team that needs what you bring, a project that could move faster with your perspective, and a problem only your mix of experience can solve, but they can't find you while you hide in a job that doesn't fit. The bridge is "built from small, consistent actions taken by someone who decided they deserved better." Her final framing is that the question is not whether you're ready but "whether you're willing."
