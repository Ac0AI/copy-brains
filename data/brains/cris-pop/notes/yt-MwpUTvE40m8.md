## Defining success and failure on your own terms

- Cris argues failure and success are narratives people write for themselves. If you set a target of "500 € at age 29" and miss it, you failed; if you aim for 10 RON and earn 20 RON, you succeeded.
- He sees the main problem in young people as an almost unreachable story: unrealistic expectations, with effort and sacrifice badly out of step with their aspirations. They then feel like failures every day.
- He avoids labeling experiences as failure or success. He treats them as experiences to evolve from, and says growth often accelerates more after failures because the retrospective is stronger.
- Business example: there were times the company was nearly out of money and he didn't know if next month's salaries could be paid. He says that is only failure if you actually run out, and that how you frame the situation matters more than the situation.
- When disappointed, he quickly flips the story toward gratitude, what to do next, and what the lesson is, rather than staying in negative emotions.
- He is patient with himself without being lazy: he still looks for progress.

## Treat the job market as a leadership problem, not an excuse

- Cris acknowledges the economy and geopolitics are hard, but says rough periods have happened before and external factors always exist.
- His advice: build opportunities consciously and take a leader's attitude of looking for solutions to whatever problem exists.
- A chaotic market is harder, but people who really put in extra effort ("go the extra mile") can stand out more to companies.
- From talking with young people: they often say the market is the problem, but in his view the problem is usually them. They don't put in enough effort and look for a quick, easy, comfortable shortcut.
- School gives a preset path to follow; the adult job market has many paths or none, so you must make choices and keep patience and perseverance on the one you pick, not wait to be handed an opportunity.
- He rejects the victim attitude and resigning yourself to "there are no opportunities."

## What leadership means and how to define your direction

- Cris and Thunder treat leadership as a life philosophy: living consciously and intentionally instead of on autopilot.
- A leader for him builds with intention, makes a plan, runs retrospectives to learn (alone and with colleagues), and stays open to change rather than protecting the status quo because it's comfortable.
- He accepts autopilot is needed for daily routine, but not at the level of life or team strategy.
- He urges young people to define their own success and trajectory, including a rough life plan: when to have kids, what job, what culture at work and in family, relationship with parents.
- He doesn't mean rigid goals like "first child at 28, second at 31"; he means strong intention so daily choices have a "North Star".
- His phrase: be "directionally right". Steps may be sideways or backwards, but if you have a direction and adjust like with a compass, compounding over months and years takes you surprisingly far.
- He notes many young people struggle to leave their parents' life project for their own, and calls it a personal decision to separate your identity from your family's, spiritually and emotionally, without ceasing to care about them.

## Hiring: judge people as they are today, plus their trajectory

- Cris says hiring is a high-risk decision, especially early on without developed intuition.
- His past mistake: projecting the potential he saw in people and choosing based on it. Seeing potential doesn't mean the person will pay the price in work, effort and sacrifice to reach it, and it may not even be what they should do.
- His current approach: look transparently and honestly at how the person is now and decide on today's context, while also considering their trajectory.
- Trajectory example: two people at the same point can be on very different paths. He says that if he started drinking heavily or using drugs tomorrow, he would be at the same point as today but on a much worse trajectory, which tells an outside observer where he will be tomorrow.
- He says he doesn't lack faith in people or their ability to grow.
- Over the past 4-5 years he and his team built the Thunder culture and team.

## Productivity: measure outcomes, not hours

- Cris separates being busy from creating value by moving from time invested (for example 7:00 to midnight) to looking at what was actually built, the outcome of the work.
- He cites the psychology finding that a task expands to fill the time you assign to it. Example: cleaning the house takes all of Saturday if you plan for that, but if you aim to finish by noon or 2:00 you are more productive and free up half a day.
- Even with a very full calendar, he reports mostly feeling fulfilled and rested, with exceptions for nights of poor sleep or deadlines.
- He measures output and quality (for example how much he wrote and how good it is) rather than "I sat an hour writing".
- He avoids black-and-white thinking: a few unproductive hours can build clarity that lets you produce something in half an hour the next day.
- Each person has their own pace and way of working, and he considers different routes to a result perfectly fine.
- He judges whether he built something by his own standard, not what others say.

## Protecting attention with big time blocks and an imperfect process

- Cris creates blocks of focused time on one objective: sometimes a whole Saturday on one project, or daily blocks of three to four hours on one thing, then another three to four on something else, instead of hour-sized slices.
- He admits it isn't always possible, since some work is granular tasks that can't be batched.
- He accepts the process can't be perfect or free of chaos. He sometimes doom-scrolls social media and realizes he lost 15 minutes.
- His key point: when you notice, stop right there and refocus on what matters, instead of spiraling into guilt and losing another 15 minutes, then an hour, until you lose hours a day or week.
- He aims for an imperfect but productive process, "8 or 9" out of 10, not an ideal one.

## Intuition, daily judgment and how he handles bad days

- If he started over, he says he would listen to his intuition more. He doesn't claim he'd be right more often, but gives two reasons: training intuition helps you understand your own emotions and read people and groups beyond what you consciously notice, and following it makes decisions faster.
- He says there were enough times intuition told him to make a decision and he dragged out the process, which wasn't very productive; he doesn't regret this, calling it natural for a founder early on.
- On a "bad day": he calls the good or bad label subjective and tied to the story we tell ourselves. He validates a day by how much he worked on his own development relative to what he could have done, which varies a lot day to day.
- Some days are low-productivity, emotionally complicated or chaotic, so he avoids judging himself against a utopian standard of always doing something sensational.
- His main value is generosity, and he wants to plant a seed of generosity in others through his work so they pass it on.
- He describes his entrepreneurship as civic, seeking social impact and not only profit, as a vehicle for personal and collective goals for himself, his colleagues and people around the company.

## CVs and reading as ways to grow

- A CV should tell your story: no single required element, but many small details that together show who you are, your values and principles, how you see work and people, what working with you would be like, and how much you'd commit. This applies with or without work experience.
- It takes real thinking time and can't be done in 20 minutes one evening if you want real impact.
- Reading: he didn't read books until about 21, because school-assigned books felt toxic and nobody explained why to read them. A book given by a friend changed that, and he found that the reading exercise (staying with yourself, reflecting, connecting frameworks to your experiences) is the real value, not just the information.
- He now reads and listens to audiobooks heavily.
- Advice: pick books that interest you and fit your current level. He tried a Jordan Peterson book after a few books, read three pages and understood nothing, which made him want to quit. Some things take effort and context first.
- Choose books at your level and in a style that is easy to read, including areas you haven't explored (for example an engineer who hasn't read about emotions).
- His recommendation is "Căderea într-o înălțare" (a spiritual take on life, easy to read, good as a starter).
