## Leadership as ownership: the chapter's core definition

Thunder's leadership book, "Leadership Before It Has a Name" (written by Andreea Popa, coordinated by Cris Pop), opens by arguing that "leadership is not a title, nor is it about authority. Leadership is about ownership." The chapter defines it as taking responsibility for who you are and where you are going. Its image is a ship. Leadership means leading your ship, and ownership means owning it. "Not everyone will sail with you. Some will come aboard. Some won't. But the journey? That's yours to command."

The book builds Thunder's philosophy on this base. Leadership is not a position that someone grants, and it does not depend on having a role.

## Lead yourself first: how Andreea Popa came to this view

Andreea Popa, the book's author, tells how she reached this idea. This is her own story, not Cris's.

- She began as a creative writer and ended up in leadership roles in tech, where she never felt at home.
- She had never seen leadership that inspired her to follow. Her attempts to bring "humanity, empathy, and understanding" were mistaken for weakness.
- She left, and at a later job she found leaders who cared. But "big bosses" who insisted on controlling everything meant she would "never be fully understood."
- About three months into her time at Thunder, "leadership found me." She watched people who "cared," "served," "thought bigger" and "moved with purpose." She saw that the software work was only part of the story.

Cris is the only action the chapter credits to him. He gave her *Leadership Strategies and Tactics* by Jocko Willink, which she says "rewired" her. From it she took what she calls the secret of leadership: "lead yourself first. Then, and only then, can you lead others." She notes that people are taught to perform before they are taught to be, and to lead others before they have met themselves. She started with self-reflection, and she found that this gave her better ways to lead others.

## A self-check: what do you feel when you think of yourself as a leader?

The book asks the reader to close their eyes and think of themselves as a leader. It lists four feelings:

- Courage, "the kind that makes your heart race because you know you're about to do something that matters."
- Joy, the lightness of knowing you might help someone or change something.
- Fear, the honest kind: that you're not ready, that you'll mess up, that people are counting on you.
- Craziness, the recognition that you will take responsibility for things, people and outcomes you can't control.

The chapter argues that feeling all of these together means you are ready. Leadership "isn't clean or comfortable. It's not a steady state. It's a constant dance with uncertainty," and courage comes "not from having answers but from staying in the dance anyway."

It also says who the book is not for. If you see leadership as holding a role, making the calls and getting "more money, more status, more control," then "this conversation isn't for you," unless you let it change your perspective entirely. Leadership is "the human you become when you realize that someone needs to care this much."

## Why you: care as the reason to lead

The book argues that people lead because they care, and that this care has a price: "Time. Energy. Comfort. Certainty." What it gives back is "Connection. Meaning. A glimpse of what's possible when people choose courage over convenience."

The chapter lists why someone chooses to lead:

- Something in them refuses to wait.
- Pretending not to care would feel like betrayal.
- They felt the weight of injustice and couldn't look away.
- Someone once believed in them, and now they carry that forward.

Leaders are described as "flawed, unsure, and still showing up." Leadership is "yours to do. Even if that doesn't come with a title or a role." The chapter says that when systems are stuck you move anyway, when the room goes quiet you show the way, and when doubt sets in you return to your why. Its line: "real leadership doesn't begin when you're ready. It begins when you're needed."

## Where and when: start now, start where you are

The book argues that leadership starts here and now, not "someday, not somewhere else." It asks: "If not here, where? If not you, who? If not now, when?"

Its points on permission and readiness:

- Permission is something you give yourself "when you decide to try, to fail, to grow anyway."
- "Polish comes later, and honestly, it matters less than presence."
- Waiting makes the leap feel further away.
- You can trust that your essence is already enough, that your clarity is already disruptive and that shaky courage is still courage.

The chapter adds that you "don't have to save everything. But you do have to start something." Its picture of a leader is not someone born extraordinary but someone who thinks, "If something must change, let it begin with me."

## How: humility, courage and honesty

The book answers "how" with three qualities, "humility," "courage" and "deep, uncomfortable honesty." It sets them against the usual image of the leader as a charismatic, certain, controlling visionary who never doubts. It calls that image "incomplete. Sometimes, dangerously so."

- **Humility** is "the kind of quiet strength that listens, that seeks to understand, that carries others on the shoulders to the top, and that leads with a grateful heart." The chapter says this is what gives leadership its moral weight.
- **Courage** means keeping your heart open when cynicism would be safer, telling the truth even if it disturbs something, and choosing "the long road over the quick win."
- **Honesty** means risking being misunderstood to stay true to your values. It opens the door for others to be real too. The chapter's reason: "without honesty, trust cannot live."

None of this happens in one defining act. It happens "in a thousand small choices. One moment at a time." Leaders do not pretend to be invulnerable. They reveal their humanity and make it safe for others to reveal theirs, and they keep showing up "even when it's thankless... Especially then."

The chapter closes with its thesis: "leadership is not a role. It's how you approach life. And who you become is ultimately how you lead."
