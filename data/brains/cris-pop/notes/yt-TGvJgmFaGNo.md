## Cris's core philosophy: culture first, success defined by himself

- Cris founded Thunder not out of a classic "I want to be an entrepreneur" urge. He wanted to do work in a certain way with people who care, couldn't find that culture, and so built it.
- The intent behind Thunder was a context for "munca cu sens" (meaningful work): work the team cares about, done with integrity, that integrates with life outside work rather than being walled off from it.
- He rejects the wall between "work self" and "life self". Walling work off becomes an excuse for bad behavior, and personal life (partners, health, family) affects performance and must be integrated into team dynamics.
- Culture is not a trade-off against performance. A healthy culture gives people the tools and context to perform, so performance follows naturally. Culture is not an excuse for mediocre work.
- Money is a consequence, not the goal: "you don't need money to build a healthy culture. The opposite is more valid." Put a healthy culture in place and business success comes more easily.
- The Thunder "North Star" is doing things "in this way", not reaching a size or timeline.
- He defines success himself, before drawing conclusions about whether he is successful. A default path handed down by society (or copied from someone else) can leave you with money but without fulfillment or sense of meaning. He says he learned this progressively, and school sets people up with the opposite mindset.
- Success is team-level for him. Profit matters because you need money to run community initiatives, but not at the price of culture or relationships.
- Thunder doesn't chase figures, titles or "biggest company" awards. Daily experience, relationships and the quality and integrity of the work matter more.

## Hiring, trust and managing people

- The best small decision with outsized impact, made repeatedly: **trust the people who join**. Create the space for them to show what they can do.
- Fake trust on paper plus micromanagement in practice doesn't count.
- Junior hire: give them room to experiment and fail, structured so failure isn't disastrous for the company. Senior hire: let the expert do the job; don't manage them at a micro level.
- He calls out the founder-ego trap ("I pay the salaries, I manage everything") as contraproductive. Put your ego aside as a leader.
- If rehiring/restarting from zero, the one thing he'd change is **choosing people**: be much more careful whom he gives trust to, and make sure there is a collective dynamic of involvement, respect and fair reciprocity, where everyone is "pulling the same cart" and earns their place on the boat. He says he made mistakes here, which he considers natural.
- On people vs. systems: systems help, but people with good intentions, integrity and character make a flawed system perform and improve it. Uncurious, valueless people will game even a good system. When people don't feel appreciated, they drift to the minimum viable effort (a reciprocity effect from how management treats them).
- Things that drain him most: people who don't take work seriously, lack interest and respect, force repeated explanations. He distinguishes inability (missing skills or experience) from disinterest, and says disinterest is the worse of the two.
- He declined potential client projects that didn't fit Thunder's culture, knowingly losing money (possibly a lot, since you never know how far a collaboration would go) to protect culture.

## Starting a business: timing, money and the intrapreneurship path

- Cris started Thunder at 23 with no real business-theory background and a modest self-collected capital. The human foundation stayed constant; running a company shouldn't change who you are.
- He doesn't recommend starting your own company at 22-23. Pragmatically, you'd gain far more experience on a team first, ideally a place that offers **intrapreneurship**, not one where you're "just a cog".
- Intrapreneurship, as he describes it: stronger, real responsibilities, owning part of the business or a department, ideally with profit share or equity, possibly without a formal title. Even a company of 8 people can have 1-2 people doing much more, rewarded more, without a rigid hierarchy.
- Entrepreneurship is complex (legal, accounting, the business itself), and these are hard to learn quickly enough to launch something that at least earns its keep.
- Starting with little or no money: it depends on the business. He knows people who started with 0 RON, paying only with their own work, especially in digital. He started by setting capital aside. Study the market and competitors first and gather information. Even digital tools usually cost a few hundred RON per month.
- Don't force entrepreneurship as an identity: it should arrive as a natural consequence of walking a path and feeling that creating your own team is the next step.
- When tempted to quit, he treats the thoughts as information to reflect on, not as a signal to stop. He rejects both extremes: ignoring them and quitting on a bad morning. Poor sleep and bad health heavily distort these voices, and a healthy culture makes them rarer.

## Decision-making, ideas and ego checks

- To test whether an idea is a good idea or an ego move: discuss it with colleagues first, then "play it out" mentally to see where it leads. If the outcome is more about him than about the team or community, that's a red flag that it comes from ego.
- Avoid answering such questions with mathematical certainty. The same activity can be ego for one person and service to others for another.
- Match decision time to decision type. A loaf of bread takes minutes; hiring or business strategy can take weeks and benefits from advisors with 20-30 years of experience. No single formula applies everywhere.
- His risk is deciding too fast, not overthinking. He calls these "leaps of faith" (commitments to just start). He checks with the team and adjusts if they don't agree. If nobody decides, he decides.
- When options are roughly equal in priority and difficulty, he goes with gut feeling, commits, and then evaluates: doing it eliminates either the alternatives or that option.
- The cost of not choosing is often higher than the cost of a wrong decision. Most decisions are reversible; truly irreversible ones are rare. He warns the "ask forgiveness, not permission" mindset can become an excuse that damages relationships, culture and business, so avoid extremes.
- Start chaotic with new things, then structure. You need to run a task a few times before you can organize, optimize or delegate it. Trying to build the perfect system on day one usually fails. Haos is acceptable if it leads to order; how soon depends on complexity.
- On "don't scale" early work: much of what Thunder does would be hard to scale, but scaling isn't the goal. Scaling can also mean doing more with fewer people. Big-company statistics look optimal on paper while burning money and resources in practice.

## Craft, progress plateaus and doing work that matters

- Valuable work usually appears **after you're already bored**. The initial excitement fades, and the real quality comes from pushing past that point. A junior engineer might need three days for what a senior does in two hours, but shouldn't stop at the quick version if they want remarkable work for their level.
- Without turning toxic: pushing hard for two days is fine, but not sleeping or resting isn't a sound strategy.
- When you feel stuck or plateaued, in nine out of ten cases the answer is to keep going. Progress arrives as "clicks" (a piece of information, an experience, an emotional unlock) after enough accumulated experience.
- He says it takes about **two to three years** to build a strong enough spectrum of experience to truly understand what you're doing, and school leaves many gaps to fill first.
- Diagnostic question when stuck: am I doing the same thing in different ways (which is effectively the same attempt), or actually doing different things? E.g. redoing the same course ten times vs. starting a project.
- The blocker is often emotional more than technical. Technical skills can be learned.
- Skills and mindset reinforce each other: an apprentice mindset makes you seek out and absorb skills; building skills builds confidence and courage.
- When trying something new, go through the full cycle a few times (practice, rehearsal, doing it) before concluding it isn't for you. Many "I can't" feelings are mental or emotional constructs, not facts. This leads either to overcoming them or to a clean, owned decision that it isn't for you.

## Learning from success and failure

- Failure pushes you to reflect more naturally; he deliberately reflects just as much on success. Ask what he did right and why it worked, and don't let success become the cause of the next failure.
- Success gets "to your head" easily if you don't see realistically what's going well and what still needs work.
- Fear before going on stage at Thunder's conference was intense (including an urge to walk out the door). He treats fear as information, not a stop sign, and prepares in advance. The commitment to colleagues is what gets him on stage: you can review afterwards and decide never to speak again.
- He does talks and events partly because they push his self-development and the team needs it. He says he never aimed to be in the spotlight. He gives talks as a privilege and responsibility, and prepares well ahead (including rehearsing his TEDx talk).

## Hiring juniors: what the hackathon recruitment showed

- Thunder recruited through a hackathon: 200+ applicants, about 9 reached the hackathon, 2 were chosen. He describes the choice as very hard and says all nine showed real qualities.
- Observed two extremes: many applicants showed little interest or effort (blank forms, weak CVs, unanswered relevant questions), while a smaller group (about 20 interviewed) was strong. He doesn't blame the young people; he sees the educational system as the cause.
- What's missing in juniors is **clarity** about what to learn, what is fine not to know without experience, and what's expected.
- His response is to build what Thunder can: mentorship seeds, a CV guide, tools. The rest depends on the young people using them.
- He has done roughly **750 mentoring discussions** in recent years; Thunder's mentorship program has five colleagues covering technical and non-technical topics. He says it is underused relative to the number of young people who could benefit.
- Most frequent problem in mentoring: relationships with parents, including people on a path (like law school) chosen for them. He calls such issues "Imaginary Dragons" and says optimizing study techniques is pointless if the person doesn't want the path.
- Advice for parents: be an example (if you don't read, don't expect the kid to), give trust (the greatest gift is telling a child you believe in them) and appreciate what they do rather than constant pressure.

## Leadership and the book "Leadership Before It Has a Name"

- The book was written by Andreea, a creative writer on Thunder's marketing team. It began as a leadership guide for students; Cris liked the first version so much he asked for a book, and it launched about a year later.
- Subtitle: *Becoming the kind of human you would want to follow*. Thesis: leadership starts long before a title, via **self-leadership**: owning who you are and what you do with everything in your "bagaj" (good and bad), including what's outside your control.
- Key line he likes: "Before there is a path, there is a pulse." For a young person, you may not see a path or have clarity, but you move toward the pulse and the clarity builds from the information you collect.
- The book is an internal tool (he rereads parts himself; new colleagues use it to understand the culture) and an external tool to give young people clarity.
- He says not everyone should aspire to run teams. Thunder's org chart is quite flat: people are responsible for areas, not rigid bosses.
- Best leadership attitude for young people at the start: be an **apprentice** (ucenic). He stresses it is a two-way relationship of mentorship and dialogue (where the company is, where it's going, the role you could play, what you want to learn), and not a boss keeping you small. An apprentice needs a leader with some level of leadership next to them.
- He plans a second, personal book about his own life, tied to Thunder's philosophy, likely next year. It isn't written to sell: "if it happens, it's a positive consequence."
- Distribution through events and community: Thunder events (including a US edition in New York last year and Austin, Texas this year), the mentorship program, Instagram and LinkedIn. He wants people to arrive through organic recommendations from attendees, not Thunder's own pushing.
