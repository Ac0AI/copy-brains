## How Hunter builds simple, fast consumer apps: the NGL playbook

Hunter J. Isaacson is a 24-year-old app builder. By his account he has built about 10 apps with roughly 150 million downloads and around 30 million dollars in revenue across them. NGL is his biggest. He puts his personal take-home at roughly 2 to 2.5 million dollars by the end of 2024, because he has partners.

His path:
- He earned 70k at his first design job and 100k at his second, plus some consulting, so he was at about 100-120k a year before NGL.
- He started college at USC's Marshall business school but never intended to graduate.
- He spent his first two and a half years failing. His first product was a Gen Z take on Foursquare (check in, pickup points, leaderboards), built with outsourced developers he managed poorly.
- He says the failure was the most important stage. In his view, people who skip failing are the ones who get delayed.

## Fail, then build a portfolio of attempts

- Hunter calls his failures "your portfolio." He likes seeing entrepreneurs with many projects listed, because it shows they aren't scared to try and are iterating.
- NGL was roughly his eighth app. He says very few people (he names Evan Spiegel and Mark Zuckerberg) hit with their first.
- He never launches something and sees it succeed the next week. His method is to launch, test-market, and spend time iterating.
- He argues that quitting too early is the common mistake. NGL got almost no traffic for its first four months.
- His turning point was cold-DMing an Instagram contact one year older who had already sold an app. They had lunch in 2020, and the contact told him "everything you're doing is wrong." Hunter pivoted and says the advice was real and raw.

## Make people comfortable, and keep apps simple

- Zoom University (2020) was a two-on-two video chat app for college students: two guys and two girls in a four-way call. It reached about 200k downloads and had a 7 to 1 girl-to-guy ratio, which is unheard of in dating. His explanation is that everyone brings a friend, so everyone feels comfortable.
- His lesson: be a safe place for users.
- At Wink (a make-new-friends app he designed at a company that hired him at 21, now around 30 million downloads), he learned that refining existing features beats adding more.
- His rule: build a very core MVP in six to eight weeks, launch it, and iterate quickly.
- Wink also taught him platform leverage. Locked features could be unlocked with in-app currency, and users earned that currency for free by posting on Snapchat.

## NGL: a tool that piggybacks on Instagram

- What it does: you enter your Instagram username and get a link to post on your story. Others tap it and send an anonymous message, which passes through two layers of AI moderation for bullying and harassment. You then reply publicly on Instagram.
- His framing: "build tools not networks." It is the Instagram Q&A feature made anonymous, which gets more, funnier and more dramatic responses. Influencers use it for that reason.
- He says the app game is making 20-30% improvements on existing products and marketing them in a unique way, noting Meta cloned Twitter.
- The launch timing was right after Instagram opened links to everyone.
- The first version was bare bones.
- Over four months of "crickets," the team renamed the company, reworked the branding, design, flow and funnel, and ran UGC and influencer link tests.
- The company was bootstrapped with about 10k, of which 4k went to a domain they never used.
- They built Android once they saw signs of growth. Hunter says if you see growth you must build both platforms immediately, though starting iOS-only is fine.

## The breakout and the numbers

- One UGC TikTok did about 1 million views in a day, mostly in Europe, the Middle East, North Africa and South America. It showed a girl getting a dramatic anonymous message.
- Downloads went from a first 1,000 in a day to 1.5 million in a day within a week.
- At peak they did about 990,000 downloads per hour, and metrics providers kept shutting them off for exceeding rate limits.
- Revenue peaked at tens of thousands of dollars a minute.
- Results: 100+ million downloads, number one in 150+ countries, and the most downloaded app in the world in summer 2023. It now sits around the top 25 in the US.
- His rank strategy: pop to number one hard, then hold in the top 50.
- The app is translated into every language Apple supports. Most downloads are overseas, and they localized in about 90 days.

## Speed to the magic moment

- His one-word answer on what mattered most: speed. "Teams that ship fast win."
- He says to build for the "lizard brain": very simple products.
- Session one matters, not day one. Users must reach the aha moment within minutes (NGL's was about 60 seconds), or they fall out of the funnel.
- The first-time experience should be a guided sandbox, as TikTok's first scroll and Tinder's fifth swipe are.
- Any product needs a clear expectation, a simple action, and an obvious reward. The reward is someone else's expectation: people see replies on your story, so they tap the link.
- His aim is to create a social movement, not just an app. Friends posted NGL on every story for months.

## Talk like your users

- He applies this to any consumer product: be memeable and funny, as Liquid Death and the Slim Jim Twitter account are.
- NGL's Instagram has over a million organic followers. It posts memes and the funniest messages sent through the app. He ran it first, then hired a social media manager.
- Specifics:
  - App copy says "IG," not Instagram.
  - Things are lowercase where possible.
  - Notifications are written like a user would text, such as "your inbox is dry" with a cactus emoji, or "flooded" with a wet emoji.
- Example: his dad called to say the deli cashier had just received an NGL notification.
- He estimates this was worth millions.

## Community as a movement

- His undisclosed-until-now tip: make people feel part of a big movement by engaging them directly.
- During the growth spurt, he and his co-founders hand-replied to every Instagram DM for months, hyped up users, and reposted users' praise to a large account.
- NGL's website has a "love wall" of 100+ DMs.
- He says users became evangelists, and the effect was bigger than he expected.

## Finding opportunities and what's next

- App revenue and download data is public. He says you can browse App Store rankings by category and find apps doing about 50k downloads a month and 250k in cash.
- Exit options for a successful app: another big app or private equity.
- His next focus is apps where each user is worth more, either monetized more or with more valuable user information, rather than chasing 100 million downloads at a low monetization rate. He says NGL has a ceiling and wants one with a higher ceiling.

## Advice for beginners

- Pick one skill (design, engineering, marketing, copywriting, editing, videography) and get good at it. Money follows.
- His dad's rule: don't chase money, chase passion, but pick a passion where people can make money.
- Build a team from your network. Other 17-18 year olds are hustling and will become co-founders.
- Make your own mistakes rather than copying his.
