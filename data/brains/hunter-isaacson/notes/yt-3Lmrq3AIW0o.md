## Path into apps and the early failures

- Hunter started building apps at 17-18, near the end of high school. His first app was a "Gen Z Foursquare": go to locations, earn points, rank on leaderboards.
- It took about 2.5 years of learning and failing. He spent about $70,000 on outsourced developers in India and says it was "gone". He also raised $150,000 at age 19.
- COVID lockdowns killed the app. He took it as a sign: "this is not my billion-dollar app, but I really love building apps."
- Within 6-8 weeks he built Zoom University as a designer with friends. It was a live college double-date video app that matched two friends with two friends. It hit top 10 in social, got a few hundred thousand downloads, and ran about a 7:1 girl-to-guy ratio.
- He says it "proved a point": after 2.5 years of failing, he could do it.
- He was recruited at 21 by a Musical.ly co-founder and former president, and worked there for just under 2 years. They built about half a dozen apps, including Wink, which reached roughly 50-60 million downloads and #1 in the App Store.
- He then built NGL, which became the largest anonymous social app and freed him from his 9-to-5.

## Failing fast and persistence

- Hunter's core advice is "fail faster." He says the faster you fail, the faster you succeed. He tells people to seek rejection and failure as the goal, throw things at the wall, tweak, and learn.
- In 2021 he built and shipped 5-6 apps. The last one became NGL. His mindset was "I'm going to ship this and if it fails, I don't care." A failed app goes on the portfolio site: "failure is just your portfolio."
- It took 5 years from starting to becoming a millionaire.
- He says the people who win are the ones who never quit. He has seen very smart people stop shipping just before one worked, and says he has never seen a smart, relentless person fail to succeed.
- After meeting many founders, he concluded most are "just dudes" who didn't quit. Seeing friends succeed made it feel possible.
- When you feel like giving up, he says, that is when to double down.
- He says to love the process: "you have to enjoy the journey," and chasing the next goal leads to a dopamine treadmill.

## Why apps, and how to build one

- He calls apps "the best business in the world" and the "greatest creative expression," since a product can reach billions of people. He admits the learning curve is steep and says consumer social is the hardest category for product-market fit: "you're selling a feeling."
- His build process: start with an idea, design it himself, have stronger engineers build it, get a minimum viable product live fast, then iterate and change things.
- NGL was built in about 3 weeks. Nobody used it for about 6 months while he tried to seed a high-school friend graph. He was convinced it would work.
- One TikTok hit a million views. In the first week of June, downloads went from about 1,000 in a day to 1.5 million in a day, then about 90,000 downloads an hour. At the time he earned $100K a year at his job, and the app was making that in a day.
- He expected a ceiling of 10 million downloads. NGL passed 250 million, with 10-20 million concurrent users at times, on Firebase. Most engineers say Firebase can't scale. Because the stack was simple, he knew where failures would occur, and the app had only small outages of up to an hour.
- He predicts a "golden age of apps" when a new compute platform (glasses, contacts) coincides with good AI tools that let people build with their voice.
- On tools, he says Cursor is very good and most of his engineers use it.

## Viral loops and influencer marketing

- Hunter builds "organic virality" through viral loops. He describes it as: you share something externally, a friend sees it and enters the same experience, and they share it because you did.
- For Bags, the loop was sharing your Bags link, which works like your wallet address and profile link. Users logged in with Twitter, generated a link, and shared it immediately. This brought in hundreds of thousands of people and "growth hacked" the first couple of months.
- His influencer playbook is seeding, testing, then paid:
  - Seed so the first ~1,000 people download or interact, then use a viral mechanic to get them to share and invite friends.
  - He prefers smaller accounts and UGC-type creators with few followers, paid on a CPM basis.
  - Test content, and once a TikTok wins, run it as an ad in every market. Translate it, make variations such as changing the sound, and push hard.
- He says using organic virality as proof of concept before spending on ads is "a great strategy."
- For Bags, he said influencer work was planned and that growth was only now being turned on after a long building phase.

## Bags and choosing consumer finance

- Hunter started Bags' company in summer 2023, expecting crypto to come back. He wanted to be live when it did, because being on chain with many eyeballs coming in generates volume.
- He chose consumer finance for a longer runway, since he said consumer social gets harder to stay tapped into as you age. He wanted something he could run for 5 years and into his 30s.
- He didn't want to compete with himself on downloads after NGL. Instead of chasing a quarter-billion users, he aimed for average revenue per user: maybe 5 million downloads and a very successful fintech business.
- NGL monetizes a small percentage of users, though it makes eight figures a year.
- Bags earns from a small transaction fee, split with affiliates. If someone joins through your link, you get half their fees. If someone copies your trade, you also earn a share. Some users have made tens of thousands of dollars this way.
- He says the hardest part has been the technical moat: finance, trading, landing transactions, and complicated data.

## Hiring, team and autonomy

- His engineers are all in Europe, including his CTO, front end, and back end, in places like Portugal, Austria, Poland and Spain. He wanted to hire in Miami but couldn't find enough devs with crypto experience.
- Crypto development is hard because there is little documentation and no forums of people helping.
- He found engineers through community members who reached out, recruiters, and various sites. His head of ops has interviewed about 50 engineers.
- His approach: hire smart people, give them autonomy, and trust the right people. Each co-founder owns a lane, such as his CTO owning engineering. He owns design and sets the flow, look and vibe, then adjusts to what engineering and marketing do.
- He prefers co-founders over going solo: "I would rather give away half the business to someone who's worth it." He says solo is lonely and he wants "to win with people."
- He tests ideas by asking friends to "roast this" with no emotion, and avoids yes-men.
- He sees himself moving from doing everything to operating, and says a founder is "never above the work." He joined his engineers for an all-nighter in Singapore right after about 30 hours of travel.

## Taste, EQ and finding your lane

- Hunter's edge is design and taste, built from Minecraft building, LEGO, art and Photoshop. He designed his first app in Photoshop.
- He thinks taste will be the most important skill as AI improves, and that AI getting real taste will take a long time.
- Much of his work is psychology: for example, making a button big, green and pulsing so users tap it. He aims to move people through an experience so "they don't even think," manage the cognitive load of a screen, and direct the eyes.
- He learned this from iterating on apps and making and testing hypotheses, not from books.
- He argues the best consumer app founders have high EQ because they understand people.
- Universal principle: make users feel comfortable and trust you. People only share things they don't think are lame, so anything that makes them cringe or feel confused kills sharing. Keep the product simple so they know what to do.
- He advises specializing: pick one skill (design, engineering, marketing, growth, editing) and get very good, then provide value to others. That builds a network and a bench of complementary co-founders. He cites Brian Chesky and Evan Spiegel as "design CEOs" who inspired him to own the design lane.
- When reaching out to high-value people, offer value first rather than asking for something.

## Habits, health and mindset

- He had no balance last year, and now travels with his team (Singapore, Thailand, Bali, Bahamas, Portugal, Colombia) while staying productive.
- His morning routine: no phone on waking (it sits on the floor away from the bed), grounding outside, stretching, push-ups, reading a chapter, music, gratitude and manifestation writing, a healthy breakfast and supplements. Then he sits down for lock-in work.
- He recommends 50-100 push-ups a day, and says training is practice at showing up daily for slow rewards.
- Health: he started biohacking at about 23 after a doctor friend told him he'd be very healthy in 20 years. He tests blood levels and takes supplements, and cut processed foods, artificial dyes and "natural flavors." He says eating a clean, balanced meal lets him focus for about 6 hours afterward.
- He advises young people to take responsibility and ownership of a product or mission, because men need a mission. Try a handful of things over several years, find your purpose, then go all in.
- He says chase passion that can also make money, and that when you're young, "pay now or pay later." Surround yourself with high-value, hard-working people.
- He believes in manifestation and the law of attraction, treating it as focus plus hard work.
