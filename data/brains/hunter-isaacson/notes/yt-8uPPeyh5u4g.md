## Social apps are selling a feeling, so psychology comes first

- Hunter started at 18 with an app idea he thought would take six months and make him a billionaire by 21. He then spent two and a half years failing.
- His view: "with social apps, you're essentially selling air... you're selling a feeling." It is not SaaS or fintech. The job is to engineer the right dopamine response so users enjoy the process more than they would a plain product or service.
- He frames social apps as selling belonging and a way to connect with other people, which is intangible.
- For social specifically, the app has to be shareable. That is the most important thing he focuses on.
- He built apps in the LA consumer scene of 2020-2021, when it was small and everyone knew each other. His co-founder on Bags was met in a Discord server called Gen Z Mafia during COVID.

## Path to NGL: Leader, Zoom University, then the anonymous messaging hit

- **Leader (first app, age 18):** "Foursquare for Gen Z." Users went to locations, earned points, got ranked on leaderboards with friends and made content. COVID killed it. He raised $150,000 at about 19 and burned through it, but learned a lot.
- **Zoom University:** built about 6 weeks after Leader failed, with a couple of friends. College kids were bored and lonely during COVID, and "Zoom University" was already a meme, which gave it ready-made branding. They seeded Facebook groups and pushed content. The first version ran on Zoom meeting links, then became an app with a couple hundred thousand downloads. It gave him the confidence that this was possible.
- **NGL:** Instagram had just launched link sharing for every user (previously you needed 10,000 followers). Within a call with friends they decided to build anonymous messaging for Instagram. Earlier apps like Sarahah and Yolo had done well on Snapchat, so they replicated the model on a bigger channel.
- NGL took about six months from launch to going viral. For those six months it was "basically crickets."
- He believed it would work because it needed enough initial density, clusters of friends all sharing at the same time. Once that happened it went super viral.

## Validating an app idea: exhaust every option before quitting

- Hunter says he has no formal validation process.
- He watches core metrics: everyone getting through onboarding, decent retention, minimized churn (at least early on), and some decent monetization.
- His main warning: people give up on an app idea before reaching those numbers. "You just need to exhaust all your options."
- Only move on if you have exhausted every option, tried every channel and gotten everyone activated and it still isn't working. Most people want to quit before doing that.
- He says the NGL "crickets" period shows why: the idea needed critical density, not a different product.

## His process from idea to App Store, and the first-thousand-users push

1. **Ideation:** think through the idea, sometimes a write-up, sometimes a "word salad" on the page. Identify the core experience and core loop, whether it is shareable, and the onboarding funnel.
2. **Design in Figma:** do branding and colors, and design onboarding first because he needs the data for accounts. Onboarding length depends on the app. Some are long funnels ending in a monetization event. NGL's is a few steps done in 15-20 seconds.
3. **Screen-by-screen design** of the whole app.
4. **Ask devs for feedback:** "Are there technical issues... Is this too complicated?" Devs often say a feature will take a week and ask if he really wants it. Often he does not, so it forces prioritization around the core app flow.
5. **Build and iterate** through multiple builds and TestFlights. He sends TestFlight screenshots with drawings on them so the team can fix problems.
6. **Submit to the App Store.**
7. After launch the goal is to "drop a thousand people into this" and see what they do.

## Organic marketing and TikTok as the app channel

- Hunter says TikTok is currently the channel for promoting apps.
- He and friends ran the "fresh account strategy" on TikTok in 2019 and heavily in 2020, learning the algorithm's tricks years ago.
- His playbook: run TikTok UGC with agencies, creators and people he knows, and test formats. When a format wins, use it as an ad, cut variations and run them on fresh accounts.
- With Bags, the team first focused on a technical moat: a reliable, fast, performant trading app. The focus has now shifted to activation: more funded accounts and more people brought into the app.
- A related sign of the app-store view: he says getting to number one overall, which he has done multiple times in 150 countries, is "extremely difficult" and has no shortcuts.

## App Store as a meritocracy, and the 30% fee

- Hunter calls the App Store a pure meritocracy: "There is no way of actually fudging the numbers." If an app is number one, it is because people are using it.
- Everyone builds with the same software, the same phone and the same tools, and whoever packages them best goes number one. He calls it a source of truth and "a window into pop culture."
- On Apple's 30% cut: he calls it "the mafia tax" and says he does not think it is fair, though it is fair that Apple takes something. He says every developer would agree.
- His critique of the Small Business Program (under $1 million a year, application required): it is not tiered like taxes. If you make $1,010,000 you drop from keeping about $850,000 of the first million to about $700,000, which he calls unfair.
- He thinks young developers entering apps will be turned off by having a "silent partner" that approves or disapproves everything they do.

## Monetization models: NGL weekly subscription and Bags fee share

- **NGL:** a weekly subscription. A small percentage of its hundreds of millions of users convert. The premium version gives insights on who sends you messages (who, where, their deal) while still staying anonymous. It does not reveal names, mostly because they do not know them. In rare cases where they do know, they display it.
- **Bags:** takes a small fee per transaction on buys and sells (not on sending and receiving). It passes 50-75% of those fees back to users. Examples: if you share your Bags link, your friend signs up and trades, you earn his fees. If you buy a token, someone sees it on the For You page and buys it, you earn the fee.
- His aim is a structure where "the people benefit, not just the platform," giving back the majority of what the app earns.

## Why Bags: mobile-first crypto for Gen Z and Gen Alpha

- Hunter has been in crypto since 2017, joining when Bitcoin was $1,000. His belief is that personal sovereignty and financial education matter for his generation, and financial literacy has been lackluster or non-existent.
- The thesis: put banking on crypto rails, make it social and fun, and build a bank for Gen Z and Gen Alpha. He wants to eventually support stocks, bonds and bank accounts.
- He chose mobile because he knows mobile better, and because crypto "has to go native mobile to really break out." Being in the App Store is a trust factor, especially for financial apps.
- Bags currently focuses on Solana and Solana memecoins because that is where the attention is. He wants a business he can work on for five-plus years: an "adult company" with a strong team, a strong tech stack and a moat.
- On memecoins: he sees them as monetizing attention and news cycles, and says "the internet is all betting on attention." He notes many people are tired of the current memecoin iteration, but each crypto cycle brings a new asset class (ICOs, then NFTs), and he says the asset class for this cycle is still unseen.
- He argues memecoins give the same effect as gambling, but simpler, for people who spend their time online.

## Gen Z behavior, building for the "seven sins," and finding niches

- Gen Z cares a lot about belonging and community, and about impact in a social sense: "can I get views, can I get clout, can I get attention." He says they grew up in instability (the 2008 era, then COVID) and live in a "post-truth era," which makes them caring but sensitive.
- He says people respond to incentives, which explains the shift from degrees and 9-to-5s toward creators.
- On "build for the seven deadly sins": the most electrically charged emotions (greed, lust and so on) hook people into a product. He admits the trade-off: it makes money but is "probably not going to have good impact," and it relies on dark patterns.
- His advice on niches: he is very bullish on niche internet communities. Find behavior that already exists on the internet (subreddits, Facebook groups), build a product for it, and "stick a paywall at some point in the flow." He says such niche products can reach $100,000+ a month in MRR and are easy to target on TikTok.
- His example: the looksmaxxing community on TikTok, for which Blake Anderson built UMAX.
- On AI apps: he expects accuracy to keep improving ("wait three months, six months, a year") and says Cal AI works because photo-based logging is far easier than the boring input flow of MyFitnessPal.

## Team building and getting started as a young founder

- **Three skills a consumer app needs:** engineering, product and growth. They can be one person or five. On NGL he was product, one co-founder was an engineer and another was growth marketing. On Bags he does product, co-founders do engineering, and they share growth.
- His advice to a 21-year-old: pick one skill (design, engineering or UGC/TikTok growth), get known for it in your circle, then find people to fill your "skill tree."
- **Hiring:** all his Bags engineers are in Europe, and he has had amazing experiences with European developers. He finds talent through networking, socials and heavy interviewing. His head of operations did about 60 interviews to build a catalog of available talent.
- Crypto hiring is hard: documentation is minimal, and it is hard to find someone who knows React Native, understands Solana and is Web3 native.
- **Mindset:** "everything in your life is your fault," good and bad. He treats total ownership as a process of asking how you contributed to something bad, rather than blaming others.
