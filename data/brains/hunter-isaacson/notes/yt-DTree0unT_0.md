## Background and track record

- Hunter's first app, a social graph built around friends hanging out, took 2.5 years (started 2017) and never shipped. It died in TestFlight and was sunset in 2020. His takeaway: "I just loved the process of designing it so much that I realized that like this is what I should be doing with my life."
- Before NGL he had 70+ million downloads across other apps. These included Zoom University, a double-dating app for college kids, and Wink.
- Other apps he built:
  - A quotes, meditation and sleep-sounds app.
  - PopStream, a creator "meet and greet" app that was sunset.
  - A voice-chat mini-games app.
  - Bags, a crypto app.
- Wink let users swipe Tinder-style and added matches on Snapchat. It reached 70 million downloads.
- The mini-games app had close to an hour of average time spent per user, among the highest he has seen.
- NGL today has 150 million monthly active users and is a multi-8-figure-a-year business. That is far more than he expected.
- What he says makes someone good at viral consumer apps is understanding how people behave in groups: what a person feels when they see a screen, and how that emotion drives the action you ask for.

## How NGL went from zero to viral

- Instagram opened link stickers to everyone at the end of October 2021 (before, you needed 10,000 followers). Hunter and friends got on a Zoom call on Halloween and decided to build an anonymous messaging app on top of Instagram. They built it in about a month.
- Then came 6 months of nobody using it. He assumed everyone saw the opportunity, and no one cloned NGL until after it went viral.
- What he tried during those 6 months:
  - Redesigned the app, then came up with the NGL name and the ngl.link domain.
  - Spent small amounts on influencers ("$100 here, $100 there") to post story links, plus TikToks.
- About 4 months in, a paid influencer activated a school on the other side of the world. It spread locally but not to other countries.
- He diagnosed it as a lower-income country, so they built Android quickly (May 2022). After a new marketing round, one TikTok went viral and took over the world within a week.
  - It showed a girl reading a controversial NGL message ("I was with your boyfriend last night") and trying to work out who sent it.
  - It got about 1 million views quickly and activated the first 1,000 users in one day.
  - The team tested many message texts and always tied them back to "you can get messages like this and maybe find out who sent it."

## Why NGL's viral loop holds up despite churn

- Hunter says NGL is a "really tight viral loop." Many churned users came back because friends still post NGL links.
- In many countries it is effectively the link in bio for 17 to 19 year olds. A churned user sees a friend's link, sends a message, and wants their own.
- So it had churn but a very high redownload rate, which he credits for its lasting popularity.
- NGL looks very similar today to when it went viral. It has been optimized and made more fun, not loaded with feeds, extra tabs or complicated profile tools.
- He notes many anonymous apps over-complicated themselves after succeeding.
- NGL augments Instagram rather than competing with it. It opens inside Instagram as a lightweight interruption, and you can close it or go do it.

## NGL's funnel, copy and onboarding

- Flow: copy link, then share. The share screen uses animated GIFs that show the steps (tap the sticker button, the link button, add the link, frame it).
- Posting through the app's flow auto-copies a branded asset with the user's profile picture.
- On the recipient side:
  - A dice button suggests what to say.
  - A persistent download button sits at the bottom.
  - After sending, the app prompts "get your own messages."
- He rates the "get your own messages" copy highly because it sells the result rather than the install: "it's way easier to get a download by saying get your own messages than saying, hey, download this app."
- Onboarding takes 15 to 20 seconds and drops the user straight onto the core screen.
- His lesson: make it one flow and one funnel, don't overcomplicate it, and focus on one north-star metric.
  - NGL's north star: how many people post the link and then post at least two replies.
- In the app, a message can show a hint (type of phone, rough location) or be replied to, which copies it onto the user's story.

## NGL monetization

- Revenue is mostly weekly subscriptions.
- Prices vary by region and GDP per capita, always a low impulse buy of roughly $1 to $7.
- A very small percentage of downloads monetize. The product is primarily free.
- Paid users are power users who want more insights (who sent a message, who viewed their profile) or the "link games."
- Volume makes the small paid percentage add up to significant revenue.
- He thinks the model of a mass consumer social product with simple monetization, grown on the back of big networks, is still open.

## Frameworks for finding app ideas

- The core pattern: take an existing behavior from another network or distribution channel, switch the distribution mechanism, and improve the product.
  - Anonymous Q&A had existed before (YOLO, Yik Yak, Formspring). NGL won by riding the Instagram graph, the biggest distribution graph.
- Pick the graph you target: Instagram, Snapchat, X, or Facebook for 45-year-old moms. "It doesn't really matter."
- Smaller or niche graphs are an opportunity, and he sees more of them emerging. He says you can also build a super viral experience on a big platform like Twitter and it will work.
- Design insight from Zoom University:
  - The waitlist hit about 40,000 people with a 7:1 girl-to-guy ratio, which is unheard of in dating.
  - Women do things when they're comfortable because they're with another woman.
  - Format: a group chat plus private chat, double opt-in on both sides, then a group chat if matched.
  - Zoom University had a few hundred thousand downloads and reached the top 10 of the social category.
- Cost lesson from PopStream: influencer pricing was too high (a top influencer posting a link cost about $20,000), so the economics didn't work.

## Share menu and Safari extension plays

- He advised a video-saving app called Fade, which blew up and went close to number one. Users tap share, tap more, scroll to Fade, and it saves videos that would otherwise be deleted from TikTok.
- The share-menu or recipe-app playbook works if users understand the value. A multi-step setup tutorial is fine then. It worked for NGL because users had already seen friends get messages.
- Rule: sell the user effectively before the tutorial, make the tutorial simple, and measure drop-off at each step.
- He also advises a company building a Safari extension (credit card recommendations at checkout). It works once you get past user education, and works best when the app runs in the background.
  - Install is five to six steps, so the user must already be fully sold before the tutorial.
- He likes adding social elements to everything, such as invite screens and nudging users to share. Sharing an app is an endorsement from a friend, and he puts it in every product when it makes sense.

## Bags: product and revenue model

- Bags is a crypto app aimed at solving token discovery. It shows influencers' buys in real time, with market cap and percent change, and a quick-buy button.
- Group chats have trading built in, unlike Telegram where you need a bot and typed commands.
- The wallet is designed like a credit card rather than a typical wallet. You can send by username and deposit with Apple Pay (via Moonpay) or crypto.
- Revenue:
  - A 1% fee on transactions, with most of the fee passed back to the friend who invited you. Someone who posts a token in a group chat also earns some commission when others buy it.
  - A token launchpad with a 1% royalty on every trade, not only on the LP transaction. His example: $2 million of volume yields $20,000.
- On crypto games: he thinks a successful one will happen but it's a whole other beast.
  - Teams often focus on money over the player experience and social graph.
  - Game user acquisition is very competitive and dominated by paid ads, so games are a hard place for a small team to start.

## Scaling an app from $10K MRR with a $10K to $20K budget

- Put a good amount of the money into UGC with influencers or ads, run as experiments.
- Track which cohort came from which ad or TikTok, and keep adapting formats.
- Run many small tests, such as a dozen creatives, rather than putting everything into one.
- Focus on product-led growth: use the ads to bring people in, then get them to invite a friend immediately.
- If a percentage of users bring someone else, your effective CPI drops considerably.
- Find the best hook to get someone in, then get a percentage to share. Once an ad format works and the invite loop works, scale the content up and look for other distribution channels.
- Spend primarily on tests until you hit a format that works. That is where "the flywheel really kicks off."
