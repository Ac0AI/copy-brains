## Building apps from failures: Hunter's path to NGL

- Hunter's first company, started at 18, raised about $150,000 from friends and family. He spent roughly 2.5 years building a gamified-life app (go to a location, capture a photo or video, stitched into a reel). It never left TestFlight, and COVID killed it.
- After that failure he dropped his founder ego and worked as a product designer, building a reputation. He shipped Zoom University (about 250,000 downloads), a college-ID-gated video-chat dating app.
- By his account, NGL was his eighth app, and the first that made him serious money. Earlier apps got downloads, but he didn't control the company, so he earned less.
- His view on how skill develops: "You acquire it through getting [expletive] on by the market." You only know a product journey works after five other versions failed.
- He built five or six apps in one year as the sole designer, handling every screen and QA pass himself.
- He scaled one earlier app to about half a million daily active users. He watched feature additions make the numbers go down, which taught him users don't know what they want.
- His rule: find the drop-off point in the numbers first. Don't build ten features to fill a hole. Usually a tiny adjustment is needed, not a new feature set.
- Simple products are often what the customer wants, even when developers mock them for it.
- Earlier work: Wink, a make-friends/dating app, did about 70 million downloads across 2020-2021.

## Picking an idea: existing behavior, Maslow, and a clean lane

- Hunter looks for behavior users already do that is unoptimized, then offers a faster flow and a better experience.
- Signal to watch: several apps blowing up in a category but not really hitting. Ask why you haven't seen the winning app yet. Usually users aren't sharing, the design is weak, or the marketing is off.
- He wants a noticeable product improvement plus a brand improvement. Brand matters: ask.fm got little use, but renamed NGL ("not gonna lie") it signaled anonymous and cool immediately.
- He looks for a niche or channel competitors aren't pursuing. Nobody had built an anonymous-messaging app for Instagram, so it was a "clean lane."
- Filter: if an idea doesn't hit a level of Maslow's hierarchy (love and acceptance, what friends think of me, purpose, money), he won't build it.
- The test: the psychological driver for sharing must be strong. If he can't figure out why user A would bring user B, he drops it. He says he has many half-baked ideas shelved for this reason.
- Empathy and brand context matter: "curate an empathetic experience" (a phrase from his friend Sam). Know exactly who you're building for.
- Naming examples: Wink (winking at someone, friend or date), NGL (anonymous feedback), Bags (what crypto people call their holdings).

## How NGL was conceived and built

- Instagram opened link sharing to everyone in October 2021; before that you needed 10,000 followers.
- Within 7 days his friend Isaiah Turner called with the idea: an anonymous messaging app. The group of consumer social builders had been obsessing over what to build with link sharing.
- NGL: post a link to your Instagram story, people tap it and send anonymous messages, you reply on your story. It's an anonymous version of the Instagram question sticker.
- It took about 3-4 weeks to build. Nothing existed for Instagram then, though older anonymous apps existed on other platforms.
- Then about 6 months of "crickets." The team worked on getting 95%+ of new users to post the link, and to share multiple replies, within about 60 seconds.
- Posting the link was a five-step manual process then (tap sticker, paste link, position it). They rewrote the explainer screens until it worked.
- NGL was built with about $5,000. He says it has generated "tens and tens and tens of millions" since.
- Scale cited: biggest app in the world by summer 2022; 150 million monthly active users and over 250 million downloads.
- Hunter said he has never raised venture as an entrepreneur.

## The viral loop: sender first, then receiver

- His core model: UGC gets the first users in, and a loop turns them into more users. Without a loop, founders keep spending on UGC, hit hard paywalls, watch everyone drop off, and reset the next month.
- Highest-signal endorsement is a friend inviting you or posting publicly. Design for "How do I get you to post it?"
- The loop must not make users look lame: "You want a viral loop that doesn't make you look lame... It makes you look socially acceptable."
- NGL's loop: your first experience is as the sender, not the receiver. You see a friend's link, send them a message, and see them reply on their story.
- After sending, a large pulsing button says "Get your own messages." You've just done the thing your friend benefits from, so you want that benefit yourself.
- Result: downloads became "free." Everyone who writes a message wants to post their own link, and the loop still drives the app 3.5+ years later.
- Activation testing: spend about $100-$200 to get an influencer to post the link, then manually watch every public story to see where users failed (forgot the link, misplaced it). Iterate in batches of a few hundred downloads until everyone understood the steps.
- Advice he echoed: if an app idea has no built-in spread mechanism, it may not be worth pursuing.

## UGC strategy and how NGL took off

- UGC means user-generated content, an actual user making content instead of a big, expensive influencer.
- In 2020 he and friends used a "fresh account" strategy. They hired TikTokers with under 50,000 followers at CPMs of $3 or less. They also ran VPN tactics, posting across For You pages. He calls this the "Wild West era."
- Now it is more regulated and commercialized, with professional UGC creators. Back then roughly 1 in 10 videos hit a million views; now it's closer to 1 in 100-500.
- Pre-launch NGL's UGC test was only 5-10 videos with scripts he wrote, at an agreed $3 CPM. Within 48 hours one video hit a million views, then 1,000 users became 10,000, and within about 36 hours it reached 1.5 million users.
- His use of UGC: get 1,000-10,000 users, then let the loop do the rest. He didn't rely on UGC to reach hundreds of millions of downloads.
- Platforms now distribute on video merit rather than follower count, so a creator with 1,000 followers can still get hundreds of thousands of views.

## Monetizing NGL and early financing

- Thesis: if NGL went viral (5-10 million downloads), carving out 1-2% of users into a pro subscription would make money.
- Desire he targeted: everyone wants to know who sent the message. NGL stays anonymous, so the paid feature is "hints," a pseudo-anonymous reveal.
- Hints use identifiers such as phone type, location, area code, carrier, and whether the sender has messaged before. This served power users without breaking the anonymity promise.
- Growing pains: a six-figure Mixpanel bill after about two days of data; he swapped analytics platforms constantly to dodge bills.
- Apple payouts are delayed: money made in January arrives in March. He was personally broke while the app made money.
- The only financing was a bridge loan from services that look at App Store MRR, for about 60 days. NGL had seven-figure MRR; the amount was roughly $100-$200K.
- Tools: Firebase for simpler apps like NGL (dashboard, databases, hot swapping); more complex apps like Bags didn't use it. Amplitude or Mixpanel for analytics, depending on his CTO's preference. Slack and App Store Connect.

## Why he avoids venture funding

- Belief: "You only understand the value of money when you have it." He has seen founders raise $25 million, lose their minds, and burn the company.
- He wants to have respect for money before raising. He doesn't rule out raising someday.
- Consumer apps: "you need two devs and a designer and then that's it." Funding is unnecessary unless the concept is capital-intensive, like rockets.
- He did help venture-backed companies, and raised venture at a company he worked at when he was 21-22, but never as an entrepreneur.

## Bags: crypto, meme coins, and royalties

- Background: bought his first Bitcoin around May 2017 (about $1,500), watched it hit $20,000, then crashed and got hacked. He kept building apps through 2018-2021, tried NFTs in 2021 without success, and got into crypto heavily around 2022-2023.
- He wanted to build a crypto app in 2017 after finding Coinbase poor. After NGL crossed 100 million users, he moved to it.
- Bags context (2023): no real mobile trading apps; trading was on web and Telegram bots. He and co-founder Finn (who left D Gods) wanted to bring crypto to mobile.
- Early product: a beta app with a large group chat and native in-chat trading. They sold an OG membership to about 35,000 people to seed evangelists. Then came a full trading app and a launchpad.
- Meme coin thesis: communities want skin in the game, and a holder identifies with others holding the same asset. Early idea: holder group chats, treating holdings as a social graph.
- Core product bet: meme coins lacked a revenue-generating business model. Bags built royalties into coins so projects earn from fees, reinvest them, split them, or pay dividends. He calls Bags the best way for meme coins to launch and make sustainable revenue.
