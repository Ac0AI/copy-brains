## Start with a simple v1, not a polished product

Hunter's playbook for a $1M/month consumer app begins with the bare-bones MVP.
- He defines the V1 feature set as "good enough to solve the problem" but "probably not optimized" and "rough on the edges."
- He gets a few hundred to a few thousand users on quickly.
- He tests content on TikTok or Instagram Reels before the app is live, even at pre-order stage. The question is whether the formats the app is premised on actually get a response.
- He designs every app himself in Figma, branding included (about a dozen apps solo). He started in PowerPoint, then Photoshop, then Sketch, then Figma.

## Pick one North Star metric and a single core action

Hunter wants one metric that "matters more than the other ones," because it makes tradeoff decisions obvious.
- **Wink** (make-new-friends swiping app): percentage of users who get a match on day one. He used the matching algorithm to pair heavy swipers with new users.
- **NGL:** percentage of users who share the link and share a few replies. Sharing was a four-step process with a tutorial. Seeing friends get replies made viewers more willing to send messages.
- **Bags** (crypto app): how many new users fund their account and make one trade on day one.
- If a decision works against the North Star, "it's just not the right decision."
- He wants over 90% of users completing the core action, with everyone going through one identical flow. Branching journeys make results hard to read.

## Build sharing or inviting into the core action

- Once the core action is proven, he builds sharing or inviting into it so growth isn't fully reliant on the TikTok video or ad that brought the user.
- Example: if 1,000 users drop in and 50% share, that is 500 more people reached, which raises the K factor.
- Hard invite gates can work. He cites Lapse (a viral photo app) requiring roughly three to five friend invites: many users hit the wall, but those who pass are extremely valuable.
- On NGL, almost every user went through the share step.
- Bags loops: log in with Twitter, claim a Bags link, share it, and invited users are credited on a leaderboard. Top Solana-ecosystem people appeared on it. The loops grew hype and followers to 100,000 on Twitter before the product was fully built out.
- For single-player apps (the Hallow example), he says to nail the single-player daily habit first. Social and multiplayer are then a growth lever, along with gating monetization behind a share or offering a discount for inviting friends and family.

## Seed with creators, then let the loop take over

- Marketing exists to "seed" the app: get enough people to download in a tight window so the growth loop activates.
- NGL spent less than $10,000 on marketing in total to reach a quarter billion downloads. He paid roughly $50 to $100 here and there for influencers to post the link on their story.
- For Wink he paid TikTokers to make videos.
- After the core action is proven, he applies more pressure: new audiences, niches, creators in other countries and time zones.
- His principle is not to pay a lot. Test small, build things that work at small scale, and "all you really need is that one viral video." Paid or free depends on creator and agency relationships, with no standard answer.

## Old ideas in new channels

- NGL was not a new idea (he names Ask.fm and Yik Yak). It launched when Instagram opened link stickers to everyone in October 2021, previously requiring 10,000 followers. He and friends were building within days and shipped in a few weeks.
- It then took about six months to seed. He also had to train users to tap the link sticker, paste, and position it.
- His takeaway: don't reinvent the wheel. Look for a proven idea plus a new distribution channel, and for something "not optimized on this platform."
- He encourages thinking in frameworks over ideas: "NGL for X, Cali for X, Locket for X, Pangu for X."
- Examples he praises: Cal AI (simple tool, packaged with an onboarding funnel that makes it seem more valuable than it is), Locket (first to make the iPhone widget social), Pangu (AI-powered virtual pets).
- He points to unexploited Apple surfaces: live activities and lock screen widgets.
- Apple's yearly iOS releases often hand builders new real estate.

## Brand and name: short, slang, on the nose

- Names like NGL ("not going to lie"), Wink and Bags are short and obvious about what the app is. He says complicated names confuse people.
- Using a community's slang as the name sets a young, comfortable, colloquial tone. He carries that language into popups and in-app copy, so the app feels like it understands the user.
- He notes it was surprising nobody had built a crypto app called Bags.
- He stresses an authentic, memeable, relatable voice. NGL has about 2 million Instagram followers and runs meme formats and funny messages. Bags has over 100,000 Twitter followers, posts daily, and has an intern who keeps it active. He says it increases affinity, trust and sharing.

## Why young builders should do consumer

- He advises people in their teens, 20s and college years to take many swings, and recommends consumer mobile apps over B2B or SaaS.
- Reasons: you learn human psychology, how to build products people love and how to go viral, and you can reach the whole world. It's about building and marketing rather than contracts.
- He says AI tools are getting good enough that you will soon not need a technical cofounder, though "we're not fully there yet."
- He's bullish on consumer crypto going mainstream with regulation, and on AR glasses driven by the phone's compute.
