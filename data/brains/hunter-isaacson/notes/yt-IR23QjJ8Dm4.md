## Hunter's first apps: Leader and the lessons from failing young

- Hunter's first app, Leader, was a "gen Z Foursquare." He built it at 17-18, convinced it would make him a billionaire before 22.
- It took about 2.5 years and failed. He calls it "a 2 and a half year lesson on everything that I shouldn't have done in business."
- His main lesson was never to outsource development. He should also have gone to people who knew how to build instead of hiding from experts out of imposter syndrome.
- He over-obsessed on pixel-perfect design and the wrong things, which made the app complicated and hard to build.
- He argues for emotional detachment in a first business: "business is really just like shoveling [expletive] and failing." If it fails, "who cares," because once you've succeeded once, you can have 10-30 failures in your portfolio.
- He applied to YC (once or twice) and other accelerators and was rejected everywhere. His view is that accelerators are good to get into but "not going to make or break it."
- His advice to early-stage founders: "just go fail." Fall on your face, and don't worry about what your high-school friends think.

## Zoom University: a simple, social product built at the right moment

- Zoom University was a double-dating app for college students, launched during COVID. The idea came from a friend, Daniel, and Hunter joined as designer. They shipped the app within about 24 hours of agreeing to work together.
- Why college students: the younger you skew, the more close friends each user has. Anything meant to spread organically and virally should target the people with the most social connections (college, high school, middle school).
- Double dating worked because girls are more comfortable going out with a friend. The app ended up with a 7-to-1 girl-to-guy ratio.
- Timing helped: parties disappeared and college kids wanted to meet each other.
- It made little money but went viral and reached the top 10 of the App Store. He calls it a "moderate W" after the big L of Leader, and says it proved to him this was possible.
- The contrast between Leader and Zoom U taught him his core lesson: build something simple that meets one specific demand. Leader was complicated and failed; Zoom U was a very simple product that went viral.

## Consulting for big brands and building at Nine Count

- After Zoom U, Hunter did consulting for Fortune 500 brands, helping them reach younger audiences. He found it frustrating because he couldn't change anything (fonts, colors, advice) without layers of marketing approval. It convinced him he wanted to own his own thing and not be "a small cog in a big machine."
- He then joined Nine Count, a consumer social holding company led by Alex Hoffman, former president of Musical.ly (acquired by ByteDance for about $1B). Alex became his first mentor.
- He deliberately chose to be a designer/product person for a while, citing Evan Spiegel and Brian Chesky as "design CEOs."
- At Nine Count he designed 10 apps and shipped 5. The first focus was Wink, which became the largest Snapchat friend-finder. Wink and others got free organic downloads from Snapchat, which taught him the importance of a constant influx of new users.
- He describes this period as "paying my dues," working with experts and tens of millions of VC dollars behind the team.
- His favorite app there was a voice-chat game app: match with a stranger, play hyper-casual games, then swap Snapchats. It had some of the best foundational metrics he has ever seen but was shut down by Apple for violating terms.

## How to build a viral app: the exchange and the share loop

- Start with the user journey. Ask what you get from the user (name, phone number, Instagram handle) and what you give them (dopamine, followers, a cool photo). He says: "It's an exchange."
- The sequence: concept and journey, heavy design work (Figma prototyping), front-end build (SwiftUI for iOS, or React Native for cross-platform), then App Store submission. He calls himself a front-end engineer who prefers to be the product lead running design, product and marketing, and says his product skills are stronger than his engineering skills.
- App Store rejections are usually mundane: a missing privacy policy or a missing restore-purchase button. Approval generally comes after one or two rejections.
- Growth rule: build something that can be shared organically. "Make sure you figure out how person A can bring person B and they bring person C." He says many people skip this step and "build products that don't work."
- His first question for any app, and the one he asks the founders he advises: "How does this grow organically?" If you aren't asking it, "you failed level one."
- He pushes back on features that nobody would share ("no one's going to share that, this is lame").
- App Store ranking is a "pure meritocracy": no cheat code, and you can't run ads to reach number one. Only about a hundred teams really compete at the top, and the business is built on failing over and over before something works.
- Staying viral is hard; most apps get 15 minutes, fail to monetize and raise at inflated valuations. Hunter designs apps so the entire app is a funnel to share, so growth continues as long as people keep sharing.

## NGL: building and growing the anonymous Q&A app

- NGL was anonymous Q&A for Instagram stories. It was built with co-founder Isaiah and others on the day Instagram opened link stickers to everyone. The team assumed every team would do the same thing, so they built it in 3 weeks.
- Nobody copied them, and the app was a dud for about 4-6 months. Hunter says he knew it would work from first principles: it grows automatically through Instagram, the gossip is juicy, teens love anonymous apps, and it is a tool, not a platform.
- The company was built with $10,000, most of which went to a domain they never used. The rest paid influencers about $100-200 each.
- The hardest problem was the share flow. Users had to copy the link, copy the asset, open Instagram and place the link. NGL effectively trained 100M+ people to use links in stories.
- Small tweaks that turned it around:
  - Renamed from "ask.fun" to NGL.
  - Made the tutorial unskippable.
  - Tweaked button copy and added a disclaimer.
  - Added "100% anonymous and secure" text on the website, which made people willing to send crazier messages.
- He describes the method as making a change, checking the numbers, keeping good changes and rolling back bad ones, "for months and months."
- The app first popped off in New Zealand (April/May 2022, about 7,000 users, roughly $2K that month). They used that window to iterate, add monetization and test social post styles.
- It went viral about a week after they launched the Android app. His takeaway: "Android gave us the downloads, iPhone gave us the revenue." Viral in places like Tunisia, Romania, Puerto Rico, Asia, Europe and South America. He now builds everything cross-platform, and notes the Middle East/North Africa and Southeast Asia download new apps eagerly.

## NGL monetization and results

- Monetization was copied from apps in the same space: a weekly subscription selling premium features. They built it in before going viral, during the New Zealand test.
- His view on copying: "everybody copies each other and they copy each other publicly... the playbook is in front of you."
- On scale: you don't need many paying users when it's a weekly subscription, because "a couple hundred people" quickly adds up.
- Numbers he gives: 150M+ downloads, about 60-70% of his total revenue from NGL, and roughly $40M total revenue across all the apps he has built. The company cash-flowed $1 million in its first viral month. His income went from about $100,000 a year to that amount per day.
- The app never fell off a cliff. Revenue went up and stayed. Partners now run day-to-day operations.
- He kept the app unchanged on purpose. Many apps ruin a good thing by piling on features (he cites Snapchat adding a feed, a map and shows). Everyone pitched him ways to turn NGL into something bigger, and he ignored them: "we're not building a social platform here, this is a tool, it's a social utility."
- When the app blew up, there was no celebration at first: press, copycats, competitors and lawsuits arrived at once. The celebrating came months later.

## Design principles: simplicity, magic moments and tolerating churn

- Keep it simple: build something that just does what it's supposed to do, and don't bog it down with unnecessary features.
- Build for the one-time user. "Build this for one person is going to use it one time... make sure the app can survive if 90% of the people use it once." If you churn on day one, he's fine as long as he got something: either monetized you or got you to share.
- He believes that this philosophy leads to daily retention, because you end up optimizing the key moments.
- Deliver the "magic moment" in the first 60 seconds: get something from the user, give them the dopamine, then let them run. If they don't come back, fine; if they do, great.
- Apps that require constant attention don't last as long because there are too many alternatives. A tool that sits on top of a durable platform (Instagram) can last a decade.
- Use human nature: people share an app when it gives them a good feeling and makes them look cool, which he calls "a very fair exchange."
- Make the work fun: "if it's a little bit more fun... you're going to much more enjoy your time in the trenches."

## Bags app and where Hunter is going next

- He is building Bags, which he describes as the Discord/Telegram replacement for financial messaging. Its core idea: "if you own an asset, you are now in a chat about that asset," starting with holder chats and Alpha-chat communities.
- He says it is a crypto/finance app where users never see the words crypto, NFT or web3. He loves the technology but dislikes the "web 3" packaging: "just give them a product that makes sense that you use the technology in the back end."
- Why fintech: the ceiling for NGL-type apps is limited and "you can't get into the billion dollar club" with them, while financial apps have more valuable data, users with more money and a different demographic. He wants "an arena where the ceiling is much higher."
- He is also working on other, unannounced products with several teams. They are social but not anonymous, built on the same thesis of sharing loops and flywheels: "I bring someone and they bring someone."
- He advises other founders and says several companies he works with have built number-one apps. He typically asks them why a feature exists and how it will grow.
- Outlook: he thinks the golden age of apps is ahead, driven by Gen Z and Gen Alpha gaining spending power and by AR glasses plus advanced generative AI in about five years. He plans to build everything he wants on the phone form factor before moving to AR/VR.
- His stated plan is to put his head down for the next four to five years, with the goal of becoming a multi-billionaire. His mindset: "the people that created everything in the world are no smarter than me." His father's advice: "Don't chase money, chase your passion, just make sure your passion can make money."
