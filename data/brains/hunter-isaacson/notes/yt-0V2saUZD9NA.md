## Hunter's route to building apps: design-first, MVP-light

- Hunter started his first app at 18, in a 3-month gap before USC, expecting a billion-dollar company in a year. He learned it was much harder but loved the process.
- He is technical on web and product (modded Minecraft servers at 12-13) but hires strong engineers for apps. His own skill is design and product. He "became known as the guy" for app design in his builder group, which brought partnership invitations.
- His career path: Zoom University (a college double-dating video app, roughly a quarter million downloads or fewer), then Wink (a make-new-friends app, 70M+ downloads, where he was an employee and designer, not an owner), then NGL as his eighth app. He says NGL took many failures to reach.
- At about 21 he was recruited by the former president of Musical.ly (which became TikTok). He spent almost two years there as the only designer, building half a dozen apps while the company grew from 12 to 50-60 people. He calls it a lab where he could test many hypotheses.
- He built NGL in about four weeks from idea to launch, then saw six months of nobody using it before product-market fit.

## Lessons from his mentor: session one and A/B testing

- Focus on "session one" rather than D1/D7/D30 retention. The goal is to get the user to do something meaningful in the first session, so that even if they close the app you got something.
- Make onboarding flawless, aiming for 99% of people to complete it.
- Test relentlessly. He cared more about UI than UX early on and learned "it's not about making an art project". A good-looking purple gradient button can lose to a red one on conversion.
- Colors that win in his apps: red, blue, green. He writes all of the copy himself so the tone stays consistent.
- Ask what problem each screen solves, not whether it looks good.

## The viral loop playbook (NGL)

- NGL spread because your friend's Instagram story post is both the discovery channel and a "stamp of approval". Seeing multiple friends post it creates social safety ("this is normal, not weird").
- Onboarding takes 1-2 steps with no auth, 15-20 seconds. The user has their link posted within about 60 seconds of download.
- Having sent a message to a friend's link, the new user already knows what receiving feels like, so the value is proven before the install. That loop is why growth kept going (over 250M downloads since June 2022, not slowing).
- NGL had no marketing budget. It unlocked Instagram as a channel because link-in-story only became available around the end of 2021.
- Instagram follow button: NGL has over 2M followers because the app has a "follow us" button. The page works as a legitimacy stamp and a retention nudge.
- Per-app comparison: Wink's session one was a swipe stack with 100 gems, minus 10 per right swipe. At the end a pop-up offered premium or "share on your story for free", which taught him people share a lot when asked at the right moment.

## Hunter's framework for a new app

1. Pick a name everyone understands and can pronounce, that works in every language (NGL = "not gonna lie"; Bags = money bags).
2. Design young and fun, with clean copy.
3. Decide what you need in onboarding (phone, email, birthday, gender, or nothing).
4. Define session one right after onboarding (NGL: copy link, share, get messages, reply on story).
5. Create an aha moment fast, then immediately ask for a share or direct invite, in context (a dating app is better asking users to text a best friend than to post on a story).
6. Pick one North Star metric (NGL: link shares and replies; Bags: users who fund their accounts).
7. Don't overbuild. Crush onboarding and session one, then add a little more.

He says to find behavior that already exists, unoptimized, on the internet or other apps. Bags came from users already trading meme coins across disconnected wallet, screener, exchange and messenger apps. NGL came from the Ask.fm-style anonymous format on a new channel.

## Bags: launch with the viral loop before the product

- Bags is a financial app to buy and sell crypto with Apple Pay, aiming to be the simplest way for anyone to buy crypto, then pay with it. He has been in crypto since 2017.
- Launch order: the first release had only claimable Bags links (every profile link is a wallet), with no wallets yet. People shared their links, tapped through to a site, claimed theirs and shared again, the same loop as NGL.
- That produced a couple hundred thousand registered profiles and over 100,000 Twitter followers. He says that is a lot for crypto and "nothing" for his other apps.
- His reasoning: acceptable user counts depend on average revenue per user. A financial app with money in it has higher ARPU and retention than a social app, so it needs fewer users. He notes most NGL users never monetized.
- Bags took 6+ months to build, with a rebuild and core feature changes. Security is handled by large external providers, with multiple login methods and authenticator-app 2FA.
- His advice: once you have a basic MVP, go on TikTok and "start pumping videos". If it works you will know.

## Lifestyle design and manifestation

- At 15 he manifested a luxury lifestyle: a fake version of a luxury watch, styled clothes, visits to Lamborghini dealerships at 17. Years later he realized he was living what he had told himself.
- His advice: "design your lifestyle before you do anything." Think 10-30 years ahead (where you live, kids, career, friendships, daily routine). Work backwards to the industries that deliver that lifestyle, then your unique edge, then pick a skill (his was design).
- Passion should be able to make money: "do not chase money, chase your passion", but check that the passion supports the lifestyle you want.
- Radical self-accountability: treat everything in your life as your doing. Each month he notes his biggest problem on his phone, and when it is solved he rereads it as a reminder.

## Questioning everything and being early

- Hunter says his money came after he started questioning things. His mind opened from 2018 and widened by 2020, after he noticed false claims in the news and asked what else was untrue.
- His self-described edge: "I'm usually just early." Looking at an issue from every angle and not following man-made protocols gives you the conviction to build early. He says this is common sense.
- His practice: doubt everything, including his own claims, in the sense of seeking truth. Debates with friends are to find the answer, not to be right.
- He believes the world is in a transition between old and new systems, expecting humanoid robots in homes in about 10-15 years with significant adoption, and says AI will free human time. He is concerned it could go toward tyranny instead of abundance.
- He holds speculative views (ancient advanced civilizations, hidden land, free energy) that are his opinions and not business guidance.
