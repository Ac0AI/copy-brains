## Building NGL and why he stopped competing with himself

- Hunter built his first company at 17-18. He failed for a while, made no money and was "dirt poor", then slowly had apps start to work. He went from failed entrepreneur to employee, then realized he was an entrepreneur after all.
- He says he learned he "could just do things" when NGL blew up: it hit millions of downloads a day.
- NGL is an anonymous-questions app where friends ask each other questions and post the answers to their Instagram stories. It has about 250 million users and, by his figures, does $100 million a year.
- He also cites Wink at 70 million downloads.
- His first app idea, "Rally", came in 2015 when he was 18. It was a Tinder-style party app where a host sets up a party and approves who can attend, replacing messy Facebook invite lists. He had a dev build an MVP, then dropped it.
- He says it matched Partiful "feature for feature". His takeaway is that ideas float in the ether and you can pick one up when you are tuned in.

## Why he left social apps for finance (Bags)

- After NGL he had many social-app ideas ready to go, each reusing the same social dynamics. He decided not to build another social app aimed at 500 million users.
- His reasoning: "sometimes in life you have to not compete with your former self." Founders repeat their first win and deliberately re-make themselves the underdog in the same space. He had already hit that level.
- He moved to consumer finance and crypto, a space he had loved for years, around 2023. He wanted to bank the underbanked, debanked and unbanked, and move people away from centralized banking.
- He built it partly for himself, because he feared being debanked. During COVID he was censored online for his views, and several tech friends were debanked.
- NGL pushed free speech (anonymous, with only bullying filters). Bags pushes personal freedom and sovereignty: "your money should be yours."
- The vision: bring a billion people into crypto and change how money moves. He wants Bags to be the app people think of for anything financial: making money, organizing and spending it, and talking about it with friends.
- He is asset-agnostic. Memecoins are today's use case, but "crypto is cyclical", so Bags will support whatever the asset class becomes.

## What building in crypto actually takes

- Hunter says building in crypto is the opposite of investing in it: "Building in crypto requires years. Investing in crypto requires minutes."
- Good devs are hard to find, especially mobile devs who also understand crypto.
- APIs are limited and documentation is thin, so you build much from scratch.
- Many infrastructure providers don't scale.
- Even basics like landing a transaction or pulling data from the chain are harder than they sound.
- His view is that crypto is only now at the point where people can build in it, and it is still hard.

## Money, wins and staying a builder

- He argues you are in the same position whether rich or broke: you are still building. Friends who went from millionaire to nothing in crypto found it "not that different"; only their egos were hurt.
- His analogy is Monopoly. Each win is passing Go and collecting $200, and then you see another level: "There's just more hotels on the board."
- He says that to be free in today's system you need billions, and he calls money "slavery in a sense."
- He expects transacting to persist in some form, such as goods or AI compute, but not as paper dollars. That is why he thinks consumer finance will always have a role.


## His view of AI, robots and the economy

- He thinks the real iPhone replacement is the humanoid robot, with glasses and contacts as companions. His comparison: 15 years ago a boarding pass on your phone was wild, and now it is normal.
- He expects a society of abundance, with robots building 24/7. Much human capital is wasted today on people working jobs they hate.
- He predicts the new status metric for wealthy people will be how many robots they own, not how many jets.
- He expects money to change radically because it represents scarcity, and abundance removes scarcity. He thinks it moves toward peer-to-peer, sovereign communities and city-states rather than large nations and central banks.
- He expects school in its current form to disappear and be replaced by something like apprenticeship. He says the best part of school is social development, such as learning to handle bullies and peer pressure.
- He expects a split in society: some go fully into the metaverse, while others keep the technology but go spiritual and build with old techniques.
- His darker thesis is a long war with AI, with robots fighting robots, tiny weaponized drones and engineered viruses. He says people will have to rediscover ancient frequency and sound technology, and he plans to leave cities for a protected island.

## Moving the Overton window

- On Kanye, Hunter says some people's role in society is to push the Overton window, so that what they said years ago becomes acceptable. He respects Kanye as probably the best artist of the last 30 years.
- He describes Kanye's pattern as "attention transmutation": he says something wild, sends people to Yeezy.com, crashes out, disappears for years, and returns.
- His critique is that Kanye should target specific people who wronged him, not whole groups.
