## How Alexandr describes AI: three ingredients and the race between them

- AI is "programming computers to be able to start thinking like humans." Traditional programming is "barebones"; AI replicates what brains do.
- Modern AI started with computer vision (ImageNet, AlexNet). A milestone was YouTube's algorithm that could spot cats in videos around 2011-2012. Self-driving cars were the first major use case and "all the rage" when he started Scale in 2016.
- Every AI system has three ingredients:
  - **Chips (compute):** GPUs/TPUs in huge data centers, such as Elon's Colossus.
  - **Data:** where the algorithms learn patterns. "Data is the new oil." Anything an AI knows or says comes from its data.
  - **Algorithms:** the math run on the chips, learned from the data.
- He uses a "body of water" analogy for data. The job is to keep the water as clean as possible and fill it with information about everything.
- Companies and countries compete on all three dimensions. Chips decide who has the most advanced hardware, data decides whose water is cleaner and bigger, and algorithms decide who has the cleverest tricks.
- His US vs China rack-and-stack, as of early 2025:
  - Chips: the US is ahead.
  - Data: China is probably ahead, because it has invested in data for a long time.
  - Algorithms: roughly neck and neck.
- He calls it a tight race and points to DeepSeek's rise as evidence.

## Scale AI's model: the "Uber for AI" data marketplace

- Alexandr frames Scale as "almost like the Uber of AI." The algorithms that need data are one side of the market. A network of people who produce that data are the other.
- Scale runs this through a platform called Outlier. People, called "contributors," log on and get paid to improve models.
- Contributors earned about $500 million total on Outlier last year, across 9,000 different towns in the US.
- Example of the work: a nurse sees an AI tell someone with stomach pain to wait it out. The nurse corrects it ("you might have appendicitis"), and the correction feeds back into the model.
- He wants experts in everything, in any subject.
- Quality control works in two ways:
  - People check other people's work.
  - Automated systems check for spam.
- Contributors get a short orientation on what to do and what expertise to bring.
- He says models are much better in English, especially American English, than in other languages. Scale hires people worldwide to improve them in other languages.
- Scale sits "under the hood" of the major models. It helps power ChatGPT/OpenAI, Google's AI and Meta's AI, mostly on the data side.
- It also works with large companies and the government:
  - Time Magazine
  - Toyota (cars)
  - Pharma companies
  - Hospitals
- The government is "not our biggest" customer but "an important one."

## Why he says AI creates jobs

- He argues the jobs story "is not told enough" and that the AI industry is partly to blame for making AI sound sci-fi and scary.
- He expects "jobs from AI" to be "the fastest growing jobs in the world for the years to come."
- Categories of new work:
  - Contributing data and expertise to models. This keeps growing because the world keeps changing, and AI can only partly generate its own data.
  - Building AI into applications and helping companies use it.
  - Managing, improving and watching AI systems so they don't misbehave, for example by spamming a million people.
- Everyone gets "promoted to being a manager." A laptop worker might manage "a little pod of 10 AI agents" and check their work and mistakes.
- He expects the economy to grow so much that the limit becomes "how many AIs can you have," which is capped by how many managers you have.
- He compares this to history. Farming got more efficient, so people moved into entertainment and finance.
- Blue-collar trades (plumbers, electricians) still need physical work.
- Education gap: he says no real training programs exist yet. He would like to help build them with colleges and "teach everybody in America how to best contribute to the AIs."
- Every company will need to figure out AI the way every company had to figure out the internet. He says to start early and find a unique twist so you stand out.

## AI as a tool to turn ideas into reality

- His pitch: people have thousands of ideas and time for a few. AI should help ideas become real, giving humans "more agency" and "more sovereignty."
- Movie example: ask an AI to draft a script, find funders, suggest cast within a budget, find locations and list talent agencies. Over time it does the legwork, reaching out to people, handling logistics and booking.
- Other uses: starting businesses more easily, planning events, and directors producing more of their ideas.
- He calls this the "agents" idea. Humans stay in control and tell the AI what they want.
- On sameness (everyone getting the same movie): human creativity still matters. You need an angle and "your own spice." The key human job is "what is my fresh take."
- He expects AI to speed up biology and medicine:
  - AI "understands molecules and biology better than humans" in some respects.
  - Work that took a PhD biologist about 5 years can take it minutes.
  - He thinks cancer and heart disease could be cured in our lifetimes, and lifespans lengthened "pretty dramatically."
- How to learn: use ChatGPT for small things. Ask how AI works, your town's history or your last name's origin. Using it shows what it's good and bad at, and that makes it less scary.
- Today's AI is "still really bad at a lot of things." It knows more facts than any person (like Google), but it is not smarter than humanity. He calls it a "very different kind of intelligence."

## Why the US vs China AI race matters

He gives three reasons the winner matters:

- **Cultural:** AI reflects the values of the country that builds it. US AI supports free speech. Chinese AI is censored, and he says Chinese ideologies would be exported if it won.
  - Example: Chinese models won't answer questions about Xi or Tiananmen. DeepSeek deflected "is Xi a good guy" with "let's talk about something else."
  - He also cites the Uyghurs, which he says a Chinese AI wouldn't discuss honestly.
- **Economic:** the winning country's economy grows faster.
- **Military:** AI is military technology. He cites cyber warfare:
  - Salt Typhoon, a hack of US telecom companies.
  - The earlier hack of the US security clearance database.
  - AI already beating top human hackers, so the contest becomes whose AI defends and attacks better.
  - Information warfare (propaganda), including mass hiring of people to seed stories on chat groups.
- Taiwan:
  - TSMC makes nearly 100% of the advanced AI chips.
  - Its fabs use machines costing hundreds of millions of dollars and are built to withstand seismic movement.
  - He says Xi ordered the military to be ready to move on Taiwan by 2027, and China could see capturing the chips as a way to win AI.
- Chip export controls: he calls them a hot topic in DC. There are good arguments on both sides (free market and bolstering US industry vs. not giving rivals an advantage). His stance: "let's make sure America wins and let's start from there."
- He sees no US government risk of curtailing free speech through AI right now, but says values must be upheld.

## AI safety and his red lines

- His biggest worry is deception and persuasion. He doesn't want AIs "specifically programmed to make me think a certain thing" or that nudge him to do things he shouldn't.
- How Scale keeps AI "clean":
  - Clean data, with filters and checks on what goes in.
  - Constant testing of models for harm. For example, models must refuse to help make a bomb or a "COVID 2.0."
- Self-improving AI: he says it's a hot industry topic and he's wary. Humans should "always have the steering wheel" and not let AI iterate and improve itself unchecked.
- Advertising: he hopes the AI industry avoids advertising as much as possible. His reasoning: AI should be a tool that "helps people first and foremost," like a thought partner or assistant. People shouldn't feel "used by the AIs."
- Fear of AI comes from not understanding it. His view: "it is a cool technology but it's not that magical." It is data, crunched into algorithms. Explaining it and its opportunities removes the "boogeyman."

## Origin story: from math competitions to founding Scale at 19

- He grew up in Los Alamos, New Mexico, where both parents were physicists (his mother studied plasma). Most kids' parents worked at the national lab, so "anything was possible."
- Competitive math drove him:
  - In 6th grade, a state math contest offered an all-expenses-paid Disney World trip for the top four. He placed fourth.
  - He became a nationally ranked "mathlete" in middle and high school.
  - He took the USAMO (a 9-hour, 6-problem exam over two days) first in 8th grade, and attended math camps.
- Competitive math scores helped him get into MIT, where he took the AI classes.
- Triggers for AI:
  - A class side project: a camera in his fridge to catch roommates stealing his food. One step "was too easy" and just worked.
  - AlphaGo beating Lee Sedol in March 2016.
- He dropped out of MIT after one year because he was "feeling really impatient." In May 2016, two months after the AlphaGo match, he flew from Boston to San Francisco and started Scale AI.
- Back then AI was a niche topic. He says he always knew it would get bigger but "could have never predicted" the scale.
- His parents (and two older brothers, all with PhDs) were worried at first. He told them he'd go back and finish school. They're now proud.
- Networking example: in 2018, when Scale was a "baby company," he "kind of YOLO invited" Jensen Huang to a dinner in Silicon Valley, and Jensen said yes.
- He credits his parents for his math skills and says he owes them everything.
- He grew up "solidly middle class."
