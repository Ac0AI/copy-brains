## Why AI depends on humans and will not replace them

- Alexandr argues AI "has always been and will always be dependent on humans." He says this belief is part of why he started Scale.
- His core setup: algorithms need data, and data needs humans.
- Expert humans are often required to keep data accurate. Only humans understand the context and nuance needed to annotate data correctly before it is fed to algorithms.
- Humans teach algorithms what to do. They make the decisions and guide the system, and the AI learns from that and repeats it.
- He sees AI as a tool, not something to fear. It is a "supercharger" for humanity: where AI is better than humans, it makes humans better.

## Why Scale was started: data is the bottleneck for AI

- Alexandr studied AI at MIT in 2016 and noticed almost no one outside of coursework was building anything with AI. Mechanical engineers built catapults, electrical engineers built robots, and computer science students built apps.
- He concluded data was the missing piece: "Bad data or lack of data leads to bad AI." Data cannot be treated as an afterthought.
- A personal experiment made it concrete. He put a camera in his fridge to predict grocery restocking. He could not generate enough data alone to make the algorithms succeed (it did reveal his roommate was stealing his food).
- The conclusion was that building large, high-quality datasets to power every AI application would be a central problem for AI. That was the motivation for Scale.
- Scale's premise: high-quality data, which produces reliable AI outcomes, requires human insight and guidance.

## Teaching AI human values and intentions

- Alexandr says AI needs humans to teach it individual values, push it toward thoughtful outcomes, and keep human intent and values aligned with the AI.
- "Machines make mistakes." Humans have to teach them and push them toward telling the truth.
- Teaching intentions and values is how AI ends up with fair, ethical outcomes in line with human values.
- Continuous alignment with human intent will always require humans, and human ideas and creativity can matter more with AI power behind them.
- Real-world problems have a long tail and there are always "unknown unknowns," so humans can never be fully removed from the AI development lifecycle.

## Human-in-the-loop example: chatbots in 2016

- When chatbots took off in 2016, just as Scale was starting, Alexandr says people assumed a fully automated system was impossible. Conversations can take many paths, and it is hard to build AI that handles all of them.
- Chatbots work because humans behind them make a decision once, and the bot repeats it again and again.
- His takeaway: it is impossible for AI to improve or change without human involvement.

## What AI automates first

- Alexandr says the first things AI automates are not what people expect. It is not the tasks humans consider simplest, but the areas "where we have the most data."
- His example is weather. Forecasting is hard because tiny factors can have huge effects (the butterfly effect) and there is far more data than any person can understand. AI can analyze those vast oceans of data and already gives very accurate short-term predictions, including for critical storms and floods.
- He says the brightest minds in AI are focused on positive use cases, not on building replacements for humans. They build tools that save human time and energy so people can focus on what humans uniquely solve.

## Healthcare and drug discovery

- Alexandr cites the Association of American Medical Colleges: the US could face a shortage of between 38,000 and 124,000 physicians by 2034.
- Healthcare is full of repetitive tasks suited to AI. Patients generate blood tests, lab results, X-rays and more, and doctors analyze all of it to make decisions.
- AI can proactively analyze that data and cross-reference it against past cases. It can flag that something is wrong long before a doctor would and alert the doctor if it needs attention.
- Doctors stay integral, but diagnosis takes less time. This lets them serve more patients.
- In drug discovery, AI processes trial data, patient data and protein simulations to guide a more efficient process for finding new drugs and compounds. He says recent advances have sped up the scientific process by letting teams process more data than ever.

## Scale's Ukraine satellite imagery work

- Alexandr points to the Russia-Ukraine war as a real-world example. AI can assess satellite imagery quickly and accurately so Ukrainian forces can respond faster.
- Scale used its platform for damage assessment in key war-affected areas. It analyzed more than 2,000 square kilometers of Ukraine and identified more than 370,000 buildings, including thousands not available in other datasets.
- The focus was Kyiv, Kharkiv and Dnipro. Scale provided some data directly to the government and users, and made data publicly available to the wider AI community.
- Uses he describes: change detection (algorithms monitor imagery continuously and alert a human when they see movement or change), resource allocation, and tracking farmland and agricultural damage in real time, since Ukraine is a major food supplier.

## Human-led AI and creativity

- Alexandr says AI will automate repetitive tasks that do not require continuous human decision or creativity. That frees time to explore new ideas.
- He believes it lets people be more creative and idea-focused, embracing "the generative aspects of human nature" so they can move faster with ideas and build better solutions to the biggest problems.
- His conclusion: "human-led AI is the way forward."
