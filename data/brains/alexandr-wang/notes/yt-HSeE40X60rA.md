## Scale AI's three pillars and the data wall

Alexandr says AI progress rests on three pillars: compute, data and algorithms. Compute is powered by folks like Nvidia, algorithms by the large labs like OpenAI, and data is "fueled by Scale". Scale's goal is to produce the frontier data needed for frontier advances, in partnership with the large labs. It also helps every enterprise and government use its own proprietary data to build frontier AI.

- He describes Scale as "the data Foundry for AI".
- He calls producing frontier data "one of the great human projects of our time". The only model for the intelligence being built is humanity, so data production is a marriage of human experts with technical and algorithmic techniques.
- Publicly available data is largely exhausted ("Common Crawl is all done"), so the industry is hitting a "data wall".
- He expects data production to be a hallmark of the next phase of AI.

## Three things data needs to do next

Alexandr names three requirements for getting past the data wall.

- **Complexity (frontier data):** Many desired capabilities are blocked by a lack of data. Agents are the example: "there's just no agent data on the internet", and no agent really works well.
- **Tool composition:** Scale has work coming out showing that all the frontier models are very bad at chaining multiple tools in a row. An example is looking something up, writing a Python script, then charting the result. Humans do this naturally, but the reasoning and agentic chains are not captured anywhere.
- **Abundance:** Capture more of what humans do in the field of work, and invest in synthetic and hybrid data, where synthetic data is generated with humans in the loop. He compares this to chips: "we need to have effectively data foundries" to produce huge amounts of data, the way chip foundries produce chips.
- **Measurement:** This is the "often underrated" last leg. The industry used to add data and check how good the model was. Now it must get scientific about what the model cannot do, and so exactly which data to add.

## Phases of language model development

Alexandr splits the field into phases.

- **Phase one:** Early, small-scale research, from the original Transformer paper up to roughly GPT-3.
- **Phase two:** Scaling, from GPT-3 until now. OpenAI scaled to GPT-4 and beyond, then Google, Anthropic, Meta and xAI joined. He says it was mostly engineering and execution: making large training runs work, avoiding bugs, and setting up bigger clusters.
- **Next phase:** Research matters more again. He expects more divergence between labs over which research directions break through.

On big tech's data advantage, he sees regulatory limits on using existing corpora, especially in Europe. His example is Meta's research training image recognition on public Instagram photos with their hashtags, which caused regulatory problems in Europe. The labs' bigger edge is profitable businesses that supply near-infinite capital.

## Why big tech keeps spending on AI

Alexandr lays out why the large companies' capex risk is lopsided.

- If they nail AI, they could add about a trillion dollars of market cap. If they under-invest and miss it, there is existential risk, since their businesses are all potentially disruptable.
- The extra spend, which he puts at roughly 20 or 30 billion of capex per year, can be recouped even in the worst case. Facebook and Google can improve their advertising systems, and Apple can drive an upgrade cycle.
- The spending benefits the industry. The cloud providers rent out compute, and open-source models like Llama 3.1 make the fruits of that investment broadly accessible.

## Model-layer economics and where the good businesses are

Alexandr says inference pricing has fallen by two orders of magnitude over about two years. "Intelligence might be a commodity."

- Renting out pure models is "likely to be a relatively mediocre long-term business", unless a lab has a durable breakthrough.
- Two things cap value at the model layer: Meta continuing to open source, and several labs reaching similar performance.
- The better businesses are above and below the model. Below are Nvidia and the clouds. Clouds have good margins because it is logistically hard to set up large GPU clusters.
- Above are applications. ChatGPT is a great business, and startup apps that nail early product-market fit can be too, because the value they create for customers far exceeds inference cost.
- He expects labs to push into deeper product integrations, citing Anthropic's Artifacts in Claude. "Boring chatbots" are not the end product.
- Product innovation is hard to predict. OpenAI was itself surprised by how popular ChatGPT was. OpenAI and Anthropic each hired a Chief Product Officer within about two months of each other.

## How enterprises should approach AI

Alexandr describes an enterprise frenzy of proofs of concept (POCs). Many companies bought AI tools to try every low-hanging idea, and far fewer POCs reached production than the industry expected. The results so far are marginal efficiency gains, such as support and some creative tasks.

- He pushes customers to ask which AI efforts could meaningfully move their stock price. He says nearly every enterprise has latent potential to do that.
- Today the gains come mostly as cost savings. Better customer experiences follow, especially where there is a lot of manual customer interaction, and those can eventually win market share.
- It is a multi-year investment cycle: CEOs may not see gains next quarter, but those who pull through will see massive transformation.
- He views small-use-case experimentation as fine, but "that's not what we're all here to do".
- On enterprise data, big data only brought better analytics. AI could change how products work. Much of an enterprise's data is not relevant, but some is hyper-valuable, such as a bank's historical customer interactions for wealth management, where little in-distribution data exists on the internet.
- Enterprises struggle to use their data. It is poorly organized, and consulting firms get paid tens or hundreds of millions for data migrations with no change in results. He frames it as a race: enterprises against startups that find a way to build a different product from a small subset of data.

## Headcount, executives and founder fantasies

Over the past few years Scale kept headcount basically flat, growing it only very slightly, while the business grew 5x to 6x.

- His lesson from the 2020-2021 hiring boom: more people feels like better results, but a high-performing team is "like this very intricate sculpture". Adding even great people disrupts it, and you regress to the mean.
- Large sales teams can still work financially if operationalized to sit slightly above the mean. He says sales is different from product.
- Startups work because of very high-performing teams, so keep them intact as long as possible.
- A common failure mode: things scale but the team is junior, investors say to hire executives, the searches are "soul crushing" and work about half the time. Then the executive says to hire a massive team, the founder gives them a lot of rope, and this "almost always results in ruin".
- Outside executives can work if they first get steeped in how the company works and why the working things work. They should make small suggestions, with trust and verify, and earn sweeping changes through a track record of small beneficial steps.
- The "exec fantasy" is coming in to fix everything and make it professional. In reality you are recruiting a teammate with good judgment over time, not "some magic wand" or "magical bag of goods". Scale has made this mistake.
- The "founder fantasy" is hiring pros who do everything the founder doesn't want to do. A founder is good because of repeated good decisions, and pulling yourself out of those loops is "kind of crazy".
- Founder-led startups carry an innovation premium, and investors believe they will out-innovate the market. So the founder must stay in the strategic decisions.

## MEI hiring and the AGI definition

Scale rolled out MEI: Merit, Excellence and Intelligence.

- In every role it hires the best possible person regardless of demographics, with no quota-based optimization of the workforce.
- It still wants diverse pipelines and a diverse top of funnel.
- Alexandr says most people at Scale saw it as implicitly true already. Codifying it gives everyone confidence that it won't change as the company changes. Scale operates in a competitive industry and needs "incredibly smart people".

His AGI definition is that AI can do 80% or more of the jobs people can do purely at a computer. He puts it at "four plus years" out, possibly sooner depending on algorithmic innovation cycles.
