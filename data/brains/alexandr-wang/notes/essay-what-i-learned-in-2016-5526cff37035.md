## Minimizing regret and taking risk while risk-seeking

- Alexandr frames his career choices as "minimizing my regret," a concept from decision theory and machine learning, rather than a plan to start a startup.
- In December 2015, as an MIT freshman, he interviewed broadly for engineering and ML roles (Deepmind, Snapchat Research, Opendoor, Stripe). He felt aimless because no offer felt irrefusable and none felt like a growth opportunity after working at a few mid-stage startups.
- Opendoor CEO Eric Wu gave him the key framing: people get more risk-averse with age (marriage, house, kids), so "today, you're the most risk-seeking you'll ever be. So, if there's a risk today that you aren't willing to take, then you might never end up taking that risk."
- That conversation convinced him to decline all offers and try to start a company, because he knew he would regret never being an entrepreneur at the perfect time.
- His test for whether you are chasing enough: "if you don't have opportunities you regret not taking, you aren't chasing after enough opportunities." He accepts that some regret is unavoidable given how many opportunities exist and how high-variance outcomes are (he notes Snapchat and Opendoor are doing well).

## Path from side projects to Scale

- In January 2016, while working at Hudson River Trading (a high-frequency trading firm), he began hacking on side projects.
- He cycled through a couple of ideas over a few months, "consistently learning and iterating," before landing on Scale, which "felt like magic."
- A precursor product, Ava, had an iOS app he built over spring break.
- Scale was started in June 2016. The Product Hunt launch and a TechCrunch article (July 25, 2016) got a very positive response, and growth was rapid afterward.
- Scale is an API for human intelligence; early customers named include Uber, Alphabet, Houzz, and P&G.
- Scale joined Y Combinator over the summer. Alexandr notes YC's pitch for Demo Day as possibly the single best fundraising environment, so summer progress directly makes fundraising easier.

## Hard work compounds super-linearly

- He concluded before YC that "the amount I learned grew super-linearly with how hard I worked," an economies-of-scale effect on effort.
- Evidence from his own experience: after a few months of 12-hour days at Quora, he went "from a code monkey to a legitimate system architect," despite years of prior coding.
- In early 2016 he juggled 5 graduate CS courses plus side projects, took no breaks, and spent spring break building Ava's iOS app, then flew to SF right after finals.
- He worked 80-100 hour weeks for a full year.
- YC partners told the cohort to always work extremely hard because time in YC is highly leveraged. Paul Buchheit said many consider YC the most productive time of their lives.
- Momentum makes it easier: "if you have a lot of momentum, it's not too difficult to keep grinding even further." He cites Paul Graham's trick for surviving startups: don't stop to think about how tired you are.
- His conclusion: "Hard work consistently seems to trump other factors."

## Idea flood and why speed matters

- Makers often get ideas faster than they can build them. When the rate of good ideas exceeds build speed, "time becomes the enemy" and you start craving 100-hour weeks.
- Higher execution speed lets you keep up with the flood of ideas.
- Returns on startup ideas follow roughly a power-law distribution, like startup outcomes themselves. A few ideas (examples: Snapchat Stories, Facebook News Feed) overshadow the rest.
- Prioritization tries to find the outsized ideas but cannot be perfect.
- Tactic: compress every idea to an MVP as much as possible and focus on fast execution so a diamond idea isn't passed over.
- At Scale this means a team-wide habit of shipping feature after feature. Examples he cites from 2016: image recognition, audio transcription, a data collection endpoint, and a Python client library.
