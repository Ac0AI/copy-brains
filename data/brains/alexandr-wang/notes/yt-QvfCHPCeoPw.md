## Scale AI's founding insight: data was the neglected third pillar of AI

- Alexandr says AI comes down to three inputs: algorithms, compute (data centers, power, chips) and data. He used to write algorithms himself.
- At MIT he took the hardest machine learning courses. He then tried to build a camera in his fridge to catch roommates stealing food.
- The project failed because he had nowhere near enough data. It kept producing false positives and false negatives.
- His conclusion was that he'd need "a million times more data," and that every AI thing anyone builds would be blocked on data.
- He saw many people working on algorithms and chips and nobody working on data. Being 19 and impatient, he thought: "if nobody's going to do it, I might as well do it."
- He dropped out and flew straight from Boston to San Francisco.
- He compares data to oil. It is the raw material fed into algorithms and chips.

## Early days: Y Combinator, a tiny team and a launch that went "micro-viral"

- Scale went through Y Combinator, which he calls "the Hunger Games for startups." About 100 startups grind for a summer and end with a demo day where you either get investment or you don't.
- Scale got investment from YC and from Accel, an early Facebook investor. Those investors helped him build the team.
- At 19 he had no team and no credibility. Experienced San Francisco engineers he met for coffee politely declined.
- He recruited mostly people he knew from school, because they trusted him. Some dropped out and some joined after graduating.
- He coded the first product himself and launched it on one of the sites for launching startups. It went "micro-viral" among people on Twitter looking for startup ideas.
- After that he spent his time coding and occasionally posting. He begged friends to upvote and like it to get "some ounce of traction."
- The first name was Scale API, because that domain was available. It became Scale AI about a year and a half later.
- About a year in he hired someone with the title "head of business."

## Finding a wedge: from odd early customers to autonomous vehicles to defense

- Early use cases were small. One customer was a custom t-shirt company that needed illegal or gory designs detected. Another was a furniture marketplace that wanted better search.
- About three months in, Scale started working with autonomous vehicle companies. This became the core of the company for roughly the first 3-4 years.
- Customers included General Motors, Toyota and Waymo. The specialty was sensor fusion: combining many sensors into one output, such as "that's one person, that's one car."
- Alexandr says there were tens of competitors, but he doesn't mind competition because of his math-competition background. Scale focused on the best possible data sets.
- Headcount grew slowly at first:
  - Year one: 1-3 people.
  - Year two: about 15.
  - Year three: about 100.
  - Then roughly 200, then 500, and now thousands.
- About 3 years in, Scale moved into defense and began working with the DoD.
- He says the youngest-billionaire outcome came about five years after founding and "did not feel obvious."

## Customer strategy: go deep with the number one player in each industry

- Scale focuses on a small number of customers where it can have a very large impact. Alexandr says the company is "pretty picky" because building these systems and data sets takes a lot of resources.
- The aim is to work with the best in each category: the number one bank, pharma company, healthcare system, telco and country.
- The pitch is to take how an organization operates today and use AI to transform its workflows and operations. For a large health system, that means better logistics, diagnosis and patient outcomes.
- Scale has two parts to its business:
  - Data foundry: large-scale data production that fuels the major AI models.
  - Enterprise and government work: deploying full AI systems on a customer's own data.
- One of the first defense jobs was solving the DoD's own data problem for satellite and overhead imagery. Scale built:
  - A data foundry that generated data synthetically, with humans validating the output.
  - A facility in St. Louis next to the NGA, staffed by imagery analysts to check AI outputs.
- Scale later moved into larger deployments such as Thunderforge, which uses AI for military operational planning. The goal is to plan in hours instead of days.
- Alexandr says Scale and Palantir target different parts of the problem and ultimately work together well.
- For international customers, his preference is to start with low-stakes uses, such as education, healthcare and permitting. He wants as much of the world as possible to run on an American AI stack rather than a CCP one.

## Founder operating principles: focus, overdoing it, and trading perfectionism for speed

- He doesn't think he's fundamentally smarter than many others. His advantage was hyperfocus: on math as a kid, physics, then programming.
- His belief: "if you overdo things... you go the extra mile, you go the extra 10 miles... you will improve faster than anybody else by many times." He says others meander or don't put in the extra effort.
- He was a perfectionist early on, partly from practicing violin. He now says "we don't have the luxury to be perfectionists" and is much more pragmatic.
- The shift came when he internalized 80/20: "do 20% of the effort that's 80% as good," and do that over and over.
- He now thinks that for most things speed is the objective, not perfection. A few things still deserve perfectionism.
- He cites an Elon Musk framing for crises. Imagine a bomb strapped to your body that goes off if you don't solve the problem. In startups there are many such life-and-death moments where you have to act and do the best available thing.
- This is what he says "remolded" his brain toward operating quickly.

## Why he dropped out, and how he thinks about credibility and learning speed

- He left high school a year early to work at Quora on machine learning for ranking and engagement algorithms.
- He went to MIT to learn AI fast by going deep. He took every AI course available.
- He talked his way into the hardest machine learning course as a freshman despite his adviser's doubts. He got one of the top marks on the first test, after which the professor "let me do whatever I wanted."
- He left MIT after one year because he saw a bottleneck nobody was working on and didn't want to wait.
- On credibility, he says early on he had none outside his school network. Investors with strong brands (YC, Accel) helped close that gap.

## Competitive and strategic framing: compounding, numbers games and strategic surprise

- Alexandr says AI differs from oil because it compounds. Smarter AI creates more economic power, which funds smarter AI, so it doesn't run out.
- He thinks the competition between AI labs is largely about who secures power and real estate for data centers five to six years out. The question that matters is how many copies of an AI you can run.
- On AI-versus-AI conflict, he argues it becomes a numbers game. A side with more AI copies can put half its copies on offense (hacking the other side's AI) while the other half does planning. The smaller side can't both defend and plan.
- He argues the keys to an edge are:
  - Strategic surprise: an AI that reasons differently, or new platforms the adversary's simulations have never seen.
  - Ambiguity about how many resources you really have.
- He thinks the transition is from humans in the loop to humans on the loop. AI agents do most of the work and humans check and decide.
- He believes this raises the consequence of each remaining human decision.
- He says Scale's long-term mission centers on "human sovereignty," meaning:
  - Data that makes models aligned with humans.
  - Oversight so humans supervise AI actions.
  - Not ceding control of critical systems.
