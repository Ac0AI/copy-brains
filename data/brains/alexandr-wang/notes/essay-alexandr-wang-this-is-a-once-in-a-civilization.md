## Early path: work at a real company, then explore at school

- Alexandr grew up in rural Southwest New Mexico, did math and computer science competitions, and knew he wanted to do "really big things" without a clear path.
- A friend into programming pushed him toward the Valley. After high school he took a gap year to work at Cora in Silicon Valley at 19, then went to MIT at 18 (as he states it) and started Scale at 19.
- He calls ages 17 to 19 "drinking from the fire hose."
- Why working at a company mattered: from the outside "you have no idea how companies work," what it looks like to build, iterate, or how groups of people make decisions.
- Why MIT mattered: room to explore what was interesting. He trained his first models there, played with TensorFlow the year it came out, and came up with the idea for Scale.
- He applied to YC after one year at MIT. He values YC for being supportive but also telling founders "when you're being a dumbass."

## Pivoting from a mistimed idea to Scale

- Alexandr arrived at YC with an AI agent to help people get medical care. He now thinks the idea will ultimately exist (such agents are "very real" today) but that it was the wrong timing.
- After a month or two, Jared Friedman pulled the team aside and said it might not go anywhere. Alexandr calls that "exactly what we need to hear."
- The team went back to the drawing board and thought deeply about where the opportunity was, which led to Scale.
- Lesson: an idea can be right and still be early. Honest outside feedback early saves months.

## Spotting the gap: training data as the missing "button"

- Training models at MIT required three things: a cloud compute account, code to run, and a dataset.
- Two of the three could be had by "pressing a button" online. For data there was no effective way to get it.
- It felt "incredibly obvious" that the future would be a button you press to get data. That became Scale.
- Method: he found the opportunity by building with the technology and noticing the missing piece, not by following what was hot.
- Investors were skeptical for many years. In the early years data was "very unsexy." Even with great numbers and revenue, VCs asked whether it was a good business and whether it was durable.
- His read: none of those investors had ever trained a model, so they did not get it. The same investors now write think pieces about data being one of AI's biggest business opportunities.

## Conviction and first-principles thinking

- Successful companies are started "long before the core idea was popular" and "toil in obscurity for years" until the space becomes consensus.
- "You need to develop conviction in a set of beliefs that nobody else agrees with."
- Scale had worked on AI for a decade. He argues you cannot base business decisions on what everyone around you says: "If you go too much with the herd, you will get immensely confused and you will end up nowhere."
- You have to build your own compass for what the future looks like, because everyone else will confuse you.
- He says deep conviction was what let Scale weather "many years of chaos in the market, in the industry, in the people around us."
- Nobody is good at starting a company when they start one. Early investors told him he grew and changed very quickly. "The whole game is how do you develop yourself to continuously improve to get better and learn quickly."

## Why this is the best time to start a company

- His view: the bottleneck is not model progress but diffusing AI through the world. Even if models never improved, there would be "decades and decades" of upheaval in the economy.
- He calls it "probably a once in a civilization opportunity" for people with vision and ambition.
- Ten years ago, startups were David versus Goliath and had to find a clever angle with far fewer resources. Now, if a startup properly embraces agents and uses their strengths ambitiously, it is more like a "mecha Goliath" against traditional Goliaths and "can easily outcompete."
- Each AI wave has been roughly 10 times bigger than the last: self-driving, then chatbots, then coding agents a few years later. He expects new modalities and form factors that are each dramatically bigger, and thinks the best AI products have not been built yet.
- Advice for picking what to work on: find the exponential with both the steepest curve and the longest runway. Right now he says that is AI progress. The starting point can look boring. When he started Scale, the state of the art was cat detectors in YouTube videos.

## Agentic loops: where he sees the most alpha

- His view: there is "astronomical opportunity" in agentic looping, meaning building systems that let you spend 1000x or 1 million x more on tokens to drive an outcome in a continuous feedback loop.
- Framing: a company is a set of large-scale feedback loops (get customers, make them happier, they spend more, hire more people, get more customers), with micro-loops inside. Agentic systems can operate and optimize those loops.
- Inside Meta, with the right agentic loop and the right eval or metric for agents to optimize, a swarm of agents can accomplish more than a team of 100 engineers "very, very easily."
- Mechanics: first figure out the metric. Then it comes down to skills, Markdown files, cron jobs, and a goal command. "It's always funny how mundane everything is once you really dig into it."
- Agent coordination is, in his words, one of the most interesting problems today.

## Building a frontier lab and shipping models at Meta

- After about a year at Meta, he describes a "zero-based build" of a frontier lab after Llama 4 was not on the needed trajectory.
- Timeline he gives: Muse Spark 1 launched within nine months, then two months later came an image product and Muse Spark 1.1 (the transcript renders the name inconsistently as "Meta Spark" and "Moose Spark").
- Talent density was the core bet, because it compounds: the more talented people you have, the more of the most talented people want to join.
- Frontier AI work is research and science, which needs a different mindset and operating model than internet products: more experimentation and scaling. The aim is a lab built like "an organism" that compounds with exponential growth in capabilities, compute, and adoption.
- Product stance: models should not be so expensive that they are rationed to the wealthiest developers. He claims Muse Spark is cheaper than Opus and offered $1,000 in free API credits to the audience.
- Coming from Meta: bigger models, open-source models, and a harness for agentic developers focused on speed, reliability, and extensibility. He expects much innovation above the harness in orchestration and loops.

## Skills, roles, and vision as the scarce resource

- Systems thinking stays essential because the abstraction layer keeps changing: from writing code, to orchestrating agents, to orchestrating a million and then a trillion agents.
- He calls it a mistake to go all in on "wordcel." Builders need to "shape rotate," plus a deeper philosophical compass on how the world should develop.
- He expects intelligence and agency to become abundant. The scarce resource becomes vision and ambition: a clear view of what the world should look like in five to ten years and the drive to push through "all the crap" to make it happen.
- Agents make that maybe 10x to 100x easier than a decade ago, so builders can dream bigger.
- Builders also have a responsibility to help enterprises and governments adapt and to address biosecurity and cybersecurity risks.
- Debating when superintelligence arrives is "a bit of a waste of time" because powerful models are inevitable.
- Meta's stated view: personal superintelligence for billions of people, and a rise in entrepreneurship from 200 million businesses on Meta's platforms today toward billions, with business agents working alongside personal agents.
