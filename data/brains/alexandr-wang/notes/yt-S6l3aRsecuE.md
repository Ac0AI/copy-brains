## Muse origin: from Personal Super Intelligence to a personal agent

- Meta Superintelligence Labs (MSL) launched around Mark Zuckerberg's June 2025 memo "Personal Super Intelligence". Alexandr, Nat Friedman and others worked on it with Mark.
- The idea was to build powerful AI that "lifts the human experience broadly".
- Building a frontier model is a long end-to-end process: build the stack, produce datasets, pre-train, then post-train.
- At the start of 2026, Opus 4.5 and OpenClaw brought agents to the foreground. Nat was the first person in MSL to use OpenClaw. Alexandr calls his experience "somewhere between terrifying and euphoric".
- Alexandr then used OpenClaw deeply himself. Nat's psychoanalysis prompt gave him "the equivalent of like 3 years of therapy" in one sitting.
- In February, Alexandr wrote a memo arguing the super agent is "in many ways the final consumer product". Nat wrote memos titled "blames the clause the law" and "trust is a must", which shaped how the products were built.
- The team built a prototype in one to two weeks and demoed it to the board. Many of the final decisions were already in it, including the Jollybot mascot and an early version of the Muse Charm.
- Muse launched 7 months after that February prototype.

## Why Meta should win consumer AI, not coding agents

- Early 2026 conventional wisdom said coding agents were the only capability that mattered. Anthropic had a clear lead there, and the industry crowded into that use case.
- Alexandr chose not to compete head-on. He saw personal and consumer AI as where Meta is uniquely positioned.
- His argument: AI will take over the things we "have to do" so people can spend time on the things they "want to do". Meta's products (Instagram, WhatsApp, Facebook) are the things people want to do.
- Meta's heritage is connecting people and helping them discover interests. Its distribution is 3.5 billion people using the apps daily.
- He told Mark early on that Meta would be a special company if AGI or ASI arrives. That idea fed into Personal Super Intelligence.

## Taking an agent from magic demo to reliable product

- OpenClaw produced magic moments but mostly didn't work reliably. Interest "peaked and then disappeared". Alexandr credits Peter Steinberger as a visionary.
- Muse's edge was "grinding out and sanding out all of the details" of the combined model and product experience.
- The team kept a spreadsheet of more than 100 specific behaviors and capabilities the model needed for an ideal personal agent.
  - They built evals for every row and trained the model on each.
  - They held regular reviews of the rows where the model was weakest.
  - Each row had a launch-blocking threshold and a "good" threshold.
- Some checkpoints were green on 80 of the rows and red on 20. That was not good enough. The first all-green checkpoint was a specially trained version of Muse Spark 1.3.
- Beyond the numbers, they judged readiness by feel: the model was clearly better than anything before it.
- He says the product is very sensitive to model quality: how reliably it completes tasks, communicates about them, and checks back when it needs guidance.
- Other agent products lost users at the start of the year because they were unreliable. If people have unreliable experiences, "they think it's trash".
- The team ran many rounds of user testing with new users downloading from scratch, plus continuous A/B tests across model versions.
- Muse is "not a chatbot", so non-developers need careful onboarding to understand it.
- Alexandr says Meta's culture is to optimize once you know what you're measuring. Pre-AI that meant conversion, referral and onboarding flows. With Muse it meant sanding every detail of the model for the product.

## Small team, single point of view

- Fewer than 200 people worked on the model and product combined over the 7 months. Everyone knew it would be one of Meta's most important products that year.
- Leadership deliberately kept the team small and focused so it would not balloon across the company.
- He calls Muse "a single work of art". It needed one strong point of view and focus.
- His critique of big-organization products, which he says now plague AI labs: they become a "Frankenstein" amalgamation. Each PM jams in their own idea and it blends into a smoothie users can feel.
- Nat Friedman's taste and sensibilities shaped Muse the way Steve Jobs's shaped Apple.
- Alexandr credits Mark with patience, restraint and trust in the process. Meta kept building publicly through months of Wall Street doubt that it could win in AI.
- Stated user numbers: "millions". He retweeted others' graphs implying Muse is the fastest-growing consumer AI app ever, but gave no figure.

## Marketing Muse: memes, self-interest and screenshots

- Alexandr says Muse needed to "break through" a torrential stream of AI launches. Meta's distribution alone wasn't enough to make people pay attention. He also wanted to "make Meta cool again".
- At launch he mostly retweeted tens of interesting user use cases. Then some quick, low-effort posts did very well. He remembered that "the internet rewards risk" and surprise, and leaned into unhinged memes, including racy ones.
- The Jolly mascot let him put the character into any situation, for example "MFW I go to work on Monday". He says it likely had a real effect on growth, though it wasn't tracked.
- His critique of AI ads (Anthropic's outdoor campaign, OpenAI's two-people-at-a-desk ad): they are about the company. His principle is "why would someone care?" People are self-interested, so lead with what the product does for them. He has millions of use cases to show.
- Modern consumer product marketing is screenshot-driven. The product must "work on the screenshot", whether shared in group chats, word of mouth or online.
  - A friend's screenshots of Muse retrieving an ID left on a Flexjet spread the product on their own.
  - Having the mascot in the screenshot instantly marks it as a Muse screenshot.
- Alexandr says the wow moment should come as early as possible, since an AI agent can do a lot that surprises first-time users.

## Retention: the trust fall

- Alexandr describes successful use as a "trust fall". Users give the agent a small task, it works, then a bigger one, and then a bigger one.
- Heavy users go as far as voice-note brain dumps that the AI organizes into plans.
- Retention depends on moving users along this ladder and having the agent work every time.
- His own use: he set up many workflows and treats Muse as a "second brain" during meetings.
- His long-term view: a personal agent is a lasting form factor. It helps people accomplish wants and dream bigger, giving "the adulthood they dreamed of in childhood" and an "escalator of agency" rather than agency that gets crushed over a career.
- He also expects AI to take over more scientific discovery and parts of the economy.

## The Meta–Scale AI deal and how Mark operates

- Alexandr was introduced to Mark by Alex Schultz around 2021 or 2022. It took about a year to schedule the first meeting. Meta was a scale customer, and they spoke roughly every six months.
- Mark called after Llama 4, which Alexandr describes as a disappointment, asking what Meta should do in AI. From first outreach to announcement took about five to six weeks.
- Alexandr first doubted the deal made sense. Meta's iconic acquisitions (Instagram, WhatsApp) were product-logic deals, and Scale was an enterprise and government business. It became clear Mark wanted talent once numbers were discussed.
- The structure: Meta owns 49% of Scale, Scale continues, and Alexandr and a few people join Meta. He called it a win for Scale shareholders, Scale and Meta.
- It took many weeks of "this is insane" and conversations with Scale staff and investors. He had spent 9 years on Scale and thought of it as his life's work.
- What tipped him: the potential at Meta. AI at Meta looked like "damaged goods", which triggered his entrepreneur side. It also let him build from scratch with the right principles and culture.
- On Mark: he asks for advice constantly, which shows humility. He prioritizes ruthlessly and writes long, rigorous WhatsApp replies fast. Alexandr says he deliberates, then goes all in, and has internalized that "failure doesn't matter", only how big you win. He keeps Meta dynamic rather than slow and bureaucratic.

## Running MSL: talent density, flat structure, coach not auror

- At Scale, Alexandr believed a founder must be able to do every job. That made the company a product of his point of view but also made him the bottleneck. He says first-time founders should take this approach.
- At Meta he can't do every job. He is not a researcher or product designer. Job number one was to hire brilliant people at the top of their field.
- Founding pitch to recruits: build a lab from scratch with a small team and a clear mission (personal super intelligence). Meta's resources meant no bottleneck on compute, infrastructure or distribution.
- He says the large pay packages were underappreciated: OpenAI and Anthropic stock had run up, so comp was "kind of net neutral" for many hires.
- The design principles: a small, very flat, highly technical team with very high talent density, as Elon has shown.
  - The researchers in TBD report directly to him. He has more than 200 direct reports. He calls this "anti-bureaucracy".
  - Technical leads of pods tie-break technical decisions.
  - The group makes many decisions by debate.
  - "They don't need managers, they need a great environment."
- His role is to set the north star and spot talent. He acts like a coach, and describes himself as a steward of the environment.
- Big companies often have exceptional people who aren't empowered to do their best work, so those people leave.
- He distinguishes two kinds of work. Diamond mining has power-law payoffs: try many ideas and nurture the promising ones, as in VC and AI research. Skyscraper building has linear payoffs and is about operational efficiency, like data annotation or Amazon Prime delivery. He says Scale was skyscraper building and MSL is diamond mining.
