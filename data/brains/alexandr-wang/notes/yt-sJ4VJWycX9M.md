## Why Alexandr started Scale from a first-principles gap, not a hot market

- At MIT, Alexandr noticed that training a model needed three things: a cloud compute account, training code, and data. Two of the three could be had by "pressing a button" online. Data could not.
- From that he concluded there would be a way to press a button and get data. That became Scale, which he started at 19 after one year at MIT, via YC.
- Scale's first market was self-driving cars and computer vision. This was before large language models had arrived.
- His first idea at YC was an AI agent to help people get medical care. Jared Friedman pulled the team aside after a month or two and said it probably would not go anywhere. Alexandr says the idea was right but the timing was wrong, and that "that's exactly what we need to hear."
- Alexandr says you cannot start a company by reading the Wall Street Journal for what is hot. You start from simple statements about the world that you know to be true.
- For years, data was "very unsexy." Investors kept asking whether it was durable, even with strong revenue, because none of them had trained a model. The same investors now write think pieces calling data one of the biggest opportunities in AI.

## Build conviction in beliefs nobody else shares

- Alexandr argues the most successful companies started long before their core idea became popular. They "toil in obscurity for years" before it turns consensus.
- Success depends on identifying truths about the world early, before everyone else.
- Scale worked on AI for a decade. He says you can't base business decisions on what the people around you are saying: "If you go too much with the herd, you will get immensely confused and you will end up nowhere."
- You have to develop your own compass for what the future looks like, because everyone else will confuse you.
- His advice to his 18-year-old self: develop your own internal compass and hold strong conviction in it. Noise is heavy, especially when you're young and have little experience. Scale needed deep conviction to weather years of chaos in the market, the industry and the people around them.

## Nobody starts out good at being a founder

- Early investors told him he "grew so quickly and changed so quickly" that they did not see it at the time. He thinks that is true of every founder.
- His view: "nobody is good at something they've never done before." Founders start out pretty bad at everything.
- The whole game is how fast you develop yourself, continuously improve and learn.
- His own path: a gap-year job at Quora at 19, then MIT at 18 (as stated in the talk), where he trained his first models and played with TensorFlow, which had just come out.
- He rates working at a real company as highly valuable. From outside, you have no idea how companies are built, how things get iterated on, or how groups make decisions.
- He values YC for being supportive but also honest. It "tells you when you're being a dumbass."

## Pick the steepest, longest exponential

- Alexandr's advice: find the exponential in the world that has both the steepest curve and will run the longest.
- Decades ago that was Moore's law. Right now he says it is AI progress, but more steep curves will come.
- The starting point can look boring. When he started Scale, the state of the art was "cat detectors in YouTube videos," which was hard to pitch as the most important technology of the era.
- His observation on waves: each wave of AI has been about 10x bigger than the last. Self-driving was the first wave, chatbots were roughly 10x larger, and coding agents roughly 10x larger again. He expects new modalities and form factors that each dwarf the previous one.
- He says the best AI products have not been built yet.

## Startups can now compete Goliath versus Goliath

- Ten years ago, starting a company meant David versus Goliath. You needed a clever angle and had to compete with far fewer resources.
- He now thinks that with agents, it is closer to Goliath versus Goliath. A startup can be a "mecha Goliath" vastly enhanced by agents and AI.
- If you embrace agents and use their strengths in ambitious ways, you can "easily outcompete incumbents."
- The bottleneck is no longer model progress but diffusing the technology through the world. Even if models stopped improving, he expects decades of upheaval in the economy.
- He calls it a "once in a civilization opportunity" for dreamers with vision and ambition.
- On the future of scarcity: intelligence and agency will become abundant. The scarce resources will be vision and ambition, meaning a clear view of how the world should look in 5 to 10 years and the drive to push through the hard parts to get there.
- Because agents make execution maybe 10x to 100x easier, he says you can now dream bigger.

## Agentic loops are the biggest near-term opportunity

- Alexandr describes companies as large feedback loops where humans operate each edge: get customers, make them happier, they spend more, you hire more people, get more customers.
- Inside that are micro feedback loops. He sees huge alpha in building agentic systems that operate and optimize them.
- The goal is to spend 1,000x to 1,000,000x more on tokens to drive an outcome in a continuous feedback loop.
- At Meta, with the right agentic loop and the right eval or metric for the agents to optimize, a swarm of agents can accomplish more than a team of 100 engineers "very easily."
- The mechanics, in his words, are mundane: figure out the metric, then skills, markdown files, cron jobs and a goal.
- He thinks agentic coordination problems are among the most interesting problems today, and that real innovation will happen above the harness, in orchestrating loops and ecosystems of agents.

## Running a frontier lab: talent density and an operating model for exponentials

- About a year into Meta, he says the company did a zero-based build of a frontier lab rather than patching what existed.
- Timeline he cites: Muse Spark 1 launched within nine months, then Muse Image and Muse Spark 1.1 two months later.
- Talent density was the core bet. It compounds, because the more talented people you have, the more of the most talented people want to join.
- Frontier AI work is research and science, which needs a different mindset and operating model than internet products: more experimentation and scaling.
- He frames the lab as an organism that must grow with simultaneous exponentials in capability, compute, and adoption and usage.
- Product direction: keep models cheap so they are not rationed to the wealthiest developers. He says Spark is about 8x cheaper than Opus. Open-source models and a Meta-built agent harness (focused on speed, reliability and extensibility) are on the way.
- Strategy: a decentralized AI ecosystem, with personal superintelligence for billions of people and an explosion of entrepreneurship. He cites 200 million businesses on Meta platforms today and says that number should grow to billions.

## Skills to build as the abstraction layer keeps rising

- Abstraction has moved from writing code to orchestrating agents, and next to orchestrating armies of them (a million, then a trillion).
- He argues systems thinking never goes out of style, and that going all-in on "wordcell" is a mistake. You still need to be a "shape rotator."
- The scarce skill beyond that is a deeper compass and philosophical view of how the world should develop. He expects humanity to change more in the next decade than in the last 100 years.
- He says builders have a responsibility to help prepare the world, including enterprises, governments, biosecurity and cybersecurity.
- On the AI debate: arguing over exactly when superintelligence arrives is "a bit of a waste of time." In hindsight, he expects it to be obvious that intelligence and agency became abundant.
