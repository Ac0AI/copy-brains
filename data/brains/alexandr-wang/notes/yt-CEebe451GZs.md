## Startup ideation and the "wandering" phase

- Alexandr describes the first half of his YC batch as a "squiggle" of existential angst, with Google Docs full of startup ideas. He felt "behind the eightball" because everyone around him already seemed to be working on something.
- He found Paul Graham's essay on coming up with startup ideas to be a good focusing framework: live in the future, work out what exists there, and build backwards.
- Scale's original idea followed that framework. In the future, human computation would be orchestrated as easily as compute, through an API, and that API didn't exist yet.
- After the idea, he spent a night searching for a domain. scale ai.com was available, and he calls buying it "unusually good decision".
- Scale launched on Product Hunt and got enough traction from it. After that came about 4 to 6 months of "wandering mode": a few customers, trying to get more.
- He personally talked to anyone who clicked the Intercom chat bubble on scale ai.com.
- About 6 months later, one client who wanted to be really big appeared and the idea "started come into its own". He calls roughly a year of wandering "on the low end honestly of most startups".
- What got him through was high conviction that the thing would exist in the future and didn't yet. That gave him "enough of a gradient" to trust a path from point A to point B.
- He is an anxious person and channeled the uncertainty into "anxiously doing things", so he felt he was making progress in a direction.
- His scariest idea was the YC Hunger Games: about 90% of companies die, but "you could already be dead but you might only find out in three years".

## Competition: blustery confidence and perception in enterprise sales

- Mechanical Turk and others already existed when Scale started. His answer to competition is "blustery confidence": "you need some kind of irrational self-belief" that you can compete.
- He says you have to believe you will recruit better, make better product decisions and go the extra mile. Over the long term those things accumulate into the self-belief you started with.
- He drew confidence from competitive programming and math competitions: "bring it, we're just going to build the better thing".
- He cites an 18-year-old YC founder who answered a question about competition with "just be better".
- As an example, he describes a Palantir executive telling a government customer, in front of him, that they would regret not picking Palantir. He calls it a "ridiculously aggressive talk track". He sees Palantir's deep belief that its software is the best as a self-reinforcing loop and part of its success.
- For large customers, "perception is more real than reality". The reality is usually too ugly for people to confront, so they live in perceptions.
- In enterprise sales or products, the job is as much to shape the perception as to improve the reality. He calls this one of Palantir's superpowers. Palantir gave new hires an acting book for a long time.
- He adds a caveat: "you also should produce value". Perception-shaping sits on top of building a good product.

## Winning by respecting unsexy problems and optimizing for economic impact

- Alexandr names Scale's edge as creative problem solving on the least sexy problems. Scale coordinates hundreds of thousands of contributors worldwide to produce high-quality data for models.
- The team treats that operational problem with the same respect as a math Olympiad problem. They ask for the most creative ways to solve it, break it down and apply the scientific method.
- Scale holds "no elevated sense of purpose" about only solving certain problems. They pair that with working "like hell".
- He sees two common failure patterns:
  - Brilliant teams that work on useless, intellectually interesting problems.
  - Scrappy teams that take any problem but fail to solve it in a scalable way or abstract it.
- Culturally, he wants employees to think about net economic impact more than technical difficulty. School teaches that mastery comes from harder technical problems, but he says technical difficulty and economic value are poorly correlated.
- He says many valuable problems are not technically hard, they just have "a lot of hair" you must sift through. He says that is where most good startup opportunities are. Climb the ladder of economic impact relative to technical difficulty.

## Enterprise vs. SMB as a startup customer

- On enterprise, he says you often can't believe what large organizations tell you. Many contacts have nothing to do with the company's success and just own a job. He calls it a "rats nest" you must learn to navigate.
- If it works, enterprise can work "incredibly well". He says the largest companies in the world are either enterprise or consumer companies, while SMB eventually taps out.
- His rule of thumb: going enterprise means spending roughly 30% of your time navigating bureaucracy.
- SMBs "don't even have time to lie to you", so you can take what they say at face value. He calls this a great benefit for startups.
- His general advice is to focus on SMBs to get liftoff. But if 99% of the startup ecosystem is chasing SMBs, there may be less opportunity there.
- He describes a startup cycle: many founders copy the currently hot companies and end up in "perfect competition", getting no traction. A company working on some fringe idea with no competition then works and becomes the next hot company, and everyone copies that.
- His conclusion: "you never want to be exactly where all the other companies are focused". Do something a little fringe to avoid perfect competition.

## Thinking independently in a hype-driven AI market

- He estimates that "80 to 90%" of what's out in AI is nonsense, and that nobody truly knows what is going on. Many people are confident and wrong.
- Investors, product leaders and others are "paid to have a thesis", so many beliefs are not grounded in reality.
- Anecdote: in early 2018, Scale's Series A deck had a slide saying data is the lifeblood of AI systems. A senior Sequoia partner loudly said that wasn't true and that AI no longer needed more data. The partner then stopped paying attention, and Sequoia passed. He uses it as an example of confident people being wrong in early industries.
- He says founders can raise $50M or $100M Series A rounds by following a trend, and the idea can still turn out to be stupid.
- His mandate for founders:
  - Work out what you actually believe, then execute against the flow.
  - Keep a mechanism for learning from the ecosystem, so you don't dig in and get "washed out in the tide".
  - Avoid being someone who "get[s] all their ideas off of Twitter and parties".
- Whatever you build will be popular and unpopular at various points, and your job is to weather that. He notes Nvidia has gone between unsexy and sexy.
- Few AI categories have a clear winner yet. Funds raised and usage metrics are weak signals, so he says not to get psyched out by competition.
- A contrarian take on a crowded sub-domain is interesting.

## Capital efficiency and focus in AI

- He says a business plan that requires raising and spending a lot of money "is in business terms a bad business". The goal should be a profitable company that can keep compounding for a long time.
- Example: self-driving cars. Hundreds of billions were raised and, in his view, close to none of those companies succeeded. Waymo is the one winner, and it has "an infinite bank account".
- Training foundation models means competing with some of the richest entities in history. He says not to compete with an infinite balance sheet.
- His advice: find something genuinely differentiated that you can keep investing in over a long horizon.
- In AI right now, "the premium is actually just picking a certain problem to care about for a really long time".
- OpenAI, Anthropic and Google each have thousands of things to worry about. Focus gives a big premium. Perplexity is his example: it spends far less and its simple search-based LLM output is still better than the others'.
- On owning a workflow (Cursor as the example): it is "pretty hard to fully monopolize a workflow". Developers are fickle, and a company must lay roots to solidify its position.

## Data as the AI bottleneck and agent product opportunity

- He argues the 12 to 18 month focus on who has the biggest cluster was unnuanced. Even with big clusters, the industry is hitting limits of publicly available data, and he believes the data wall is real.
- Pre-training limits have been hit. Progress now comes from post-training, which is bottlenecked by specialized, high-quality data that doesn't look like internet data.
- On synthetic data: it works and is part of a world-class post-training effort, but it is not a "philosopher's stone". Each trick yields a bit more data, and it doesn't work "to infinity".
- Reasoning-trace data from models didn't pan out for pre-training, since synthetic data loses much of the richness of the real distribution.
- His view of the path to AGI: compute must scale exponentially, and data production needs a similar curve alongside it. New forms of human-generated data will be needed. Scale's role is producing that data.
- He thinks the $1T, $5T and $10T cluster talk is normalizing, but $100B clusters are probably still needed.
- Agents: models do well in a single turn and then "fall off a cliff" over more turns. Models don't know what they don't know. Labs must improve multi-turn reliability.
- He calls product design the biggest blocker for a breakout agent moment. Models are already good enough for a mind-blowing agent product.
- Cursor shows the pattern: it put the model into an engineer's workflow, and most engineers hadn't realized how good the models were.
- He says the opportunity is to break models out of the chat paradigm and bake them into fundamental workflows. He calls finding the right agent modality perhaps the biggest startup opportunity of 2025.

## Inputs, customers and political literacy

- To form good opinions, Alexandr relies on:
  - Writing on the internet, including Substack, where experts post high-quality detail.
  - Talking to specific experts who "truly do not care about what other people think". He describes them as independent thinkers who seem strange and weird.
- He treats customers as always right: "you have to be super super subservient to your customers".
- He says Silicon Valley is under-investigated politically. Getting the broad political and demographic threads right can reveal 20-year tailwinds and big growth trends. He names populism, nationalism and isolationism, and says greater political literacy in Silicon Valley is underrated.
