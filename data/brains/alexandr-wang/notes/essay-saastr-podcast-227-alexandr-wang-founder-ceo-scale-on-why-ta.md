## TAM matters less than growth rate and your odds of dominating it

- Alexandr argues that TAM "in the traditional sense" barely matters. When Scale raised its Series B, its TAM was not "tens of billions of dollars." It was smaller but growing very quickly.
- What mattered more was that Scale could dominate that market. Because the market was growing fast, the company grew fast along with it.
- A big TAM only means you are playing in an established market. It does not show how likely you are to capture a significant portion of it.
- The two things he looks at: is the market growing quickly, and do you have a real chance to win it.
- TAM estimates are often wrong early:
  - Uber's early pitch deck put its TAM at $4 billion, and the company was about to IPO at $100 billion.
  - Airbnb's TAM at the start was "literally zero," or very small, for home sharing. It is now a $30 billion company.
- Focusing on TAM means you miss the best ideas and ignore the fact that the market you are in is growing and you have a chance to win it.

## Competition: row your own race

- Alexandr believes in "ignore it and row your own race" over embracing or maniacally monitoring competitors.
- Most startups die by suicide, not competition. His phrasing: "startups die of indigestion, not starvation."
- The real risks are hiring a bunch of the wrong people, which kills culture and productivity, or focusing on the wrong things.
- It is very hard for any business to catch up to a startup with momentum, fast growth and a strong culture. So maximize your own growth rate.
- One caveat: it helps when there is no very threatening incumbent. Competing with Uber and Lyft in ride sharing would be threatening. A "laggier" incumbent is better.

## Invest in customer success before sales

- Early on, Scale was focused only on growth: get a customer, then go get the next one. Alexandr says he later realized it matters far more in the long run to build a culture where customers are "supremely, supremely happy."
- After the first one or two customers, he argues the next hire should be customer success, not sales.
- Once Scale embedded customer success and customer centricity in its culture, it grew far faster. Happy customers told their friends, and those friends came saying they had heard great things about Scale.
- Customer success becomes a primary acquisition channel through word of mouth. He calls this unintuitive: investing in customer success over sales makes you grow faster, and many of the fastest-growing SaaS companies do it.

## Who to hire for early customer success

- Early-stage customer success is very hard because everything is breaking, customers have many demands, and the startup moves fast and can look chaotic. The job is to make it seem stable to the customer.
- He would not hire someone from a big company. There, customer success is cookie cutter and sits on a much more stable platform.
- He prefers people with consulting backgrounds (McKinsey, Bain, BCG). They work with Fortune 500 clients who often dislike the consultant and where the service is nebulous.
- They get good at uncertain environments and at building relationships even when seen as the adversary.

## Let early customers drive the roadmap, but dig into the pain

- Early customers are extremely influential, and Alexandr says you should build a lot of what they ask for.
- The number one startup failure mode he sees is being too "in their head," being principled and philosophical about products and building things nobody wants.
- Pick your first couple of customers very well. They will define the roadmap for a while. Choose ones representative of where the future is going or of a much broader set of customers.
- Question them and understand the pain so you can maybe find a better solution. Still, the roadmap should be dictated more by customers than by anyone else.
- On "customer informed, not customer driven": hear the pain first, dig into it, then reach your own conclusion about the best solution. Focus on pains, not customer-proposed solutions.
- You have only a couple of big, innovative bets (the Henry Ford "faster horse" case). Everything else should be intuitive and "just solves the customer's problem, plain and simple."
- To validate a launched product:
  - Remove onboarding as a risk by teaching customers in person how to use it.
  - Then ask customers directly whether they love it. Usage and revenue metrics can help, but asking is better.
  - A trusting customer success relationship makes customers open with you.

## Don't optimize deal size early; flip to pricing later

- Early on, the only focus should be customers who "absolutely love you" and will pay, even if not much. Don't over-optimize.
- Scale's path: first deals were "a couple thousand dollars," then hundreds of thousands once it had a sales team, then a couple of million-dollar deals.
- It is hard to predict early how much people will pay. Get in the door, get feedback, get people to use and love it, then optimize pricing, ACVs and revenue.
- He cites Segment: it started as open source, then, once charging, roughly doubled its price on every contract until people said no. He recommends companies do this once they have enough customers to experiment on.
- When Scale flipped the switch:
  - After a handful of happy customers, about three in autonomous vehicles, all innovative companies.
  - After hiring the first sales rep, who was told not to worry about pricing and just land great customers, because Alexandr did not want value extraction before there was enough signal the product was right.
  - Then the rep said they could charge more, and Scale "turned the switch."

## Raising money when early ACVs are small

- Alexandr says VCs "get it wrong all the time" and miss big winners. He points to Airbnb's YC story and Robinhood's early rounds as examples. Form your own opinion.
- At seed and Series A, what moves a VC most is customers genuinely loving the product, especially in reference calls, more than financials or ACVs.
- You still need a story for the ACV question. Either:
  - Data points showing people will pay much more, or
  - A plan to blanket the market with very low customer acquisition cost.
- Mike Volpi found Scale for its Series B because he heard from customers how much they loved the product.

## Other views: hiring, API companies, ignoring the noise

- Hardest role to hire: marketing. It moved in a few years from brand and corporate marketing to very technical and metrics-driven, and good marketing is very product-specific, so you need very creative people.
- Admires API companies (Stripe, Twilio, Plaid, Checkr). They grow quickly and become important infrastructure for customers.
- First job advice: join a high-growth startup that is winning. You see what winning looks like, and you get more power and impact, which builds the founder mindset.
- What he wishes he had known: "a lot of people will get it wrong at the start." Nobody knows what you will accomplish, so believe in what you are doing and don't over-rotate on what others tell you.
