## Copycats: how Alex thinks about being cloned

- A friend cried because someone copied his project "one to one." Alex says it has happened to him many times, and it hurt at first.
- Alex was copied while at his full-time job, as Cyberly's was nearing his salary and the chance to quit. Competitors:
  - stole his lists and resold them at 1/10 of the price
  - leaked the lists free on Reddit
  - cloned him one-to-one
  - emailed his customers
  - ran ads against his name
- He used to spy on copycats. He no longer does, because "you get used to it very, very quickly."
- None of the people who copied him stayed in business long term. He is unsure why.
- His tentative theory is that one-to-one copying acts as a filter attracting two types:
  - complete beginners, whom you shouldn't fear
  - far more experienced people, who will more likely copy something more lucrative than your side project
- He places himself in the experienced group as he looks to build a new business. He plans to copy again, but not one-to-one.
- Practical takeaway: don't fear one-to-one copycats.

## Why people copy you (it isn't flattery)

- Alex rejects "imitation is the highest form of flattery" and "nobody copies a loser." He says people copy losers all the time, and he takes being copied as annoying, not as a compliment.
- Being copied doesn't prove a good product, niche or distribution strategy. At best it might suggest you're making some money.
- Many people copy $1K/month projects instead of $100K/month ones.
- He got more copycats at 1K MRR than at 50K MRR. His reasoning: "oh [ __ ] 50, there's no way I can do that. Let me copy something that is more in my league."
- People copy you because they think they can also do what you have.

## Where the defensible value is

- Copiers can take your product, features and design.
- They can only copy what you've already built. They cannot copy what you haven't created yet.
- That is your advantage: "Wake up tomorrow and do it again."
- He says the value is not in the code.
- A story he critiques: a founder who researched the market and took inspiration from existing apps, then found a niche within a niche. Alex's view is that he shouldn't be surprised when his product, which AI could build, got cloned.

## Audience building vs building cool things

- Alex thinks many people over-focus on building an audience, assuming that with one everything becomes trivial.
- He agrees with Mark Lou's red pill / blue pill post, where the blue pill is "I need to build an audience" and the red pill is "Build cool shit."
- His view: you don't build an audience in a vacuum. You do interesting things and share them with the world.
- That is how he built his own audience. He built for years before reaching a thousand followers and was far more prolific then than now (he calls himself a "lazy [ __ ]" compared to five years ago).
- The two aren't mutually exclusive: you build cool things in order to build an audience.
- An audience helps, "but not as much as you think."
- Caveat: this is his experience and may differ for others.

## An audience doesn't guarantee a hit

- Alex has about 45,000 followers. If a post is bad, he gets about 10 likes. If it's good, it can go viral.
- Jonathan, the creator of outbid.lol, has about 20K followers. 20K followers doesn't mean everything he posts takes off.
- An audience helps to an extent, but it doesn't mean anything you build will take off.

## Playful experiments and the outbid.lol case

- outbid.lol is a site where you pay to be at the top. It starts at $1 for the number one spot, then someone bids $5 and takes it, and so on.
- It got so much traffic that companies bid around $1,000 to be on top, expecting to earn it back from the traffic.
- Jonathan made more than $100,000 in a couple of days.
- Variations of this idea have existed for decades, but this one took off for reasons nobody could predict.
- Alex's lessons:
  - You cannot predict what is going to work.
  - Not everything has to be serious. Many serious companies started as jokes, and he cites Facebook starting as a fun project.
  - He likes it for reminding him of the early internet, when many things were for fun.
  - He found Cyber Leads the same way and did not expect it to take off.
- He wants to be "playful and experimental," including with his new podcast, even though he doesn't see it making money directly or indirectly.

## Turning viral attention into a business

- Alex says Jonathan shouldn't be written off, because he hasn't yet found a way to harness the attention.
- Idea he floats: a service helping companies build viral products, or run a launch, for $5-10K. It could be "the most boring" offer and still make crazy money quickly.
- It may not be easy, but a viral joke project can become a business through a spin-off.
