## The collapse of CyberLeads: what Alex learned shutting down his $500k/year business

Alex's agency-style business CyberLeads peaked at about $500k/year and then declined. He fell apart personally, killed the service, switched to consulting, and rebuilt on a smaller, calmer base.

- **Signal that something was wrong:** he could work 16 hours a day happily when the business was thriving, but struggled to work 10 minutes on a failing one.
- **Reaction:** "It is lack of direction, progress and results. Not hours worked. That makes me feel like I want to quit."
- **Symptoms:** pretend work, ghosting employees, skipped marketing and follow-ups, and lost passports, flights and gear.
- **Cost cutting:** he was close to breaking even for the first time ever. He cancelled unneeded software and let go of two contractors to roughly halve expenses.
- **Honest takeaway:** morals like "I would never fire anyone" "don't mean shit until they are tested in the real world."
- **Trigger for the change:** a friend asked what he would do if he retired today. His answer was to stop traveling, shut the service, keep only the newsletter and write his books.

## The $10k/year plan that backfired

When clients stopped closing deals, Alex tried a radical annual offer.

- **Offer:** $800/month on a $10k/year plan, with no monthly billing, plus access to his calendar.
- **Logic:** with a full year, surely a referral, partnership or cold email campaign would pay the client back. His LTV would go from $6k to $10k, or $20k on renewal.
- **Result:** his calendar flooded. One client booked a weekly call, and Alex ended up doing product launches and "founder therapy".
- **Lesson:** "the more I offered and gave, the more expectations rose and they asked for."
- **Fallout:** one client said he felt Alex "didn't even care about my business". Another cut a 12-month deal at 2 months, took the refund, and still issued a chargeback.
- **Outcome:** after refunds and chargebacks he broke even that month, which helped push him to shut the service down.

## The consulting offer: $3k single payment, $8k/month

Alex replaced the service with consulting and it worked quickly.

- **Offer:** everything he did for clients at $3k/month, sold instead as a single $3k payment through a self-checkout page with no sales calls.
- **Delivery:** calls where he walks clients through each step and says what he would do in their position.
- **Results:** $20k in the first month, all profit, then stabilizing at about $8k/month (about $100k/year).
- **Belief behind it:** "Marketing shouldn't be outsourced... It's a founder activity. It's the oxygen of the business. And no one is going to care about your business the way you will."
- **Honest expectation setting:** marketing isn't a science. He might try 3-4 experiments for a client, but they may need 30.
- **Team:** he kept two team members to run the newsletter and the new service, and parted ways with a contractor, giving another several months of salary.
- **Feeling:** consulting felt easier. "I don't feel sad like I thought I would. I feel relief."

## Why CyberLeads declined: waves, not a bad model

Alex concluded he had mistaken success for mastery and that a pile of changes outside his control had hit him.

- **Success despite his habits:** he says he is a "terrible entrepreneur" by conventional measures (no contracts, slow replies, soft on refunds, avoids competitors). Looking back, CyberLeads had "great genetics" (channels, retention, pricing, brand, market timing).
- **The plateau:** at about $500k/year, nothing he did moved clients signed, retention or price much.
- **What changed:**
  - Tools cut the cost of leads (to $49/month) and email sending (to $99/month) by roughly 10x, removing barriers to entry.
  - Influencers promoted the model, creating thousands of competitors, fake testimonials and spam, so buyers went from "default excited" to "default suspicious".
  - Google and Microsoft anti-spam changes forced system rebuilds roughly every 6 months, and cold email stopped working.
  - Twitter's "For You" algorithm meant follower count stopped mattering. He gets fewer views at 40,000 followers than he did at 4,000.
  - AI threatens to generate his monthly funding list from one prompt.
- **Prediction error:** he was right about agencies, funded startups and email persisting, but "I was trying to predict the tiny earthquakes... while missing the invisible giant tsunamis."
- **Probability point:** 1,000 independent events that are each 99% likely all happening has only about a 0.43% chance, which is why hindsight feels obvious.

## Riding waves and living in the future

Alex says he wrongly believed he never rode waves.

- **Waves he did ride:** the internet itself, bootstrapping/solopreneur/build-in-public, Covid (companies hiring remote agencies), post-Covid money printing (US companies hiring agencies), and paid newsletters being trendy.
- **Wrong lesson:** he ignored Crypto, NFT and AI waves because they didn't affect him, then concluded he should ignore every wave forever.
- **His view:** "The magic of entrepreneurship is deciding how to respond to each wave." That means choosing which to capitalize on, adapt to, or ignore, and when to adjust sails versus jump in the lifeboat.
- **Growth curves:** he says they are "a crazy, random squiggly line", not linear, compounding or S curve models. Every framework he made eventually broke.
- **Catching waves:** he didn't spot them; he lived in the future. "The future is already here, it's just unevenly distributed." You only need to be earlier than most. He planned a remote, hire-online, travel business in 2017 when family thought it absurd.
- **Risk:** once ideas are mainstream you lose that edge. A competing agency at $500k/year was breaking even with co-founders, a London office, about 15 staff, paid marketing and custom work. Alex ran solo at 80% margins with a niche, productized offer, organic channels, newsletter upgrades and about 90% automation.

## Adapt or die: ships vs fleets, and why one win isn't enough

Alex's father asked how he stayed in business 40 years answered that every industry demands adaptation.

- **Father's point:** "Whoever stops adapting, ends up having the same fate as the dinosaurs." Nothing from his business in the 80s is the same except the name.
- **Ship of Theseus:** old companies are really new ones with old names.
- **Fleets:** it may be easier to navigate with multiple boats than to endlessly adapt one. Large companies often hold multiple businesses.
- **Entrepreneurial judgment:** know when to stay focused on one boat, when to adapt it, and when to add a new one.
- **Dropped belief:** he thought he only needed to reach escape velocity once and then coast. "I was wrong. Again... I will have to find another CyberLeads again. And, who knows, it might take me another 20 tries."
- **Retirement math:** plans are fragile (US dependence, 5% vs 3% inflation, war), so he reframes the goal as loving work enough to do it by choice. "Not to retire young. But to love your job and life so much that you enjoy it every day."
- **Durable assets:** some skills fade like cardio (idea generation, stress tolerance, work ethic) and some persist like motor skills (coding, design, marketing, hiring, management, audience, customer list, intuition). He also notes: "the only thing that cannot be taken away from you is your story."

## The updated "Shotgun to Sniper" framework

Alex's one remaining framework, which he expects to break too.

- **Idea phase ("artist mode"):** shotgun many products and cut losers fast. Don't fall in love with ideas; everything has a "next day version". What worked for him was choosing the distribution channel and market first, then the idea. CyberLeads came from copying someone and adding his twist. The goal is a good product plus a good channel, and "when something works, you will know it."
- **Growth phase ("athlete mode"):** no more creativity; put in reps and ride the S curve. Height is set by "business genetics": how many people you sign, how long they stay, how much they pay.
- **Plateau:** if numbers are large, optimize. Improving signups, retention and price by 25% each doubles the business; 8x if all double. Doubling CyberLeads from $250k to $500k/year was worth it, but doubling the newsletter from $4k to $8k/month wasn't, so he looked for a new S curve.
- **Optimizing:** work back to front (fulfillment, then sales, then marketing), systematizing, productizing, delegating and automating first.
- **New S curves:** look at marketing (new channels, hardest), product (retention), and pricing (easiest, most effective). His price went from $5/month (GitGardener) to $300/month (newsletter) to $3k/month (service), a 600x rise, while revenue went from $100/month to $50,000/month, a 500x rise.
- **Loop:** shotgun products, sniper the winner to the plateau, squeeze it if worthwhile, otherwise shotgun new experiments for the next lever, and repeat. "Just do shit."

## Reflection: building during uncertainty

Takeaways Alex draws on starting again.

- **Being lost is normal:** "For the vast majority of the past 8 years, I've been lost."
- **Reverse sabbatical:** when in doubt do the opposite of what you've been doing. He stopped traveling, worked on writing instead of business, and settled in a beach apartment with a daily routine. This restored his spark and made work feel interesting again.
- **Ownership principle (Little Prince):** he can't help everyone, but "my frog, my employees, my family" come first, which is why he tried to keep his team employed.
- **Wrote 13 books in a year** and gave them away free, accepting an opportunity cost he estimates at $100,000+. He says he did it for himself, and the audience response was payment.
- **Just build:** you can't predict how technology will be used, and intentions and expectations don't determine outcomes. "Maybe just build. And invent. Anything. For whatever reason."
- **Current state:** after the sabbatical, he missed working, lost money for the first time last month, and plans to pick an idea from his ideas file and start building.
