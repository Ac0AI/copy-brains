## Freedom from his job was the happiest day, but stress changed shape rather than shrinking

- Alex quit his job at 25 and calls the last day "one of the happiest days of my life". He promised himself he would never work in an office again: "I don't care about material success. Or becoming a millionaire. All I want is to be free."
- He rejects the idea that employment means less stress and self-employment more. In his experience the stress is different: employed stress is chronic, predictable and constant (meetings, deadlines, reviews). Self-employed stress is acute and swings wildly, for example when a new competitor appears, then weeks of cruising.
- He argues the swings make him feel more alert and alive, like wild lions versus zoo lions, which are bored and waiting to be fed.
- He says having already built projects and earned revenue before leaving a job kept him from believing that a business needs a full team of designers, marketers, developers, advisors and investors.
- After going full time, his resting heart rate dropped by 5 beats per minute within a few weeks.

## Founder take-home is what matters, not revenue

- When registering as self-employed in Italy, Alex learned that income tax is one of many taxes: social contributions, regional taxes, corporate taxes, VAT and capital gains. Even with a 90% income tax exemption as an expat, a paper simulation left him taking home 70% to 75% of revenue, with zero employees and almost no expenses.
- He turned this into a viral post: a $10K/month solo business at 90% margin means the founder takes home $9K per month. A $1M/year business with two co-founders and a 25% margin means about $10K per month each. "Yet they call the first a side project and the second a business."
- Critics said the second can scale, be sold, or work without the founder, but "they couldn't argue with the math."
- His simulations of million-dollar Western companies with co-founders and employees showed take-home of $5k to $10k per month is considered great, often as salary, with the rest reinvested.
- He concluded he was already successful at 25, taking home about $5k per month, with an alarm-free, self-scheduled life.

## Lessons from nearly losing his money: avoid catastrophic mistakes

- Alex moved more than $40k of his roughly $50k into one bank account in a single transfer. The bank flagged it, demanded proof, gave no usable customer support, rejected his paperwork twice without explanation, and froze the account. Forum posts described people losing access for months. He sent corrected paperwork a third time and it was approved about 11 at night.
- He says he knew not to keep all his money in one account or send it in one go, just as he knew to launch fast and charge from day one but only did so after 2 years and 19 failed attempts. "It seems like I learn mostly by making mistakes."
- His fixes:
  - Split money across multiple bank accounts in multiple countries, and move money in several smaller transactions.
  - Rewrite CyberLeads in code, because his no-code tools kept breaking and going out of business.
  - Move from a merchant of record to a payment provider, accepting the invoicing and accounting work, because he had seen people lose accounts and subscribers.
  - Build a second distribution channel, since over 90% of customers came from Twitter.
- His principle: accept that mistakes are inevitable but avoid the catastrophic, avoidable ones. He quotes a film line: the biggest fish in the river gets there "By not getting caught."

## Diversification: two is enough

- Alex treats diversification as insurance, to survive long enough to recover or build a replacement, not as a goal of having many options.
- His math: if each of two independent channels has a 10% chance of failing, the chance both fail is 10% x 10% = 1%.
- He says this "theoretical 99% safety is good enough for me" and anything beyond is paranoia. Target: two bank accounts and two marketing channels.
- He applies the same logic to banks, businesses and any other risk. (He jokes about "two girlfriends" too.)

## Building programmatic SEO as a second channel to replace daily posting

- Six months after going full time, CyberLeads revenue was up only $1k/month, despite all the extra time.
- He disliked having to post on Twitter every day and wanted a channel not dependent on daily motivation. He framed the progression as hunting with a spear (direct sales), fishing with a net (Twitter), planting seeds (SEO).
- The strategy: use code and automation to create pages for the 25,000 funded startups in his database, such as contact information, founder information and tech stack pages for each company. Searchers land on the page, see the data but with the email hidden, and must sign up and pay to unlock it. He notes most competitors do this.
- Problems he hit:
  - Near-identical pages were treated as duplicates, so he added unique info per company.
  - Much of the early traffic was bots from a few locations.
  - Pages ranked but got no clicks until he changed titles.
  - Titles too similar to competitors failed to stand out, while distinct titles missed the right keywords.
- He ran a data-driven phase: 25 title variations (one per 1,000 companies), then split tests on showing or hiding data points, mobile layout, buttons, testimonials, FAQs, metadata, navbars versus dropdowns, popups versus buttons, company pages versus list pages, pricing and CTAs.

## When data-driven optimization misleads: bots, bad data and talking to humans

- Session recordings showed that most of his "users" were bots, so months of split-test decisions were based on bot clicks. A test of two identical pages still produced a clear winner.
- While correcting a data point, he found that competitor databases all had the same mistake. They pull from the same sources and repackage them, whatever proprietary process they claim. "Maybe the giants are blind too," so he questioned copying their marketing.
- He admitted he would not pay for his own company pages: "This sucks, it's clickbait, and it's annoying." His reset: "Back to old school. Let's talk to humans. And use common sense."
- Tactics: collect emails, send surveys, add a chat box so visitors can message him directly.
- Real-world problems:
  - Most collected emails were garbage, so he built a custom email validator.
  - Survey dropdowns were skewed: options B and C were always most popular regardless of shuffling. He will only use text fields from now on.
  - Chat messages came from people who thought he was the company's support (for example the Amazon contact page), founders threatening to sue unless their data was removed (he removed all founder pages), and people wanting their phone or address updated.
- Result: about a month later he got 3 paying customers from Google company pages, confirmed by asking them, roughly matching Twitter's traffic. Google needs no daily effort, unlike Twitter. He says early customers always feel like flukes, as Twitter once did, and that "having patience and expecting non linear results is key."

## Spending on quality of life: the $500 versus $1,000 housing decision

- In Palermo, Alex first tried to "be a cockroach": spend as little as possible and take the cheapest Airbnb, a tiny dark studio. He hated it and was depressed, which made him question whether his income depends on his mental state now that he has no fixed salary.
- His reasoning: a $1,000/month house versus a $500/month one is double in percentage terms but only $500 in absolute terms, or 3 CyberLeads subscribers. The question is whether a better house makes him productive and happy enough to make up those 3 subscribers.
- He decided yes and rented the apartment he loved, near a clean beach in a safe area. He stopped checking grocery prices, started taking trips and days off, and stopped feeling guilty about taxis. He calls it the wisest move he made, while noting he would be writing the opposite had it gone badly.
- The motivation was his grandfather, who worked 50 years at a London printing plant and died of lung cancer under a year after retiring: "Worked from the age of 15. For a retirement that never came." Alex no longer postpones happiness.

## Designing a timeless life: trade-offs, memory and the Athens test

- Time accelerates when life is routine. Alex says a routine in a nice place feels enjoyable in the moment but short in hindsight, and that new experiences and travel (for example a 2-day hike up Olympus, a 5-day Sicily road trip) create lasting memories. Doing so means breaking his flow, which he finds painful because he loves his work.
- He found he can do anything but not everything: when he focused on training, his Italian stalled; on dating and trips, training and diet slipped; on everything at once, he ignored CyberLeads. His projects that year included a marathon the year before, a triathlon, Brazilian Jiu Jitsu, learning Italian via the 1,000 most common words, and a diet toward a six pack.
- He argues that things tied to our biology are timeless, since technology and society change faster than biology, like gold jewelry that looks new after 3,000 years while stone and bronze decayed.
- His thought experiment: in ancient Athens, would you swap with a wealthy elite with carts, gold, slaves and dynasty plans, or with a healthy, free trader with a strong family, friends, travel, deep conversations and art? Alex envies the trader.
- His heuristic: live a life that would be enviable in ancient Rome, modern New York and futuristic Shanghai.
- His conclusion: his business growth was modest, but his quality of life grew enormously, "And maybe that's enough."
