## Funnels are a story marketers tell, not how buying works

Alex West argues that "marketing is not a science" for solopreneurs, and that funnels "don't exist". He says they are made up by professional marketers trying to turn their job into a science.

- The tidy model is: sign up, fill a form, join a list, enter an email sequence, upgrade on the third follow-up. He says he has never upgraded from a drip campaign.
- He tells readers to ignore books like Peter Thiel's "Zero to One" and rely on their own direct experience of how they buy.
- He describes a more chaotic path:
  - You see a product and forget it.
  - Six months later you see it again and leave your email, then forget it again.
  - A friend mentions it, but you are moving house.
  - Months later you find it buried in the promotions tab.
  - A month after that you see it once more and buy.
- He notes that a team may have tweaked its landing page or email sequence the day before you convert, and be "100% certain" that change caused the sale. His point is that the world does not work that way.

## You cannot convert people, only keep them in your orbit

Alex's view is that you cannot persuade or force anyone to buy something they don't want. In his words, "all you can do is keep them in your orbit."

- The tactic is simple: show what you have built to more people, again and again, until some decide to buy.
- He says changes are fine, but the core of the work is repeated exposure, not optimizing a mechanism.

## Don't track conversion rates at low traffic

Alex agrees with a tweet that many people, especially in tech, lack basic statistics: "Conversions are down 30%" from a sample of 17 with no concept of statistical significance leads to bad calls. He admits he has made this mistake himself, gathering data and making decisions from it.

- He says you should not track conversion rates unless traffic is in the tens of thousands or millions per month.
- He goes further and says that "arguably even then maybe you shouldn't track."
- At low numbers he prefers working from gut feeling over data, because the data is probably noise.

## Example: dropdown data that was just mouse position

While collecting emails for Cyber Leads (he was focused on SEO then), Alex used a questionnaire with dropdown questions.

- Answers B and C were always the most popular.
- He shuffled and reordered the answers, and B and C still won.
- He did this several times with the same result, whatever the question was.
- He eventually realized that when people click through a form quickly, the mouse tends to rest over B and C when the dropdown opens.
- His lesson: bad data can look meaningful, and people then extrapolate from it and let it guide their product roadmap.

## Pretend work vs. true work

Alex says people turn to optimizing because it feels productive when they don't know what to work on. He calls this "pretend work" and admits he still does it, for example writing books that lose money.

- True work is trying to make money: launch, show it to people, follow up with them, and send payment links.
- His examples of pretend work:
  - Refactoring code or moving to AWS with zero users.
  - Setting up an A/B test with 300 visitors a month.
  - Redesigning a landing page when conversion is fine.
  - Setting up a business email, business cards or paperwork before making any money.
  - Adding a feature nobody asked for.
  - Improving site speed or setting up meta tags.
- He says "true work is about making money; pretend work is stuff like this."

## Entrepreneurship has no recipes

Alex says this is not just a knock on marketers. He compares it to his time at a company, where people complicate their work to look professional.

- In daily standups he would just say he fixed a bug and would continue a feature.
- Colleagues who "knew the game" gave long, jargon-filled updates, for example about investigating database structures for a move to Aurora DB.
- Designers and product managers often could not follow them, and he could not either.
- He says marketers do the same by trying to make their job a science, and that entrepreneurship is not a science either: "There are no recipes."
- He doubts the scientific approach in Thiel's "Zero to One", while acknowledging he can't argue with Thiel's results (he says he has made about $1 million, versus Thiel's billions).
