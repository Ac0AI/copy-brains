## Pricing only works when trust and service quality back it up

Alex raised his lead-generation service (Cyber Leads) from $2K to $3K per month and saw no drop in conversions. His business sends cold emails and tracks the results, at about $500K per year with roughly 90% profit. On pricing, he says:

- He has "raised my prices from 29 per month to three thousand", a 100x increase, mostly by adding service as he went.
- He is all for raising prices, but not "just because I can". He wants to improve the service first so he "feels good" about the price he charges.
- Bro advice says "you went too low" if nobody objects. He doesn't chase that; the price has to feel justified by the quality of the service, the conversations clients can have and the deals they can close.

## The "10x your price" thought experiment

Alex uses a question borrowed from Airbnb's founder (he is unsure of the name; the "seven star hotel experience" idea): what would a seven-star experience look like?

- His version: "What would my service look like if it cost 10 times more?" For him, that means picturing Cyber Leads at $30K per month.
- Step 1: write down everything that premium service would include.
- Step 2: ask why you don't already do those things.
- Step 3: either improve the service, raise the price, or both.

## Selling at $3K/month to a cold prospect is mostly about trust

He took his first sales call for a $3K/month deal with a prospect who found him only through Google. They had not seen his Twitter, blog or podcast interviews.

- He realized he is "spoiled": most callers already know him, so there is a "parasocial relationship" doing the selling.
- The cold prospect raised many objections: "why should we trust you", "how are you different to everyone else", "what can you guarantee us", "how do we know this actually works and you're not going to run off with the money".
- His conclusion: when you charge a lot, trust is "the ultimate leverage point". He applies the same rule when hiring: he cares less whether his VA is the smartest person and more about whether he can trust them and whether they are competent.
- Building an audience beforehand is a major advantage in high-ticket sales.

## Marketing results lag, so keep working in good times

- There is a lag between marketing effort and customers. Funnels make people think of a clean path, but he sees consistent marketing over months or years producing results.
- A month where he is "killing it" is usually the result of hard work from earlier, stressful months.
- When things are smooth and he works less, that sets up the next downturn. Other causes include breaking changes, a new competitor, surgery or lost motivation.
- His rule: "aim for peace, but be prepared for war". Every business has problems ("there's [expletive] in every garden"), and peers he admires go through the same.
- At the time of recording, he had done little marketing lately because he was sorting out back-end work. Revenue was still around $500K per year, and his goal was $1M per year within about three months.

## Treat a boring business like a science

- He calls his business "very boring": "I'm sending emails". But he treats sending cold emails, analyzing responses, tracking metrics and helping clients close multi-million dollar deals "as if I am building rocket ships".
- Benefits: it makes the work more enjoyable, and treating it with respect makes you a pro who can see the underlying science.
- Applies even to something dull that you have done daily for five years.

## Guarding against shiny object syndrome and the "new rat race"

- He is naturally good at focusing on one thing but still gets shiny object syndrome, usually because he wants something to be excited about (crypto, AI and so on).
- His reminder: the goal of the business is "not to be fancy, it's just to make money".
- Current motto: "boring business, exciting life". Do something boring, make money, and set goals for your personal life rather than only business goals.
- He doesn't want to be one-dimensional, "just a business guy" with five businesses.
- He swings between wanting to build businesses into his 80s and wanting to get off the grid. He warns that escaping the 9-to-5 can turn into a "new rat race" of building a business and chasing millionaire status, and that goals like $1M per year can be arbitrary when you already have enough money.
