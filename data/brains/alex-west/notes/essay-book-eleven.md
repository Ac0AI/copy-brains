## Making the first $1M: what the numbers and milestones actually looked like

- Alex hit $1,001,490 in cumulative revenue on June 1, 2024, a few days before his 30th birthday. He calls it revenue only, not profit or income, and says it technically missed his goal of $1M/year by age 30.
- He summed Gumroad, PayPal, every bank account and every Stripe account in a calculator app.
- His timeline: started bootstrapping January 1, 2018; first successful launch May 13, 2018; first dollar June 1, 2018 (two customers at $5/month each on the premium version of GitGardener); moved to Milan January 4, 2020; launched CyberLeads February 14, 2020; first blew up on Twitter April 22, 2020; surpassed his salary July 27, 2020; first day of self-employment January 6, 2021; discovered "the service" November 1, 2021; started it February 1, 2022.
- When he launched the $5/month plan, his father's reply was "Alex.. have you thought of finding a job?"
- He says it took about 20 tries to figure out something that worked, and that "you only have to figure it out once to change your life."
- He celebrates small milestones (launch dates, first dollar, hire dates) as anniversaries. His reasoning: "They mean something to you. So they are important."

## Why the milestone felt flat: ants, grasshoppers and "enough"

- Alex asks whether a moment will matter in a day, a month, a year or a decade. Most things won't. Even though hitting $1M felt anticlimactic, he expects it to give fulfillment for a long time.
- He separates short-term happiness (exercise, swimming, food) from slow-release fulfillment (his fighting journey, his entrepreneurship journey). He wants both.
- His ant/grasshopper model: the ant works all day on one project; the grasshopper lives for the day and does the minimum. He treats them as complementary, not contradictory.
- He says he used to confuse the two. He chased fleeting pleasure and ended up unfulfilled, then chased big goals and found they didn't make him happy.
- In practice he lets life swing naturally. Recent years alternated between one "ant" year and one "grasshopper" year. The aim is to know which phase he is in and feel zero guilt about it. His line: "it's called work-life balance. Not work-day balance."
- After the milestone he says he feels "enough" for the first time. His anxieties about age and accomplishments faded, he stopped envying anyone, and he found himself celebrating other people's success. He judges people by whether they are kind, authentic and fun, and by how they treat waiters and taxi drivers, not by money or fame.
- He never did the backpacking-hostel phase. By the time he traveled at 25 he had a business and wanted a nice apartment and routine. His takeaway: "whatever you don't experience today, you might never experience. Not because the opportunity will be gone. But because you will."

## Why advice often fails: his friend's launch with no customers

- Alex helped a friend launch a product using his full distribution:
  - Posted on Product Hunt from his personal account (tens of thousands of followers).
  - Posted on his Twitter (millions of views) and his newsletter (5,000 businesses).
  - Ran email campaigns almost identical to ones he ran for clients that made them millions.
  - Spent hours consulting on the idea, offer and product.
- Result: zero customers.
- The friend moved from excited to neutral to disappointed. Alex's only advice was to keep trying, with this or another idea, until it works.
- He says even a perfect playbook often fails because he can't know the friend's terrain, and he admits he recommends his own path to fewer and fewer people each year.
- He says it's easy to forget what starting from scratch is like without a platform, experience, network or audience: "It's fucking hard. I am very lucky."

## Maps versus compasses: when to follow playbooks and when to trust instinct

- **Maps** are other people's explicit knowledge: frameworks, playbooks, books (his examples: "How to become a millionaire", "The 5 laws of marketing", "The 3 best business models", "The ultimate guide to hiring"). They are accurate but hard to match to your own circumstances, and the terrain keeps changing.
- **Compasses** are your own intuition and experience. They are vague and only directionally correct, but apply to many cases.
- Rule he states: the earlier you are in a domain, the more you should rely on external knowledge; the more experience you gain, the more you should trust your own intuition. Don't burn the maps and don't follow the compass blindly. "Like a real captain, you consult them both."
- Early on he distrusted himself ("full of cognitive biases") and followed mental models, decision frameworks and playbooks. Books "changed my life." As his situation got more unique, that started backfiring, and decisions based on his own experience worked better.
- His caveats on his own advice: he may misremember, confuse luck for skill (and skill for luck), and some of it is outdated.
- His example of timeless versus dated advice: "don't camp near water at night" holds, while "turn left at the oak tree" may no longer apply.
- Musashi's Book of Five Rings, as he summarizes it: "Learn the rules. Abide by the rules. Break the rules." You can't skip the sequence. You start with maps and shift to your compass over time.

## On business coaches and mentors

- A client introduced Alex to a business coach who had run a large business. Alex took two calls.
- He found it felt like "founder therapy": questions about stress, exercise, goals, hiring and delegation. The growth ideas for CyberLeads were things he had already thought of, already tried, or found irrelevant.
- His reasoning: nobody can know his business in two hours when he has looked at it from every angle for half a decade.
- He says he does the same when helping others: "think out loud and throw shit at the wall."
- He still sees value. A conversation like this is how he discovered "the service" (November 2021). But he says it's never one person handing over a precise map; it's mostly swapping experiences.

## Setting a big goal, and knowing when it's costing you

- Advice to his younger brother: pick any big goal and become that person. It could be getting lines on your stomach. Motives don't matter either. Alex started Muay Thai because he got beaten up, and started building businesses to prove to himself and others that he was better.
- What matters is that you start, from anywhere, and "improve over time and adjust your sails."
- Skills transfer across fields. He draws parallels between fighting, business, photography, writing and travel, and says taking anything to depth teaches you about the rest. Diving into a new subject is a good way to go deeper in an old one.
- Warning from a school friend: for five years, every time they talk, the friend is "one week away" from hitting it big. His life is on hold (no socializing, no girls, only gym and work) until it happens, and he has sometimes self-sabotaged. Alex admits he has encouraged the cycle.
- Alex's fix: write down exactly what you would do, feel and experience if your craziest dreams came true. When he did this years ago he was surprised how little he wanted. Much of it (travel, friends, a photo album, music, a film, a book, a language, love, family) may be reachable now, for example through a good remote job. "Work isn't the only place you can get fulfillment from."
- His stance: set big goals and give life everything, but be aware you may not reach them, may stop wanting them, or may waste years.

## The $50K/month peak and the decline

- In the appendix Alex says that a few months earlier he hit a record month of $50k/month.
- He says his business "has began to die and is free falling."
- He is afraid and trying to work out what is going wrong, but doesn't "allow myself to complain too much."
- He frames it as the cost of the path he chose, alongside other fighters, entrepreneurs and artists: "The highest highs. And the lowest lows."
- He gives no cause or fix yet: "I hope I can fix this."
