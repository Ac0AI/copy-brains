## Alex's goal was $500/month from personal projects, and he started by building the wrong thing for two years

- Alex began building at 20 after a 3-hour conversation with a funded founder. He spent two years on "City Vibes", a nightlife app where bars and clubs uploaded real-time photos so people could pick where to go out.
- He made the classic mistakes: kept the idea secret, perfected it for 2 years before showing anyone, and left marketing for last. It died within weeks of launch.
- At a startup accelerator he saw that everyone talked about raising money and no one was making money. He decided he did not need to be a billionaire and would be "ok being a millionaire".
- City Vibes needed funding and only made profit at large scale, in a network-effects market where "either you go viral or you die". He killed it and slept well: "I was not my idea."
- Watching talks by DHH and Pieter Levels, he took away that the goal is to sell and make a profit, ideally from day one. That led him to "Indies" on Twitter, people building tiny independent businesses.
- He set a goal of $500/month from projects or a remote job, enough to live in a place like Thailand.

## Year 1 "shotgun" strategy: ship fast, low expectations, and what the first launches taught him

- Strategy: build many products, launch fast, and "throw virtual spaghetti to the wall until something sticks", then double down.
- Jan, MMAmatchups (fighter matchup voting site): built in 2 weeks, Reddit banned him for self-promotion, journalists did not reply, 300 visitors and 30 signups. Traffic died the day he stopped posting. Abandoned.
- Feb, MultiNewTab (browser extension): he had 1 week to build it instead of the 3 he thought he needed, and suspects 3 weeks would have taken 3 weeks. It hit 67 Product Hunt upvotes and 100+ installs, which he only saw 10 days later when the Chrome dashboard refreshed.
- Mar, TalentShare (platform for companies to share top candidates they could not hire): he collected emails, but no founder or hiring manager would share applicants. He could not launch an empty platform, since "an empty platform is not a product".
- Apr, RemoteJuniorClub (community with leaderboard, chat and a scraped junior remote job board): 8 people joined, but communities collapse if you do not push them, and he disliked chatting online.
- Pattern he noticed in May: 3 of his 4 products were communities or marketplaces, which are worthless without users (City Vibes, TalentShare, RemoteJuniorClub). The exception, MultiNewTab, "offered the same value, regardless of users" and was his most successful.

## May: building in public and Product Hunt launches that worked

- After four duds, he picked the easiest idea from his list: GitGardener, automated daily GitHub commits to keep your contribution graph green. It was not a business idea, but it worked regardless of user count.
- He had zero followers but tweeted publicly that he would build it in one week. Saying it out loud made quitting harder "even when no one was listening".
- GitGardener launched on a Sunday on Product Hunt: number 3 product of the day, 4,000 visitors, 400 signups, about 100 Twitter followers. Reddit bashed it and he did not care.
- The following week he built MakerFeed (a site to find and follow makers building on Twitter), tweeting each step, and hit number 1 on Product Hunt with 350+ upvotes. Followers went from 100 to 200.
- His lesson: "The 2 ideas I thought of the least, MultiNewTab and GitGardener, have been my most successful ones." The big ideas (City Vibes, TalentShare, RemoteJuniorClub) went nowhere.
- Formula that worked: "Low expectations. Fast feedback loop. Building in the open." He committed to building and launching 1 product a week and sharing the behind-the-scenes.
- He did not try to monetize MakerFeed because he could not imagine people paying for it.

## Making the first dollar: a user request turned into a paid tier

- A Twitter user asked if GitGardener could work with private repositories, so the green graph shows without exposing the automated commits.
- Alex built it as a premium feature at $5/month. No one paid in the first 24 hours, so he forgot about it, then GitGardener went from $0 to $50/month in a week, all from free users upgrading.
- A GitHub engineer encouraged him to apply to the GitHub Marketplace. He expected GitHub staff to hate a hack, but they found it funny. He was not accepted.
- Later, GitHub was acquired by Microsoft and made private repos free, which widened the audience for his private-repo plan. With an extra $10/month tier and doubled prices, GitGardener reached $130/month.
- He aimed for more website visitors because he got one paying customer per 300 visitors.

## Telemonetize: two months of building, a viral launch, and the wrong customers

- Idea: connect a Telegram channel plus Stripe, get an automatic dashboard and landing page, and have members added or removed automatically as they subscribe or cancel.
- It took two months of full-time coding and was "way too complex": Telegram bots, payments, a website builder, webhooks and automations were each full projects.
- The launch went "viral" with hundreds of thousands of impressions, front page on Product Hunt with 300+ upvotes and a 300-like tweet. It reached the wrong people: zero customers and zero free trials after a week.
- His takeaway: "even a fake landing page with a 'buy now' button could have done the same job."
- Crypto channels told him they would subscribe if he supported crypto payments. He built his own crypto payment gateway and they never subscribed.
- Trial users were mostly scammers, betting gurus, someone selling prostitutes, an apparent arms dealer, and an amateur porn performer. They were needy, rude, stingy, used burner cards and asked for extra trial time.
- He ended with 1 customer at $29/month (later 2 customers, $58/month), then killed it as "serving scammers".
- Peer validation helped: Pieter Levels called his turnaround from seeing demand to building what people need "10/10".

## Epilepsy Blocker: a hard build, the wrong audience, and a B2B detour

- Idea came from a NYT article about a journalist sent to hospital by a flashing GIF. He found no existing solution and built a browser extension that scans pages and hides content that could trigger photosensitive seizures, using linear algebra, histograms, Python and optimized C, plus scientific papers and broadcast telecom guidelines.
- He added a $10/month price only because his server crashed under even one user. It got 1 paying customer on day one from a Reddit post that was removed within 2 hours, after which he was banned.
- A tweet with 600+ likes and 100,000 views brought designers and engineers at big tech companies, not customers. Facebook groups also gave zero customers. "Vanity metrics" and "the wrong audience, again."
- Selling to individuals hurt: many people with epilepsy have other conditions or rely on benefits, $10/month was too much, and he kept giving it away free. A blunt "not interested" reply hurt more than he expected. He stopped "chasing $10 checks" and tried B2B.
- Setting a north star metric per product: GitGardener got more visitors, Telemonetize got retention through user conversations, and Epilepsy Blocker got visibility plus a B2B version.

## Going B2B: non-profits, clinics and TV channels

- An MIT startup founder in the epilepsy space found Epilepsy Blocker and offered Alex a job (offices in Boston, Seoul and Milan, work with MIT, Johns Hopkins and NASA). He accepted to start at the end of the year as a "Plan B" while still hoping to hit his goal first.
- Non-profits would not promote a for-profit product. Neurology clinics and doctors were friendly but nothing came of it, and they told him innovation of this kind was hard in Greece.
- TV route: a new Greek law would require warnings before dangerous content, starting Sept 1. He called the ministry, reached the responsible lawyer and confirmed it was real. He was surprised at "how easy you can reach and contact people at the top with a little persistence and an open mind".
- He got a CTO meeting via his accelerator coach, who called an ex-CTO, who then called the current CTO in front of him. He wanted to close the deal before building, and noted industry pricing of about $350 to analyze one video file.
- The CTO laughed: the law had been discussed for 5 years and would not take effect for another 5. Years later he still has not seen the warnings on TV.
- After an anonymous user said "You just do anything for money", he made Epilepsy Blocker free, killed Telemonetize and shelved GitGardener.

## Second-half spiral and what he learned from researching markets

- Doubting himself ("If you start thinking, you lose", a lesson from Muay Thai) left him swinging between the shotgun and sniper strategies with no conviction.
- He went back to fast launches that produced $0: Orthios (chatbot uptime monitoring), LocalTweetTime, IndieChannels (a distribution channels directory with 500+ upvotes, "but that doesn't mean anything, this isn't a business"), Epilepsy Blocker for Designers, DuckDuckGoSometimes, and Splash Search.
- Splash Search got about 250-300 installs and was copied into Unsplash's own product, indirectly reaching millions, but it earned nothing. Designer version: he asked on Reddit first, got lots of feedback, built it in 2 weeks, got hundreds of downloads, but no one paid.
- Market research: he cold emailed cybersecurity professionals asking for advice, not pitching, and offered to pay. About one Zoom call a day, all friendly, no one took money. He learned pain points but "can see the tree. But I can't see the forest."
- Accessibility market was the opposite: few replies, one asked for $150/hour, others would not help a for-profit product. His conclusion: pick a good market.
- He admits he is good at building and bad at marketing, and that he kept making the same mistakes.

## Two-year results and key numbers

- Year 1: $55/month, 8 products launched, 2 profitable. First 6 months "felt like a dream", last 6 "like a slug".
- Year 2: peaked at $198 to $200/month (GitGardener $130, Telemonetize $58, Epilepsy Blocker $10), then fell to $100/month after shutting products down. 7 products launched, 1 profitable. No paycheck from a company in two years.
- Later in his life, per his preface: he became self employed at 25, hit a $1M net worth at 27, made his first million at 29, and scaled down at 30 after burning out. He states plainly this is "not a playbook" and that luck is a real factor.
