## Moats are mostly irrelevant below the unicorn tier

- Alex says he used to be obsessed with moats while running Cyber Leads, because he wanted a business that would last decades.
- Looking back, he sees that obsession as "another word for perfectionism": looking for an ultimate, magical business that lasts.
- For his second business he thinks about moats "zero". He treats "assume there are no moats" as the useful heuristic.
- He might guess that community or personal brand could be moats, but thinks founders spend "way too much energy" on moats, and customers care less than founders assume.
- His working model: you cannot build a moat and then "just chill". In a free, dynamic market you must keep adapting, improving and staying competitive.
- He cites an exchange: the Snapchat CEO said software has no moat, so Snap is moving into hardware. Elon Musk replied that constant innovation is the moat. Alex agrees with Elon's framing.

## Stability-seeking and the 1M-in-3-months vs 200k-a-year choice

- Alex paraphrases Daniel Vassallo: many entrepreneurs try to replicate a salary or a job, something stable.
- Five years ago he would have picked a business making 200k a year for 5 years over one making 1 million in 3 months and then disappearing from bad luck. He now thinks the logical answer is to take the 1 million in 3 months and move on to the next thing.
- He says he wanted that stability and peace of mind "to sleep like a baby". He now sees chasing it as a trap, not a strategy.

## Labels and identity: "indie hacker", "solopreneur", "nomad"

- Alex says labels help at the start by pointing you in a direction. His 2018 profile at age 23 described him as a nomad, and he had just discovered indie hacking and wanted to be one of those people.
- The danger is letting a label become dogmatic and box you in. He had to break out of several self-imposed boxes:
  - **Solopreneur box:** he refused to hire anyone because it seemed cool and he wanted to make 1 million a year alone. He asked himself whether looking cool to others mattered more than lifestyle, then hired a few people, worked two hours a day and traveled.
  - **Passive income box:** he had a subscription business, the indie hacker dream, then got a chance to build a service on top of it. Services are not passive, and he had to break that identity to take it.
- Each time he broke out of an abstract box, it "freed" him.
- His analogy: being an athlete means you don't smoke, you run and exercise, but you still have a glass of wine at your sister's wedding. Keep identities, just not dogmatically.
- He agrees with the idea of being like water. Use identities as tools, consciously, and notice when one is making you suffer.
- He says he is currently trying to break out of another box again and may discuss it in a future episode.

## Why a bidding or "sponsor my laptop" website is a good thing

- Alex likes the wave of joke projects such as bidding websites, "sponsor my laptop" and ads on someone's baby. They remind him of the early internet, where "not everything has to be serious" and some things can be for fun.
- He sees building for fun as the spirit of building on the internet.
- He links this to constraint: his best work ever came when he had a full-time job he hated. He had only two hours a day and made them count, and calls it his most productive year.
- His view is that pressure, the "fire", produces your best ideas, more than having money in the bank and lying around.

## Pressure from family can sharpen, not limit, a founder

- Alex answers a tweet claiming nobody makes it as an indie hacker while married with young kids. His answer is "yes and no".
- Upside: ultimate motivation. At 18 he thought "I cannot be a loser" for his 10-year-old brother to look up to. Friends who became fathers told him they felt the same.
- Downside: for him, locking in to build a new business is easier with all the time in the world and no distractions. Single life can also be distracting.
- He says the quality of the home environment matters, and that a partner can influence you a lot.
- He frames kids as "tiny little humans that never asked to be born", and says that responsibility can spark things in you that you would never have lying around with money in the bank.

## Stage-based advice like "no paid ads until $5-10M" ignores business "genetics"

- Alex uses ads and outreach mainly to validate new offers and ideas, not as a substitute for organic or social. He currently sends about a thousand outreach emails a day to test new ideas.
- He agrees with the narrow risk in the tweet: a business fully dependent on ads faces rising ad costs as competition grows, "a ticking bomb". He rejects the blanket rule.
- His objection to "0 to 1M do this, 1 to 3M do that" advice: it depends on the genetics of the business. Variables he lists: LTV, acquisition channels, retention and churn, and the market.
- Weightlifting analogy:
  - Benchmarks like repping 100 kg on bench press depend on bodyweight. His brother is 60 kg, he is 85 kg and his best friend is 105 kg.
  - His friend hit the benchmark in his first 3 months at age 16, while it took Alex years, and his brother may never reach it.
  - Businesses are the same.
- Example: Cyber Leads went straight to half a million a year without him doing much, then hit its business "genetics" ceiling.
- He admits he was once stuck at about half a million and followed the "0 to 1M, stick with one thing" videos, which kept him doing what he already had. He says he was "so dumb" to listen.
- Churn: in his experience you cannot influence it much in a high-churn market. You may be top quartile but you "revert to the mean" and won't be a different animal.

## Writing style: protreptic over didactic

- Alex says many people love didactic writing, being told what to do and how. He planned his books that way, but changed course.
- He chose protreptic writing instead: give interesting thoughts and let readers derive their own lessons. He accepts that he lost readers who wanted step-by-step instruction.
- He says he thinks people need to figure things out themselves, not be told "post on X three times a day and don't do anything until you reach 10K a month".
- On X, the medium shapes the message. It is punchy, attention-driven and absolute, so he would never write "you will not make it and will suffer" in a book, where he would see both sides.
- His approach: write books to say things completely, and treat X as the short, punchy channel.
