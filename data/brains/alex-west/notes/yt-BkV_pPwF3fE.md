## Alex's journey: 19 failed products before the 20th worked

Alex built 19 products that didn't take off before his 20th, CyberLeads (getcyberlist.com), went well. CyberLeads is a monthly newsletter that sends digital agencies high-quality leads, sold on a monthly subscription. He had about two and a half years in the maker community by this point, and the product took off only in the last six months. He still held a full-time software engineering job while building it.

- **2018:** he took a "shotgun" approach. He launched about 15 products from zero experience and reached 200 dollars MRR.
- **2019:** he took a "sniper" approach. He locked onto one idea, an epilepsy-blocker Chrome extension, and spent about two months coding it. He cold-called charities and TV networks and met a CTO at a Greek telecom, and none of it paid off.
- **The extension today:** it ended up free. Real-time analysis of GIF pixels was computationally expensive, so he had planned to charge, but people with photosensitive epilepsy often live on benefits, and he disliked charging them 10 dollars a month. The project still led to the job that moved him abroad.
- **2019-2020:** he sat between 50 and 150 dollars MRR for about two years "no matter how many products I build."
- **Then:** he took a job in Milan, wrote "two years of indie hacking" (a blog post listing every product with its numbers), and went back to the shotgun approach. He had studied the Indie Hackers archive and noticed the makers he looked up to "had traction from the beginning." He quotes Taleb: "don't tell me what you think, show me your portfolio."

## Picking boring ideas and validating with a landing page

- **Switch:** Alex stopped chasing "unique ambitious" products meant to change the world. He argues that wanting to improve humanity is mostly ego, and that you can "build something boring and give money to charity."
- **New rule:** build something he knows people will pay for, and build it without code. He fell out of love with programming and realized he loved "creating something out of nothing," not the code.
- **How CyberLeads started:** he found a Reddit post listing recently funded startups. Googling showed about 10 companies selling the same information to different buyers: journalists, sales teams, job seekers, and business-intelligence users. They delivered it as a database, newsletter, or report, and all were making money. That was validation.
- **Why a newsletter:** it "reduces friction" and he could launch it without a product.
- **MVP:** a landing page plus a Gumroad subscription, with no Stripe checkout coded. He launched on Product Hunt and got 10 paying customers.
- **What failure taught him:**
  - A .com domain doesn't matter for validation.
  - A professional logo won't make or break you.
  - Extra features don't either.
- **Reddit idea hunting:** he tried the "idea hunting on Reddit" method from a blog post. It produced ideas and some launches, with no great results.
- **On experience:** he says 2018 Alex would have noticed this opportunity and dismissed it as "taken" (his phrase: "it's a [expletive] bus seat"). He compares spotting ideas to a chess master's pattern recognition and says it comes only from repeated launches.

## Competition, copycats and niching down

- **Bus-seat mindset:** an idea being taken isn't a reason to skip it. "Mate, you're against yourself and that's it." The internet is big enough for everyone.
- **Fishing analogy:** two people fishing the same river is fine if they target different fish. The problem is "bashing heads" with the same product, same audience, same channels and same approach.
- **Energy-drink example:** two identical drinks aren't competing if one is positioned on taste and the other on gym performance. Products then diverge as features are added.
- **Pivot in one day:** he started with general B2B sales leads. Going through customers, he saw that agency CEOs were his favorites: easy to work with, same language, and one closed deal repays the subscription 100 to 1,000 times. He pivoted to agencies in a day.
- **Features per niche:** he added agency-specific features such as forecasting which design or engineering agencies will need help, and non-profits that need agencies.
- **Copycats:** he is open about numbers and strategy. People have copied his blog, run ads against his name, and stolen and resold his data. He believes "they cannot stop you because the internet is so big."
- **Big players:** a big company has 50 people, but you really compete against about five. The other 45 are bored and "log into Jira" and do tasks. A hacker moves faster, while a company "needs meetings, plural, to change the color of one button."

## Distribution: the tweet that turned it around

- **After launch:** with 10 customers and no growth, he tried Quora, Reddit, Hacker News and cold emails. Twitter began as a side effect while building an audience.
- **The tweet:** he tweeted about his first 50 dollars from a customer. He almost didn't post it; he only wanted to tweet once a day, and posted just before watching a film with his girlfriend. The next morning "it's chaos" and he gained roughly another 10 customers.
- **Lesson:** "now I have my product and my distribution channel." He says "that's the most important thing that no one talks about," and he doesn't want to only have product-market fit: "Facebook, you have it now. What you need is the distribution channel."
- **Moving from creative to athlete:** once product and channel exist, "you have to become an athlete," putting in the work on things you know produce results.
- **Role model:** Jordan O'Connor's blog documents going from zero to 30,000 dollars a month with one product and one distribution channel. That convinced Alex to "double down" and not chase SEO and blog posts.
- **Traction book:** he read *Traction* (19 channels, the Bullseye framework). He dabbled with everything, then doubled down on Twitter when the tweet went viral.
- **Second product:** he launched another product on Product Hunt that "went nowhere," and he regretted splitting his energy.
- **Marketing style:** instead of "use promo code X for 10% off," his marketing is "Hey, I'm Alex, I'm trying to gain my freedom and I'm not selling anything to anyone." He says "the best marketing is no marketing, no one wants to be sold anything."

## Work habits and mindset

- **Constraints help:** with no job he did "pretend work" all day. After starting a full-time job, two hours in the morning produced more than a whole day before.
- **Mornings:** he used to wake at 11 or 12. After work he was too drained to be creative, so he switched his side-project block to mornings. Finding an idea and distribution channels is the hardest part and needs the most energy.
- **Habits over motivation:** *Atomic Habits* changed his view. "Motivation is super overrated," and discipline is overrated too. You want one or two things that are like brushing your teeth, which "you don't even think about."
- **Daily blog:** it helps him see that "pretend work" like market research or redesigning a landing page is not real work. His filter is "what are the two things you can do today that will move your business forward."
- **Doubling down:** he stays with CyberLeads and Twitter, "nothing super creative," and waits. He says inputs are yours but outcomes aren't: "you cannot make someone take his credit card out."
- **Ideas vs execution:** he rejects "ideas are nothing, execution is everything." He quotes Courtland Allen: a ten-times-better idea is easy to find, a ten-times-better executor is hard.
- **Uncertainty:** "when in doubt, do the exact opposite of what you've been doing." For him that meant taking a job, which he dreaded, and moving abroad.
- **Learn by doing:** "people learn from doing and not from learning." He cites Bruce Lee: "keep what works, discard what does not work, try new things, rinse and repeat."

## Money, freedom and staying grounded

- **Numbers:** a few years earlier he earned about 500 dollars a month full time in Greece. By this interview, CyberLeads had brought in 4k in net revenue in a month, and one annual subscriber paid in a morning about what a month's engineering salary used to pay.
- **Why he doesn't upgrade:** he has run it for only about six months, so "I don't feel like I'm standing on solid ground." He says "I like money because of what it gives you: security, freedom, time-wise, location-wise." He doesn't like things and hasn't bought anything since.
- **Gratitude:** he reminds himself daily that "I'm living my dream." The question "what's next" is one he hates, because it stops you admitting you have what you want.
- **Closing advice:** "Don't give up." If you quit after two tries you "don't want it bad enough." You "only need one product."
