## Why Alex's metrics didn't save CyberLeads: tracking 30+ metrics and the three-input equation

Alex's business plateaued at about $42k/month, then fell to $25k/month, and then to roughly $20k/month in year 7. His first response was to track more. He and Jonathan held daily multi-hour calls through 30+ metrics grouped into fulfillment, sales, marketing and business. They tried to bring every metric back to its level from when CyberLeads made $50k/month, reasoning that matching the inputs would reproduce the outputs.

- Revenue kept falling anyway. Some metrics improved 5X, some stayed flat and some declined, so he couldn't tell whether the business was dying because of the metrics that hadn't improved or because results hadn't kicked in yet.
- Historically, marketing and product work took about 3 months to show up in revenue. At this point it had been 4.
- His conclusion: "We are tracking too much. And we are working on too many things."
- His own explanation for not fighting at his best with a camp: he tried to focus on too many things at once and "fucked up". His best fights came with few variables and no camp.

## The equation of business: customers, retention and price

After years of tracking 30+ metrics, Alex concluded that only 3 inputs mattered: how many clients he signs per month, how many months they stay, and how much they pay.

- For years he signed about 4 new clients per month, who stayed about 4 months and paid $2k/month. CyberLeads therefore plateaued at 4 x 4 x $2k = $32k/month. The newsletter sat around $10k/month, giving about $42k/month, or $500k/year.
- The plateau happened because the number of customers coming in equaled the number leaving, an equilibrium. He had assumed things would compound just by staying in business.
- Not on his list of what mattered: average onboarding time, emails sent per client per week, follow-ups to hot leads, social post counts, or even churn.

## Working backwards to $1M/year: the "3-4-5"

Alex used the equation to reverse engineer a $1M/year target.

- The "3-4-5": clients pay $3k/month, he signs 4 new clients per month, and they stay 5 months on average. That doubles the agency side.
- He judged it realistic because he had charged $3k/month, signed 4 clients in a month, and kept clients 5 months many times before. The task was lifting the averages.
- Adding another $10k to $20k/month from the newsletter and other offers (which he had done before) would reach about $83k/month, or $1M/year.
- He found one "golden metric" per lever, and none were the obvious ones:
  - More new clients correlated most with the number of meetings with potential clients (not traffic or Twitter impressions).
  - Longer retention correlated most with the number of leads forwarded to clients (not deals closed or lower prices).
  - Price: higher prices didn't mean clients stayed less, and $3k/month appeared to maximize LTV.

## What he tried, and why every lever backfired

Alex pushed all three golden metrics hard.

- Meetings: "book a call" links went into the free newsletter signup, the weekly email, the monthly list, cold email campaigns and welcome emails. He offered free AI demos, free introductions to other agency owners and free consultations. Meetings went up 5X.
- Leads: he built an agency network matching agencies with complementary services, and a referral network asking each new newsletter agency if it wanted to hire another agency. Leads forwarded went up 5X. He also posted 5X more on Twitter.
- Result: all of it backfired past a certain point.
  - More booked calls did not close more deals. Calls were mostly unqualified: no-shows, a prospect who only wanted a free lead first, a prospect interrogating credibility, and one who feared being burned by agencies after losing over $10k. Only one prospect closed, the one who found him on Twitter and booked on his own, and only at $2k/month. $3k/month was too expensive for everyone.
  - More leads forwarded became overwhelming. His best client cancelled after months of daily leads: "we would prefer to have 1 solid call per week than scheduling calls every day with bad fits."
  - The $3k/month price made it impossible to close anyone.
  - A free newsletter tier increased lead volume but killed paid newsletter growth.
  - Posting more on social media eventually dropped engagement and caused unfollows.
- His general lesson: optimizing a metric beyond a point backfires. He compares this to chasing "super-optimal" blood pressure, which ended with him fainting in a pool of blood.

## Why the dashboard lied, and going back into the work

Alex says "numbers don't lie, but in my case they did." Dashboard metrics looked fine while the actual work had decayed, because he had removed himself from the business.

- He jumped in himself: tweaking campaigns that weren't generating leads, doing slow tasks, and watching recorded sales calls.
- He found mistakes in client campaigns, embarrassing emails going to clients, outdated systems followed blindly instead of common sense, extremely salesy emails to hot leads, and sales calls with no structure.
- He then held many meetings with his team to fix systems, client communication and campaign writing.
- He had told himself he could work 2 hours per day, make $500k/year and enjoy life. His honest read was that those days might be over and he needed to do the work he disliked.

## Asking customers for the truth: honesty and brutal feedback

When Jonathan said he no longer believed in the service ("we could do so much more with AI and all these new tools, but we're still running things old school"), Alex contacted every current and past client.

- His rule: "When you don't know what to say, just tell the truth." He told them he had tried to remove himself, things slipped, he was back, and he would personally handle their campaigns.
- Clients responded well to the honesty. Some stayed, some left, a couple came back.
- He asked for brutally honest feedback and took notes through hour-long conversations. He called it painful but necessary.
- He has a no-ego rule with Jonathan: when talking business, it's about the business, not the people, so they can be honest.
- He had been skeptical of AI tools because earlier attempts only marginally improved things while adding cost and complexity. He reconsidered because it had been years.

## Founder mode vs. manager mode, and rebuilding the offer

Alex realized he had built the perfect business for himself (minimum work, maximum money, industry-standard quality) and forgot to build the perfect service for clients. He had never aimed to be the best at what he did.

- His new view: founder mode and manager mode are cyclical. Founder mode invents, sets up and turns the business into a machine. Manager mode oversees, steps back and enjoys life. Removing yourself is not a one-way destination, and you may need to return to fix things.
- To redesign the service he imagined it at 10X the price, $20k/month. That version would include:
  - Joining clients as a co-founder, sending leads from day one, and weekly consulting on offer and business.
  - Partnerships with agencies serving Silicon Valley startups, retweets from his account (millions of views per year), showcases in his newsletter (5,000 business owners) and launches on his Product Hunt (tens of thousands of followers).
  - Introductions to VC funds, a founder community as a moat against copycats and AI, and a network for boosting each other's content.
  - Custom campaigns per client instead of only funded startups, using AI and the latest tools, "even if they only help marginally."
- Lesson he re-learned: "It's not about what you think your customers 'need'. It's about what your customers 'want'." They all want AI and to feel they are not being left behind, so give it to them.
- It took about 3 months of full-day work to rebuild. For the first 2 years CyberLeads was a monthly list, then for 3 more years a simple service on top.

## Bloat, the first idea, and changing one thing at a time

The rebuilt CyberLeads diluted its core, and prospects got confused. A typical reaction: "we were interested in the funded startup list and the cold email campaigns. But all the rest is cool too, I guess."

- Why competitors fell away: for years he added almost nothing to CyberLeads, so positioning stayed clear. Most copycats kept adding features until they were no longer competing with him. Keeping a product extremely simple for years is hard, especially as building gets easier.
- His takeaway: "Sometimes the first idea is the best idea. And then you just destroy it over time. That's why launching fast is important." He sees the same in photography, where the first photo is usually the best.
- A painter he met in Cyprus said he knows a painting is finished when he names it, and after that he is not allowed to touch it or he destroys it. Alex wonders if he had already "christened" CyberLeads.
- Powerlifting rules he applies to business:
  - If you are progressing, change nothing.
  - If you are stuck, make one change at a time, and the smallest possible: add a rep, then a set, then a day, then an exercise.
  - Don't nuke the program and start over. Change reluctantly.
- Injury lesson: he changed too many things at once and spent half a year troubleshooting a back injury.
- Organism, not machine: after his friend (a medical student) told him the body is a complex, interconnected system that dislikes extremes, Alex suggests CyberLeads may be more organism than machine. It responds to simple levers, dislikes extremes and many simultaneous changes, is shaped by its environment, and may have a finite lifespan.

## Limits of control and knowing when to stop searching

Alex ends unresolved, with CyberLeads dying and no fix found.

- He feels less control the older he gets. He now distrusts research studies (few people, narrow conditions) and prefers adapting his lifestyle around injuries to eliminating them.
- He uses the book "Uncle Petros and Goldbach's Conjecture" as a warning: a mathematician spends his life searching for a proof that may not exist, like looking for keys that might not be in the house.
- His fear: he has already wasted years searching for keys that may never have existed, with City Vibes, Epilepsy Blocker, and CyberLeads when it was stuck at $500k/year.
- He doesn't want to give up, but doesn't want to keep searching a room with no keys. His conclusion: "The only place left is the external."
