## Building a solo business through many failed products: the shotgun approach, validating before building, and the "progression" from build-first to market-first

Alex describes his own arc as a progression from building first, to marketing first, to earning before building:
- **Product 1:** two years before he showed it to a single person. He perfected every button, played with the copy, built a server, a mobile app and business cards. He calls it "every mistake in the book".
- **Cyberlies (2 years later):** launched without building anything and reached $100 per month.
- **Service on top of Cyberlies (another 2 years later):** he was making $100K per year before he started building anything.
- His heuristic: "consider that you have built your dream product. Now what?" Do that part first (marketing, finding customers), before building the product.
- "Real work begins when the to-do list ends." Finishing the MVP is when the real work starts.
- The path ran from building, then marketing, then earning, to marketing, then earning, then building.

## Failing forward: how a cyber security idea turned into a lead-generation business

- Alex set out to build a heavily technical product in the cyber security market.
- To validate it, he scraped GitHub by hand for cyber security consultants and reached out to them. He got on calls with 10 people and received strong feedback.
- That was the first time he thought: "if someone provided me with leads every month, specifically what I wanted, I would pay 50 bucks for a month". That insight pointed him toward the lead generation market.
- His conclusion: "You fail forwards, that is the way you progress." His ideas got better with every failure, and the progression went from two years building a product to making $100K per year without building anything first.
- A reader asked what to do if they cannot find any B2B ideas. Alex replied: start building your b2c ideas, and while building them B2B ideas come organically, because by then you are already a business.
- He has advocated B2B and higher prices as generic advice (with exceptions).

## Shotgun approach: many projects to find traction, then focus (diversify only once rich)

- Alex has "always advocated for a shotgun approach": throw many things at the wall and focus solely on the one that sticks.
- He thinks most success stories start with a bang, meaning traction. With traction you are willing to "pull your soul into it".
- Focusing on a failing project out of belief has worked for some people, but he sees it as the exception.
- He distinguishes finding wealth from keeping it. "Diversifying is great for retaining your wealth, not for making your wealth."
- When you are up and coming, focus on one thing and try to hit the home run. "You've hit the ball, it's up in the air, start running. Don't think, oh let me diversify."
- He has seen people making less than $1K per month talk about diversifying revenue streams, and he doesn't think that's the way to go.
- Diversify once you are already where you want to be and just want to secure your lifestyle. Rule of thumb he gives: if you want to be a billionaire, don't start diversifying at another million per year. If you want to make 50K per year, you can diversify once you hit that.
- The remark about "19 failed products" in the title is not elaborated in this conversation; the only concrete numbers he gives are the progression above and the five years he says it took him.

## You don't have to be your own first user, and impostor syndrome is a wall to walk through

- Alex says "be your own first customer" is "one of those myths that really makes people not do something".
- When he started Cyberlies he did not run an agency and did not do cold outbound or cold emails at the time. He sold email contacts to agencies.
- Later he started an agency charging people $2K per month and felt impostor syndrome again. His view: "The impostor syndrome is a wall you have to bomb and walk through."
- When he started the service he "had no idea". He read a few books, tried techniques, bought a few courses and experimented, and got "amazing results".
- His advice: "You can learn anything." Find something you enjoy that you think is a good business, go deep and learn.

## Why it takes years: obsession, not part-time effort

- Alex says a reader DM'd asking to be his assistant or mentee because he is "exactly where I want to be in six months". He replied that he is not the person to look at, because it took him five years.
- He describes those as five years of "it's the only thing I'm thinking about" (in the shower, on a walk, on a run, on an airplane, on a train, sleeping), not five years on and off or part-time.
- "It has some benefits no one can argue against": freedom of time, freedom of movement, location independence, financial independence, doing what you want with who you want. "Everyone wants that, but do you want it enough?" Are you willing to sacrifice five years or more?
- He feels "extremely lucky" and thinks luck is a huge part of it.
- He cites his own drivers as ego and proving he can do it, and not wanting to work for someone else.
- On stress: working for someone else gave him constant, flat stress (daily stand-ups, a meeting at 12, Friday one-on-ones, a task that has to be done by Friday, performance reviews every two weeks). Working for himself the stress is different, not higher or lower. After he quit, his Fitbit showed his heart rate dropped about 10 beats per minute.
- He separates passions from revenue: he loves running his business but won't try to make a podcast a revenue activity.

## Where the economy is heading: commoditization, shrinking firms, trust and attention

- Humans have a terrible track record of predicting the future, but he points to trends. He quotes Naval: the optimal size of the firm is shrinking.
- Alex says the work in his business (Cyberlies) that would once have taken about 20 people is done by him solo plus one assistant, because he automated it.
- Everything is being commoditized. Starting a software business needed capital 20 years ago (local servers). Now servers cost around $5 per month, and you can build a full Airbnb clone with no code in a weekend.
- Barriers to entry will keep dropping everywhere. "The only thing that cannot be commoditized is trust and attention." Ads are a race where prices go up as more people join.
- He expects businesses to be fronted by influencer-like faces, such as Joe Rogan-style personalities, even in B2B, with companies behind them making millions on maybe five contractors.
- Positioning example: if you hacked Red Bull's recipe and made an identical drink, you couldn't beat them head on, but you could position it differently (for example "Beast Mode" energy drink for people hitting a gym personal best) and get different customers. As everything commoditizes, trust matters more and more; brand marks like the Nike tick are worth billions.
- Alex compares small solo companies to small fish eating scraps from a shark's teeth: he competes with ZoomInfo, a public billion-dollar company, and they "don't give a [expletive] about me". The scraps are enough to make "a few million per year", which is nothing for them. He expects the middle class of business to disappear, leaving huge corporations and individual brands with companies behind them.
- He defines "small" as making up to $10M per year.

## Financial goals and the work-until-lunch lifestyle

- His financial goal is "living as if money doesn't exist": at the supermarket he doesn't look at prices, and he buys things unless they're wildly disproportionate.
- His day-to-day goal used to be a fully automated business. He now thinks that is "so overrated". His new goal is to work until lunch, about four hours per day.
- He cites old newspaper articles predicting that automation would stop humans working by 2000; today there is more automation and more work than ever. He ties it to an innate need to build and improve things ("I find a stick, I want to collect that stick").
- His forecast: the work you don't want to do will shrink a lot, and the rest of the time you'll still be doing things, but things you enjoy. His rule: "the rest, delegate, eliminate or automate."
- He also reads the architecture/computer-science story as: "everything comes down to our fear of death", the urge to build things that outlast you. Computer science is to computers what astronomy is to a telescope: "the computer is the telescope, computer science is the stars".
