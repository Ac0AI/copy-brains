## From zero to $2,000/month: the sequence that worked (and the two years of "pretend work" before it)

Alex's core claim is that CyberLeads took "two weeks of true work" to reach $290/month. That came after two years of pretend work that built the skills to do those two weeks well. He lists those skills as: recognizing a good idea, recognizing a good distribution channel, killing perfectionism, launching fast, and knowing which idea suits his skills and resources.

- Month 0: no idea. He launched in February, on Valentine's Day, with ten paying customers at $29/month, which was $290/month.
- The first list shipped March 1st.
- April 21st: a tweet went viral and distribution kicked in.
- 4 months old: $1.5k/month.
- 6 months old: $2k/month.

His takeaway: things get easier once you find the product/channel/system combination, and you only need to find it once.

## Plan: non-unique, B2B, no-code, expensive

After a month at a day job, Alex rebuilt his strategy. His final plan was: "build a non romantic, non unique, B2B product, build it without writing code, charge a lot of money for it, and whatever happens, give 10% of its profits to charities."

- He stopped chasing unique, world-improving ideas. Epilepsy Blocker's market was small: about 1% of people have epilepsy and only about 5% of those have photosensitive epilepsy.
- He noticed his incentives were warping. He caught himself hoping a cure wouldn't be found. His conclusion was that a business should fund his passions, not be them.
- For two years, every customer he had was an individual paying about $10/month. Meanwhile his employer spent hundreds of dollars a day on salad bowls for lunch, so he moved to B2B.
- He fell out of love with coding after eight hours a day and realized he loved creating, which can be done without code.
- He targets "humble" $5k to $25k/month solo businesses in large B2B markets where many players can coexist.

## Why competing with companies isn't scary

From working inside a company, Alex drew four lessons:

- **People:** a 50-person company is really "5 motivated people and 45 yes-men". Employees care about their own lives first, and he promised never to expect hires to care like a founder.
- **Speed:** employees try to minimize work: tickets, handoffs, meetings, QA, regulatory review. "You can pivot 180 degrees in one day. Large companies have to hold meetings to change the color of a single button."
- **Budgets:** companies spend freely on small things, which makes them better customers than individuals.
- **Ugliness:** a life-saving product brings refunds, bug reports and fear of downtime. "You have more responsibility. And when you charge money to cover your costs, they hate you even more."

## Finding the idea: market first, already validated

Alex stopped inventing solutions and went idea hunting in markets that were healthy and not winner-take-all. He picked cyber security, recruiting and lead generation, and he favored audiences he could reach on Twitter and Product Hunt (tech people and startups).

- **Cyberflake** (pen-tester collaboration tool): too technical, wrong market for him.
- **Scrapcat** (uptime monitor for scrapers): landing page and Reddit mockup got no real interest.
- **Cyberhound** (developers to reach out to): no response from a landing page, Twitter, Reddit or cold emails.
- **Birdleads** (Twitter keyword alerts): skipped because it required code.

CyberLeads came from a Reddit post listing recently funded startups. He found at least 10 businesses already selling this information as a service, database, list, newsletter or intelligence platform, to investors, salespeople, journalists, job seekers and tech followers. For the first time he treated an existing competitor as validation instead of "bus seats" ("Oops. This is taken!"). His own day job helped him see why funded startups buy: they have money and scaling problems.

## Launch with no product: sell first, build the list after

Alex chose a monthly newsletter format because it lowers signup friction and needs no code. The offer was company, funding and contact data for hundreds of newly funded startups, delivered on the 1st of each month.

- He built the site in a no-code tool and launched on Product Hunt, which none of the competitors had used.
- He had no list yet. He built the first one after launch, in two weeks, waking at 04:00 to work until 09:00, then working again 19:00 to 22:00. "I was building my parachute while falling."
- He says he would never have worked that hard without paying customers waiting, and that building the list first would have taken months and changed the product.
- Even mockups, soft launches, Reddit posts and cold emails had started to feel like pretend work. His rule: "Just launch already."
- The monthly list still takes 50 to 100 hours to compile, even with automation.
- He later narrowed positioning to agencies.

## Finding the distribution channel

After launch he had no repeatable acquisition. He tried Facebook Groups, LinkedIn, Reddit, cold outreach, Hacker News, direct sales and Twitter for almost two months while revenue fell back to $100/month. The lead-gen market has high churn: buyers blast a cold email template, get no results and cancel, so "you have to keep rowing".

- He had a habit of tweeting daily. On April 21st, with about 600 followers, he tweeted "Got my first $50/mo customer!" and went to bed. It reached about 100,000 impressions and 2,000 likes, and brought 10 paying customers.
- The next week he posted a monthly update that someone shared to the top of Hacker News. It brought another 10 customers.
- He was not promoting or selling: "I was sharing my personal journey, lessons and milestones." His view: "The best marketing is no marketing. No one wants to be sold anything."

## Focus, the failed detour, and artist-to-athlete

In March he spent nearly the whole month on a second product, a monthly list of actively investing investors for startups raising money. It got zero customers despite more effort than CyberLeads, and he took it as a lesson in humility: "You have a gift in your hands... Double down and show it the attention it deserves."

- After the viral week he resisted using other channels and new products and focused only on CyberLeads and Twitter: "Less is more."
- Before the system exists you must be an artist: creative, trying different things daily. After it exists you must be an athlete: disciplined, doing the same things every day.
- His routine became 2 hours of marketing in the morning and 2 hours on the lists in the evening.

## Work habits and filters against "pretend work"

- **Mornings:** evening sessions failed because the gym and day job used up his creative capacity. He moved to 05:00 to 08:00 work, then the gym until 09:00, then the office by 10:00. The limit helped: "These constraints set me free."
- **Pretend work:** restructuring code with zero users, A/B tests at 300 visitors/month, redesigning a page that converts fine, business cards and email, features no one requested, speeding up the site.
- **True work:** emailing 10 potential customers for feedback, posting in FB groups, Twitter and Reddit, a landing page with signup and a soft launch, asking local businesses for feedback and payment, launching on Product Hunt.
- **Gun test:** "You have to generate revenue online by the end of the week. If you don't, you're dead." Today he asks the same about CyberLeads growing by month end.
- **Lessons from five launches:** the .com domain, the logo, extra features and upvotes from the wrong audience didn't matter, and faster launching is better. All he needed was a landing page and a payment button.
- **Habit tracker:** he marks daily habits green or red in a notebook.

## Shotgun vs sniper

Alex reviewed hundreds of business podcast episodes by observing what founders did rather than what they said. The pattern: they built stuff, launched without overthinking, got some traction, then kept going.

- His first year: 12 products and almost $200/month.
- His second year: only Epilepsy Blocker, which didn't work.
- His conclusion: the answer is in the middle. Persistence is needed, but you need initial traction to know it's worth it.
- On ideas, he quotes: "It's very difficult to become a 10 times better executioner of ideas, but it's easy to have a 10 times better idea." The right idea is one you can build, run and get in front of customers repeatedly at a good cost.
- He is clear that luck mattered, and that imitators who copied his steps quit within weeks. His line: "If you show up every day for two years, one day you'll get lucky."
