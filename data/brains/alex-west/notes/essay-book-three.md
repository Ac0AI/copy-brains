## Pricing and positioning: niche down, raise prices, and let conversions rise

Alex launched CyberLeads (a hand-compiled monthly list of fast-growing startups) with a generic pitch: "Find fast growing startups to do business with." He cast a wide net on purpose, because he didn't know who would subscribe and didn't want to assume. He notes you "can catch bigger fish with a speargun" (higher prices, easier conversions), but it's also easier to miss, so he advises starting generic and niching down later.

When competitors and "how are you different to X/Y/Z?" questions appeared, he niched down and raised prices:
- He changed the homepage tagline from "Find fast growing startups to do business with" to "Find new clients for your digital agency."
- He added testimonials from agencies that closed deals ($50k, $25k, $10k) and redesigned the landing page. His customers had collectively made more than he had from CyberLeads.
- Prices went from $29/month to $49/month to $99/month. "Conversions kept going up at the same time."
- After this, questions like "how are you different, and why are you more expensive?" dropped to zero.

He adds that there are no universal rules: staying generic or being the cheapest could be right in some cases. "The only rule is that there are no rules. It's about trying and seeing for yourself." Worst case, you revert.

## Choosing a niche from customer behavior, not surveys

After a few months, his customers were a mix of SaaS companies, freelancers, consultants, agencies, journalists, investors and solopreneurs. He found surveys not very useful ("I had been burned many times asking for peoples' opinion") and observed behavior instead: who pays the most and stays the longest.

Agencies won every category:
- Largest customer segment
- Willing to pay the most
- Stayed the longest
- Best results (one closed a $50k deal)
- Busy founders who never bothered him

He connected this to a podcast idea: sell to someone's number one pain point, not number two or three, which moves a product from "nice to have" to "must have" and makes paying easy. A poll showed finding new clients is agencies' number one pain point; SaaS companies and others didn't share it.

He had hoped freelancers and consultants would win, since one client could transform their lives, but they didn't want it enough to pay agency prices. Lesson from past projects: people with epilepsy wouldn't pay $10/month for Epilepsy Blocker even though he felt they needed it, while people found and paid $10/month for GitGardener on their own. "Maybe it's not about who 'needs' your product, after all. Maybe it's about who 'wants' your product."

## One small niche feature beats big differentiation

Months after the tagline change and price increases, the only differences from competitors were homepage copy and testimonials. Then he added a single feature for agencies: an extra column in the monthly list called "Likely to outsource to agencies" with values like "Design", "Development", "Marketing". He filled it in manually, one startup at a time, and made it obvious on the homepage. People loved it.

His takeaway on competition:
- You don't need to be crazy different at first. Tweak the copy, niche down, maybe build one feature.
- Most customers weren't comparing him to competitors; many didn't know they existed. His real competition was "their time, boredom and a spreadsheet."
- Competitors copied his site word for word, resold his lists at a third of the price, gave similar lists away free, ran ads against his name and leaked lists on Reddit. He still grew.
- His rule: "When competing, ignore everyone and focus only on yourself." He later muted copycats instead of spying on them.
- He argues sharing everything (including revenue) creates copycats, but the internet is usually big enough for another player and sharing grows you faster.

## Boutique positioning: turning weaknesses into strengths

In week one, at $300/month, a prospect asked for a phone call before committing to the $29/month plan. Alex was nervous because the lists were compiled by hand with no team, AI or systems. He recalled a talk saying to run an online business like a boutique, not a supermarket: few customers, high prices, exceptional service. He flipped it and owned it, pitching: exclusive, brand new leads, verified and handpicked one by one by a human, not outdated robot-collected lists thousands of companies spam.

The prospect said it sounded "too good to be true for $29/month" and subscribed. Alex's realization: low price can signal low quality, and to be a boutique he needed boutique prices.

He reframed his perceived weaknesses:
- A simple newsletter is better for some people than a bloated platform, and takes 10 seconds to set up.
- Not being a big company means leads are exclusive and convert better.
- Hand collection means leads are up to date and manually verified, "something no other platform can promise."
- Being a one-person team is a superpower for connecting with customers, especially other founders. "Don't pretend to be a company."

## Revenue focus: annual plans and a lifetime deal

Alex realized monthly recurring revenue was "a mirage": subscribers stay a few months, then cancel, unlike a salary. So he focused on revenue per customer.

His math: customers paid $49 or $99/month and stayed 2-4 months, so about $150 per customer on average. He added an annual plan at $299/year, which roughly doubles revenue per customer, collects it upfront, and feels like a steal to buyers. It was very popular.

Results: he passed his salary (about $2k/month at his day job), reaching around $3k/month from CyberLeads on top of $2k/month from the job.

Black Friday affiliate deal:
- A Twitter friend, Dru, was preparing deals for his community of 30,000+ founders. Alex pitched featuring CyberLeads and he agreed.
- Worried a discount would be "off brand" for an exclusive, expensive product, he offered a single $1k payment for lifetime access to the lists: still expensive, still a good deal.
- Dru took 30%.
- Result: more than 10 lifetime customers across November and December, over $10k revenue, and double his salary in profit in both months. Once he woke up to 2 lifetime customers overnight.

## Marketing focus, one channel, one or two metrics

Alex observed that competitors didn't quit because of a bad product but because of bad marketing, and he decided to put his limited time there:
- "No pretend work. No new features. No new marketing channels." Just post on Twitter, focus on revenue, ignore other metrics ("athlete mode").
- Twitter was volatile but consistent: some days zero traffic, other days viral with thousands of visitors and many customers.

His related principles:
- Prioritize marketing over product: most startups die from lack of traction, not lack of product-market fit.
- Stick to one distribution channel; better to be great at one than average everywhere.
- Track one or two metrics rather than turning all the screws a little.
- Eliminate "pretend work": two hours on the right things beats twelve on the wrong ones.
- Volatile systems that consistently work beat stable systems that are dead.
- Don't be a perfectionist when building; it won't matter to users.
- After finding success, double down on what you have instead of launching new things.
- Share ideas early; people won't steal them and feedback improves them.
- Niching lets you be the best in a segment and take advantage of power laws.
- Exposure beats prediction: "It has been easier to become ten times luckier by increasing my exposure to the world and shots on target, than it has been to become ten times better at understanding and predicting the world."

## Handling the pressure of fast growth

Going viral brought severe anxiety at first. Alex got personally attacked on Hacker News for running a lead generation business (and for GitGardener), and refreshed the thread all night. Days later the crowd had moved on to attacking another company. His conclusion: "I may be the hero of my own movie, but in everyone else's I am nothing but an extra." That freed him to fail publicly and double down.

His coping habits:
- Control inputs, not outcomes: he cites Epictetus's Enchiridion (some things are up to you, others aren't). He can't make someone subscribe or a competitor quit, but can focus on inputs.
- Daily exercise (especially running) as a deliberate dose of stress that makes the rest of life feel easier.
- Comfort zones expand: the first viral moment cost him sleep, but after three in a row he "slept like a baby." Podcast interviews went from a one-hour pre-run to a friendly chat.
- Reframe fear as excitement, a trick from fighting: the pre-fight heart racing and dry mouth feel like excitement once warmed up, so say "I'm so excited" like you mean it.
- Nuance only comes from real-world trials, not books or practice. He learned this in fighting: after moving from a technique-only gym to a hard-sparring one, beginners with real-world reps beat him. Progress is slow, then arrives in breakthroughs.

He ends the book about to quit his job and go full time on CyberLeads, estimating the market could support $1M/year and that "I am the actual ceiling of my business."
