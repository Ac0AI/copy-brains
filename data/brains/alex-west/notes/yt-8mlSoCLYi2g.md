## Boring business vs. cool business

- Alex built about 20 "cool" products (e.g. a tool for people with epilepsy) that sounded great at parties and earned $100-$200/month. He then had to take a full-time job.
- He flipped his priorities: "change my life mostly rather than the world." His reasoning is that nobody cares about you beyond a "wow" that is forgotten in five seconds, so build for your own lifestyle.
- He is "so happy to be running a simple, quote unquote boring business" and warns against shiny object syndrome.
- Cyber Leads (cyber leads) is his business: a monthly list of companies that raised funding in the past month and are likely to outsource a service (design, cybersecurity, etc.), with decision-maker emails. He hit about $300k annual revenue as a solo founder.
- His biggest earner is a done-for-you service on top of the list. He sends the cold emails and books meetings on the customer's behalf, because many people don't know how to use cold email properly.
- He is not taking new service customers at the moment (waitlist).
- Being featured on the My First Million podcast brought newsletter subscribers and service leads, "but nothing insane."

## Simplicity as a differentiator

- The newsletter is almost exactly what it was two and a half years ago. He swings between "feeling like an idiot and a genius because it's so simple."
- Competing databases and platforms hold outdated or wrong data, lack decision makers, and make users log in and build their own queries.
- His edge: "use simplicity as a differentiator." People don't have time to learn your product or decode a clever landing page.
- Data quality is manual: he cross-checks every funded company by hand so there are no mistakes.
- The "likely to outsource" label combines statistics with manual judgment:
  - A 50-person company with one or zero designers, or one that has used agencies before, fits a design studio.
  - A company storing medical information whose website says "trust" five times fits a cybersecurity agency.
- He criticizes early-stage startups with five people who copy Enterprise-style landing pages where "you don't understand anything." Enterprise sales teams can get away with that because they run live demos.
- Gumroad is his positive example: the same product for close to 10 years (excluding last year's rebrand). "Adding things is very easy but keeping things simple and being focused and grounded... is difficult."

## B2B vs. B2C, and why high ticket helps

- He moved to B2B because most solo founders who made it were doing B2B.
- He noticed his own behavior: in business he will pay $1,000/month without thinking if it might save 10 hours or make $10,000. People balk at a 99-cent app for personal use.
- Contrast in his own numbers:
  - In B2C he struggled to sell $5/month subscriptions with churn. Reaching $1K/month needed 200 customers.
  - With Cyber Leads, 200 customers "could be like a million per year."
- Exception: B2C education sells well because people spend heavily on education.
- The done-for-you service costs $2K/month, so $1M/year needs 40 customers worldwide. With numbers that small, he says there is no point worrying about competition.

## Copycats and competition

- He does not worry about copycats at all. People did similar things before him and will after. "There's enough room for everyone as long as they don't overlap."
- Overlap would mean the same product, wording, target customer and acquisition channel, which he calls "plain stupidity" in a space as large as the internet.
- He notes that 99% of people quit, as he quit his own 19 products, so most copycats fade.
- Early on he wrongly believed an idea had to be unique and dropped ideas that were already taken.
- Going high ticket makes the customer count needed so small that competitors matter less. For the newsletter he worried slightly more about price erosion (everyone selling at $10/month instead of $100-$200).
- Affiliate program: it sat on his homepage "just in case" and only started taking off after the My First Million mention.

## Finding the first customers and one distribution channel

- He launched on Product Hunt at $29/month and got roughly 5-10 customers. Then came the "Finding Nemo" moment: "okay, now what?"
- His principle: have one product people will pay for and one distribution channel you can use "again and again and again." Product Hunt is not repeatable daily.
- He tested LinkedIn cold outreach, sponsored blog posts, Quora, Reddit, Twitter, SEO and his own network.
- Twitter worked: a tweet he posted with no intention of going viral brought five customers.
- Traffic today: Twitter is the all-time revenue leader; lately word of mouth is picking up even more, which surprises him. He notes word of mouth may partly be Twitter indirectly. Twitter works well if your niche is tech or business.
- His pattern: about 20 products to find one that took off, many channels to find Twitter, and about 20 experiments inside Cyber Leads (e.g. turning it into a database, building an agency network) before finding the done-for-you service.

## Artist to athlete, and one project vs. many

- Early on, be an artist: try new things, stay open-minded, find a smart way to get customers. Once something works, "become an athlete": do the boring thing that took you from 0 to 10 customers repeatedly. He has done that for about two years.
- Keep experimenting inside the business, find the next lever, and then repeat that. He expects to run the done-for-you service for the next few years.
- On one project vs. many: both approaches have worked for different people.
  - 2018: he built 13-14 products in a year and went from zero to about $100/month, later about $500/month.
  - 2019: he fell in love with one sexy product and stuck with it for a whole year, and it went nowhere. He admits he can't know if sticking longer would have worked.
- His conclusion: you need early traction to justify sticking with something, and trying many things is how you get that early traction. Then stick with it for a long time, even without a hockey-stick trajectory.

## Mindset and lifestyle

- His book recommendations are the Stoics (Epictetus, Seneca, Marcus Aurelius): practical philosophy that counters an internet that feels like a "positive sum game"-style comparison trap.
- His own weakness is anxiety, not shiny object syndrome or perfectionism. His goal is to run a tech business, competing with the whole world, while staying as calm as possible. He reminds himself of this when he sees lead-generation companies making $100 million a year.
- Travel: he did one month per place (Sicily, London, Cyprus, Greece, Paris and others) and found it unsustainable. You don't make friends or see the city while working. He now plans at least two to three months per place.
- He works from home or a neighborhood coffee shop, not coworking spaces.
