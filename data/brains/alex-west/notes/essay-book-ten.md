## Giving up possessions to find peace instead of clutter

Alex gave nearly everything away and went fully nomadic. He kept only a laptop, phone and chargers, five white and five black t-shirts, two pairs of jeans, two pairs of shorts, a haircut and beard machine, and his documents. Clutter has always given him anxiety, and having little gives him peace of mind. He remembers being happier on a carpet on the floor than on any anatomical bed. Looking at his suitcase, he smiled at how little he needed. He also decided to do the things he always wanted, even trivial or childish ones: tattoos, laser eye surgery, training martial arts again. With money, time and low stress, he re-explored subjects school ruined for him (history, ancient Greek and Latin texts, microscopes, telescopes) and wants a small museum of ancient coins, meteorites and similar pieces someday.

## Why he drops the "rich businessman" image and the full circle fallacy

When he moved to Milan and started making good money, he adopted slicked-back hair, glasses, a briefcase, an expensive watch, business class and fancy restaurants. It never felt natural: "I always felt like a monkey dressed in clothes and clown shoes." He realized he had circled back to his high school self (buzz cut, baggy clothes, fighting, traveling).

He describes a repeated cycle: experiment with a million things, go overboard, then keep one or two that are worth keeping.
- Skincare: kept only a moisturizer and one soap.
- Biohacking: kept a sleep/heart-rate wearable and intuitive protein and calorie tracking.
- Workouts: ended at powerlifting and Muay Thai, which he already loved and which cover most bases, so he stays consistent.
- Diet: a few supplements and a few boring balanced recipes.
- Clothes: simplest clothes from one Japanese brand with good fabric.
- CyberLeads: out of everything tried over the years, the only thing added and kept was the service.

It feels like wasted time, but the circle is not perfect. He likens it to the Shotgun to Sniper strategy, applied to life.

## Boring business, exciting life

Alex loves the setup of a boring business and an exciting life. He prefers working 2 hours a day on a boring business, making a ton of money and enjoying life, over the opposite. He knows because he has done the opposite: cool projects like Epilepsy Blocker made no money and left no free time.
- He can keep doing something forever if he can attach a mission to it and see progress over time, like adding even 1kg to the bar in powerlifting.
- His main charity is boring too: bednets against Malaria. They are the best at it, among the most cost effective charities, statistically preventing one death (usually a child's) per $5,000 spent, and have prevented millions of deaths.
- At a rooftop social event he left a table when people talked software, AI and business. After years of imagining admiring conversations about his business, he rarely talks about it now, sometimes says only that he runs a tech business and changes the subject, and wonders whether business is boring once solved.

## Disconnecting from the phone, news and unvetted information

Alex argues most online content is fake or exaggerated: fake news, fake revenue numbers, fake fitness and business advice, fake connections.
- Learning: apps got him nowhere in Italian for months. Learning the most common 1000 words and practicing with friends and his Italian girlfriend made him fluent enough for a 2h conversation inside 3 months.
- Dating apps: he deleted them in Colombia, because they were a leading way people got kidnapped, robbed or killed. "Ironically, it was the best thing I ever did for my dating life."
- Comparison: a travel video of someone in Bangkok doing Muay Thai, dating and making friends made him feel like he was wasting his life, though he was also in Bangkok, training Muay Thai and sleeping next to a woman he was dating.
- Night runs: he used to run in silence and solve business and personal problems "like tidying my room, but for my mind." He had drifted into running with podcasts, adding thoughts instead.
- Information overload: he hired an online personal trainer (a school friend) because too much information left him unsure what to eat or how to train. He wanted someone to say "these are the only 3 things that matter." He concludes knowledge is subtractive and would rather grow up with a few shelves of vetted books than unlimited unvetted information.

What he did: threw his airpods away, stopped following the news, downgraded his phone and deleted everything except navigation, messaging and calling. "I never missed anything. And I never felt calmer." Earlier he deleted YouTube from his phone and blocked it on his computer, "the best thing I've ever done for my productivity." He also stopped stressing about capturing moments; the best moments are never on camera.

## AI sycophancy, echo chambers and the need for real feedback

His brother's friend believed he had a mathematical theory explaining the whole universe, would not show it to a professor or forum for fear of theft, and talked only to AI that kept assuring him he was right. Alex admits he does the same: share work with AI and, depending on what he shares, he is "the second coming of Steve Jobs, Miyamoto Musashi or Fyodor Dostoevsky."

- He has seen long-term solo, online travelers become "insanely eccentric and weird" from confirming beliefs through echo chambers, sycophantic AI, superficial friends and bot-filled comments.
- It also affects people who live alone with no real friends or family to observe them, successful people surrounded by yes men, and people cut off from nature or the free market, "forces that don't care about your feelings and give you real feedback."
- He worries about this for himself.

## Books, old books and the limits of knowledge

Alex weighs books against scrolling. Arguments for books:
- Effort is a filter: months or decades of work versus minutes.
- They are purer, because the selling is over by the time you buy.
- They are more intentional, since you pick the topic and teacher.
- Reading is less passive: you highlight, take notes and form your own realizations.
- Range: thoughts from the past 3,000 years.
- Retention: he remembers books from decades ago but almost nothing from videos, articles and tweets.
- Repetition can change a life: re-reading one idea through different examples, finger tracking the line, can engrave it deeply. "Even a single strong idea can literally change your life."

But books are not perfect or real life, and a best seller can implant a wrong idea. He trusts older books more because of the Lindy effect: if people have copied, translated and read something for hundreds or thousands of years, there is probably a reason. Terrible books existed in ancient Rome too; he and a friend keep the tradition of buying each other terrible self-help books as a prank.

His London bookstore test: he bought three books on travel that were 10, 100 and about 3,000 years old. The 10-year-old, by a life coach, was a collection of client case studies and he could not finish it. The 100-year-old, by an American explorer who died in 1939 crossing the Pacific on a raft, he loved. The third was the Odyssey.

## Why his own advice-writing sounded smart but aged badly

While writing his books, Alex found it easy to support any argument and make it sound smart by naming frameworks, laws and philosophers (Parkinson's Law, Aristotle's Sophrosyne, Pareto, Occam's Razor, Skin in the Game, survivorship bias and so on) and drawing analogies, like comparing business scaling to physics scales or to civilization's virtues. Those takes "always aged like milk." Each time he returned to edit, they were wrong, simplistic, changed in his mind, or already said better by someone 2,000 years ago.

After writing daily for almost a decade, he concluded the only things that are true are the ones that reflect reality. So he writes the way he started: "Just say what you did. And what happened." He says real morals are actions, real intelligence is behavior, real philosophy is scars, and real teaching is leading by example; the rest is words. He is not here to teach or moralize, is sick of everyone prescribing the perfect routine, diet or business model, and thinks reading should be like hitting two rocks together until you get your own spark, not being spoon fed.

## What travel does: environment shapes you more than you think

- Alex returned to his hometown after years abroad and within weeks was his old self: waking late, anti-social, no drive, messed-up routine. "I hadn't changed. My environment had."
- He has a baseline character, personality and happiness, and can change, but not as much as he thought.
- He is strongly influenced by weather, home, routine, people, language and body chemistry: introvert or extrovert by setting, different on acne pills, different after weeks of bad sleep and food versus working out and sleeping well.
- Most people are in the middle on most traits, so they fluctuate with their environment.
- Travel forces a different environment on you, so you explore yourself, not just the world. At home you rarely meet new people or try new things; abroad you are forced to.
- By living in many climates, cities, houses and neighborhoods, trying hobbies and dating, he knows what fits him through experience, not guesses. A place alone won't make you happy, but it is far easier in a place you love, with people you love, doing what you love, and money helps by letting you create that.
- Experiences make time feel slow in memory; routine feels fast looking back because it leaves no memories.
- Travel also teaches your own country through contrast and can produce reverse culture shock.

## Places, advice and "little moments"

- No place is perfect. Big cities inspire him but make him feel broke; nature relaxes him but he gets bored and itches for a challenge; comfortable places change him little; culture-shock places stress him but change his perspective permanently.
- Experience of a place depends on luck, company and mood, so he avoids recommending places. He once confidently sent a friend to a Spanish city where he had great weather, friends and a great life; it went badly for the friend, then he sent him to the other side of the world, which also went badly.
- At a beach fire, a woman asked for his craziest story and he blanked. He had no robbery or arrest stories. He concluded his real travel is "a collection of tiny moments": eating alone at a restaurant, a neighbor practicing opera in Sicily, Friday pizza with flatmates in Milan, working in libraries, a stray dog at a cafe, a favorite table and route, a social circle.
- Big stories he could have told (scuba diving with sharks, sailing through a storm, sparring Muay Thai champions) sounded more dramatic than they felt.
- He tries to learn the language everywhere: it signals respect and effort, carries humor and culture, and "you are talking to the child within them."
- Seeing the world with a "kid's eye" came from a film-viewfinder camera in Vietnam; understanding how things work (why sunsets are red, how undersea cables carry the internet) makes them more magical, not less.

## Accepting a finite life and not maximizing everything

- He cites roughly 4,000 weeks of life expectancy and the cost of traveling: watching his parents age over video calls and missing small shared moments.
- Business skill carries over: "One of the most important meta skills in business is being ok with not finishing your TODOs." Sometimes the most productive thing is to stop and go to bed, or delete half the list to focus on the few important items, because work never ends and desires never end.
- He rejects the belief that everything happened for a reason or that he is the best possible version of himself. He probably could have made $10M with a different business, had more fun, been a better fighter, spent more time with family, or had more impact via charity. The point is not to maximize everything, which is impossible.
- His target: one great career and business that lets him live comfortably, one place that feels like home, one routine that makes him a happy version of himself, and one person to share life with.
- He is not enlightened: some days he is the grasshopper enjoying life, others the ant wanting money, status and fame. He still compares himself, feels behind, and falls off the horse; his life is "Sisyphean." What matters is being proud and happy with his current life.
