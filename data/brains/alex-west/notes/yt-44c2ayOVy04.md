## Hiring an agency on purpose to learn the client side

Alex, who runs a roughly $500k/year agency business and is aiming for $1M/year while traveling and working 2-4 hours a day, hired an agency deliberately to see his own industry from the buyer's seat. He had run an agency but never hired one. The experience produced a list of "intangibles" for running a service business, beyond the obvious need for leads and fulfillment. He kept notes daily and published them in a daily blog.

## Alex's four service business fundamentals

He lists these as the top intangibles after being a client himself:

1. Be proactive and push your client as if you were their boss.
2. Show progress and update them constantly.
3. Be friendly and transparent.
4. Have a strong fail-safe strategy.

## Lead the client: "you are their boss"

- Alex hired a video editor (short-form clips for social media). The premise of a done-for-you service is that it takes work off the client's plate, and "the last thing I want is extra work to do."
- He was sometimes late on deliverables. What helped was the editor following up and keeping him accountable.
- His old belief was that the person who hires you is your boss. He now says the reverse: clients come for your expertise, so you tell them what to do.
- He implemented this with his own clients and reports "amazing results"; they appreciate it.
- Most founders are busy, so chasing them for inputs and deadlines is part of the service.

## Communicate progress constantly, even when results are small

- Alex's view: "there is no such thing as over communicating" in a service business.
- Update clients even when results haven't arrived. Example updates: "we sent 50 emails, got no replies, but I tweaked this and that," or "we got two subscribers last week, this week three" (a 50% increase that is really one subscriber).
- Visible progress is enough for many clients.
- He argues that a client who demands instant results and instant ROI, and won't accept progress, is probably not your dream client.
- Always state the next step. Every email reply should say "the next step is X," and the client must be clear on it.
- As a client, he was happy with even a tiny bit of progress plus transparency.

## Be friendly and transparent: both sides feel awkward

- Alex used to feel awkward being the person hired, as if the company were his boss and he had to navigate being paid by them.
- He realized the relationship is just as intricate from the client's side: they want to feel as comfortable as you do, because "we're all human at the end of the day."
- Tactics: vibe with clients, show you care about them and not just their money, and don't "churn and burn" them.
- Be transparent about progress whether it goes right or wrong. If results are bad, he says he is the first to tell the client he's unhappy with them and to try to make it up to them.

## Fail-safe strategy: send unhappy and lukewarm clients away happy

- Alex uses an MMA analogy: confidence in striking really comes from good defense, ground game and takedown defense. With strong defense you're not afraid to "let your hands fly."
- In a service business, defense means keeping clients happy even when you don't deliver results.
- Results can't be delivered for everyone: "if you could do that you're literally printing money for people." Agency testimonials come from a percentage of clients. Most clients get lukewarm results, a smaller share get good results, and another small share get bad results.
- Lukewarm and negative clients should still leave happy. Tools he names: a money-back guarantee, friendliness and transparency throughout, making it up to them, and leaving on goodwill.
- Payoff: clients with lukewarm results (some calls but no huge client closed, one client closed but still leaving) or even no results left happily and referred new clients to him. He calls service businesses "just so different to any type of business."

## Follow-ups: forever for warm leads, not for cold ones

- Observation from running hundreds of cold email campaigns: "the money is in the follow-ups" is true for warm leads, not cold leads.
- Cold leads typically reply to the first email. If they haven't replied after two or three emails, further follow-ups are just annoying and spamming.
- Once someone replies and is interested, or jumps on a call, the philosophy is to follow up forever. He says this has closed people years later.

## Time horizon and delayed gratification

- Alex thinks delayed gratification is over-praised as a virtue and the reality is more nuanced.
- He rejects both extremes: "live as if it's the last day of your life" and living for your deathbed.
- His middle-ground heuristic: live as if you'll die in 20 years. At 28, he assumes he'll die at about 50 and asks what he would do and what his goals would be.
- He notes he lives a lot in the future and loves setting and chasing goals, which gives him mission, focus and happiness. After a sales call with a 45-year-old pillow agency owner from Boston who also talked about what he wants by age 50, he concluded the future-focus never goes away: exciting, but also frustrating that it never changes.
