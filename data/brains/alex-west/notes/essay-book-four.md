## Quitting the day job at $4,000/month: why Alex left, with $50k runway and CyberLeads outearning his salary

- **Where he stood.** CyberLeads was Alex's 20th product; the first 19 failed. After about a year of CyberLeads, it had paid more than his salary six months in a row, some months double.
- **Journey recap.** Year 1: 10+ products, up to $100/month. Year 2: focused on one product, up to $200/month, not enough, so he took a full-time job. Year 3: launched CyberLeads while employed and passed his salary. Year 4: quit.
- **Core framing.** He had a cool job (MIT startup, NASA work, life-saving medical devices, good pay) and says "it was just another job."

## Decision rule: quit when the numbers are consistent, not when the business is ideal

- Alex lists what he dislikes about CyberLeads:
  - Twitter is the main channel, which is unstable.
  - High-churn market; customers don't stick around.
  - Revenue is wild and fluctuates, unlike a salary.
  - Growth is not smooth.
  - People compete with him constantly.
- His ideal is predictable systems, very low churn and Google-driven customers on autopilot, but "we don't live in a perfect world. I have to adjust my sails to the wind. Not try to change the weather."
- He says "I cannot argue with the numbers and the facts": the business had grown consistently despite being messy.
- On timing: he predicted that when the time is right, leaving "won't even be a dilemma anymore," and that is how it felt. Staying another year to save an extra $10-20k "will change nothing. It will probably just keep me back." Sometimes you "say 'fuck it' and go for it."

## Risk framework: be conservative with ruinous risks, wild with capped-downside risks

- Alex took this idea from a book. Two categories of risk:
  - **Ruinous risks** (large loan from criminals, robbing a bank, all your money on a new crypto coin): be super conservative. Take enough and you pay for it.
  - **Capped-downside, big-upside risks** (starting a business with zero capital, moving countries, a new hobby, going to a party, tweeting): be wild, experimental, playful. Take enough and you get "blessed with luck."
- His tweeting example: best case you get customers, an audience and friends; worst case "there is none!"
- He asks which category quitting falls into, then answers by engineering the downside (next section).

## Stacking the odds: runway math before quitting

- He did not leave the moment he could. He stayed to honor his contract and keep saving.
- Savings of almost $50k:
  - $10k from his Greek grandfather
  - $10k from the day job
  - $30k from CyberLeads
- That equals about 2 years of runway even if revenue dropped to $0 the next day.
- Outcomes he weighed:
  - Success: freedom, scaling CyberLeads, travel, more savings.
  - CyberLeads goes to zero: two years to figure things out.
  - Fail completely: find another job and try again, "with a cool story to tell."
- His verdict: "That's a good bet. I'll take it."

## Why a day job stops being worth it once the side project works

- **Dream jobs don't exist.** He feared the job would be so good he'd forget his side projects; it turned out to be just another job.
- **He fell out of love with engineering.** He realized he loved creating, not coding, and software was just his tool. Eight hours a day of code made him sick of it, which is why much of CyberLeads is built without code. He enjoyed product talk (competition, positioning, features to add or remove, funding, pivots) over scalability, legacy code and sharding.
- **The learning curve flattened in 2-3 months.** What he took away was intangible:
  - Companies are slow, so competing with them isn't scary.
  - Priorities flipped: change your own world first (make money, escape the rat race), then worry about changing others'.
  - You don't need to work all day; he worked about two hours a day and made them count.
- **He was spinning his wheels.** Focusing on the job slowed CyberLeads' growth, and focusing on CyberLeads dipped his job performance. "It was impossible to do great work, at both, at the same time."
- **The "can't unsee it" moment.** When CyberLeads revenue passed his salary he posted it online, the post blew up and revenue grew further. A commenter told him: "You'll never be able to go back now. It's one of those things that once you see it, you can never unsee it again." After that, every meeting and review felt different. When his manager suggested he become "the respected, go-to guy for the 'X company dashboard'," he saw the contrast clearly.

## Quitting for clarity of mind, not for more hours

- His main reason was to stop thinking about irrelevant things (meetings, sprint deadlines, what his manager thinks).
- He does not plan to work ten hours a day on CyberLeads. He recalls working 10 hours a day on the wrong things in Greece and going nowhere, versus 2 hours a day on the right things in his job year, which changed his life.
- "I don't want more time. I want clarity of mind to make sure that I'm rowing in the right direction."
- He also wanted a big change, like moving from Greece to Italy a year earlier. Small habits matter, but "sometimes you are not looking for a small incremental change, but for a big earthquake that will shake your foundations," and that is when you have the best chance of exponential growth. He compares it to conquistadors burning their ships.

## The exit: how the notice period went and the lessons from it

- **Notice.** He gave 1 month's notice (4 weeks left) by not renewing his contract at the start of the new year. Legally he could have given none, but wanted a clear conscience.
- **After notice.** Meetings felt meaningless. He used them to work on CyberLeads or to write; the book itself was written during those meetings. His daily updates shrank back to one sentence, while teammates gave jargon-heavy updates. His take: the complexity is by design, to justify salaries and positions and look like "a genius at work."
- **The dark side.** Had he joined a company before building products of his own, everything (engineers, designers, marketers, salespeople, a CTO, CEO, CFO, funding) might have seemed too complicated to start. He says that is a trap for first-time builders.
- **The firing.** His manager refused to extend him 3 more months, and then the company fired him 1 day before his last day, citing bad performance in his final 2 weeks. His accountant warned that being fired could cost him tax benefits for his new business.
- **The money.** Salary cuts from the pandemic were to be repaid at the start of the new year. The company did not want to pay him that money (about $1,500) as he left, and offered to avoid firing him only if he signed away the money. He accepted, reasoning it was "just a speeding ticket" and "not every fight is worth fighting."
- **Outcome.** A few months later the company paid him the owed amount without explanation. His guess: fear of being called out on Twitter, a change of mind, or an accident.

## What came next

- To-do list after quitting: incorporate the business, move to a different city, design a new lifestyle.
- He had no idea if CyberLeads would keep growing or how he would handle "the weight of having zero constraints," but says he had "never been more excited."
