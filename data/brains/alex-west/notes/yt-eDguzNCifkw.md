## Starting without money, then choosing markets first

- Alex studied computer science and joined a startup accelerator, where he saw many founders talking about raising money while none were making a dollar. That felt backwards to him: "businesses run on profits and not on valuations."
- Videos from Peter Levels and DHH convinced him he didn't need to be a billionaire and could be "a humble little millionaire." He has worked on this full-time-in-mind since 1 January 2018.
- He built 19 products (17 at one earlier point he recalls) that never got past roughly $100 per month, which wasn't enough to live on in Greece. They were "cool" products, the kind you can mention at a party, but he says cool products usually don't make money and boring ones do.
- Working a first real 9-to-5 (leaving in the dark, returning in the dark, in winter) pushed him to pick something boring that makes money. His logic: change your own world first, then worry about the rest of the world.

## Market-first idea selection (the three-part filter)

Instead of going idea, then product, then hunting for buyers, Alex went market first: pick healthy markets and look for ideas inside them. A healthy market has three traits:

1. **Many players all making money.** Not one big incumbent (his example of a bad market is trying to build a search engine). The earnings can be small; even $2k per month per player is fine.
2. **Something you can actually build.**
3. **Something you can promote.** It's not just about building; it's about getting customers "again and again and again."

He had two such markets in mind, cyber security and lead generation. On cybersecurity subreddits (around early 2020) he saw a post asking for lists of companies that had raised money, with many commenters begging for the data. Looking closer, he found many sellers of the same data in different packaging: a platform with exports, a business-intelligence tool for recently funded startups, a report, a newsletter. They sold to journalists, companies and sales teams.

## Launching with no code

- He had a full-time job and was falling out of love with coding, so he picked the format needing no code: a newsletter, which he could launch in a day without building anything.
- That became Cyber Leads, a solo business now at about $300k per year (over $30k per month at the time of the interview; he mentions roughly $3.5k MRR the previous year).
- He argues everyone should start with no code, even if they can program: "we're not building rockets, we're building websites." Nobody visiting a site asks what it was built with.
- His analogy: arguing about programming languages and frameworks is "painters arguing about brushes." What matters is making something people value.
- He moved to code about two years later, once full-time, only because some features couldn't be done with no-code tools. Making money is "a nice problem to have" after validating with no code.

## Finding a channel after launch: try everything, then go all-in

- Most founders have a short time horizon: build, launch on one platform, expect virality. Alex says that's wrong because you need customers repeatedly, every week; you can't relaunch on Product Hunt daily.
- After launching on Product Hunt he had a "now what?" moment. He got a few customers (going from the $100-200 range to about $200 per month in a day), then churn pulled him down toward $100 and below $80.
- He tried LinkedIn, cold email, Reddit, Quora, reaching out to YouTube influencers, Twitter and more. He followed the book *Traction* by Gabriel Weinberg and JJ Mares, whose advice he summarizes as: try everything, and when one thing works, drop the rest and focus on it "one thousand percent."
- A tweet went viral with no existing following and brought about 10 customers. He recognized that he couldn't go viral daily but Twitter was a place to "fish for customers."
- He dropped the other channels and focused on Twitter: writing blog posts, tweeting, selling the business through sharing the journey. A post later hit Hacker News and went viral, adding traction.
- He admits "it's not really good advice" to hope for virality; the transferable lesson is that you never know what will work, so test broadly and then commit.

## Writing every day as a business habit

- Alex started a daily writing practice at the end of 2019, after failing with his products and fearing he'd forget everything he'd learned. He wrote down the lessons from the products he'd built and compared it to "cleaning your room, but for your mind."
- He began with daily, weekly and monthly entries; now he does daily and monthly, skipping sometimes.
- Benefits he names: mental clarity, and a record he can reread years later to see what he was thinking.
- He keeps it going while learning stress management, communication and writing, which he sees as byproducts of building the business.

## Money, quality of life and "enough"

- His goal moved over time: first $500 per month (equal to his salary in Greece), then $800, now chasing $1M per year. He admits "i'm not proud of this," and calls it the hamster-wheel pattern of "when I have that I'll be happy."
- His definition of financial freedom: "living as if money does not exist," meaning not thinking about bills, rent or supermarket prices. He says he was already living that way the previous year, when he earned much less.
- He now sees the game as a vehicle for self-improvement rather than money: managing stress, working out, communicating, writing.
- He warns about optimizing the one quantifiable metric: "money is so quantifiable... it's a number," whereas quality of life and happiness are hard to measure, which is why people fixate on revenue.
- Taking on more work raised his income about six times versus the prior year but lowered his quality of life. He may downscale and says more is not always better.
- He agrees with Daniel Vassallo's approach (as relayed in the conversation): work toward a cushion, then take the risk, and once the business is established be selective about customers and be willing to leave money on the table to protect peace.
- Comparison trap: "money Twitter" and "money YouTube" show anomalies who were lucky or talented, giving a distorted view of reality. A visit to friends in Athens earning around $500 per month (what he made four years earlier) humbled him: even $1,000-$2,000 per month doing your own thing is success.
- Rapid-fire preferences: marketing over product ("thousand percent"), beaches over mountains, writing, curation over creation ("I don't believe in uniqueness"), books over podcasts.
