## Why competition is mostly irrelevant for small businesses

Alex's claim is that solopreneurs and indie hackers should stop worrying about competition. He scopes it with two caveats:

- **Size:** he means small businesses, from about $1K/month up to roughly $100K/month, topping out around $500K to $1M per year. He says he has no idea how to build a billion-dollar company.
- **Healthy market:** it is not winner-take-all, and he sees many players making the amount he wants (many at $1K/month, many at $10K/month, many at $1M/year). He has a separate video on how to identify a healthy market.

Markets he found when he looked about four years ago: lead generation, cybersecurity, hiring and recruiting, and social media management, posting and automations.

If both caveats hold, he says competition shouldn't be on your mind.

## Your customers don't know your competitors, and probably not you

- 99.9% of potential customers don't know you exist yet.
- He tells founders to check their analytics: a homepage may get around 1,000 to 10,000 visitors a month, which is "nothing" in a large market.
- Founders know every competitor; customers know none of them.
- His advice: instead of focusing on other people, spend the energy bringing more traffic to your own product.

## Companies are slow, so they are the least scary opposition

- He used to think a 50-person company meant competing against 50 people like him.
- After working inside a company, he concluded you are competing against "45 yes men and five people that are actually doing work".
- Those five move slowly: tasks sit in to-do columns, get blocked, get passed to other departments, requirements change, and meetings multiply. Changing a button color can take five meetings.
- A solo founder can pivot 180 degrees in a day. His example: change a product's positioning in 5 minutes without getting dressed.
- Employees have their own lives and aspirations, and it isn't their company, so they don't care as much. He says he was one of them.
- He is more afraid of someone like himself competing against him than of a big company.

## The four-part test for whether two products really compete

Alex defines competition as an overlap of four things. If any one differs, he says they aren't really competing:

1. **Product:** identical products alone aren't enough.
2. **Target customer:** the same SaaS sold to SaaS companies versus sold to agencies doesn't compete.
3. **Positioning and pricing:** the same product and customer can differ as a self-serve tool at $9/month versus a high-end, custom, onboarded, almost done-for-you offer at $999/month.
4. **Acquisition channel:** one competitor on Google and Facebook ads, another posting organically on Twitter and Reddit, are not competing.

His fishing metaphor: the market is a river. If someone fishes right next to you with the same bait and nets, tell them to go to the other side because there are plenty of fish. Fighting over the same spot is just "bashing heads", and it is rarely what actually happens.

## Your real competition is intangible

- Most of the time you aren't competing with other companies.
- For his product Cyber Leads, he says he competes with prospects hiring a VA or doing the work themselves.
- Others are "boredom and Excel sheets".
- His conclusion: there are many ways to escape competition, and even when all four factors overlap, there is enough room for everyone.

## Copycats: the midwit meme

Alex uses the midwit meme (a normal distribution with a dumb person on one end, a smart person on the other, and an overcomplicating average person in the middle) to sort the people who might copy you.

- **Low end:** these copycats appeared far more when he was at $1K to $4K MRR than now at $40K. They usually quit within about two months. They are too scared to copy a product making $40K/month once they see strong testimonials and his 40K Twitter followers. He says he gets almost no copycats now.
- **Middle:** these people overcomplicate things and want a unique angle, so copying is too simple for them. They usually don't copy.
- **High end:** copying is a smart strategy, and he might do it in his next business. These people tend to copy a much better business than yours.
- Even in the worst case where someone does copy you, the market is big enough for more players.
