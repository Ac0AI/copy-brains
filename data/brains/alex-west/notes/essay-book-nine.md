## Starting the service on the side, and why he never wanted a team

- Alex ran CyberLeads, a lead-generation service for clients, mostly solo at first. He never wanted to hire and become someone's boss, partly because he disliked every boss he ever had.
- He admits being solo felt "cool": a $1M solo business was rare, a $1M business with a team was normal.
- His first attempt was to build a "tribe" from girlfriend, best friend, childhood friends and siblings: "No suits. No politics. Just us." It went badly. His girlfriend was insulted by being paid, he and his sister disagreed on everything, and his best friend moved slowly.
- He describes the problem as dancing on "the thin lines between friends, colleagues, bosses and employees", and concluded the surface was too slippery.
- Lesson: he stopped hiring close loved ones and became "cold and strategic" instead. Later he reversed the guilt, deciding employees don't owe him anything and he should create an environment they don't want to leave.

## How he hired his first three people

- **Where:** he surveyed regions by role: South Africa for sales, Colombia and Brazil for US timezones, Egypt for finance, Eastern Europe for engineering, the Philippines for virtual assistants, India/Pakistan/Sri Lanka for cheapest. He chose the Philippines because his first delegations were simple.
- **Hire 1 (Nneka, virtual assistant):** messaged people on a hiring platform, one replied within 10 minutes, call the next morning, hired in about 12 hours. Her timezone was ahead of his, so tasks were done by the time he woke up.
- **Hire 2 (Joel, client management):** interviewed several, chose the final two, picked the one with relevant experience and an extremely warm personality.
- **Hire 3 (Rayvin, campaign copywriting):** posted a job with a small writing exercise. Of 100 submissions, 5 stood out, interviewed those, one clear winner. He needed no training: "I only needed to say things once."
- **Insight:** he hired Nneka not because she was the best in the world but because she was competent enough, a good person, reliable and replied fast. He applies the same to himself: to win clients he doesn't need to be the world's best at email marketing, just competent, reliable, trustworthy, caring and top of mind.
- **Pay it forward:** he shared the top applicants he couldn't hire with fellow business owners, as the CTO of Product Hunt had done for him.
- **Business is not a science, hiring included:** Nneka was hired on luck (the only person interviewed for the role), Joel mostly on character then trained, Rayvin mostly on skills, Jonathan via personal network. All worked out.

## What to delegate, automate, eliminate or keep

Before hiring he had three options for a task: eliminate, automate, or do it himself. Delegating became the fourth. His lessons (the "Goldilocks zone"):

- **Not everything that can be automated should be.** Some automations got complicated and broke constantly; a human was more reliable. Better: automate the first 90% and let a human finalize.
- **Not everything that can be strictly defined should be.** Over-strict instructions froze quality on tasks meant to keep improving, like writing client campaigns.
- **Don't delegate as fast as possible.** Only on the 3rd or 4th time doing a task did he sometimes realize it could be done completely differently, automated, or dropped.
- **Some things shouldn't be delegated.** Reviewing client and business numbers weekly by hand sparked ideas and fired him up. "The point is for you to lift the weights, not send a robot or employee to do it for you."
- **Delegate daily and weekly tasks, not quarterly, yearly or one-off ones.** The feedback loop is fast enough to correct and improve. Delegating personal errands (gifts, travel booking) failed: weird gifts, flying to the wrong city, booking a hostel he couldn't stay in. Personal stuff interferes with work.
- **Write text and image instructions, not video.** Documents can be edited and searched; videos can't.
- **Invisible automation compounds.** When a lead-forwarding automation broke, Nneka needed 1 hour to forward 3 leads manually; he took hours himself. He fixed it immediately: "invisible work from the past, paying dividends today."

## Instruction levels and management style

Instructions get more abstract the higher the role:

- **Tech automations:** extremely dumb and specific ("left click on the button that says 'Next'"); break if the button text changes.
- **Virtual assistant (Nneka):** equally explicit, but trusted to notice changes and update the screenshot and text.
- **Client management (Joel):** heuristics, examples and best practices for drafting reports and email replies. Even nuanced tasks can be broken into steps, just more abstract. Training took many hours of calls over months.
- **Copywriting (Rayvin):** "write good campaigns that generate leads" based on best practices and past successful campaigns.
- **Operator (Jonathan):** "run the day to day with the team, handle edge cases, take the sales calls and close clients."

Management style per person differs: Nneka works from a TODO list, Joel remembers things himself and prefers calls, Rayvin likes reminders and texting. Incentives differ too: Nneka prefers a higher stable salary, Joel chases bonuses, Rayvin wasn't moved even by a $1k bonus per closed client deal. "Everyone is different."

## Org design, pay and keeping the team happy

- **Pay structure:** base salary plus a monthly bonus per active paying client. He thanks great work with bonuses, even small ones.
- **Cold and pragmatic after "running a cult":** he caught himself designing travel budgets, gym memberships and retreats for his team, then realized he was forcing his own ideals (traveling, working 2 hours a day) on people with different lives. Nneka had just bought a car, Joel was opening a gym and raising kids.
- **Communication:** zero meetings, text only, tracked only whether tasks were completed. Employees could take on extra clients if it didn't interfere. He referred them to business owner friends.
- **Daily client report:** a report he reads in 10 seconds each morning showing next task, time with the client, performance numbers, closed deals, blockers, and emails with drafted replies. It replaced the stress of opening his inbox and wondering what he forgot.
- **Structure experiments:** (1) contractors execute tasks, he handles edge cases; (2) contractors handle their own edge cases; (3) hybrid, contractors do explicit tasks and an operator handles all edge cases. None is better, all have tradeoffs.
- **First all-hands meeting** felt corporate and he cringed at saying "aligned". He later said that stripping out corporate rituals is why he quit his job.

## Fixing the bottleneck: himself

- Even after delegating, he procrastinated on the creative, important tasks (crafting, reviewing and tweaking campaigns). A 10-minute campaign tweak took a week, he ignored Joel's reminders, and a client was lost because he was late launching campaigns. His fix was to hire Rayvin and remove himself.
- **Self-check while working:** "Am I iterating? Perfecting? Or procrastinating right now?"
  - Procrastinating: working on unimportant tasks, e.g. improving accounting processes while client campaigns got no results.
  - Perfecting: building a week-long automation instead of writing a quick instruction doc for an employee in a few hours.
  - Iterating: his most productive days, working on the most important tasks in a somewhat efficient, inelegant way.
- **"Next day version":** almost everything has a version you can launch or hand off tomorrow.
- **Context switching** between systems, marketing, client fires and sales was killing him; separating roles fixed it.

## Client experience lessons from being a client

After hiring an agency himself, Alex noticed:

- He didn't need them to be the best, only trustworthy and visibly caring and trying.
- He wanted them to act like a co-founder or boss: say exactly what they need, by when, remind him if late, drag him on a call, push back on bad ideas.
- A short message with a new idea for the future made him feel safer than a good or bad week of results.
- Soft skills beat tech: copying a London bartender who remade a late cocktail from scratch, apologized and asked for feedback, he raised client satisfaction more with intangibles like this than with technical innovation or performance work.

## Hiring an operator and building "a machine"

- Dealing with daily fires took about 1 hour each morning, but the adrenaline dump killed his energy for the rest of the day.
- He hired Jonathan, a friend who previously ran a similar agency, as operator and closer. Pay: 10% of the profits of the service part of the business, for taking all sales calls and handling edge cases in fulfillment. Jonathan was better at sales calls than Alex: native English speaker, likeable extrovert who loved calls.
- **Entropy fighter:** order is one state, chaos is infinite states, so you constantly intervene. "It feels like a garden that starts turning into a jungle as soon as I turn my head... I need a full time gardener."
- **Machine tuning:** with Jonathan they eliminated unneeded weight, reduced drag by automating, and improved the "cockpit" with tools useful to everyone.
- **Allowing inefficiencies:** he could cut costs by thousands per month (one contractor, cheaper tools, full-time work), but savings would be less than 2 paying clients. Inefficiency is a shock absorber against someone getting sick or quitting, and chill is sustainable. His rule: stay above 80% margins and don't stress.
- **Numbers at that stage:** over $40k/month revenue, about 80% margins, most client campaigns hitting minimum benchmarks. Revenue didn't grow with the new structure and margins dropped, yet he felt he had a "real business."
- **Day-to-day:** he moved full time to marketing and systems, a weekly meeting with Jonathan reviewing every number, a morning client report glance, and only Friday sales calls previously.

## Sales, marketing and results are not a science

- **Sales:** some buyers are allergic to bullshit, others want to be lied to. A prospect asked him to guarantee a six-figure deal like the testimonials; he answered that if he could 110% guarantee $250,000 in 3 months he'd charge $60k, not $6k, and pointed to the money-back guarantee. He lost the deal.
- **Marketing:** identical campaigns (same words, offer, target, market) for different clients produced wildly different results. Marketing depends on the messenger and uncontrollable factors. He doesn't take friends as clients because he can't guarantee results.
- **Honest statistics:** on aggregate clients made far more than CyberLeads did and the average client multiple times their money back, but the median client didn't. "Whenever you hear someone talk about total numbers, assume that they are hiding the average. And whenever you hear someone talk about the average, assume that they are hiding the median."
- **Principle:** "business is more like jazz." You do it your own way and jam.
