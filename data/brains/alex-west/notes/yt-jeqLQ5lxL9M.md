## Picking a market before an idea (Channel, then Market, then Idea)

Alex reached his 20th product, Cyber Leads, by reversing the usual order. Up to 2020 he went idea, then market, then acquisition channel. The reverse worked better.

- **Channel first.** He asked where he already hangs out and can reach. That was Twitter (about 600 followers then) and Product Hunt, mostly tech.
- **Market second.** He wanted B2B after being burned "chasing people for $5 a month invoices" and haggling over discounts.
- **Idea third.** Action beats theorizing: "action is way more important than pontificating and theorizing."
- His test for a good market:
  1. Not winner-take-all (a search engine is, since "it's very difficult to overthrow the king").
  2. Buildable solo, with a low barrier to entry.
  3. Something he enjoys and knows, with multiple players visibly making millions.
- He found three such markets: cybersecurity, lead generation (Cyber Leads) and recruiting.
- He credits Justin from Transistor FM, on an Indie Hackers podcast, for the market-first idea. He says the market you're in often matters more than effort. His blog post "Two Years of Building Products" reviews 15 products to show this.

## Early traction: launch everywhere, then find one channel

- Scale is relative: "if you're at zero users, scaling means getting your first user."
- Launch everywhere: Twitter, Reddit, friends and family, warm inbound and outbound.
- His Product Hunt launch gave him 10 B2B customers. It was the first time he saw a business email sign up.
- Launches don't repeat: "if we could launch on Product Hunt every day we would all be rich." After launch you have to build a channel.
- The hardest stretch was the months between launching and finding his channel. Revenue was falling and he feared failure number 20.
- Twitter then took off, along with a blog post that went viral on Hacker News. That got him his first 100 customers and moved him from hundreds to thousands of dollars a month. He then doubled down on building in the open.
- His rule: "one product and one channel, that's it."

## Staying simple and doing things by hand

- Cyber Leads is still essentially a newsletter and has not been bloated with features or integrations. He admires Trello and Bear for staying simple.
- He started on no-code, sent the newsletter and welcome emails manually, and over-engineered nothing.
- The business now makes about half a million a year and runs on a $7/month Heroku plan.
- Automation came later. Today clients pay, fill in a questionnaire, book a kickoff call, and tasks are created daily via Slack, Trello and Notion automations.
- Early on, don't plan for 10K or 100K a month: "go from zero to one user, that's a scale; one to ten, that's a scale." Setting up foundations for a 10x year is "procrastination."

## Time and prioritization

- Working a day job, he found about 80% of his real work happened before lunch, whether he worked 2 or 10 hours. That stayed true at $400, $4,000 and now.
- His mental model, from his "2.5 years" blog post: sit down for two hours as if someone with a gun to your head says you need a customer by the end of the week. Do what you'd do then, every day.
- Quitting his job on January 7, 2021 did not speed things up: "it takes the same amount of time to boil" no matter how many cooks. He was stuck around $4K/month for a year.

## The pivot to a done-for-you service (about $4K to $6K/month in a day)

- After a year at about $4K/month he ran 20 experiments inside Cyber Leads instead of selling or starting a new product. He points to the book Traction, co-written by DuckDuckGo's founder. He says you need "a really open mind" because you can't know what will work.
- A blog reader who had run a SaaS at roughly $1-2M ARR got on a Zoom coffee. He asked why people churn.
- Alex's cancellation form kept giving the same two reasons: they didn't know how to reach out and book meetings, or didn't have time. The reader replied: "why don't you do it for them?"
- Alex's first reaction was no: he wanted a lifestyle business, not clients or meetings. But after two years of making lists he knew what worked.
- In his next monthly list he offered to email prospects and book meetings for people, personally, for $2K/month.
  - 10 people said yes immediately.
  - One signed the next day.
  - Revenue went from $4K to $6K (about +50%) in a day.
- He says he was "terrified of that decision" but it was the right one and gave him the life he wanted. He wrote about it in "Four Years of Building Products" on alexwest.com.

## Customer feedback: watch payment, not opinions

- "I don't care what people think or tell me, I care what they pay for."
- He doesn't ask what to build. He makes an offer ("it costs 2K per month, are you in or out") and keeps what enough people pay for.
- He rarely iterates on the main product and keeps it simple by default.

## Idea generation, failure and motivation

- Early ideas are bad. As a student his were all B2C marketplaces because that was his life (e.g. an app to find coffee in your city).
- The fix is to start building, launching and failing fast. "Idea is like a muscle": he ended up with a list of ideas, with Cyber Leads at number 20 and seven more below it.
- Don't chase uniqueness. Cyber Leads entered a crowded market and became unique later: it now serves agencies and productized services by finding clients that just raised millions in funding. He says wanting a unique product was a crutch that cost him "many entrepreneurial years."
- Expect up to roughly 20 products, but not by launching random ones. Review what went right and wrong after each.
- "Artist to athlete": at first be the artist, trying ideas and channels to find one product and one channel. Then be the athlete and "do the same thing every single day."
- The hardest part is the beginning, when nothing works. It gets easy once a product grows past your salary and you have savings.
- It took him about 2 years and 20 tries. Others have done it faster.
