## Sniper vs shotgun: choosing by stage, and why Alex runs one product

Alex frames the question as two strategies. The sniper strategy focuses on one product until it works. The shotgun strategy builds many products to find one that works. He refuses to give advice ("I hate giving advice") because anything can work for some people and fail for others. Citing the idea that "ask someone successful for advice and they'll give you their lottery numbers", he only shows what he did.

His background: he started in 2015, took things seriously in 2018, and has posted online since 2018 and daily since 2020. He calls himself a decent case study because he is neither super smart nor super lucky, and he spent almost 10 years getting to where he is.

## Shotgun strategy: build, launch, learn, repeat

- **Pre-product-market fit:** Alex recommends shotgun for anyone without a product that can change their life or hit their income goal, especially if they have no paying users yet.
- **His run:** He started on 1 January 2018 as a New Year resolution. Within one year he went from never having a paying user to launching 8 products, 2 of which made money. That gave him his first dollar and first recurring revenue.
- **Total count:** Across 2018 and 2019 he built about 20 products. The 20th was Cyber Leads, found in early 2020, after roughly two years of building.
- **Why it works:** Each launch closes a feedback loop, so you reassess and go again. He says "we learn with our hands, getting burned by the fire", not with our intellect.
- **Lessons from specific launches:**
  - Launch 1: the domain he chased did not save him.
  - Launch 2: the perfectly polished button did not matter.
  - Launch 3: built in 2 weeks, deliberately scrappy, and it was his most successful launch.
- **Takeaway:** Knowledge is mostly subtractive, about what not to do: don't obsess over the name, design or backend. After 20 products he concluded all he needed was a landing page and a buy button. He knew this from day one but had to learn it empirically.

## Sniper strategy: too slow and too risky early on

- **His attempts:** He tried it from 2015 to 2017, when he wanted to be the next Mark Zuckerberg and raise money. All he learned in two years was that he did not want to raise money or build something grandiose. He wants to bootstrap a small business and be "a humble little millionaire."
- **Second attempt:** In 2019 he fell in love with a medical-related product, made it his life mission, and lost another year. It made only about $50 MRR (he says "if I remember correctly").
- **Verdict:** Sniper is too slow and too risky for learning. He notes it depends on your goal, and his ambitions are low: change his own life, not the world.
- **Compare:** In the shotgun years he learned enough to write a book, and he did write a blog post on it on his site ("two years").

## When to switch: shotgun before PMF, sniper after

- **Before product-market fit:** Use shotgun.
- **After finding the product:** Switch to sniper. When Alex kept shotgunning after launching Cyber Leads, he "spread himself thin and made less money" instead of growing it.
- **If you love building:** Focus on one product and express that side of yourself elsewhere. He says he has a solution for this, which leads into the fractal strategy below.

## The fractal strategy: shotgun inside the business to find distribution

- **Pattern:** Shotgun to find a product, sniper to grow it. When you plateau, run a shotgun again, but this time inside the business to find new distribution channels instead of new products.
- **Shiny object syndrome:** Funnel that energy into distribution experiments. If a channel works, go sniper on it, then repeat at the next plateau.
- **His examples with Cyber Leads:**
  - Mid 2020: discovered Twitter, focused on it for about 1.5 years.
  - 2021: stuck at around $4K, so he shotgunned every distribution and marketing channel he could think of.
  - The next lever of growth was adding a productized service on top of the business. After that he went to half a million a year. His advice: be very open-minded and "follow the money", even though it is unromantic.
  - He says he is doing it again now, and that this video is one of those experiments.
- **Book:** He recommends *Traction* (co-written by the DuckDuckGo co-founder; he warns there is another business book with the same title). Its thesis is to spend 50% of time on marketing and 50% on product, because distribution matters more than product. It covers roughly 17-19 channels. The loop is: try all channels, focus on the one that works, grow, and rerun at the plateau.

## Pace and iterations: 20 tries per level, about every two years

- **His anecdotal pattern (not a law):**
  - 20 products to find Cyber Leads.
  - 20 experiments within Cyber Leads to find the next lever of growth.
  - Another 20 iterations to remove himself from fulfillment.
  - Each level took about 2 years.
- **Pace varies:** Others can go faster or slower. What matters is to keep going, avoid burnout, and hold reasonable expectations.
- **Iterations over hours:** Paraphrasing Naval, it's "not the 10,000 hours that lead to mastery but the 10,000 iterations."
- **No sunk-cost entitlement:** "The universe doesn't owe you anything." Launching again and again does not mean you will hit. You must improve with every iteration. He admits he once felt something was bound to happen, which he calls a sunk cost fallacy.

## Is one product risky? Timeless or timely markets, and channels over products

- **When one product is safe:** If the product is either timeless or timely.
  - Timeless: something that does not change with time. Cyber Leads has run for 4 years, nearly 5, and he expects it to last 5-20 more.
  - Timely: a market like generative AI. Go full speed, maybe exit.
  - The risky middle: not timeless, not timely, and dependent on a platform (for example a marketplace plugin that the marketplace later builds in as a core feature).
- **Why he thinks Cyber Leads is timeless:** People will always want to make more money; agencies (companies paid to get things done) will always exist; email is a very "Lindy" technology; and the VC-backed market will still exist. He admits he could be wrong.
- **His goal:** He needs only one home run and does not care about doing it again.
- **Genetics of a business:** You can't pick your parents, but you can pick your business's genetics. Good genetics let you focus on one product.

## Statistics: one product with three channels beats three products on one channel

- **Thought experiment (from Nassim Taleb):** Compare three setups: (a) one product with three distribution channels, (b) three products on the same channel (e.g. all built on one Twitter personal brand, or all programmatic SEO), (c) three products with one different channel each. He argues (a) is best.
- **His own case:** After Elon bought Twitter, Alex worried because about 80% of his customers had come from Twitter. Word of mouth is now his number two source, but he excludes it because he can't control it. His three solid channels are Twitter, LinkedIn and cold email, plus a tiny bit of SEO he doesn't count.
- **Illustrative numbers:** Assume a 10% chance each of Twitter, LinkedIn and cold email failing within 5 years. The chance all three fail is multiplicative, about 0.1%. He sees this as safe enough because a bootstrapped business with about 90% margins, low overhead and cash in the bank only needs one channel to survive ("like cockroaches").
- **Why not three products on one channel:** If that single channel dies, all three businesses die, and you've spread yourself thin for the same safety. This is his stated reason for running one product, not several.
