## Quit when the results are consistent and the downside is capped

Alex quit his job at the end of 2020 (he handed in notice in December and was fired anyway). He had been building products since 2015, posted online daily since 2020 and tweeted since 2018. He frames himself as an average person with no special luck, and says these were his reasons, not the "correct" reasons for everyone.

- **Revenue beat his salary six months in a row.** CyberLeads, a newsletter of companies that just raised funding and are likely to outsource, launched on 14 Feb 2020. By quitting time it had made more than his salary for 6 months straight, even though it was only about 10 months old.
- **Revenue was wild but trending up.** He got customers mostly from Twitter, so some weeks brought none and a viral week brought 10-15. Monthly revenue swung like "1,000, then 500, then 2,000, then 1.5, then 4,000, then 2.5", but "up and to the right if you zoom out". His point: consistent growth matters more than smoothness.
- **Social media is a wild beast.** "You cannot put a saddle on a wild beast." Embrace its unpredictable viral nature instead of trying to change it.
- **The tipping point was the day his revenue passed his salary.** He posted it on Twitter, it went viral, and a commenter said he would "never be able to go back now". After that he showed up at work with more confidence and a limited-days mindset.

## Stack the odds: save a runway and keep the job until the bet is asymmetric

Alex did not quit the moment revenue crossed his salary. He rated the risk first and then reduced it.

- He kept the job to honor his contract and to keep saving.
- He set aside 50k, which was about 2 years of runway at roughly 2K/month of personal spending.
- His worst-case reasoning: if CyberLeads dropped to zero, he had 2 years to figure things out. If he still failed, he could "always just find another job and try again" and would at least have a story and have traveled.
- Best case: "If things go well I'm free for life."
- He says the exact savings number would not have changed his decision (50k vs 60k), since the business was already growing and paying his living costs. Today the same 50k would be about one year of runway because he spends more (roughly 10-20% of income).
- He admits no amount of reasoning predicts the future: "sometimes you just have to say [expletive] it and go for it". The time is never perfectly right.

## Take asymmetric risks, be conservative with ruinous ones

Alex draws on Nassim Taleb (he has a Black Swan tattoo) for how he sorts risk.

- **Be very careful with risks that can ruin you** if they go wrong: gambling, fighting, unprotected sex with a sex worker.
- **Be wild and playful with risks where the downside is small and the upside is large:** starting a business with zero capital, building in the open on Twitter, going to a party, moving abroad for a year.
- His own track record: he built about 20 products and one worked. Inside CyberLeads he ran about 20 experiments, most failed, one took it to the next level. He did not spend 20-40K to validate an experiment; experiments stayed cheap and playful.
- His view: take enough of these asymmetric risks and you "will be blessed with luck". The skill is checking whether each risk is asymmetric in your favor or against you. Quitting the job felt unclear until he built the runway.

## Warning signs the job is holding you back

These were the "push" reasons in his decision.

- **You stopped learning technically.** At his engineering job he mostly built dashboards, and the only lasting lessons were business ones. He realized he loved creating, not coding. CyberLeads v1 was built with no-code tools because he was sick of programming after 8 hours a day.
- **You are an entrepreneur, not a specialist.** He found scalability meetings, legacy code and Kubernetes clusters boring, and only enjoyed competition, product direction and funding talk. His analogy: some people are like Formula 1 engineers optimizing one car component; he likes high-level business.
- **Splitting energy makes you average at both.** After 2 hours of real work on his own business before the office, his brain was fried. He was a very average employee, and when he focused on the day job, CyberLeads suffered. "Very difficult for me to do great work at both at the same time."
- **Your manager's picture of your future is small.** His manager asked where he wanted to go and proposed being "the dedicated go-to guy" for the internal dashboard. He nearly laughed, and says he would have cried without CyberLeads.
- **A big change can beat small improvements.** If you feel stuck, do the opposite of what you are doing: change city, move abroad, "burn the ships". He had moved from Greece to Italy for the job a year earlier. Small habits matter, but sometimes you need an "earthquake that shakes your foundations".

## What the job taught him (business lessons that transferred)

- **Companies are not scary to compete with.** A 50-person company is really about 5 people and 45 "yes men" who drag tasks from todo to doing to blocked, hold meetings about them and change requirements. He says he was like that too, because pushing the company forward was not his job.
- **Change your own world first.** He worked on life-saving medical devices, a dream job for someone who wanted to change the world. He flipped that: go boring, go B2B, build something useful people pay for, make money, give to charity, and have impact that way. He is open that he wants to be rich rather than a starving artist.
- **Constraints reveal what matters.** Working two hours before his day job taught him that 80-90% of his useful work happens in the first two hours of the day, and he still works this way. When he was a student with the whole day free, he scrolled Twitter and the news, went to the gym, and got almost nothing done.

## Quit for clarity of mind, not for more hours

- His main reason for leaving was a clear head: no meetings, deadlines or worrying about his manager's view of his performance. It was not to work 10 hours a day on CyberLeads, and he says he didn't. He worked briefly all day in 2022, but was fairly relaxed when he quit in 2021.
- For software businesses he says about 2 hours a day is enough. "Taking a simple correct decision and scaling it is far more important than the number of hours you put in." A calm clear mind outperforms a team "running around like a headless chicken".
- Caveat: service or agency-style "hustle" businesses need more time. CyberLeads was a newsletter plus a done-for-you service on top, and the service side takes more hours.

## Downsides of his model he accepted

- **Copycats copied his product word for word,** including his founder story, because he published his numbers, strategies and lessons in his daily blog. He treats this as part of the cost of building in public and cannot do anything about it.
- **The market has high churn.** Lead generation is easy to sell at a high price but people leave quickly, which makes revenue volatile.
- **Revenue and growth were not smooth,** but the consistent upward trend was what counted for his decision.

## His recap: when to quit

- You have consistent results (income above your salary for months).
- You are not learning anything from your day job.
- You have enough runway if things go badly.
- The worst case is not dangerous and the best case changes your life.
- You are ready for a big change and want to feel alive again.
