## Ideas are underrated: finding a better idea beats executing 10x better

- Alex says most people think ideas mean nothing, and he agrees only if you can't execute. His point: "it's a lot easier to find a 10 times better idea than it is to become 10 times better at executing any idea."
- His own proof: five years before this video (2024) he had the same work ethic and skills and was stuck at about $200/month. At the time of recording he was making about half a million per year.
- He says the main difference is working on the right thing, "a good idea."
- His process for finding ideas: drop romance about passion, stop needing uniqueness, enter proven markets, sell B2B, and work from channel to market to idea.

## Don't build your business around your passion

- Alex used to want his business to be his "ultimate passion" and a natural extension of his personality. He now sees that as a product of designing his lifestyle "from a place of extreme comfort" as a university student with no stress.
- Passions tend to be poor business ideas. They often revolve around sports or B2C, which he calls a red flag.
- Passion can also scramble your incentives. His example was Epilepsy Blocker, a tool that helps people with photosensitive epilepsy browse the web safely (listed on his blog and Product Hunt).
  - He charged $5/month, but users often lived on benefits and had other disabilities, so he usually gave it away free.
  - One morning he read breaking news that a new therapy could potentially cure epilepsy. His first reaction was "why now, now that I'm building this business," and he caught himself.
- His takeaway: you can mix up your incentives so much that "you don't even recognize yourself," or even hate what you once loved. His advice is to be more coldblooded and stop trying to make the business your passion.

## Your idea doesn't need to be unique: look for a market where several people already make money

- Alex used to treat ideas like dibs and would drop one if it was "taken." That changed when he started a full-time job and shifted his priorities.
- His rule: if you search online and nobody else is doing your idea, that is a red flag, not a good sign.
- What he wants to see is multiple people (not just one) doing the thing and making money from it. He calls that a healthy market.
- Uniqueness comes later. When he entered lead generation (about four years before the video, when it was less saturated), he was not unique. His business became unique through accumulated choices:
  - He delivered leads as a newsletter rather than a database, a twist, though he wasn't the only or first one.
  - He focused specifically on agencies.
  - He added a product and a service on top.
  - He built it on his personal brand and a personal touch, including sending presents to every customer.
- His conclusion: "I entered the market and slowly it moved into something different." About two years after entering a healthy market without trying to be unique, he had a very unique business.

## Don't be afraid of competition, especially big companies

- Alex used to be terrified of competition, which he attributes to competing in Muay Thai and kickboxing, where one person always loses.
- He argues business has no winner and loser because winning is subjective. Example: if he makes $1M/year and someone in his market makes $10M with the same business, he isn't losing, because he doesn't want more and may be more content.
- On big companies: he used to think a 50-person company meant competing against 50 people like him. After briefly working at one, he concluded you are "really competing against five people and 45 yes men" who move slowly to avoid being fired.
  - Tasks bounce from to-do to doing to blocked to another department, and "we used to have five meetings to change the color of a button."
- He has many copycats. He isn't afraid of a big company trying to compete with him; he fears "a small five person company where they're coding all day and they're crazy motivated."

## Go B2B: businesses pay far more than consumers

- With Epilepsy Blocker he chased people for $5/month subscriptions, they asked for discounts, and he often gave it away free. He notes that reaching $5K/month would need about 1,000 concurrent paying subscribers.
- On the flip side, "you can charge 5K per month to a single business."
- A personal example: when he started taking his lead generation business (Cyber Leads) seriously, at about $300/month, he paid roughly $25/month for something for the business and realized he'd never do that for himself. He had even unsubscribed from BetterHelp at $200/month because he wasn't using it weekly, while business subscriptions run without a second thought "cuz it's a business expense."
- The mindset difference: the same CEO who won't pay $1 to download an app as a consumer will spend $10K/month on something as a business owner.
- Exceptions where B2C works, as far as he knows: education, self-improvement, and AI. He also wonders about healthcare but says it's hard to break into as an online business person.

## Work from channel to market to idea, not idea to market to channel

- Alex's old approach was idea, then find the market, then find the channel to reach it. He says it never worked for him, and top-down approaches are easy to mess up because of stacked assumptions.
- His rough illustration: with a generous 50% chance of being right at each step, idea times market times channel gives about 12% (and he thinks the real number is nearer 5%). He built 20 products to find the one that changed his life.
- Cyber Leads went in the reverse order: channel, market, idea.
  - Channel: a Twitter account with about 600 followers at the time.
  - Market: tech and business people, who are on Twitter.
  - Idea: then ask what those people want and find a healthy market you can reach through the channel you already have.

## Build a channel and personal brand, then stop thinking and build

- If you don't have a channel yet, Alex says to invest in one because it "is going to pay dividends." He admits he is biased but says building a personal brand is "never the wrong advice."
- It can be LinkedIn, Twitter, a big blog with SEO as the acquisition channel, or TikTok. Learn one channel really well and ideally build a personal brand on it, then use it to market your products. He says that is what he did and what he would advise someone he loves.
- A challenge to skeptics: "If the cost for you to make a million per year is to tweet once a day would you take it or not?" He can't guarantee it but says it's a good investment.
- Once you have an idea, stop analyzing: with a roughly 5% chance of being right, "don't lose time, go go go. Start building." He says all the thinking is pontificating, and the next video covers launching and validating the idea.
