## Why most solopreneurs fail, and the four phases from $0 to $1M/year

Alex (2018 solopreneur and indie hacker, building products since roughly 2016) frames this as his own opinion from experience. He splits the path into four phases, each with a different barrier. At the time of the video he was at about $43K in a month, with a goal of $83K per month (the $1M/year mark).

## Phase 1 ($0 to first dollar): not enough time

- First-dollar online is the biggest entrepreneurial filter.
- Alex says "you cannot expect to change your life in 6 months", it takes years.
- When he started, many people were more talented than him, got instant traction and went mini viral. He was building and nothing took off: no likes, no followers.
- Nine out of ten of those faster starters have since quit. He no longer sees them.
- His point: persistence over years beats early talent or early luck.

## Phase 1: bad ideas and bad markets

- His early ideas were social-network style ideas (for example an app to decide with friends where to go for a drink), because he was a university student and his life was social. They may be good ideas in general but they are not good business ideas.
- His definition of a good market:
  - Not winner-takes-all; multiple players win at once.
  - Those players make the amount of money you aspire to make.
  - You can use acquisition and distribution channels you already have (for example a Twitter account), and you know the ropes of that market.
- Bad ideas are unavoidable early: you have to build many products and watch some get no payment before you improve.
- On ideas: they are "everything and nothing at the same time". Nothing without execution, but he cites the Indie Hackers quote (he attributes it to Courtland) that it is very hard to execute 10x better yet easy to pick a 10x better idea.

## Phase 1: fear of exposure and fear of charging

- Fear of failing in public: people hold back because others might see them fail. In reality "no one cares", and even the people who quit are not remembered by anyone, which he calls liberating.
- Fear of charging: it is an identity issue. People who are not businesspeople, or do not spend money themselves, assume nobody will pay.
- His moment of realization: when Cyber Leads began growing, he typed his credit card number into a new software tool for his business. He would not have paid even $10/month for his own mental health, yet paid readily for business. Conclusion: B2B is not that hard to get people to pay, because business owners pay easily.
- His pricing comfort moves up with revenue: charging $500/month felt hard before and does not now, and he expects $10K/month to feel normal in some cases later.

## Phase 2 (hundreds to thousands per month): focus on distribution, not product

- Going from the hundreds to the thousands per month is the most life-changing leap, since it can let you make a living doing what you love.
- Common failure: with a product making roughly $200 to $500/month, people try to improve the product when they actually need more customers. If people already pay, it is probably good enough.
- His rule: spend about 90% of time on distribution and only maintain the core product.
- Keep the product as simple as possible and be proud of it, especially in a world of bloated products. Cyber Leads is "still the same damn product", simple.
- Second failure: launching new products when stuck. People end up with a portfolio of products each making $200 to $500/month, none life-changing, and collectively not enough to quit a job.
- His alternative: do not shy away from shiny object syndrome, but funnel that energy into building distribution instead of new products or features. (He covers this in more depth in his video on one versus multiple products for solopreneurs.)
- The shift: from artist to athlete.
  - Early on, you think out of the box, come up with ideas, ship an MVP.
  - At this stage, do the same thing every day, double down on marketing, focus on one product, and "be a little bit more boring".
- Reference point: at about $4K/month he was living his dream after quitting his job, and he says he would be about 80% as happy as now if still at that level.

## Phase 3 (thousands to tens of thousands): learn to delegate and re-explore

- Barrier one: wanting to stay solo forever. He hated every boss he had, so becoming someone's boss was hard. His reflection: you either die a hero or live long enough to become the villain.
- His setup: three virtual assistants working online, paid full-time, working about two hours a day on average. No meetings except one weekly team meeting. He sees them as people he works with, not employees.
- Delegation matters because going from thousands to tens of thousands often takes volume one person cannot handle alone.
- Barrier two: you often have to go back to square one, reconsider everything with an open mind, and use shiny object syndrome to find the next growth lever.
- His lever at each level:
  - $0 to $1: exposing himself and not being afraid to fail in public.
  - Hundreds to thousands: focusing on distribution, picking one channel, going B2B instead of building a unique product. He found Cyber Leads' channel on Twitter.
  - Thousands to tens of thousands: experimenting about 20 times with Cyber Leads and new offerings, then adding a done-for-you service on top of the newsletter, followed by building a team.

## Phase 4 (tens of thousands toward $1M/year): volume again, via the midwit meme

- At about $43K last month, he thinks the barrier now is sheer volume, after dropping everything else and focusing on one thing: "the 80/20 of the 80/20 of the 80/20", then "pouring gasoline on the fire".
- He uses the midwit (bell curve) meme to explain the stages:
  - Very early stage: volume. Launch multiple products and get in front of many people.
  - Middle stage: direction. Test several things, complicate your life a bit, and find where to point your vehicle.
  - Later stage: volume again.
- Example of too little volume: his best friend built a product for three months, posted on Reddit twice, got about 25 comments, DM'd five people and concluded no one wanted it. Total reach was about 45 people, and he expected 10 customers from that.
- Alex's comparison: across Twitter, LinkedIn, SEO and cold email he reaches roughly a million impressions a month and has fewer than 100 customers. So he says early stages need far more volume than most people try.
- He says he does not know how to get to $1M/month or $10M/month, but up to $1M/year he believes this is the roadmap.
