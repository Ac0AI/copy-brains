## Launching to learn, not to get rich

- Alex tells people to change the philosophy around launching: "you're not launching your idea to become a millionaire in 30 days or 90 days or whatever, you're launching to learn."
- He says you learn through "scars", not videos: close YouTube, build something, make mistakes.
- He treats scale as relative and dynamic. At zero users, success is getting one user. At one, it is ten. At ten customers, it is 20, then 30, 50, 100, 200.
- He argues against the master plan of "0 to 1,000 paying customers in 30 days", saying it "never works like that" and only about one launch in a million does.
- He ran about 20 products over two years, mostly "stupid little products" without real traction. The product that changed his life was built, launched and validated within two weeks, because earlier launches had taught him what to skip.

## Every launch should strip away something unnecessary

- Alex holds that "knowledge is subtractive, not additive" and "progress is removing things". Every launch should be simpler than the previous one, otherwise "you're doing something wrong".
- What he learned from successive launches:
  - Launch 1: the logo doesn't matter. He spent a whole day designing it.
  - Launch 2: the perfect .com domain doesn't matter. He still got zero users despite a great domain.
  - Launch 3: pixel-perfect buttons and gradients don't matter.
- He applies the same logic to a running business: he often removes automations, product offerings or features he spent a long time on, and counts that as progress.
- His simplest product ended up being his most successful.

## Validate with a landing page before writing code

- Alex once spent months coding a Stripe-for-crypto product, launched it, and got zero customers. He says he could have validated it with a landing page and a "get started" or "pay now" button, with nothing on the back end.
- He launched Cyber Leads, which started as a newsletter of companies that had just raised millions in funding (so they have money to pay for outsourcing), with only a landing page and a buy-now button.
- If someone bought, then on the 1st of the next month they received a list of every company that had just raised money and was looking to outsource.

## The MVP must be able to charge money

- Alex says you can launch with just a landing page, but "you should be able to ask for money".
- His reasoning: an MVP that can't make money isn't viable. "It's not viable minimum viable product if it doesn't make money."
- The rule: make the MVP as simple as possible, yet able to charge, then launch.
- He says validation doesn't take long, but "it's simple but it's not easy".

## One-to-one launch tactics (warm and cold outbound)

- Alex blocks a single day as launch day and keeps the process "simple, sweet, fast" because the goal is to learn fast.
- Warm outbound: DM, text or call friends who might be interested. Instead of asking "do you want to pay me?", you can ask "do you know anyone who might be interested?". They may pay themselves or refer someone.
- Cold outbound:
  - Email people who might be interested.
  - Email journalists if the product is fun enough to cover.
  - Email bloggers with a relevant post (for example a "top 10 whatever" list) and "shoot your shot".
- Example: a blog ranked for the perfect keyword for one of his small projects. He emailed the owner, who agreed it was relevant and added it. The post brought him customers "for years".

## One-to-many launch channels

- Alex describes these as the big shots. If one works, "the others are going to be a margin of error".
- Reddit:
  - Every niche has a relevant subreddit (even pet food has a massive one).
  - It is difficult: he usually gets banned and rarely gets love from it, but a hit there is massive.
- Product Hunt:
  - Mostly suited to tech products.
  - Quite easy to pull off even without an audience.
  - He suggests launching on weekends for less competition.
- Twitter and LinkedIn:
  - Post a story, not just "here's what I built".
  - Explain why you built it, how long it took, that it's your third project and the first two failed.
  - "Make it interesting, make it relatable, make it human."
- Facebook groups: similar to Reddit and tricky. He says he has never really cracked them.
- Then launch everywhere at once and see whether you get traffic and convert it into money. If you do, the idea is validated.

## After validating: focus on one product

- Alex's advice is to launch as many products as you can until one works, then focus entirely on that one.
- Once focused, he uses what he calls "shiny object syndrome" to find new distribution channels for that product and grow it.
- He says he covers one versus multiple businesses for solopreneurs in another video.
- He admits most viewers won't follow the advice to be scrappy, because "you have to learn by doing".
- He describes his main business as making about half a million per year, which he calls "good enough".
