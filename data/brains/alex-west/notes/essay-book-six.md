## Replacing the "second channel" idea with a service-first model at $6k/month (year 4)

Alex had spent a year treating Google as the safe second channel for CyberLeads and got almost nothing from it. By the end of year 4 he reached about $6,000/month, with most of the jump coming from a service he sold in a week.

He argues the lever he needed was hiding in his own customer data. His cancellation form kept saying the same thing: customers had no time to set up and send the outreach emails.

## Why the Google and database bets failed

- Alex spent roughly 12 months on Google. Result: "1 million impressions and tens of thousands of visitors" and only 5 customers.
- He notes that "sometimes I get 5 customers from one good tweet."
- Since quitting his job he had grown only about $1k/month. He thinks the same growth was reachable with "10 minutes per day to schedule a tweet."
- He calls the Google work "pretend work" and says his perfectionism disguised it as responsible risk reduction ("eliminating my final single point of failure").
- He also tried turning CyberLeads into a database SaaS. It took a month to build and ran for 2 weeks. The one customer who showed up wanted the newsletter's startup funding updates, hit a bug, and demanded a refund. Alex killed the database and concluded: "I'm so lucky to be running a newsletter."

## The shotgun approach: low-expectation experiments

Alex ran many experiments fast, with no attachment to any of them. Results:

- **Upsells:** A customer dashboard, older lists for purchase, annual-plan upgrades, custom lists (location, industry, funding stage) and a retention flow with bundles and discounts. No one bought lists or upgraded. Custom lists produced 1 sale in months. The cancellation flow didn't work either.
- **Affiliates:** Self-signup program with a 50/50 split. More than 100 partners signed up, only 4 brought customers, and only 1 brought more than 3. The emails, questions and manual monthly commission payouts were painful, so he killed it.
- **Lead magnets:** Free lists on Reddit and Twitter. Hundreds accessed them, almost no one bought. He calls it "a reverse filter" that attracted beginner freelancers with "a million questions, feedback and critiques. But no money."
- **Podcast:** Interviewing agency founders was easy to book but he disliked scheduling and prep and felt "like a radio host."
- **Going broad again:** He tested dropping the agency niche to triple his audience. Conversions "dropped to the floor," which confirmed the niche. He was relieved.

## Customer conversations turn up the lever

- Asking customers how they find clients showed that most agencies get clients through referrals, including from other agencies that partner and pay each other commissions.
- Alex now makes intros between agencies in his network as part of the subscription, and is waiting to see if it works.
- The real find came from a call with Vic, a reader who had bootstrapped a software company to over $1M/year. Vic asked why customers unsubscribe, then asked: "Then why don't you send the emails for them?"
- Vic said he was paying a company $2k/month to send emails for his startup.
- Alex's first reaction was that he only wanted scalable work that doesn't trade time for money. The idea stuck anyway.

## Testing the service offer cheaply

- Alex emailed his customers offering either a referral to an agency or sending the emails for them.
- 8 people replied. Usually a similar experiment gets about 1 reply "if I was lucky."
- One prospect wanted him specifically, citing years of following him, long customer tenure, email conversations, and a welcome gift he had sent. They trusted him.
- He quoted "a crazy price of $2k/month just to see what they would say." They accepted instantly.
- He closed two more clients at $1,500/month.
- Start dates were set for January (this was November), because clients didn't want to launch new marketing at year end.
- Pre-call, he rehearsed and paced. He treated impostor syndrome "like a compass": it meant he was pushing his limits.

## Redefining scale and software vs. service

- It took 4 years to reach $5k/month with software products and about 1 week to reach $5k/month with the service.
- He had believed scaling meant laying foundations for future explosion and infinite customers. Now he defines it as "simply taking your revenue to the next level. Just one step": if you're at zero, make $1; at $1, reach $100/month; and so on.
- He doesn't need infinite scale because "$1M/year has always been and will be end game."
- He had thought he must pick a lane, product or service. He now sees the "messy hybrid model" as possibly the answer.
- He also concedes he'd been working all day on his "passive income" business anyway, so trading time for money wasn't much worse.
- He doubled the business with a few manual emails and calls.

## Decision rule for a move that goes against his instincts

- Alex worried about running a service business, hiring, and being a boss (he disliked every boss he had). He asked everyone he trusts, and all gave the same answer: "Alex, just follow the fucking money."
- His framework was to imagine both outcomes. If it works, money worries are gone. Worst case: he hates the lifestyle, delivers no results, feels embarrassed, refunds everyone and goes back to the newsletter.
- Because there was a clear way out, he says he ran out of excuses.
- His personal rule, quoted from years earlier: "When in doubt, do the exact opposite of what you're doing."
- He still feared turning a perfect lifestyle business into "a monster."

## Perfectionism and reframing

- Alex says perfectionism keeps reappearing in disguise, including as "responsible" strategy.
- A friend whose father had died once asked whether Alex's complicated relationship with his father might be "just perfect." Alex's takeaway: he sometimes complains about things that could already be perfect.
- Late in the year he planned to relocate the business to Cyprus, after earlier moves to Italy and Sicily timed around tax deadlines.
