## Selling a productized outbound service: first-client reality and how results actually came

**The setup (year 5).** Alex launched a productized service on top of CyberLeads: he sends cold emails (and LinkedIn messages) on behalf of clients to help them close deals.
- Pricing: $2k/month, 3-month initial period.
- He signed 5 clients, then 10, because "I can't say no. I will figure this out."
- Early lessons: setup that should take 30 minutes per client turned into a full day. He picked the wrong tool for some clients and only found out after setting up everyone, wasting 2 weeks and hundreds of dollars.
- He works 3am to 8am sleep cycles at this stage.
- Taking risks on behalf of clients is a different kind of stress than selling a newsletter.

**What the first campaigns produced.**
- He followed books and guides on outbound to the letter. The first replies were all negative, sarcastic or rude.
- Failures: a client's friend got emailed and mocked him, one campaign went out with no subject line, someone threatened to sue a client, LinkedIn warned it would ban clients' accounts, and another client's emails were blacklisted.
- Zero positive replies for the first month, even though clients had paid thousands.
- Conclusion: "Following the experts was not working for me. So I decided to do the opposite and think for myself."

**The shotgun approach for clients.** Alex tested many variables and doubled down on whatever worked:
- Channels: LinkedIn, email, and combined flows.
- Openers: generic compliment, AI-generated specific compliment, or straight to the point.
- Subject lines: long, short, clickbait.
- Calls to action: ask for a call, or offer something free.
- Proof: a case study, name-dropping big clients, founder credibility, or nothing.
- Angles: save money, save time, make money.
- Targets: funded startups only vs. hotels, ecommerce and enterprise.
- Tone: long professional emails vs. very short LinkedIn messages joking about the weather.
- Also tested: send days and times, founder vs. CTO vs. marketing officer, and number of follow-ups.

**Why copying a winning campaign did not work.** The first positive reply came for one client, David (software engineering services), from a startup that had recently raised millions. The next morning brought a second, so David had two leads from startups that had raised $15M collectively.
- Alex copied David's campaign text to all other clients. After a week and thousands of emails, only negative replies; only David kept getting positives.
- He then tailored each campaign to the client's individual strengths: strong LinkedIn presence meant LinkedIn; strong brand meant name-dropping clients; a Fintech case study meant a Fintech campaign; a client in London or New York meant reaching out to local founders to suggest a friendly coffee.
- Around 3 clients were getting replies after this. It was a lot of work and "nothing about it felt scalable, productized or right."

**Results were random, and clients cared only about deals.**
- Around month 3, none had closed a deal. He lost 4 clients in 2 weeks, about $100k/year in ARR.
- "They didn't care about my effort. They didn't care about leads either. They wanted deals."
- Then one difficult client reported 2 closed deals worth roughly $120k each, and another client closed one weeks later. Alex considered both flukes: a random LinkedIn "casual coffee" campaign and a campaign that looked like all the others won, while campaigns he was excited about went nowhere. David kept getting leads but had not closed anyone.

## Refunds, guarantees and why they were not a real escape

**The refund policy.** Alex charged $2k/month for 3 months. If the client was unhappy at the end, he would:
- refund one month in cash,
- give access to his database and newsletter for 1 year,
- keep running campaigns for 3 more months.

He called it "a full money back guarantee, but structured in a smart way."

**Where it failed.**
- Some clients demanded a full refund and threatened chargebacks.
- Others did not want "ping pong with their money." They had goals and were losing time and opportunities regardless of a refund.
- In the early failures he fully refunded clients and offered 3 free months of campaigns to try to turn things around. They took it well, but he lost hundreds of dollars per account.
- One client told him he would have referred Alex to all his agency-owner friends but would now tell them to stay away. Alex: "I could refund money. But I couldn't refund my reputation."

**Risk he did not foresee.** A client's outreach named JPMorgan as a customer, which broke an NDA, and the client's largest customer demanded it stop. The client had a panic attack and cancelled. Lesson: name-dropping clients in outreach carries real risk to the client.

## Turning a custom service into a system: document, automate, then cut

**Step 1: document everything.** Alex wrote down every task "word for word, comma for comma, click for click," with screenshots, arrows and text. The client process covered:
- onboarding questionnaire sent to the client,
- a gift for the client, varying by country and which store supports it,
- a dedicated to-do board and a dedicated folder per client for meeting notes, info and campaigns,
- setting up LinkedIn and email accounts and gathering leads,
- writing campaigns from best practices and his own best performers,
- reviewing and launching with the client,
- monitoring inboxes daily and forwarding leads,
- a weekly report.

**Step 2: automate.** He realized documenting step by step "was exactly like programming. Just in human language," so he translated it into code. Onboarding time dropped from 45 days for the first client to 10 days for the latest. Campaigns ran on autopilot. Still manual: monitoring inboxes, weekly reports and writing campaigns.

**Step 3: kill complexity.** Every process "had to fight for its life"; if it was just ok or good, it was killed.
- Omnichannel LinkedIn plus email went to email only.
- Weekly client meetings went to zero meetings.
- Any language went to English only.
- Any target customer went to funded startups only.
- 10+ niche campaigns became one "monster campaign" to his whole database.

Result: an easier life, faster process, same or better results, room to take on more clients. Clients were happier because he told them exactly what would be done, when and how, without too many options.

**His framing.** "The real business is not the final product. It's all the behind the scenes stuff that's invisible." His CyberLeads landing page had not changed in months, but behind the scenes the business was unrecognizable. He compares it to a restaurant's kitchen or warehouses and delivery drivers.

## Narrow focus creates expertise and results

By focusing only on funded startups, email only, and seeing the same agency offers repeatedly, Alex found nuances he would not have found otherwise:
- which angles work best depending on when the startup last raised,
- which angle works best by funding stage,
- predicting when a startup will raise again and reaching out before everyone else.

Campaigns were still very custom and results felt random, but quality improved. One morning every client had received a lead that week, and these were high-quality leads (startups with millions in revenue that had raised millions). Comparing notes, he learned this was considered good: some clients had previously worked with another agency and got 0 qualified leads in 6 months. More clients started closing deals, and his landing page filled with testimonials from clients who had collectively made millions.

## Why playbooks mislead: everyone is "talking their own book"

Alex argues that recipes and guides failed him, and results came only once he thought for himself. Most guides he followed were "marketing in disguise."
- Competitors' Google articles all plugged their own product or affiliate links.
- The person telling him to build a personal brand sold personal brand building as a service.
- The person telling him to surround himself with successful people ran a founder community.
- The person saying Google is the best channel sold a course on it.

"Everyone is talking their own book. And everyone's advice somehow perfectly aligns with what they are selling."

He applies this to himself. He was a privacy advocate until CyberLeads succeeded selling business data, then told himself he was helping small business owners get past gatekeepers. After Epilepsy Blocker failed, he told himself he preferred "a boring business with an exciting life." His takeaway: "it's a million times easier to change your beliefs and the advice you give, than change your actions and your business. Words are flexible. Actions are not."

His heuristic for testing beliefs:
- A belief that benefits him: be suspicious of it.
- A neutral belief: treat as cosmetic, "just words."
- A belief that costs him and he still holds it: real.

## Scaling pains: automation fragility, marketing vs. fulfillment, and no pipeline

**Automations break constantly.** His automations imitate a human clicking around websites, so any site change breaks them. Custom client requests (e.g., direct bank transfers, report formats) break them too, as do Google updates to email registration and configuration. Maintaining automations and handling edge cases was "almost as much work as doing it manually."

**The marketing/fulfillment cycle.**
- With many clients, he stops marketing to focus on results.
- When clients leave, he rushes back to marketing, but has no pipeline, so revenue dips until marketing takes effect.
- He "pulls punches on both" and does a half-hearted job at each.

**Public revenue posts get awkward.** Posting revenue and behind-the-scenes updates was "cute at $100/month," but at this scale it can make people feel bad and make clients think he is boasting instead of getting them results. Posting the ugly side got no engagement.

**Distribution spike.** "My First Million" mentioned him and CyberLeads after finding him on Twitter. Effects:
- about 10 new newsletter subscribers per day, plus multiple calls booked for the service,
- over the next month the newsletter doubled to $20k/month and the service made over $20k/month,
- combined, CyberLeads reached around $500k/year.

He could not take more clients and wanted it to stop.

**Income did not fix the job.** He felt he had created a "second cage": hated Mondays (weekly reports, questions), carried his laptop 24/7, and said that at a normal salary he would quit. His answer was to decide to hire people and build a team.
