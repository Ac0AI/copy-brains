## Open source contributions should be a byproduct, not a goal

- Theo says most developers shouldn't set "contribute to open source" as a goal. His position is that open source is great, but pursuing contributions as a career hack is the wrong approach.
- He was prompted by a Reddit post from a beginner who wanted a good-first-issue React repo without TypeScript, writing "as I need to start doing open source". Theo's objection: "nobody needs to start doing open source."
- His framing is "cart before the horse". The horse is having a real problem with software you use. The cart is the issue or fix you create for that problem.
- Open source is "about sharing contributions to things you use so others can use them", not a way to get started in development.
- He says that if you can't figure out which projects to contribute to, you shouldn't be contributing yet.

## What open source is not (mentorship, free jobs)

- Open source is not a place for free mentorship or a free job. Theo calls it "an ecosystem of people working really hard to keep the web and all of software development alive".
- Maintainers are already struggling under the weight of issues, PRs and noise, now including AI-generated pull requests.
- A new developer with an empty GitHub account sending PRs of not-yet-great code mostly frustrates maintainers and wastes their own time.
- Telling new developers they'll get mentorship and jobs from open source is, in his words, "incredibly toxic", and he says it could damage open source if recommended carelessly.
- Mentorship can still happen. Theo has mentored contributors on create-t3-app and UploadThing.
- create-t3-app has over roughly 300 contributors, about half of them first-time open source contributors. He credits that to people who used the project, hit problems, or saw issues they knew how to solve.

## How to contribute well

- Best project to contribute to: one you use, that has a problem you've hit, where you've read the source and think you can fix it.
- Start with an issue, not with a pile of code you hope someone will mentor you through.
- A well-written issue with detailed reproduction steps is "huge". Theo says articulating problems well shows you're "in the trenches" and is more valuable than getting a weird PR accidentally merged.
- Good contributions come from understanding what the project does, not from the goal of contributing.

## Why open source helps hiring (and why the shortcut fails)

- As a hiring manager, Theo values seeing real contributions on someone's GitHub. Reading the code builds trust he couldn't get without having worked with the person.
- The contributions are valuable because they show you ran into real problems, fixed them, and took part in the community, not because they exist.
- Treating the contribution as the trophy skips the part that actually carries signal.

## Hacktoberfest as the origin of the problem

- Hacktoberfest started in 2013 as a DigitalOcean and GitHub collaboration, before Microsoft owned GitHub. Participants made at least four PRs in October to earn a limited-edition t-shirt, with a few hundred participants in year one.
- Theo says the idea was fine at small scale, but the arbitrary "four merged PRs" target led to spam: single-line fixes, grammar opinions, and PR floods to hit the threshold.
- By 2020 maintainers were fed up. He quotes a maintainer calling Hacktoberfest "a corporate sponsored distributed denial of service attack against the open source maintainer community".
- That maintainer reported closing 11 spam PRs in a day on one repo with 485 watchers, at a rate of about 4 per hour before October had even started.
- A Twitter account, "toober fest", documented the worst PRs, such as "please accept my request I will get a free t-shirt".
- His takeaway: telling people who don't understand open source that it's a magic route to a t-shirt or a job produces nonsense.

## Better path: build things you understand

- Theo's constant advice: the best way to get better at coding is to code more, building and solving problems you understand.
- Examples he gives: a web app tracking what you mine in Minecraft, or a site tracking your favorite sports team's scores.
- Building real things leads you to use open source projects, which leads to hitting problems, which leads to good issues and contributions.
- He says you need to know the thing well enough to judge whether your solution works.

## Goal-setting lesson for new developers

- Theo says new developers set bad goals like "make an open source contribution" or "learn JavaScript". You don't understand these well enough to know when you've achieved them.
- Goals should come from things you understand, such as a game you play or an app you want to recreate, so you can tell when it's working.
- His rule: "work backwards" from something you understand rather than setting a goal in something you don't.
- He points to his separate goal-setting video for more on this.
- Refusing to learn TypeScript while chasing contributions is, to him, a symptom of this: a developer who won't do the hard thing and treats open source as a hack.
