## Trust as the core job of an engineering manager

- Theo says almost every management decision he makes is aimed at building trust across the company.
- He makes sure everyone on the team is bought in, understands what's going on, and feels trusted in the work they contribute.
- If anyone has reservations about how something is being built, he wants the team to discuss it openly.
- His stated philosophy: "strong opinions weekly held". It doesn't matter if the person is a principal tech lead from Amazon with eight years there or a brand-new intern. If someone has a strong belief and can back it up, everyone should listen and learn from it.
- He frames the aim of management as smoothing the roughness between engineers and everyone else (CEO, product, design, users) and setting engineers up for success.

## Let engineers fail

- Theo calls letting engineers fail one of the best things an engineering manager can do, especially when you know they're going to fail.
- There are three outcomes when someone pushes a strong opinion: they're right and the team learns, they're wrong and you teach them, or (the underrated option) they're wrong, they're hard to convince, and you let them go be wrong for a bit.
- If you know a technology an engineer wants to adopt won't work and will fail early, you can say so, but if they don't listen, let them do it.
- Reasons he gives:
  - It shows you trust them.
  - They might be right, and then you get to adopt the better solution.
  - If they're wrong, they build trust and capability, and make better-informed recommendations next time.
- Caveat: if someone consistently pushes bad things and consistently breaks stuff, that's a different conversation.
- He thinks most engineers aren't given enough leash, especially when the manager has a technical background.
- He admits it's been hard to manage teams where the engineers know more and are more tuned in than he is, and says he's consistently surprised by how capable they are.

## Energy is the most valuable resource a team has

- Theo argues codebases don't live or die on technology choices or on how good the hired engineers are. They live or die on who has the energy to keep things moving through maintenance windows, hard problems and all the pain a project has until it finds its users in the state it needs to be in.
- He says managers too easily throw this away by saying no to an energized engineer because their idea doesn't immediately line up with business goals.
- His claim: "there is no business goal that isn't accelerated by a team that's energized by the work they're doing". Even if the thing bringing the energy points in a different direction, that energy gets you where you want to go much faster.
- He says this is the reason startups can exist at all. Companies like Twitch could in principle "run circles around" a two-person startup, but they can't because people with energy get burnt out before they can apply it.
- The manager's job:
  - Find the people bringing energy.
  - Give them everything they need to maintain it and share it with the team.
  - Manage the team's energy and excitement, and be willing to take big risks and get into fights with executives to protect it.
- He sees devs who learn for fun (e.g. those watching his videos as soon as they drop) as having exactly the energy a company needs, and thinks managers should feed into the people who care enough to keep learning in their free time and bring it back to the team.

## Case study: Luke Lafreniere at Linus Tech Tips / Floatplane

- Theo holds up Luke as a stellar example of engineering management, noting it doesn't always look like someone who's coded for 10 years.
- Luke recently became CTO of all of LTT, having never been a real engineer, never coded for a living and never had an engineering manager himself. He ended up in charge of building Floatplane.
- Theo's description of how Luke operates:
  - He knows nearly every engineer he hires is better than him, embraces that, and uses it to improve.
  - His main role is translating between developer speak and timelines, communicating to the rest of LTT what the engineers need. Theo notes that on the business side deadlines get hit, while in tech "deadlines are imaginary".
  - He explores new things and brings them to the team. He became a regular viewer of Theo's channel to find things to bring in, and noticed what the team needed.
  - He brought Vercel and Next.js technology to Floatplane and LTT because he thought they'd add value.
- Results Theo cites:
  - Researching and adopting those technologies gave his engineers excitement and motivation to keep iterating.
  - It significantly widened the pool of engineers they could hire, and raised the energy level of people coming in.
- Theo says Luke reached out because he had no other engineering managers to talk to. They had a four-hour Discord call, and most of it was Theo reinforcing what Luke was already doing.
- Theo says Luke's YouTube-grind background taught him how important energy is, and he pushes to maintain it on the team.
- Theo is now excitedly referring engineers to Luke for the next era of LTT.

## The dumb question rule for onboarding

- Theo enforces a rule with new teammates: they must ask a minimum number of dumb questions every day for their first couple of weeks. His example is at least two dumb questions every work day.
- The reasoning:
  - It feels awful to be stuck on something you feel you should know, like it should be in a doc somewhere or was said before and you missed it.
  - A big part of development is feeling dumb, and it's the manager's job to help people through that.
  - Feeling dumb for too long drains energy, so providing a comfortable way to work through it preserves it.
- The things that typically hold people up are dumb ones, such as getting a dev environment set up or figuring out which GitHub account to sign in with, and most engineers are scared to ask about them.
- Forcing people through those questions makes them far more excited to contribute by the end of their first week.
- He claims he has seen this take month-plus onboarding windows and knock them down to a few days.

## Summary of what makes a team successful

- Theo's three takeaways: let people fail, build trust, and feed into the people who bring energy.
- He says team value doesn't come from how many tickets close each week or how good the top engineer is at TypeScript. It comes from cohesiveness, focus, trust and the energy people bring.
- He frames it as the manager's job to make people comfortable and excited to bring all of that to the workplace.
- He notes that many engineering managers don't think about engineering management at all, much less in a way that's cohesive and conducive to growth for their engineers.
