## Why the junior dev market is harder now: leverage and team size

Theo frames the hiring market with three axes: devs needed per feature, devs available to hire, and money available to hire them. For years these trended in developers' favor. He argues that is no longer true.

- Devs needed per feature has slowly fallen, helped by better tools, lower product expectations, shipping faster for earlier feedback, and AI.
- Devs available has risen much faster, and money to hire them has dropped.
- The gap between "engineers needed" and "engineers available" is leverage. It used to favor engineers so strongly that companies hired people just so competitors couldn't (he cites Facebook hiring web framework creators).
- Now he says a company is more likely to reorg existing staff, or lay people off, than hire new people.

His example: the Twitch Mod View team (about 2020-2021) needed five people. Two were already on the team, one was moved over from another team, one was an intern converted to full-time, and one was poached from another company (he believes Disney Plus). Roles got hundreds of applications. Building the same thing today, he says, two of those roles would simply not exist, and the team might be two people plus AI, which he calls "person 3.5 Claude". Where does a junior fit on that team? "They don't fit and it sucks."

## Hard problems build skill, and AI is the easy out

Theo says juniors improve by leaving the layer they are stuck in. Before AI, he had to isolate a problem, search, then ask his manager, who would often reframe it entirely ("Why are you rendering that element at all?"). The common junior mistake is staying stuck on the console error when the problem lives a layer higher.

- Humans instinctively avoid hard, painful things, so "Claude, fix this error" is the easy out.
- Becoming a great engineer means accepting you are not one yet and being willing to feel stupid constantly. "Feeling dumb becomes the best feeling once you learn to embrace it."
- He learned this from skateboarding: you improve by hitting the ground, so you go in expecting to fall, which makes landing it feel magical.
- When building, expect the code not to work, so reduce the number of moving parts so any single failure is easy to identify, fix and replace.
- He relies heavily on Cursor himself, but says it works for him because his brain was already wired to solve hard problems.

## Compensation is a step function, so trust is the scarce resource

Theo argues that developer pay is not a smooth upward curve. In the US it is zero until you cross a quality bar, then jumps. His line: "If you're not worth $100,000 a year as a software dev in the US, you're worth zero dollars a year as a software dev in the US."

- Previously the shortage of devs lowered that bar, and companies bet on lower-tier hires hoping they'd be promotable in 2-3 years. He says that investment no longer makes sense.
- Being good enough is not sufficient without trust. When he reviews a stack of resumes, he ranks by how much he trusts the person to perform, not by school or resume content.
- In the Mod View example, he trusted the existing teammates, the transferred employee (visible commits), the intern (paid a small amount to prove themselves) and the poached hire (proven record elsewhere).
- In a market of 500 to 5,000 AI-generated applications, you can't win by being slop application number 5,001.

## How to build trust and stand out

Theo says the way to beat the bar is to grow in public and show you care.

- Show your work: write a blog post on two solutions you tried and what you liked and didn't. Admitting "I didn't understand this part" is a good signal, because it shows you'll tell the team when you don't know something.
- Seniors can quickly tell whether you understand what you're writing about. He saw two Twitch candidates whose "React site" was a pasted HTML template in a single component with no state or logic.
- Be present in communities where people care about the same things; his own team formed from people who hung out in the same places and showed that care.
- Don't buy a resume course, pad commit counts with AI-generated repos, or spam open source with low-quality PRs. He has a separate video arguing open source contribution isn't the key to a job.
- Useful open source: file a detailed issue with a reproduction (for example a StackBlitz link), then offer a PR to fix it.
- Aim to talk with people at higher salary levels about things they own. At Twitch he found himself mostly talking with people making twice his pay because he was interested in what they built.
- Some capable people are insecure and don't realize their level; working publicly lets them compare themselves and get noticed.

## How to reach out to senior people

Theo says clear, concise, relevant communication is rare and gets people hired.

- Bad DM: a life story, several paragraphs, then a vague "what do you think?" It is unclear why you picked him, and it reads like you think he is rich or can hand you a job.
- Good DM: context first ("In your video about X, I noticed you did Y"), then the specific point of confusion, then a tiny ask such as a resource, not personal teaching. "I will remember you if this is how you DM me."
- Treat senior people as a last-resort resource: use their public content, solve what you can, then ask a small, calm question.
- Even better, follow up with proof of effort (a blog post about what you struggled with) or bring a solution to a problem he has. He cites a developer who sent three sentences and a link to a plugin that formats TypeScript errors prettily; he installed it, made a video, and has sent him job opportunities since.
- He hired a community member for video review and info-gathering because she was concise and dropped useful links rather than essays. He later paid another helper, Gabriel, and referred him to a better job.
- He learned this on Minecraft plugin forums: a specific error, steps tried, and a clear ask got responses (and led him to contribute to WorldGuard).

## What Theo says he is not saying

He adds clarifications about the advice:

- He is not saying avoid AI. Use it to improve understanding (for example, explain React rendering by comparison to Unreal Engine rendering), or to handle problems you already know how to solve. Don't use it instead of improving. If you can't find the balance, drop the tools and revisit later.
- He is not saying give up. Impact can come quickly once in; he points to shadcn, who he believes had no full-time engineering job before Vercel.
- He is not saying GitHub is worthless, or that you must learn React. Half the people he hired weren't React engineers.
- He is not saying spam famous people with questions.
- His bottom line: it is a better time than ever to be an engineer if you care and a miserable time if you don't, and "trust is a rare commodity nowadays." He would hire a junior who shows a year of growth and asks great questions over a more experienced dev he doesn't trust.

## Theo's own start, and the old path being closed

Theo says he was not qualified for his first dev job. He got a three-month contract because the manager liked him (including his music taste) and he had a few projects. The contract was renewed once rather than converted, and the following six months were the hardest he has worked and where he became qualified.

- He says the route he used, throwing a resume at enough companies that one was desperate enough to hire him, is closed.
- A company now has 10 qualified applicants per role, so betting on an unqualified person and training them is not worth it.
- It may take longer: he suggests hanging out in communities for two years after college while doing a part-time gig, but "you will make it" if you care enough and keep going.
- He got into code through hosting Minecraft servers, and says motivation should come from love of the craft or curiosity, not just from wanting the salary.
