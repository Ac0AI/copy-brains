## Luck is surface area, and you build it with shots on goal

- Theo says luck "is a thing that occurs, not a thing that you have." It is a function of opportunity plus prep, and you can increase its surface area by being deliberate about where you spend your time.
- He calls himself "a pretty lucky guy" (a startup doing well, a YouTube channel, work he loves) and says anyone can position themselves to be luckier.
- He refuses the "you're only successful because you're famous" take. His view: "Success doesn't come from a lot of wisdom and foresight and genius. Success comes from the willingness to try and fail over and over again."
- His analogy is skateboarding. Nobody calls a skater lucky for landing a hard trick after months of bailing; they credit the wherewithal. He learned skating by getting hurt repeatedly, not by watching videos.

## Rate of experimentation: the math of shots on goal

- Theo endorses the idea that unusually successful people just try a lot of things; the rate of experimentation matters more than the success percentage, which stays low even for the successful (he cites Levels.io, with 70+% of projects failing).
- Example math: with a 1% success chance per attempt, 10 attempts gives about a 9.5% chance of at least one success. About 70 attempts gives roughly a 50% chance.
- Cadence changes the timeline: 2 attempts per day reaches that point in about 45 days; 2 per week takes about 45 weeks (315 days), and realistically longer because weeks get skipped.
- Failures are not pure loss. They teach you, make you friends and connections, and show you which markets are cursed, so each additional attempt also has a higher success chance than the last.
- "Trying" is not only building new apps. It is making connections, talking to people, going to events and spending time in the right circles.

## His own failure history before T3 Chat

- Products before T3 Chat: Ping (capped early; he had to lay off most of the team, "one of the saddest and hardest things I've ever had to do"), a webhook product (a couple of loyal users, never a real business), other creator tools, Marker Thing, and Pick Thing.
- UploadThing came from realizing, while building Pick Thing, that upload was the slowest part. It got heavy package adoption and users abused the free tier, but it made no real money.
- T3 Chat was not the one he expected. He would tell people "why would you build a chat app right now? That's stupid." He built it as one of several small-to-medium projects meant to make a couple thousand a month and keep Ping from running out of money. It became profitable within a month.
- He attributes the outcome partly to luck: people wanting all models in one place cheaply, a genuinely good app, his audience, and things he doesn't fully understand. He argues UploadThing and Ping are arguably better products, so quality alone does not decide it.
- He says "I got lucky, but I got lucky after getting unlucky working harder on other things for years," and expects more future products to fail.
- He rejects the "he stopped building for devs" take: Ping and Pick Thing were both dev products.
- Offer mentioned: T3 Chat is $8/month with every relevant model, and a promo code gave the first month for $1.

## Build runway first, then place bets

- Theo admits his own surface area was large: college friends, rich ex-Twitch coworkers, low expenses, no kids, and money saved after his job. That let him take repeated risks. He calls this a privilege.
- His response to people who lack this: don't complain about missing runway, build it. Get a decent job on a good team (he says to optimize early career around good teams), network at a boot camp, save enough to coast for 6 months.
- If you only get to place one bet and it fails (and "nobody gets it right the first try"), you have nothing to fall back on. A 1% chance for him may be 2% for you, but "you're still going to die if you have one roll."
- His own path took about 5 years of working before the startup, and he says a 4-year job to build the position isn't a big difference.
- He thinks starting a startup before you have the surface area is probably not the right move yet.

## Peers, college and where you spend time

- Peers set your pace. His analogy: marathon runners swap pacers so they can focus on the hard part, not on holding speed.
- He pushes college even if you drop out or skip the paper: the value is being surrounded by people to pace against and learn from, and getting better at making connections.
- His early investors came from college contacts and his first job, which let him raise enough to quit and start the company. Those connections also got him into YC after rejections from easier accelerators, and then "I failed for 4 years."
- Avoid peers who blame the world or have shipped the same codebase for eight years without growing. Optimize for the agility you want in your own life.
- A lucky chain example: posting in college Facebook hacking groups led to a friend of a friend who knew Saurik (creator of Cydia), who is now a close friend who has helped him through rough situations.
- He mentions living in San Francisco as another surface-area lever, without elaborating.
- He agrees with the heuristic that someone meeting 1-2 interesting people a day is a good bet; 1-2 a week is too slow to reach escape velocity.

## Don't use shortcuts like open source or fame as a job strategy

- He says the mindset matters more than a checklist: "what matters here is how you think, not what you do."
- Open source: contributing in order to get a job is cart-before-horse. Good developers end up contributing because they use the tools and fix real problems; the contribution is evidence of that, not the cause of the job.
- Same ordering error: thinking YC acceptance guarantees a startup, or YouTube/Twitter popularity makes people care about what you build. He says people care about what he does because he made it worth caring about, not the reverse.
- Being popular does not mean people care about everything you do; he cites posting music on his tech-heavy Twitter.

## How to build connections: curiosity, giving first, and being useful

- Approach with genuine curiosity, not extraction. "Don't hit me up because you want something from me. Hit me up because you're curious about something I'm also curious about."
- Don't open with "can I get your time" and a calendar link. Ask something interesting and "you'll get time for free." Same logic in fundraising: ask for advice and people offer money; ask for money and you get less.
- Example: a thumbnail critique with a redesign, or a thoughtful question about a post, will get a reply from him, whereas "I need a job" cold asks get blocked. He applies the same to Ryan Carniato: a deep question about a post gets a stream rant, a job ask gets silence.
- Friendships he lists that began from shared interests: Sasha Gray (speakers and audio gear), Luke from LTT (phones), and smaller YouTubers he DM'd to help with audio.
- Give before you take: he says he works to make everyone around him feel they get more than they give, because his team can't make what he makes since the channel and business are his.
- Be useful before asking. A developer first appeared by dropping a useful Zen/Excalidraw hotkey extension in his chat; when that person later said he was starting a company, Theo's first response was to ask if he could invest and "write the check."
- He reframes "don't meet your heroes": meet them, but arrive to thank them or help, not to ask. He's met nearly all his skateboarding heroes, including Tony Hawk, and says it's been great.
- Generosity can take the form of attention (responsive), time, or resources.

## Concrete examples of surface area paying off

- The best "I need a job" DM he's received (from Sahaj, creator of Tweakcn): four sentences, a clear diagram of strengths (DevOps on one side, design on the other), the type of role, and a remote preference. Theo posted it publicly with "Hire him"; eleven days later Sahaj joined v0 at Vercel. Theo credits Sahaj's own communication, not his referral.
- SnitchBench: he re-recorded the video four times and automated the benchmark after realizing it deserved building properly. The benchmark became more relevant than the video, led to connections including Simon Willison, and Theo plans to forward half his sponsors to Simon since Simon earns almost nothing from his blog.
- Student at a college event: asked him on the spot to speak at their school after noting he likes Waterloo students; Theo said yes because he wanted more Waterloo students to know him, and says he has never made a bad hire or investment from a Waterloo student.
- Advent of Code: competing on the leaderboard turned a chatter, Chris, into a moderator and friend who got him to record a talk for his college.
- Putting two people together: he brought a ten-year college friend, Von, to SF and into his friend circle, which he calls his favorite thing to do.
- Acting like a bigger role: behave like the Michelin chef covering a brunch shift; people notice effort that wasn't strictly required.

## Writing, events and tolerating awkwardness

- Share your genuine thoughts online. The writing needn't be original or literary, just reflect your real interests. Theo says a blog is among the first things he'd do with no professional connections, noting the author's Substack has helped her hiring.
- Don't change topic to chase audience; share what excites you with others who care. Example DM that works: "you said X in a video, I thought about it so much I wrote this post."
- Hosting events: he doesn't do it himself and finds it socially draining, but sees a pattern where being the place a group meets works. His "Theo orbiter" SF group runs its own Discord and Friday events without him.
- Suggestions from the article: coffee, a stoop meetup, pizza. Monthly bagel brunch is cheap, low-stakes and rarely competes with other plans. If others want a thing and you initiate it, you win even if it fails because you learn demand.
- Expect to feel socially flailing and awkward at the start (he felt awkward in his first programming Twitter Spaces and now talks to rooms of 800 without a thought).
- Don't judge people from outside: he is often assumed to be a "drama farmer" but says he quietly defuses far more drama than he covers, and that people he helped defend him. Let assumptions be corrected quickly by new information.
- Showing up and being accessible leads to unpredictable loops: trying to calm the WordPress drama led to an invite to Matt Mullenweg's birthday party and meeting Joshua, creator of Delicious.
- His bottom line: "There is no growth hack that is more powerful than talking about the stuff you care about with others who also care about the thing."
