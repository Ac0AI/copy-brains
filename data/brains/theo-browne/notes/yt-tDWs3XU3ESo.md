## Founder mode vs manager mode: what Theo takes from Paul Graham's essay

- Theo frames "founder mode" as a mindset pushed by Y Combinator and Paul Graham. Graham's argument is that standard advice for scaling a company ("hire good people and give them room to do their jobs") is how to run a company you didn't found, as a professional manager.
- Theo agrees from experience. He is a YC founder (Ping Labs, which made Ping and UploadThing). He says outside advisors and investors only see correlated signals, such as raising money and then hiring people, and wrongly infer that this is the process.
- He argues success comes from "building the right thing and obsessing over it and the people who want to use it". Obsession with individual customers' needs is the driver.
- He still takes all the sales calls for the services he has built.
- He notes the essay's thesis: there are two ways to run a company, founder mode and manager mode. Most people assumed scaling meant switching to manager mode, and founders who tried it found it felt broken.
- He agrees that founder mode isn't well understood yet and hopes it becomes as well understood as manager mode.

## Hire for the work you love, not the work you want to avoid

- Theo calls this "within my best advice": people screw up by hiring for the work they don't want to do.
- His own example: he hired a YouTube editor because he disliked editing. His hiring bar was low, he couldn't relate to the editor, and he had no feedback or process to give them.
- He went through four editors before finding the current one. Once he started editing his own videos he got good at it and fell in love with it. He now says he'd stop recording before he stopped editing and doing thumbnails.
- He applies it to startups. Founders hire sales and support too early because they don't want to think about them. His line: "no [expletive] support's important, you got to do it enough to like it", and only then hire.
- How will you know if someone is good at support if you haven't learned it first?
- At Ping he did the same in reverse. Mark and Julius do basically all the code, and Theo spends his time "doing everything else because that's important, that's my job as a Founder".
- He says he learned more about being a good founder through YouTube than through software development and big companies. YouTube helped him unlearn things from Amazon and Twitch.

## Why you shouldn't take advice from investors

- Lonely founders get preyed on. A founder leaving a company with constant colleague interaction is isolated, and VCs fill that gap with feedback and conversation, then use it as leverage for a better deal and to isolate you from other advice.
- Theo's explanation for VCs trashing YC on Twitter: YC drops you in a room full of other founders, which reduces dependence on investors and improves founders' deal terms.
- YC's incentive is different. If 498 of 500 companies fail, one multi-billion-dollar company pays for the rest. YC would take 7% of a future giant over 20% of a company that fails, so it does everything to set founders up.
- Other investors may care more about your Series B, or about selling shares early, than about your long-term exit.
- Cap table warning: many VCs want real equity immediately. YC invented the SAFE ("secured against future equity"), an IOU that converts only when new stock is created (Series A, sale, IPO). All of Ping's investors own 0% of Ping right now, and the SAFE makes the cap table "comically easier" to manage. He has seen even a16z-backed founders do odd things to their cap tables after talking to investors too much.
- Investors never tell you the real reason they pass. His estimate (attributed to Michael Seibel): 95% of pitches are a gut "no", about 1% a clear "yes", and about 4% "I'd hate to say no". For that 4% bucket, a direct ask with a number makes it much harder to refuse.
- Because saying no feels bad, investors make up a justification on the spot, and founders then rethink their whole company because of it.
- Practical advice:
  - Do mock pitches with other founders you trust, not investors.
  - Gut-check yourself honestly.
  - Ignore investors unless they say "I'm in".
  - Don't let them onto your table or into decision-making too early.
- His own data: his batch's pitch got passed on by about 30 investors, and he did 5 to 7 calls a day for two weeks. With the right VCs, "the deal was closed in 15 minutes from when the call started".
- The best advice a VC ever gave him: don't listen to VCs. His quip: "those who can build build, those who can't build invest".
- He is an investor himself, about 40-ish companies, and a scout with Andreessen Horowitz. He says he is very hands-off, and he asks founders to send a cohesive two-sentence pitch in Twitter DMs.

## Why founder mode keeps teams small and shipping fast

- Theo made what he calls possibly his biggest career failure: hiring up too early at Ping. They had 6 people (7 with a part-timer), which slowed them down because keeping everyone aligned and changing direction was hard.
- Before the late-2022 layoff crisis he cut back to himself and Mark and went all-in on developer tools. Julius contributed so much open source code that Theo brought him on part-time (full-time hours, contracted), and calls him essential to their success.
- He shows founder mode as "move fast, fix things". Julius fixed a dashboard upload bug while Theo watched, joking he pushed straight from main via GitHub mobile to production. Theo acknowledges a big business might hesitate at that, but says "you're [expletive] shipping and nothing else".
- Loyalty lesson from Ping: the most loyal users weren't the ones who never saw a bug. They were the ones who reported a bug, got a fast fix, were told and were thanked. A fixed failure builds more confidence than no failures at all.
- Related principle: safety nets over guard rails. Things will always break regardless of unit tests, so fast fixing beats prevention for loyalty.
- He argues it's even better to have bugs than not, as long as you can fix them quickly.

## Examples of founder mode at any scale

- Twitch mod view: it had the most positive sentiment of any Twitch launch since gift subs. The product manager and designer were so embedded with moderators that the team thought the tracking was broken. The PM was a mod on a creator's channel and was in her group chat, which he used to collect feedback.
- Theo's takeaway: that PM wasn't a founder but acted in founder mode. Responsiveness and collaboration between users and the business is essential, and users should be excited to report problems because they trust a response.
- It works at Twitch scale, Amazon scale and with five customers, but "you have to hold on to it tightly or you will lose it".
- CJ at Axiom finds things to work on by reading Discord and Slack, not Linear.
- Discord example: Theo tweeted about buttons wrapping to one vertical character in a narrow window. A 19-year-old intern, Adith, fixed it, and Theo urged Discord's CEO to hire him and put him in charge of PR and product.
- Theo expects Discord engineers hostile to that intern would respond "we have a process for this" and that "the quality never dips". He says he had that exact conversation many times at Twitch and it's a big part of why he quit.
- Ownership is "the biggest thing without question". He cites a designer, Phil (uiux Phil), who rebuilt the PickThing homepage unprompted during a stream. Theo says it's founder mode and that he will pay him.

## Org structure: skip levels, black boxes, and fakers

- Theo hates the modular "black box" org model. At Twitch he'd hear that another team wasn't interested or needed headcount. His response: "put me in touch with the person inside so I can tell them what to do".
- Advice for employees at companies like that: ignore the black boxes and reach out to the individual contributors. To find the most driven person on a team, look at who is contributing most in the GitHub repos.
- Every team has someone more driven than the rest, and that person is probably lonely. Building that relationship gives you an inn to get things fixed and can lead to referrals, early hires, coaching or investing later.
- He got in trouble when his manager felt insecure about how often he met his manager's manager. Theo says that insecurity meant the manager wasn't fit for the role, and the manager stopped managing soon after Theo left.
- He agrees Graham's founder mode implies skip-level meetings should become the norm. He says it's "silly" that a name exists for meeting your manager's manager.
- Steve Jobs example (from the essay): an annual retreat for the 100 most important people at Apple, not the top 100 on the org chart. Theo says org charts shouldn't represent impact, and impactful people should be able to unblock and ship directly with leadership.
- His commitments: every person at any company he runs will have blanket ability to contact him on or off the record. He won't use a service if he can't reach the CEO or an executive, even if a competitor is worse.
- Fakers: "professional fakers" hire other fakers, and good engineers leave. He says they are hard to remove and hide in the shadows, and he describes a coworker who tried to get him fired because his output made them look bad. In his view this is how a company ends up like Google.
- His only tentative cure is obsessing over details to catch their mistakes, though he's "not comfortable saying micromanagement is the best way out".

## Delegation and the limits of founder mode

- Theo's own balance: he handed UploadThing's biggest, most fun infrastructure rebuild to Mark and Julius so he wouldn't be distracted from running the company. The rebuild moves uploads from direct-to-S3 URLs to a server-side flow that can validate files, respond to the user with data from your server and resume chunks.
- He stays involved: asking questions, dogfooding, and giving blunt feedback like telling Julius a URL format was too long, which got fixed quickly. He admits this reads as micromanagement and that it's a hard balance.
- His approach is hiring people who love the things you love so you feel bad not giving them those tasks.
- He agrees with Graham's caveats:
  - Founders can't run a 2,000-person company like a 20-person one, so some delegation is needed.
  - Founder mode may be misused by founders who can't delegate things they should.
  - The modular approach limits damage from a bad CEO.
- Theo is not endorsing a blanket rule; he says what's known about founder mode is still small.
- He expects that once founder mode is understood, many founders will be seen to have already been most of the way there, previously labeled eccentric.
