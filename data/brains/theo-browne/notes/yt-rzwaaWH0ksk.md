## Why Theo says "be an engineer" and "learn React" are bad goals

- Theo argues vague aspirations ("become a software engineer", "learn React", "improve test coverage", "contribute to open source") are not goals because they have no clear start or end. Nobody can say when they have "learned React" or "become an engineer".
- He says he is maybe 60-70% through React and is good at it, so "learning" is not a measurable state.
- He treats aspirations as places you end up, not things you drive toward directly.
- He frames engineering goals as specific, pointed deliverables: things you can create and finish.
- He separates intrinsic wants ("I want to be", "I want to learn") from extrinsic goals ("I want to create", "I want to deliver"). Intrinsic ones usually mean "I want to feel like an engineer", which has nowhere to go as a goal.
- His rule: "engineers treat aspirations like goals. Aspirations are not goals." Aspirations are poor drivers; goals with deliverable ends are "a fantastic driver".

## How to turn a vague goal into a deliverable

Theo's method is to keep asking questions until the goal becomes a thing you can ship.

1. Start with "I want to learn React". Ask what that means: build an app, or get a job? He gives very different advice for each.
2. Pick the app path and ask what app. He tells people to pick a specific thing, not a vague "something".
3. Choose something concrete, such as a Twitter clone, and follow a tutorial for it.
4. Alternatively, build a tool for a problem you personally have. His example: in the game Outriders, a web app where he enters the number of runs and sees how likely a gear piece is to have dropped.
5. You know you have succeeded when the app solves the problem you had.

- His summary: "Building goals around specific problems you can solve will make delivering on them significantly easier."
- Best-practice one-liners he gives: the best way to learn React is to build things with it; the best way to improve test coverage is to write tests around the things that are breaking; the best way to be an engineer is to build things.

## The "circle" model: aspiration on the outside, small goals as points

- Theo draws a big circle as the aspiration (e.g. "contribute to open source") and a tiny dot in the middle as the new dev.
- A vague goal is a whole ring you are trying to reach, so people wander chaotically. A good goal is a specific point.
- His worked example for open source:
  1. Go through the Deno tutorial.
  2. Build a chat server with Deno.
  3. Hit a bug in the websocket implementation or out-of-date docs.
  4. File a PR to update the docs.
  5. You are now an open source contributor.
- He notes that finding a mistake in the tutorial itself could equally lead to a PR. The aspiration is reached as a side effect of specific goals.
- Those goals are picked because there is something you want to do, not because of the aspiration. Chasing the immediate deliverables is how you reach the higher aspiration.

## Contributing to open source: start from the project, not the goal

- Theo says "contributing to open source" is "just the straight up wrong way to go about it" as a starting goal.
- Instead, use more packages and try new things. When a doc has a typo, an example is unclear, or you understand a bug and can fix it, that is the moment to contribute.
- Genuine interest in the project first, then contribution, goes much better than choosing to contribute and then hunting for a project.
- He criticises Hacktoberfest for this: it encourages people with no idea what the project is to search by "beginner friendly" tags and throw in contributions hoping something merges. He calls that "the opposite of both good goal setting and meaningful contributions".
- He says the most meaningful contributions come from people who use and care about the thing.

## Detours and failure are still progress

- Theo's example path: aspire to be an engineer, set a first goal of building a Twitch clone, then find the hard part is the infrastructure (video / WebRTC), not the UI.
- You drift away from learning React but still become a better engineer. He says it is "okay to ignore" the higher-level aspirations in favour of immediate deliverables.
- It is fine to give up on a goal, for example learning enough WebRTC to get video working, then building a calculator instead.
- A better goal wording he suggests: "learn enough WebRTC to have video working", not "learn WebRTC".
- He says how someone handles failure is one of his biggest signals of a successful engineer, more than how many big things they shipped.
- If a candidate fails at every project but discusses it meaningfully in an interview, "I probably hire you still".

## Passion, discipline and the "make money" goal

- Theo says that if the only goal is money and the person never intends to enjoy the work, his content is not useful to them. He says that is fine and a flaw in him, not them.
- His own claim: he threw away a 400k a year salary to stream on Twitch and run a company, and he loves engineering.
- Engineering "kind of sucks" at times (dev environment fights, React Native not spitting out an app, mobile loading bugs). Being an engineer is a bad goal for that reason: if that is all you are working toward, you will give up.
- Excitement about the thing on the other side makes the pain worth it: "the journey sucks way harder if you don't like the destination", so pick destinations you are excited about and proud of.
- He says do not rely on passion for one specific project. Feed passion generally, and it will carry you through hard parts. If a project kills your passion, a new interest (e.g. WebRTC) can take over.
- Pure discipline can work. His example goal: "get a job at FAANG for greater than 100k a year", driven by studying interviews and Cracking the Coding Interview.
- He warns the risks: you either burn out before getting there, or arrive and have no idea what to do next. He says he has seen many people in that state and has made it a hobby to help them.
- With small goals you keep feeling the highs and lows along the way, instead of one big payoff at the end.

## Examples and why software is different

- Aiden (a younger dev Theo says is more talented than him): built FossaBot to solve specific problems high-traffic Twitch chats had, then set a goal of keeping hosting costs reasonable, and learned a lot along the way. Theo says his goals are better and his delivery on them is too.
- Jason Docton (Urad project): learned to code to build his charity's website with donation systems and integrations, because paid developers were too expensive and Squarespace was not good enough. The specific goal made him learn and deliver much faster.
- Theo notes even principal engineers ask "how do I learn your stack". His answer: you don't learn it, you build something with it and bring questions.
- Software is unlike law or medicine, where there are direct paths. In software, the indirect path (building things, breaking things, reverse engineering sites) is more effective than the direct one.
- His skateboarding analogy: his goals were "land this trick", "kickflip this stair set", never "be a skateboarder" or "get good at stairs". He brought that mindset to engineering.
- He says developers are closer to musicians and skateboarders than lawyers.
