## Cosplaying as a CEO: copying the side effects of success

Theo calls out founders who raise money and then "cosplay as a CEO." With a few million in the bank and few users, they inflate the metrics that are easy to inflate:

- Headcount.
- Infrastructure complexity.
- Ad spend.
- Fancy marketing materials.

He says these are second-order effects of the "horse" (the business) moving fast. If growth is fast you must hire or fail; if conversion is strong you must buy ads. Doing them without the growth does not make the horse move.

Examples he gives:

- Companies have spent $100,000 on a single video and still have worse MRR than T3 Chat.
- A 40-person company has worse revenue metrics than his team of "two and a half."

The same applies to engineers copying his stack or opinions. He tells people not to copy what he does. They should understand how he reached it and find their own process by building something they wish existed.

## What counts as a startup, and thinking in funnels and filters

Theo separates a startup from a good small business. A million dollars a year is something to be proud of, but it is not a startup. A startup can go from zero to 50k a year to 1 million to 100 million. Investors want to 200x their money about 10% of the time, not 2x it.

He rejects ideas with layers of niching. His example is "Tinder for dogs with three legs in Alaska": every extra word shrinks the market. Build it if you want it, but it is not worth investing in.

He still encourages indie products, which he keeps finding:

- A teleprompter app for iPads that he says makes about 1.2 million a year.
- Flighty, a solo dev's flight-tracking app he thinks makes about 5 to 6 million a year. He pays about $100 a year for it.

His funnel logic:

- Everyone flies, but most fly about once a year on roughly $400 of tickets.
- Each layer cuts about 90%: has flight problems, wants data, flies enough to care, finds the app, signs up, keeps the sub.
- Ten 90% cuts leave a couple thousand people.

The way back up the funnel is to remove layers:

- **Ping:** start with live video for Twitch, extend to other platforms, drop the live aspect, abstract the output format, then abstract where the output goes.
- **T3 Chat:** a deliberately low price widened the market, and a mobile app could potentially 2x to 3x it. The pitch was never niched: "the best AI chat app ever made."

## How T3 Chat actually happened

T3 Chat was a "weird Hail Mary" to get the company default alive after he "[messed] this up." The company had been burning money, which he put at 20 to 40 grand a month.

- The previous product for managing assets as a creator was at about 1.5k MRR.
- His math was that if he shipped five more things making 1 to 5k a month each, the company would stop burning money.
- Before T3 Chat he had built what he guesses is around 30 products before any succeeded. Most of the T3 Tools had to "basically fail" first.
- They expected T3 Chat to make "a few grand a month if we're lucky."

What drives it:

- Top of funnel is people who want to try and compare all the models.
- Users lapse or cancel after the $1 deal, go back to ChatGPT or Claude, then return because the others feel worse. He says subscription revival rates are "insane."
- Distribution is not a moat. He believes he is near market saturation for his own audience, and the mobile app might add about 50% more of it.

He also argues that having an audience hurt the business:

- Guaranteed first users meant they could not see product-market fit.
- They stayed on the wrong idea too long, three separate times.
- Retention charts lined up with his YouTube schedule.
- He is a risk to the company: a flight risk, and a controversy risk.

He builds only things that teach him something and benefit him, and he builds what he wishes existed.

## Founders with social media presence are not winning

Theo says founder and company-led social media is mostly a distraction. He went through his investor database and found the successful companies mostly had no meaningful social presence:

- One high-valuation company has none.
- A company he invested in at seed and Series A has a CEO with under 800 followers.
- Most of the founders pushing social hardest were not making money.

On vibe-coding apps (he is invested in several, though not Vercel's v0):

- **Lovable** has the most revenue.
- **v0** has by far the most social presence. Its big announcement got about 200 likes.
- **Bolt/StackBlitz** has little social presence. Its founder Eric has deep ties in the web dev ecosystem and built WebContainers.

His ranking: "The social media presence is for third place. The love of the game is for second place. And then pure luck is first place." He credits Lovable's lead to branding, timing and luck.

On company content:

- Nobody watches company videos; people watch people.
- The most popular video on Vercel's YouTube channel for years was his own talk about abusing Next.js.
- A founder competing with full-time creators will struggle.
- If a newly funded startup pays a creator to promote its product, that is "significantly better than trying to do it themselves."

He adds that you should get advice from people who have what you want, not people who want what you want. Exited founders say to work on yourself and your product, not on social media.

## Getting the job first, accelerators and risk

His order of operations: get the job, get the experience, then build, ideally on the side.

- He had 10 years of real engineering experience before starting a startup, and says he has never seen someone hurt by starting too late, only too early.
- He moved to San Francisco with a job paying 120k a year and covered moving costs from his parents and the sale of his car. He argues that is not real risk.
- Companies will move you to SF. His advice: "Don't move here looking for a job. Look for jobs that move you here."
- He is not seeing many companies hiring remote.

He talks about building "privilege": savings, equity, reputation and investor trust from being a good engineer. That reduces startup risk. You must be able to "eat a lot of [stuff]," because nobody lands the startup kickflip on the first try. He says people with dependents on their income and health insurance should not do the startup yet.

On accelerators:

- Only YC has produced billion-dollar companies, with hundreds of them. He calls every other accelerator a "joke."
- About 5% of YC companies reach a billion-dollar valuation.
- Even a $1B valuation might mean around $150M to the founder after dilution and taxes.
- He would still encourage going to school before YC.

On what cannot be beaten:

- Bundles (Microsoft 365, Google Workspace raising prices from about $4 to nine or ten dollars a seat with no pushback).
- Infrastructure that loses money at scale (YouTube, Twitch live video).

Everything else is up for grabs. Individual engineers are far more productive thanks to Vercel, Convex, PlanetScale and AI, so he thinks "two 20-somethings obsessed with this problem" outship a 2,000-person company every time. He calls this the best time ever to quit and build.

## Creator economics and sponsorships

Theo's blunt advice is to not become a YouTuber or founder unless warnings do not stop you.

- YouTube resembles the NBA: the top tier gets most of the money, and the middle is brutally risky, because one bombed video or lost sponsor can mean you cannot make rent.
- His channel cost him money for three years, and about six months before this recording he was still losing money on it, partly after the Vercel drama.
- Early on he took sponsors only for products he shipped, and was accused of bias for it.
- He later moved to ad slots where he cannot say anything bad about the product for roughly two minutes. He admits it feels "more dishonest," and it is what made the channel profitable.
- He turns down about 50 sponsor deals for every one he takes. He only takes products he would pitch to a co-worker.
- He keeps a safety net (a job) if doing content or startups.

On running the channel:

- If you start a channel, edit your own videos and make your own thumbnails.
- He has made over 3,000 thumbnails in four years.
- Editing made him a better speaker, because he became aware of filler words and stammers.
- Hiring an editor before the channel makes money is a mistake he made four times.

## Feedback filters, hiring and operating habits

**Feedback.** He takes signals from people he trusts and not from "yes men." If he is unsure about a risky tweet he shows it to Mark (his CTO) and Ben (his channel manager), and any hesitation means deleting it. His filter is facts: people who deny known truths (his example is Apple's "battery gate" throttling) are acting in bad faith and are hidden from the channel. He never deletes comments, only hides them. He unlisted a video once after realizing a harsh commenter was usually supportive.

**Hiring.** Hire when you would miss doing the work, not because you hate it.

- Offloading work you dislike makes you a bad manager with no insight.
- A founder who likes engineering should hire an engineer, then spend time on sales.
- After he had to edit his own videos, he fell in love with it. He then hired an editor, Faze, whose work excites him and who he describes as exceptional.

**Habits.** He says his best skill is tapping out: he goes deep and then stops once something is good enough. His examples are cameras (done until a new body tempted him again), custom keyboards (stopped after the Vortex Poker 3) and databases (happy with Convex).

For execution he says start by building, hit a wall, plan as little as needed to get around it, then build again. He also quotes a Sam Altman line he uses on himself: ask what someone with 10x more agency would do right now, and then do that.

He did not want to be a founder; he had to be, because he wanted creator tooling to exist. He says being the founder cured his habit of blaming others: "Every single thing that goes wrong is 100% on me."
