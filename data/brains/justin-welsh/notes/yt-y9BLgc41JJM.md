## Why he left corporate and how he started

- Justin spent 10 years in high-growth startups. He was an early sub-1 employee at Zocdoc in 2009 (5 years), then VP Sales and Chief Revenue Officer at PatientPop, where he grew the sales and marketing team from one person to 150 and revenue from the first dollar to $70M.
- On December 16, 2018 he had his first panic attack and called 911. He says that day made him know he needed to "do something different". Becoming a creator or starting a one-person business was not the plan.
- His burnout came from loss of control: problems stacked up faster than he could solve them, and everything became urgent and important. He coped with wine, burgers, 4 hours of sleep and weighed 240 lbs (about 210 now).
- He gave notice and took about 7 more months to leave so he could hand off his team. In that period he lost 40-50+ lbs.
- He planned to try working for himself for six months, then go back to a VP Sales or CRO job. He started on August 1, 2019.
- Burnout warning signs are hard to see because feeling bad becomes your normal. His fixes: journal daily (what is going well, poorly, what he is grateful for), build a business that supports your life, and have a peer group. He has Slack and Discord groups of people doing the same work.

## Life first, business second

- His core principle is "Your life should come first, your business should come second." Build free time, breaks and reflection into the business.
- Step one is defining what you enjoy and what you want to avoid. You cannot cut every disliked task from day one, but you can plan to eliminate them over time.
- He calls his temptation "shiny outcome syndrome" (different from shiny object syndrome). He sees a peer like Hunter Hammond building many businesses, then lists the work required and decides "I don't want to do that." He then returns to the grounding question of how he wants his life to be.
- He wants growth only if it does not disrupt his life. A Slack community he launched two years earlier fit his lifestyle badly, and he regretted going against that judgment.
- His regimented day:
  - 6:00-8:30am: coffee and time with his wife, plus 30-45 minutes on social media with his audience.
  - Then a 5-7 mile walk or 90 minutes at the gym, nearly every day, with his wife.
  - About 3 hours of work at his peak, 1-4pm.
  - 4-5pm: open slot for meetings and calls.
  - Evenings free.
- He treats the creator job like a 9-to-5 so that freedom does not turn into drift.

## Choosing what to build on and getting started

- His four-part framework for what you are good at:
  - Skills from your work experience.
  - What you are obsessed with and could talk about for hours.
  - What people ask you for help with.
  - What you have solved for yourself, since others behind you want the same fix.
- It does not need to be the right answer long-term. Five years in, he no longer talks about healthcare tech or SaaS, the topics he started with, and has pivoted three or four times.
- "Start then learn, don't learn then start" (he credits Sahil Lavingia). He bought one book, read it, and went to LinkedIn. He chose LinkedIn because he was afraid of Twitter and nobody there was treating it like Twitter.
- He wrote about what he had lived: building an SDR team, inbound marketing, working with the VP of marketing, setting up customer success.
- Pick the platform you will use daily, because "consistency and persistence are the two most important traits." If it fails, you have eliminated a third of the options.
- He picks "interesting" over "fun". If you pick something you are not interested in, the work is never fun and the foundation is off.
- You need real skill. "It pays to be good at something," so get good first and then show others how. Many creators have no big resume; the easiest route to an interesting online business is doing interesting things in real life.

## From content to first dollar: listen for the problem

- Start by teaching: share what you have learned, the journey, mistakes and wins. If no audience gathers, improve the writing, podcasting or video rather than blaming the idea.
- Then talk to the people who reach out. Use DMs, comments and 10-minute calls to find the recurring problems.
- His first product came from this. He wrote about healthcare SaaS but kept getting asked how he got so much engagement on LinkedIn. He turned that into a 60-minute video called the LinkedIn Playbook, priced at $50 in 2019. It sold about $75K in 18 months, and he first made money while he slept.
- He says he might still be consulting for healthcare companies in 2023-24 if he had not made that choice.
- If you hear the same question 50 times, build a product or service for it even if you do not love it. The lessons transfer to what you care about more.
- "It's not Revenue, it's problems." Examples:
  - Students kept saying they still struggled with the same thing, so he added templates.
  - A big company wanted to own the newsletter sponsorship slot with no competitors, so he built a new, higher-priced package and signed his biggest partner.
- Match the format to the customer problem, not to what you want to do. If they need hand-holding, build a service. If they want to learn on their own, build a course. For monthly tips, use a subscription email. For exposure, use a partnership.

## Discovery calls and selling a service

- There are two kinds of discovery: asking questions to make a sale, and asking because you are curious about the customer's problem. He prefers curiosity, with follow-ups like "why do you think that?" and "who did you hear it from?"
- Specificity wins. When he was a buyer at PatientPop, every SEO agency pitched generic "we do SEO for companies like yours", and he picked the one who described how healthcare SEO had changed.
- Structure of his solution call:
  - Recap the problem in their words: the drop, what they tried, what it costs, the personal toll, the deadline.
  - Reference similar clients and name the differences from their case.
  - Lay out the method in steps, with a checkpoint to test the direction.
  - Give a contingency plan if the checkpoint fails.
  - Give a timeline and cost against the money recovered. His example: recover about $85K for about $5K in about 65 days.
  - Ask for a longer commitment afterward, painting a "positive future" (for example, growing on three more platforms).
- The pricing test: a business exists if you can solve a $30,000 problem for $4,000.
- He dislikes feature dumps. Solve the one problem the person named instead of listing 30 features.
- Use the customer's own language for pains and for the positive future. He writes his content, copy and emails with words people have said to him.

## Courses and the trust tripwire

- Courses have low friction to enter, like books, so he does not blame the medium.
- His "trust tripwire": pick a problem you are 100% sure you can solve, build the best solution on the market, and price it so low it feels like a steal.
  - His LinkedIn course is $150, has 17,000 students, and the most common feedback is that it should cost $1,500.
  - He argues someone who gets a huge return at $150 will trust him at $500 later. He is playing the long game against people selling $2,500 courses with no trust.
  - He captures testimonials with a frictionless process and puts thousands on his landing page. More testimonials raise conversion, which brings more students and more word of mouth. He has 23,000 students and about a 4.98 rating.
- Teach simply. He explained LinkedIn to an 81-year-old friend and uses a "teach a fourth grader" approach. His wife dislikes social media, so he tests courses on her. When she is confused, he finds the step where it breaks.
- Test on people outside your audience. If a newcomer follows a 30-minute video, it works.
- Pre-sell before building. Early courses were sold before they were made. His example of validation: talk about the topic, then offer a pre-sale at $99 against $400 later. If it makes $50K, build it. If not, refund. His newest course had a 16,000-person waitlist, so he built it first.
- He says questions about landing page tools, launch timing or exact follower counts matter less than basics. The basics are find a problem, understand it deeply, learn how to solve it, show you can, build the product or service, and paint a positive future.

## Reducing risk: one audience, many revenue streams

- He admits he worries daily that revenue could vanish (he was fired from his first three jobs). He de-risks by diversifying within one audience.
- The sequence he recommends:
  - Start with a service business using your skill. It is the fastest way to backfill a salary.
  - Quit your job only after 6-9 months of side income at roughly 60-70% of take-home pay and trending flat or up. Ask whether 40 more hours a week could get you to 100%.
  - Niche the service to the customers you love and who love you, then raise rates. Work less for the same money and use the time to build a digital product for people outside your ideal customer profile.
  - Add a newsletter with sponsorships once you have a large following. His example: 50-60K followers could grow a list to 10-15K subscribers, and at $1,000 per issue weekly that is $52K a year.
  - Add narrow, cheap upsells. His monthly templates are 5 social media templates for $9 a month, with 3,000 subscribers, about $27,000 a month, from one email sent.
- His public claim: $5 million in revenue in 1,548 days with no ads and a 92% margin. The tweet says "my little one-person business crossed $5 million".
- He is clear he is an outlier. Of 23,000 students, he knows of 3 who built $1-2M businesses and at least 100 emails from people making $80-100K. He does not promise "be the next me" and presents solopreneurship as an option, not the only path.

## Scaling and building a movement

- Getting from $100K to a million: "You do the thing that got you to $100,000 longer." Revenue has grown with followers, impressions and subscribers, and it is a marathon where most people quit.
- Other levers: eliminate what does not work, automate, outsource what you lack time for, focus on creative work, and understand customer lifetime value and cost to acquire so you know how to spend $1 to make $3.
- Three things to get right: build a movement, use emotional language about people's desired future, and outlast competitors.
- His movement is "life first, business second", which he has talked about since October 2021, and the one-person creator movement. People who buy into a movement are more likely to buy products that get them there, and he hopes for customers for life.
- Movements need an enemy, and it does not have to be a person. His enemy is "living a life of regret". He cites Nike as a commodity turned movement, and credits Russell Brunson for the idea of a common enemy to "throw rocks at".
- Pain is useful. Asked what he would tell his 2018 self, he says he would not wish it away: "you're about to experience something very life-changing, lean into it."
