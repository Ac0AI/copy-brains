## Getting out of the burnout trap and taking back control

- Justin was a VP of Sales who grew a sales team from one person to 150 and hit his target 16 quarters in a row.
- He argues burnout comes from losing control, not working hard: "burnout starts to happen when you lose control." His analogy is Tetris, where each mistake compounds (1 becomes 2, 4, 16) as the pieces fall faster.
- A panic attack in December 2018 forced the change. He stayed about eight more months to recover, started writing online about eight months before quitting, and was later rehired as Chief Revenue Officer.
- He says he "got lucky" because he had no choice but to take a break. He also credits timing: LinkedIn wasn't yet a popular place to write in 2018.
- His advice is not to copy his path or try to be an outlier. Take a gradual, phased approach over many years and be patient.
- Phase 1: find 15 extra minutes a day to get attention (online or local) by sharing your skills and knowledge.
- Phase 2: practice communicating your value for a long time, because "communicating your value is the thing that matters the most."
- Phase 3: monetize a little with coaching, a product or micro-consulting, then grow.
- He says he'd keep the day job until the side income is real. Roughly 60-70% of take-home earnings and 40 hours a week back is the threshold where you're likely to keep growing.

## Time audit: the quarterly 80/20 task ranking

Justin runs this every quarter to decide where his time goes.

1. Pick the intended outcome (e.g. grow business revenue).
2. List every task in a typical week. His own list included writing, ideating, creating content and templates, editing and sending the newsletter, podcasts, login support, tracking setup, booking sponsors and finding affiliates.
3. Log the hours each task takes.
4. Score each task 1-10 on how much it contributes to the outcome. Scheduling a podcast interview by email scores 1/10 for revenue; newsletter quality scores 9/10.
5. Divide value by time to get a ratio, then rank the tasks.
6. Spend your time on the top 20% (e.g. the top 12 of 60 tasks).
7. Deal with the rest in this order: eliminate, simplify, automate (Zapier, AI tools), then delegate.

It's not rigid. If you hate one of the top tasks, outsource, automate or eliminate it and do the other nine.

## Four ways to find your niche

He says he isn't "the authority" but gives four sources:

- **Practical experience and credibility.** He did sales leadership for about 16 years, so after quitting he built a consulting business helping healthcare startups grow. It was an easy transition.
- **A learned obsession.** Something you're obsessed with but don't do for a living. His was digital marketing and solopreneurship: books, podcasts, blogs, tools, lead magnets and webinars. He became an expert without it being in his job title.
- **What people come to you for.** Friends and family say "I know you're the guy who can solve this." People move too fast to notice these signals.
- **Problems you've solved for yourself.** He's currently fixing a problem in his own business and asks how many other creators would pay to solve it.

## Validating an idea: start one-on-one

- Find 5-10 people with the problem who will put skin in the game. The amount doesn't matter.
- Validation means four things: they spend money, they spend time, they show up and do the work, and you get them the outcome you promised.
- "If nobody's willing to spend money and nobody's willing to spend time then you have an idea but you don't have a business."
- The commonalities across those 5-10 people become your framework, then your process.
- Once demand is confirmed, productize it. Raise rates on the service side, lower the price on the product side, and offer a self-paced product for people who don't want one-on-one time. Keep the service for those who want handholding.
- He says this is exactly how he started his business.
- You have to live the customer's problem before you can solve it.

## Marketing: pick an enemy and build a movement

- He compares marketing to politics: candidates spend most of their time on what the other side does wrong. Citing Blair Warren, "help people throw rocks at their enemies."
- The enemy can be a mantra, ideology or movement, not just a person. Having one lets you build a movement against it.
- His enemy is the traditional career path: work for a company until they don't need you, fired, 60 years hoping to save enough to retire. The movement is solopreneurs taking control of their time and freedom.
- He is candid that solopreneurship isn't for everyone and says he's "not a babysitter."
- His example of another creator's version: Dan Go's enemy is the entrepreneur who lets themselves go physically, and his movement is entrepreneurs in the best shape of their lives.
- For an engineering audience, the enemy might be the idea that you must lead people or manage teams to grow. This is Justin's suggestion for the host, not a proven case.

## The four-step funnel: discover, trust, deepen, monetize

- **Get discovered:** put your name, face and message in front of as many people as possible through organic social (LinkedIn, X, Instagram, Threads). His goal was hundreds of millions of impressions a year.
- **Build trust:** use long-form content (newsletter, blog, 25-30 minute YouTube videos, podcasts) to show you're a real expert.
- **Deepen the relationship:** needed for expensive offers (his arbitrary example is $1,000+) via events, communities and real-time access. "We buy from people we know and like."
- **Monetize:** a $50 or $99 offer may not need the deepening step. Someone who has read your posts and newsletter may be ready to buy.

## Optimizing the funnel with data and AI

His improvements come mostly from showing up daily, plus ratio analysis.

- He has made about 5,000 pieces of content over five years and uses AI to find what the top 50 have in common.
- The hook and opener drive impressions. The body and "call to conversation" drive comments and reshares.
- He builds a "perfect post" template covering hook, body, call to conversation and a link to a website article.
- He tracks impressions-to-clicks. A post with 100,000 impressions and 1,000 site visits has a better call to action than one with a million impressions and 1,000 visits.
- He then compares articles on read-to-purchase rate. If one converts at 10% and another at 2%, the first is five times more valuable, so he makes more like it.
- Tools: Tapleo and Tweet Hunter (social analytics), Fathom Analytics (website), Kajabi (purchase history).
- He exports all three into one spreadsheet and uploads it to Claude or ChatGPT for a breakdown. His line: "keep your creativity, let it manage the data."

## Specific vs. broad audience, and the stacked offer ladder

- **Specific path:** talk about the same narrow topic every day. Justin does solopreneurship and Dan Go does founder time and bodies. Your options widen as the audience grows.
  - A micro offer first. His first product was $50, with the goal of delivering far more value than the price and setting up a relationship for the next decade.
  - A core product a few years later.
  - A high-ticket offer once the audience is large (he cites a million to a million and a half followers across platforms). That was the Creator MBA, possible "because I've been beating the same drum for so long." Something even higher ticket could follow.
- **Broad or viral path:** a generic topic like general productivity makes it hard to define a micro offer or say you're the right person for a specific problem. Monetization takes longer but the eventual payback can be bigger. His example is James Clear, whose Atomic Habits took about a decade of building a habits audience before a five-time New York Times bestseller. Justin says he doesn't know exactly how it unfolded for Clear.
- He says no path is wrong, there are outliers in both camps, and the narrow path takes discipline.
- Specificity example: a hired SEO consultant who pitched a new approach to healthcare SEO that lets early-stage companies compete with entrenched ones at a fraction of the price beat generic "I do SEO" pitches. "Be the guy who solves a very specific problem that's painful for a very specific company or person."
- Content should match your pitch. People who visit your profile should find material aligned with what you promised.

## Why he sells low-ticket, self-paced products

- Cohort-based courses weren't really a thing when he started, and Maven hadn't been built, so he didn't consider that route.
- After a year of consulting he was working 40-50 hour weeks of meetings and wanted to decouple time from earnings.
- His hypothesis was that making it cheap and valuable would let word of mouth spread. He says it proved right for him but may not suit everyone.

## Advice for high-earning people with a comfortable job

- His goal isn't to convert people into solopreneurs; it is to show there are other options.
- If big teams, built-in benefits and a steady 9-to-5 motivate you, keep the job.
- Either way, expect that most careers include a layoff at some point.
- Build a profile, share your knowledge and get a "megaphone" while employed. It's much easier to get re-employed with a network that knows you than to start posting after a layoff.
- If you crave autonomy and can handle the risk, start dabbling: share skills, specialize, test monetization. If you can monetize a bit, you can monetize more.
- "Burning the boats is the worst decision that you can make."
- He sends a weekly newsletter, The Saturday Solopreneur, with one tip readable in under four minutes.
