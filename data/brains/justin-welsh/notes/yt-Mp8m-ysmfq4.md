## Why Justin runs an intentionally non-scalable business

- Justin says "I don't think of my business as scalable. And that's okay." He thinks about profit and margins more than revenue, and runs "lifestyle first, business second."
- His reasoning is that income stopped changing his life past a point. He says you can make "500 grand or a million or 2 million or 4 million" and "life doesn't get any better," as long as you can save and support your family. When profit reached "enough," he stopped chasing more.
- He has no employees on purpose (his wife helps operationally; a part-time VA handles some customer service) because he wants to travel, e.g. to Japan, whenever he wants.
- He built this partly as a reaction to managing 8 direct reports and about 150 indirect reports, and to executive time lost on legal and HR messes.
- Early on he felt a business needed staff to be "real." He dropped that, saying "I don't need anyone's validation."
- A seven-figure business changed nothing in daily life: "it doesn't matter. Not nothing in your life changes."

## The simple funnel and weekly operating system

- Time cost: about 3 hours a day, 4 days a week (~12 hours a week) writing content. Everything else is built. No services, no trading time for money.
- Funnel steps:
  1. Social media is discovery and awareness. Post daily on LinkedIn, X, Instagram and Threads, one idea reused across platforms. Show a problem so people think "I have that problem" or "I didn't know I had that problem." Keep the same headshot, name and tagline everywhere.
  2. The newsletter ("The Saturday Solopreneur", readable in 4 minutes or less) builds expertise and authority. Format: the problem, how most people solve it, why that fails, how Justin solves it, why that works better.
  3. At the end, he positions the most relevant product: if this nugget worked, "imagine what you can do if I taught you for 75 minutes."
- No email sequences. No ads. Products are accessible, low cost, quick to consume (watch in about 60 minutes, know exactly what to do next), "tactical and actionable," not theoretical.
- Writing the content as problem, agitate, then tease the solution: "show people the problem, agitate it a little bit, make them feel the problem, twist the knife."

## Pricing: the $50 "trust tripwire" and incremental ladder

- Common advice says "it's just as easy to sell something for $2,500 as for $25." Justin says that is "just not true."
- His first product (2020) was $50. He says it felt risky, but it became "one of the best moves I made." Just under 2,000 people bought it.
- Buyers told him it "could have been $500, could have been $5,000." Delivering 100x to 1,000x value at a low price builds goodwill and a customer pool that buys the next, pricier product at $250 to $500.
- He rejects launching straight into a high-ticket offer and ramped up incrementally instead.
- Selling digital products with a sales background felt comfortable. His line: everything is free online, but implementation is the paid shortcut: "pay me $79 and save yourself 10 hours... watch a 75-minute video and get started."
- Flagship examples: LinkedIn Operating System (about $79 to $97 to $150 range mentioned), Creator MBA (19 hours, 111 lessons, one expensive product at about $879).

## Margins, LTV and the continuity "Trojan horse"

- Margins: about 91%, because acquisition cost is minimal (no ad spend, mostly software and his time).
- Use the main product as a Trojan horse to a monthly continuity offer that feels like a downsell: e.g. five social media templates a month for a few dollars a month.
- Average customer stays about 13 months on that, adding roughly $117 on top of the ~$97 course (about $203 to $206 total).
- With about 45,000 customers and roughly $15 to $20 spent per customer, he cites about a 20x return. He tracks return on his time rather than dollar-in-dollar-out ad math.
- Advice: one core product plus an ancillary monthly product that helps buyers succeed, so revenue continues downstream.
- Referrals: roughly 15 to 18% of his course sales come from student recommendations, which he reads as proof the courses work and are priced correctly.

## Purchasing power parity and course design

- Purchasing power parity: he cuts prices roughly 80% for buyers in lower-income countries (India, Pakistan, Nigeria, Mexico), applied on all his launches. Reason: "I don't think that knowledge should be limited to people based on their economic capability."
- The business case: with an already-recorded digital product, "if you sell digital products, you're missing a lot of revenue, happy customers, word of mouth if you don't do that." Each extra buyer can tell others. He says "making this affordable" is among the most common compliments he gets.
- Product design advice: pick something specific and get someone from point A to point B. Break courses into small, outcomes-based courses (e.g. "set up your lead generation funnel") rather than one giant one, like his own 19-hour Creator MBA, which he calls not the best format.
- Think incrementally: the first, cheap, narrow course unlocks the next problem, which leads to the next and more expensive course. Example order for building a business: identify the skill to monetize, then get attention for it, then the rest.
- Production: no editing or production value; he just sits in a chair and teaches a specific thing in a set time.

## Distribution: maximize one channel, then bolt on others

- Each channel has a ceiling (e.g. he might cap at about 40 course sales a day on LinkedIn).
- Sequence: maximize the primary channel first (LinkedIn, writing about business, from October 2018), then open a secondary one. He added Twitter in October 2021 (about 3 years later), ran the same playbook with a second product, and sold roughly 10 to 15 courses per day there on top of LinkedIn.
- Later he added Instagram (repurposed: screenshot the tweet and add context; grew to about 80,000 followers; a few sales a day), then SEO for organic discovery (another 10 to 20 courses), a "bolt-on strategy."
- Taking about 70% of lessons from one channel to unlock the next is how he builds "a predictable business, maybe not a scalable one."
- Threads (about 45,000 followers) and Instagram are mostly "social proof points": a visitor to his LinkedIn (about 780,000 followers) sees about 550,000 on X and thinks it's not a one-trick pony.
- He does not chase format trends. Algorithms and formats change, so he sticks to fundamentals of human psychology, marketing and sales in a format he likes. He has posted on LinkedIn every day since October 2018 (over 2,200 days) and says consistency only works if you enjoy the format. He does no video so far, but is testing YouTube as a possible next channel.

## Starting out, niche focus and positioning

- Origin: he left a VP of Sales/CRO role in August 2019 (healthcare software business grown from under $1M to about $70M in recurring revenue over about four and a half years) and started writing online. Initially about healthcare SaaS go-to-market; LinkedIn thought leadership was nearly empty then. Inbound leads let him sign five clients on day one (Aug 1, 2019).
- A breakout post was a "fake" perfect morning routine that flipped into his real, mediocre one. It got about 15,000 engagements versus the usual 30 to 40, and attracted people asking how to write content. He then pivoted from healthcare SaaS to teaching content and audience building, since far more people care about that.
- Staying focused: be known for one problem. Example: if you talk about SEO for flower shops, talk about that daily, not Italian food and your dog. People must know "who do you help, and what specific problem do you solve" from your headshot and profile.
- He treats himself as "a character," not a broad brand: relatively serious, business-only content, deliberately aligned to deliver info effectively, even though in person he is "a very unserious person."
- Lesson he sees repeatedly: people undervalue their knowledge ("you don't know until you know"). Skills that feel obvious to you are hard for others, even CEOs asking basic questions.
- Trade time for money first. You can't build a good product until you've helped about 100 customers solve the problem and seen the patterns.

## Community, customer research and the pre-sell survey

- Community: he deliberately avoids a 24/7 Slack-style community because of anxiety. His membership "Unsubscribe" (about 4 months old at recording) is built around life-and-business design, with one chat room, virtual events and in-person dinners in cities such as London, San Francisco, Chicago, Austin and Toronto.
- He spends about 3.5 hours with a table of best customers and says that feedback beats click-button surveys. Intimate dinners give product roadmap input ("here's what we wish you would build next") and keep him close to customers.
- Tools: began on a one-page site plus Gumroad; later ran everything in Kajabi (landing pages, email, website, courses). He then split into best-in-class point tools, which created tech and spending bloat and lost data visibility (he could not tell why a day was good or a week bad). He says all-in-one wins when it works 95 to 99% of the time and that integrated edge cases (churn, failed payments) matter.
- AI view: AI will take a bite out of knowledge businesses, but it gives output, not proven results. Experience-tested courses win on trusted outcomes. He uses AI to ideate, edit and analyze data.
- Product validation assignment (his recommended method): build a simple landing page, send social traffic for a week, and ask:
  1. What is your primary challenge? (A or B)
  2. What have you tried? (A to D)
  3. Why didn't it work? (3 to 4 choices)
  4. What would you want in a solution? (A to D)
  5. A final free-text question for extra thoughts.
- Then feed the free text into ChatGPT or Claude to see what product the audience wants, so you avoid spending 30 to 180 days building something that gets zero sales.
