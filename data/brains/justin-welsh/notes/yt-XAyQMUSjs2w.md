## How Justin built his LinkedIn start and what he wrote early on

- Justin started writing on LinkedIn in late 2018 after watching one of his employees, Kevin Dorsey, do it. His hypothesis was that attention would help if he later left his executive role to go solo.
- He wrote about what he knew, SaaS sales, and wrote almost every day, often several times a day, for about three years.
- His early posts got almost no engagement. He copied the style of Josh Fechter, author of "The Copywriter's Bible" and the father of "LinkedIn broetry" (one line, space, one line), then dropped it because it didn't feel like him. He says writing every day is what lets you find your own groove.
- In April or May 2019 a morning-routine post (a perfect morning versus reality) was seen about 1.5 million times. A venture capitalist had told him over a beer that on Twitter "all you got to do is write your morning routine." Justin deconstructed why it worked and tried to repeat it as often as possible.
- By October 2022 an average post got roughly 200,000 impressions. He had nearly 250,000 LinkedIn followers and over 175 million impressions.
- He set an arbitrary north star of one million followers, imagining the leverage it would give him. He sees perseverance as exciting rather than a chore.
- On the rules of the game: "I'm not trying to get a seat at the cool kids table, I'm trying to build a business." He plays proven formats even when others call them cringe. He thinks of business as a multiplayer game and says people who build an ecosystem of other builders win over those who silo themselves.

## Setting up a LinkedIn profile and picking what you're known for

- Justin compares a profile to the foundation of a house. It should make clear who you are, who you help, what you help them do and how.
- Clean it up with a banner, a headshot, a tagline, an about section and a featured section linking to your website and newsletter.
- Pick the one thing you want to be known for. His line: "Learn how to say one thing a thousand ways. It's better than saying a thousand things one way."
- The headline shows under your name on every post and comment, so it works as a calling card. He sees three approaches:
  - Who you help, what you help them do and how.
  - "Follow me for more of X."
  - A mission. His is "Building a portfolio of one-person businesses to $5M in revenue."
- He notes that "I teach X to do Y through Z" is getting overused. "Ceo at company" tells the reader nothing, and the experience section already covers it.
- On linking out: links in a post dampen reach on LinkedIn and Twitter. Workarounds he uses:
  - A first comment.
  - The featured section.
  - The new link at the top of the profile (the "Grow and monetize your LinkedIn" link).
  - Writing the URL in brackets, such as justinwelsh[.]me.
- He teases his newsletter every Friday with a short summary and the subscribe options. On Saturday he publishes. On Twitter he pre-sells it on Friday and post-sells it afterward with a number ("23,955 people just learned the top four tips for improving their social media profiles").
- He grew the newsletter to almost 25,000 subscribers in 90 days (over 30,000 in the intro), noting this is easier with a large following.

## The creator funnel: awareness, trust, relationship, monetization

- Justin uses a funnel he borrowed from his chief revenue officer experience. Stages:
  1. **Awareness/discovery:** short-form social such as Twitter, LinkedIn, Instagram or YouTube.
  2. **Trust:** the newsletter, which shows how he thinks at a deeper level. It also moves followers off rented platforms onto an email list he owns.
  3. **Deepening the relationship:** access to him, such as a private community, coaching calls and events.
  4. **Monetization.**
- His private community has about 700 members across 57 countries, with a Slack channel. He and his wife have held in-person happy hours in New York, London, San Francisco, LA, Lisbon and Madrid.
- He argues that deep relationships keep customers buying.
- He is open about running people through a funnel: "My goal is to get customers because that's what I do for a living." He also says he makes friends and helps people, and that being transparent about the business is part of how he operates.
- His advice for beginners is to focus on awareness and trust first. He says he'd have waited longer before launching his first product or service, because the leverage from a large following of the right people is "so incredible."

## Expanding from LinkedIn to Twitter and email

- Justin says to pick one platform you enjoy, so you stay consistent. He chose LinkedIn because it was text-heavy and he likes writing.
- Master it until you hit an invisible ceiling. Then add the next platform. He spent about two and a half years on LinkedIn before moving to Twitter in October 2021. What he'd learned there still applied, so Twitter went faster than trying everything at once. He says he tried Instagram, YouTube and TikTok before and "it was kind of a nightmare."
- To seed Twitter from LinkedIn, he used LinkedIn's newsletter feature. LinkedIn promotes a new newsletter once to all your connections. He had about 50,000 LinkedIn newsletter subscribers early on. He wrote a short piece as the newsletter and linked to a tweet thread that continued it. That pulled 50,000 people from their inbox into the thread, which then spread to people who had never heard of him.
- He tries to give every audience a touch point on another platform. His thinking is that some people will like LinkedIn, some Twitter, some the newsletter, and that the goal is to be wherever people want to see him.
- He doesn't recommend being everywhere from the start.
- Established fans help the newsletter. When he publishes, someone on some platform is usually saying something nice about it. Recommendations from others speed up the move from awareness to trust.
- To study growth, he deconstructs how favorite creators grew from zero. He read a Trends.co breakdown of Sahil Bloom going from zero to 560,000 followers. He looks at what Dickie Bush wrote before he was popular and rebuilds it for his own topic. He says he copies "what works."

## Why people spend years on a platform and see no results

- Justin's first reason is not being niche enough. "I help small businesses grow" is a commodity. A narrow, specific topic makes you "the only" rather than one of many. People should land on your profile and think either "this is 100% aimed at me" or "this is 100% not for me." If they're unsure, "you're losing already."
- The second reason is treating it as a hobby instead of a craft. He recommends:
  - Reading books on copywriting.
  - Breaking down other creators' formats, structures and styles.
  - Studying marketingexamples.com (Harry Dry) and verygoodcopy.com (Eddie Shleyner).
  - Treating it like a job if you have the time.
- The third reason is not understanding networking. He gets about 100 "can I pick your brain for 60 minutes" requests a day. He says online networking works like offline networking, with give and take and value added.
- On content ideas: "Do interesting things offline to be interesting online." Going out, figuring out something hard, and sharing it simply beats talking a good game.
- His own networking approach is to connect with people at a similar follower level, who aren't too busy, and support each other's content. He moved up to people like Sahil Bloom, Dickie Bush, Dan Koe and Josh Spector as he grew. He has three to five of these calls a week, mostly to build friendships, and he tries to stay around people who are smarter or bring different skills.

## Building products from audience demand and pre-selling

- Justin lets the audience decide what to build: "It doesn't matter what I want. What matters is what the customers want."
- **Pre-sell rule:** he never builds anything unless he makes a certain amount on it first. He announces it ("this is coming in 45 days, it's on sale now"), and once sales cross a threshold he builds it.
- **LinkedIn OS:** he had an older $50 LinkedIn playbook from early 2020. He noticed a trend in DMs and emails from buyers saying it was dated and asking whether a new one was coming. He treated that as an indicator and pre-sold the replacement.
- **Content OS:** after a Ship 30 for 30 podcast with Dickie Bush and Nicholas Cole, where he showed his behind-the-scenes content system, many people emailed asking to buy it. He pre-sold it, built it and shipped it.
- **Monthly templates:** buyers of the Content OS then asked for ready-made templates. He added a $9/month one-click upsell and noticed others charging a fortune for similar things. It became a $9,000 MRR product in 45 days.
- Total earnings from his two courses as a solo creator is over $2 million, per the intro.
- His content system uses templates, for example one for "this topic is dying." In the example, the strong negative word is "the nine to five," the opposite is a strong positive statement ("the great resignation is growing faster than ever"), and he fills in the template. A post built this way got 2.4 million impressions.
- His daily structure is a content matrix with prompts such as "educate me," "motivate me," "make me think," "empathize with me," plus newsletter follow-up and previous-week newsletter. He wants to remove thinking when creating. For "empathize with me" he often throws rocks at the nine to five as "the enemy."

## Making cheap, fast courses

- Justin delivers courses as simple Zoom recordings with slides, not high-production video. He recorded from his office in Nashville.
- He was inspired by Chris Johnson ("Wealth Squad Chris"), the highest-paid creator on Gumroad. Justin bought a $40 Twitter-growth course that was a 40-minute Zoom with black-and-white slides, and he got a foundation to start from.
- He contrasts this with seven-hour courses like Sam Ovens' Consulting Accelerator: "I don't have seven hours."
- His design goal: "get people from point A to point B as fast as possible as cheaply as possible" so they can finish in one sitting and have enough information to take action.
- He doesn't use private planes or skyscrapers to look richer than he is. Keeping it cheap and simple makes it easy to ship fast.
- He admits he assumes others are like him, a risky assumption.

## Workload, hiring, and setting expectations at a day job

- Justin has no traditional employees. Six weeks before the interview he hired his first virtual assistant, from Virtual Staff Finder. Constant context switching from emails and DMs on LinkedIn and Twitter hurt his writing focus. His wife also helps with the community.
- He says he is a workaholic and was at the computer from about 6 a.m. to 7 p.m. Overwork at his previous job led to weight gain, heavy drinking and poor sleep, and he didn't want to repeat that, so he spent money and time on help.
- At his previous job he set expectations with his team and boss before writing on LinkedIn, explaining why it was good for the company, which helped recruiting. He says doubters later joined in.
- To someone lower down the ladder, he says to tell your manager that building your professional brand matters to your career. If a boss refuses, it's a candidate's market and he'd go to a company that encourages it.
