## Starting a one-person business: service first, then product

Justin's core sequence for a beginner:

1. Pick the people you like spending time with. "Misery doesn't scale."
2. Choose people 2-3 years behind you in their journey (e.g. a sales manager helping account executives who want to become managers).
3. Talk to them for free for 15 minutes and learn what blocks them.
4. Sell a service first. He prefers a simple coaching business, with a no-brainer price such as 100 dollars for an hour.
5. Sell to about 20 people. That is 2,000 dollars, and it shows the most common, most painful problem.
6. Find the one fix that works every time, then turn it into a self-guided product (e.g. "become a sales manager in 90 days").
7. Raise service rates once the product exists, from 100 to about 250 an hour.
8. Send people who can't afford the service to the product, and people who don't want to self-learn to the service.
9. Repeat with other groups. The end state is about five impulse-buy products and five higher-ticket service offers.

He says he is "a big fan of service before product" because the service teaches you what the product should be. He turned his own service businesses into products this way.

## Why a one-person business is mostly marketing

- The most common misconception: having a niche and a solution is "only two pieces of the pie."
- The missing piece is attention: people knowing who you are, what you do and why.
- That means copywriting, hosting podcasts or live video, running YouTube, building Instagram.
- "Doesn't matter if you know how to solve a problem. If people don't know about you, it's all a moot point."
- He read Cashvertising, Ogilvy on Advertising and Russell Brunson's books on creating characters.
- He says getting attention is addicting, so you keep studying and writing.

## Be the niche of one

- Competition worry: "There's a million of me", but he treats more people entering as validation, not competition.
- "I'm the brand, I'm the niche. The niche is me. And when the niche is me, I don't compete with anyone else."
- He tells people to be entertaining, humorous, funny or polarizing, so people want to follow them.
- He references Kevin Kelly's 1,000 true fans.
- His own example: he built a sales org at PatientPop from zero to 50 million. He could have said Mark Roberge (HubSpot, zero to 100 million) is "the expert", but many people struggle to reach a million and need someone closer to their stage.

## Starting on LinkedIn (late 2018)

- He chose LinkedIn because a former report, Kevin Dorsey, was writing there as a sales thought leader.
- Twitter then looked like "news" to him, and he didn't want video or Instagram.
- He first tried a video series, long blog posts and mini webinars. Then one post got roughly 3-4 million impressions, and he committed to writing daily.
- LinkedIn is seen as a resume or jobs site, so writing there "like people use Twitter" made him stand out.
- His goal was to become a better writer. He treats it as a platform-agnostic practice: "you're just typing into a box and hitting send."
- He applied his sales-leader habits: analyze the data, double down on what works, cut what doesn't. He studied formatting, being compelling and being polarizing.

## Getting initial traction and post structure

Two early levers:
- Comment under large accounts, with value, and be first.
- LinkedIn has no notification bell, so he suggests messaging 3-5 favorite thought leaders to ask when they post.
- He says this alone can take you from 1,000 to 10,000 followers.
- Publish consistently with a fixed structure.

His post framework:
- Write the meat first: what you want to teach (steps, like a Twitter thread).
- Then the hook: line 1 is the scroll stopper, line 2 is the validator, line 3 gets the click on "see more".
- End with a recap (a TLDR so people can join the conversation without rereading) and a call to conversation ("what would you add? agree, disagree?").

## Content system and cross-platform "suction"

- He built a "content matrix": topics down the left, structures across the top (observations, step-by-step guides, how-tos, analytical, X vs Y, past vs present, listicles).
- Once a week (Saturday morning), he pairs topics with structures and writes about 10 headlines, then drafts about 10 pieces in 2 hours.
- Tools: Hypefury for scheduling and templates, Hemingway app for readability (clear, low grade level).
- He posts the same content to LinkedIn, Twitter, Instagram and his website.
- Audience at the time: about 130,000 LinkedIn followers and 50,000 newsletter subscribers.
- Suction trick: post an article on LinkedIn, share a snippet and redirect to Twitter. LinkedIn followers like the tweet, which exposes it to strangers.
- Result he cites: roughly 5 weeks on Twitter after he started, taking him to about 22,000 followers.
- His daily rhythm: post at 7:15 a.m. Central, then 45 minutes of engagement.

## Selling: organic reach, impulse-buy pricing, and the promotion flywheel

- He has never run paid ads and doesn't know how. He'd consider them for a SaaS with strong lifetime value, and would hire someone to run them.
- His belief: a large organic following plus clear positioning sells more than ads.
- Pricing: "impulse buy" products. First product 50 dollars. Current product 150 dollars, often with a 25-30% discount. No 1,200 dollar masterclasses, because they need a lot of trust.
- He calls these "trust tripwires": a low-cost, high-value product that earns trust.
- Promotion steps:
  1. Prove expertise (every post gets 100,000 or more impressions).
  2. Show money results (screenshots of revenue from LinkedIn and products).
  3. Describe the outcome of a big following: job offers, podcast and stage invites, sales, awards.
  4. Name the gap, offer a "peek behind the curtain" (his copywriting friend Eddie Shlainer's "curtain language"), then state the price.
- Flywheel: buyers get a video-testimonial request 2 weeks later. He has about 2,500 students and hundreds of video testimonials, auto-fed to the landing page. Conversion rises as more testimonials arrive.
- He says everything in his paid product has been shared free somewhere. People pay for aggregation: everything in one place and getting from A to B in an hour.

## Private community and diversification

- His private community costs 99 dollars per quarter, another impulse-buy price.
- He tried yearly first, but it hurt his optionality (stuck for 9 more months if he dislikes running it).
- Quarterly keeps people long enough to see value (about 90 days) and keeps him able to close in 3 months. He hasn't tested monthly.
- It gives him about 2,000 people to whom he can drop links for future products.
- He frames it as a diversified portfolio of income and distribution.

## Small bets, diversification and bios

- Advice he thinks people skip: spread risk. "I'd rather build 10 50,000 dollar businesses than one 500,000 dollar business." He credits Daniel Vassallo's small-bets thinking.
- Pattern he describes: build a service business, productize it once understood, then build another.
- Vision: instead of product studios, a "distribution studio" where creators with large audiences bundle reach (he imagines collectively 500 million impressions a year) and take equity in products they launch.
- Platform-specific bios: LinkedIn says "one-person businesses" (solopreneur wording resonates); Twitter says "no-code businesses" because it is tech-heavy, and he wants to signal he's not an engineer.
- He now calls himself an entrepreneur, not a "personal brand": "a walking business," not a brand.

## Habits that support the business

- Background: after about 10 years in high-growth SaaS (Zocdoc, PatientPop), he burned out. He says burnout is about loss of control, not hours worked.
- After a panic attack in December 2018, he stepped down and stayed about 8 more months. He quit alcohol for 90 days.
- With his wife he walked 10 miles a day (3 miles three times) and lost about 40 lb.
- Today's schedule: coffee with his wife from 6:00, post at 7:15, 45 minutes of engagement, walk or gym, about 90 minutes of writing daily.
- Tuesday to Thursday afternoons are for advising early-stage SMB SaaS clients. Monday and Friday are for business systems: eliminate, automate, delegate.
- He plans to test moving writing to the morning, when he's sharpest, and exercise to the afternoon.
- His principle: do more of what gives energy and less of what doesn't.
