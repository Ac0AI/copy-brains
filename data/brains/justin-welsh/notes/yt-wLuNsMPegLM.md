## Building a content system: start with one daily habit and add structure as friction appears

- Justin says there is no single right system, and his own is just one version.
- To start, he'd make a system out of producing one piece of content a day. Most people "read a lot, study a lot, research a lot" and call it "procrastination dressed up as action."
- Simplest system: block 30 minutes on the calendar every day for content.
- Each time you hit the block, new friction shows up, and you add one thing to remove it:
  - Don't know what to write: add prompting questions ("did I learn anything today?", "did I help someone?", "what do people ask me?").
  - Can't get it into a good format: add format templates, such as listicle templates.
- The system grows iteratively, and after four years his is close to automated and runs faster each time.
- His system exists because he is forced to do everything himself. He has no employees and no ghostwriter. His only helper is a virtual assistant who handles customer service and support tickets, with no content work.
- His output includes a weekly newsletter, 12 LinkedIn pieces, 12 tweets, a Twitter thread, and events like this one. Without a system he would burn out, as he did at PatientPop.

## Hub-and-spoke: one newsletter as the pillar, chopped into 5-7 pieces of short-form content

- Ideation is the starting point of all content. Set aside 15-30 minutes a day to brain dump ideas, however good or bad. He keeps a running Notion page with 150+ ideas.
- Two ways to run the flow:
  - Short form first: turn one idea into several posts, see which resonate, then extend the winner into a newsletter, blog or guide. The posts act as small tests in the field.
  - Justin's way, the opposite: ideate, write the newsletter, then chop the newsletter into 5-7 pieces.
- Result: he always has one pillar piece (the newsletter) plus outlying posts that all point back to it.
- Social posts can say "if you want to learn more, go to my newsletter." That moves thousands of people off social platforms onto his website, where they can subscribe, buy a course, sign up for coaching, sponsor the newsletter, or learn more about him.
- One idea can become many angles. Example idea "four fastest ways to grow a YouTube channel" becomes:
  - a story about someone who grew a channel
  - an observation about five channels you watch
  - eight tools top channels use
  - a present vs. future take (how creators will work in 2027)
  - the most uncommon channels that are growing fast
- Same lenses on a newsletter about LinkedIn copywriting: a story, an observation, a listicle (top eight LinkedIn copywriters), a contrarian take (why a popular formula doesn't work on LinkedIn), present vs. future.

## His weekly workflow: ideate, research, write with a template, edit, chop, distribute

1. **Ideate.** Two 30-minute blocks a week. Aim for 6-10 unique ideas a week while needing only one, so the bank keeps growing. He picks the idea Monday morning. Sources:
   - favorite YouTubers' channels sorted by most popular (popular videos signal topics people care about)
   - curated newsletters in his inbox, e.g. a piece on getting your first 5,000 Twitter followers prompts "my own spin" on it, not a copy
2. **Research.** Google, advanced Twitter search and LinkedIn search for a quote, article, book, tweet or post that backs up or adds context to his point. He also looks for contrarian takes.
3. **Write the newsletter through the same template every week.** Writing a newsletter is hard and most people quit, so templates are the fix. His structure: introduce a problem, why it matters, how most people try to solve it, why that's wrong, then how he would solve it.
4. **Edit with a checklist of questions.** Is it clear and concise? Does it deliver on the headline's promise? Is everything linked? Are there illustrations? Is it easy to read? Any rambling run-on sentences?
5. **Chop and distribute.** Push the finished newsletter through the lenses above to make posts for LinkedIn and Twitter.

He summarizes the system as "ideation, research, writing, editing, chopping and distributing."

## Newsletter copywriting formula: Pain, Amplification, Intrigue, Positive future, Solution

- Justin uses a formula he made up, a take on Pain-Agitate-Solution. He calls it Pain, Amplification, Intrigue, Positive Future, Solution.
- Steps:
  - **Pain:** state a problem his audience has. He knows them because after four years in the space, readers tell him. Example: you spend an hour on a post, 30 minutes editing, then hit publish and get "crickets."
  - **Amplification:** twist the knife and make it existential. You miss podcast invites, can't build your business, can't drive your career forward, can't get attention.
  - **Intrigue:** set up the content, e.g. "what if I told you there were five tips most people don't use on LinkedIn to write faster, better, more predictably and at scale?"
  - **Positive future:** describe the outcome, e.g. content in 10 minutes, 5x engagement, new opportunities, a podcast appearance, your first business.
  - **Solution:** "here's how I would do it."
- Rationale: it takes the reader top to bottom through human psychology and emotion. Most of his writing is "psychological" rather than just putting ideas on paper and hoping they're interesting.
- Topics stay on one theme, building a one-person business, with subtopics: audience growth, efficiency and productivity, monetization, products and services.

## Writing for each platform: study why you stop scrolling, then obsess over the opening

- Never copy-paste a newsletter into social posts. Reformat for each platform.
- His first trick is being cognizant. When he scrolls Twitter or LinkedIn, he asks why he stopped before reading the post. Was it the first line, a word in the second line, a number, a big number, a combination?
- Second, understand how people make decisions:
  - **Twitter thread:** mostly the opening tweet matters. If it's strong, people commit to the rest.
  - **LinkedIn:** the lines above the "see more" fold matter most. A click on "see more" is a commitment to read.
- His formula for LinkedIn posts:
  - a first line that "punches you in the face" (he credits Sean Perry)
  - empathy or context in the second line
  - a hook line before the see more, teased "like a radio teaser" to get the click
- Emotion drives sharing: belonging, pain, education, entertainment, empathy.
  - Personal-life platitudes (for example, a restaurant's egg sandwich) don't interest people as much as you think.
  - He aims to elicit a positive or sometimes polarizing reaction, not to be "divisive, cruel, angry, nasty." The aim is to make people think. "No one's ever going to like you" across the whole crowd, so say something you believe with intention.

## Choosing a niche: start tight, be "the only," and bring a non-obvious solution

- Start with the smallest, tightest niche, where you can become an expert and resonate deeply with a smaller audience. Then add larger concentric circles around it as you grow. Justin says Monday leadership, Tuesday fintech, Wednesday government never gains traction.
- Example: don't say "I help small businesses grow." Say you work with flower shops doing six figures to take them past seven figures with email marketing. You are then probably the only person in that niche and have no competition.
- Two steps:
  1. Niche down so you're the only, or one of 5-10, people in the space.
  2. Bring a non-obvious solution to an obvious problem. Agencies that all pitch "Facebook ads for SaaS companies" are commodities that race to the bottom on price. He cites Chris Walker, who talks about "dark social" and argues marketing teams are doing it wrong.
- Justin's own path: entrepreneur, then solopreneur (one person, no employees), then diversified solopreneur. He is building five revenue streams toward $5 million in recurring revenue, a very specific phrase and goal. He says that on that path he is one of a very few options for people who want it.
- Once you've built an audience, you can bring in more interests. Fans who followed you for one reason will follow you into others. Be patient: start with one and expand.
- He keeps his craft-beer interest off LinkedIn.

## From discovery to trust to impulse buy; engagement pods and growth

- Justin says he rarely makes a traditional "ask." He thinks of the path as awareness, then trust, then purchase:
  - **Discovery:** two tweets and two LinkedIn posts a day.
  - **Trust:** the longer-form newsletter, free guides, articles and blog posts, which move people from social platforms to his website where they see how he thinks.
  - **Purchase:** priced as an impulse buy. He has no $2,000 masterclass or $4,997 coaching program. He sells $150 courses.
- He positions products "very passively": if you have one of these three problems, grab one of these courses for $150.
- Distribution: be where the audience is. His newsletter is on the web and by email, and he plans to add it as a LinkedIn newsletter (he has about 50,000 LinkedIn newsletter subscribers). He doesn't force email signups.
- Newsletter growth: 53,000 subscribers 10 months after launch. Many people read each Saturday on the website and never give an email, and he's fine with that.
- He is considering a podcast that reads the newsletter with extra context, since some people prefer to listen. Format audiences overlap less than people expect.
- Repetition: he has explained this content system about 30 times, yet people still say they've never seen it. People forget what you posted; his wife didn't remember a post she saw a year earlier.
- Engagement pods:
  - Justin reached 300,000 LinkedIn followers and about 70,000 Twitter followers without ever joining one.
  - He declines "trade a like for a like" requests.
  - He builds real relationships instead. Example: he and Austin Belsack met on LinkedIn four years ago, traded ebooks, recommendations and client introductions, and now support each other's content. "He's my friend."
  - Pod audiences are false engagement, not the right customers.

## Differentiating a local service business and running a company page

- For small businesses in a small town, Justin says to look at your four or five largest competitors, who probably all use similar messaging, features and benefits. Instead:
  - Create "existential pain" and show you understand the prospect's problems better than competitors do, so they say "you must be in my head."
  - Say what competitors will recommend (A, B, C and D) and explain why you don't think those are best. Frame it as the game changing.
  - Present a different process, such as a custom audit that leads to a custom strategy.
  - Then quote prices and propose a next step.
- He says nobody cares that you're cheaper or have been around longer. "They care that you can solve their problems."
- On LinkedIn company pages, Justin says to be an industry expert and an incredible resource, not a pitch for your offering. Shine a light on other people in the industry so they give the page love in return. A personal page can add opinions and step-by-step teaching "two to three steps behind you on the journey."
