## Raised to run, not to rest: how Justin's work ethic got built

- Justin's dad grew up poor, in a mill-town family where nobody went to college, and became the first in the family to get a white-collar job.
- His dad's rules: work hard, be excellent at your job, "pay yourself first," and save.
- Justin took a McDonald's job at 14 on July 11, 1995, got fired, and was banned from that location.
- He became the youngest manager at Burger King at 16, running the store from 6 a.m. in a shirt and tie.
- He then followed his dad into pharmaceuticals and med devices and hated it. He says he didn't care how beta blockers or angiotensin receptor blockers worked.
- His conclusion: "I don't like doing things I don't like to do and I don't think I'll ever be good at doing things I hate."
- Around age 28 his dad's lessons "came rushing back" and he became "my dad in a really weird way." Work ethic is the one that stuck.
- He argues that grinding through work you hate was more necessary in past decades. Today, with resources and connections, most of your time can go to things that are at least interesting.

## Why he works hard without burning out

- Justin says he doesn't see burnout as working too hard. He can work hard and not burn out if he loves what he's doing.
- He defines burnout as a loss of control: being unable to affect the outcomes the job demands.
- As VP of sales and then CRO he grew the company past $50M in about 4.5 years. He started in 2015 at 32 or 33, expecting to reach $1M in run rate and then be replaced by someone more experienced.
- Each new revenue level, at $20M, $25M and $30M, brought problems he had never seen. His team and responsibility grew daily and problems stacked like Tetris pieces.
- He coped by sleeping less, eating more and drinking heavily for about a year, which compounded the stress.
- He had a panic attack on December 16, 2019, in year four, the morning after a Christmas party. His wife called 911, and he had a second one later.
- He says numbing emotions with food or alcohol only dulls them, and they come back stronger.
- He fixed the basics immediately: daily walks with his wife totaling 8-10 miles, no alcohol for 90 days, and home cooking instead of takeout.
- Telling his CEOs he needed to leave took the pressure off. They kept him on for 6-7 months with extra support, and he "went out on top," hitting every target in those last months.
- He left healthy, lean and sleeping well, and launched his own business in August 2019.

## The five-year arc and why he starts over

- Justin describes every job he's had as a curve. Enjoyment starts high, dips when the real difficulty hits, climbs once he figures it out, and peaks around year three or four.
- After the peak it degrades slowly toward what he calls a five-year shelf life.
- That held at his sales jobs and at patientPop. He's in year five of his current business and doesn't expect to do this for much longer.
- He wants to "live multiple acts": reach the top of one field, quit, then start again at the bottom of another. He says he's "fascinated by paths."
- He believes he could go open the best restaurant in his county despite not knowing how to run one, and he says that confidence makes shiny-object syndrome a real danger.
- He later softens the five-year idea. The urge to do new things can be satisfied "without burning the boats."
- He is currently investing in healthcare companies he believes in, because he wants to be helpful in that space again.

## How the solo business actually started (and the goal he set)

- He did not start out planning to build an online business. In August 2019 he began a healthcare-tech consulting practice, expecting it to be a small side thing before he went back to a VP or CRO job.
- Clients were early-stage healthcare companies under $1M in revenue. He had "seen that movie before," so client work was not stressful.
- The stress that did exist was operational, like automation breaking or not knowing how to bill someone. None of it had a deadline, so he could fix it in his spare time.
- Consulting traction made him realize the solo life paid better and let him serve many clients.
- His first digital product then sold, which showed him a larger world beyond healthcare SaaS, with social media and building your own business as topics.
- Around mid-2020 he set a goal of making $5M in five years. It felt impossible at the time and was a deliberate stretch goal. He says he loves numbers, goals and checking boxes, and stretched the target further as the business grew.
- He treats money as an indicator of success. When he started he wanted to set the standard for the work, not just recoup his salary, and he notes that many creators settle near their old salary.

## Why LinkedIn worked: first-mover timing, not the solopreneur topic

- Justin started on LinkedIn in 2018 because he felt less intimidated than on Twitter or YouTube, where everyone else was creating. He calls that a lucky choice that gave him a head start.
- He says he brought the creator mindset to the platform when almost nobody was writing like that there.
- Most people in jobs couldn't say what they thought publicly, and he had just quit, so he could simply show up and post.
- He wrote about healthcare SaaS for years. He didn't pivot to the solopreneur topic until around 2022.
- By then he had 4 years of posting and roughly 300,000-350,000 followers, and many followed him through the pivot.
- His view: the solopreneur topic is "the iceberg above the water." The four years as one of the few creators on the platform is the part that isn't visible.
- His age helped too. He started his business at 38, and he says he'd have been too immature at 24 to build an internet business well.

## Building the product: first course and the Creator MBA

- His first digital product was a LinkedIn course. He says it took him "maybe 90 hours" to build.
- It was his favorite build because everything was new: framing a narrative, scripting a video course, becoming an instructor, pricing it, promoting it, designing landing pages and setting up emails. He says courses are about stories, with you as the guide on someone's journey.
- The Creator MBA is built to teach thinking, not paint-by-numbers steps. He says a business can't be built that way, so it gives frameworks like "if you're here, ask these three questions, then consider these five things."
- Its content draws on his knowledge plus that of other people he knows who built successful businesses.
- He enjoys unlocking friends' businesses. When someone can't make money, he often spots the problem and the fix right away, and he finds it rewarding when they then do.

## Outsourcing a launch to Brennan Dunn

- For the Creator MBA launch he hired Brennan Dunn for segmentation and personalization. Justin calls it the first time he'd outsourced heavy work.
- It started with a half-day consulting session that Nathan Barry, CEO of ConvertKit, introduced. Justin had moved to ConvertKit to build a better newsletter. After the session he asked what it would cost for Brennan to do the work instead.
- Brennan's customer acquisition cost was close to nothing, because Justin paid him to acquire him as a customer.
- They worked asynchronously over WhatsApp for roughly three to five months, building the personalization and segmentation, with Justin paying him to do it.
- Justin chose Brennan because they share high standards and neither is "internet markety" (no "Lambos, private planes"). Brennan is practical, thoughtful and valuable.
- The launch was a success. Justin then walked his private companion community through how it was built.
- He plans to work with Brennan again to segment and personalize more of the business and automate it, so he can keep creating content and get more return per piece.

## What he does and doesn't outsource; how he guards his time

- He never wants to outsource the creative work: writing, videos and building his products.
- He likes building websites and systems and says he'd happily spend five hours on a site he never publishes. Combining system-building with creative writing at once is what wears him out, for example designing an email segmentation while writing the emails.
- Solopreneurs can't outsource everything, because many small tasks still have to be done by the owner, and switching costs are high.
- He cut himself to a 3-day work week this year, Tuesday through Thursday. He says engaging on LinkedIn or Twitter for about 30 minutes in the morning doesn't feel like work to him.
- He keeps distractions off. A WhatsApp message during deep work threw him off, and he normally has notifications muted.
- He admits he checks his numbers daily and worries about money, partly from his dad. A drop in sales can keep him up at night, and he tends to think he can "effort" his way to a fix.
- To manage this he has done therapeutic training on money, run the math on his finances, and concluded that even if the business dropped 80% the family would be fine. He says this reduced the worry.
