## The viral opener template: relatable enemy, hero, gasoline plus teaser

Justin treats a LinkedIn opener as the whole game. A post has a 3,000-character limit, but only about 210 characters show "above the fold" before the "see more" button. His framing: "You have 210 characters to get people interested in the rest of it." A LinkedIn post works like a Twitter thread, where the first lines decide whether anyone reads the rest.

- Format the opener as 3 short written lines with blank lines between them (3 lines of text, 2 lines of space) so it is easy to read.
- Lines and spacing alone do not make a post viral; the written content does.
- On February 21, 2022 he wrote a post purely to test whether he could produce something viral. He notes his goal is never to go viral, but it was a fun experiment.
- Result: 4.766M impressions, 50,583 engagements, 1,444 comments, 1,589 shares, his most successful LinkedIn post at the time.

## Step 1: The scroll-stopper (relatable enemy plus negativity)

Justin says the first line "is more important than 95% of the rest of the post. It must be excellent if you want people to read your LinkedIn content."

- Pick a "relatable enemy" the audience already dislikes, then "throw rocks" at it so readers stop scrolling to join in.
- His example: he knew much of his audience dislikes their 9 to 5 jobs, so the enemy was "The 9 to 5".
- Pair the enemy with a negative word so the audience leans in "in glee".
- Formula: "The {RelatableEnemy} is {Negativity}". Example: "The 9 to 5 is getting pummeled."
- Talking negatively about a relatable enemy should draw a positive response from the crowd.

## Step 2: Flip the script (the hero gains ground)

Once the reader has the positive feeling from line one, he accelerates it by painting life without the enemy.

- Choose the hero, the opposite of the enemy, and say it is gaining ground.
- He compares it to politics: a candidate says the opponent is weakening, then flips to say their own candidacy is gaining ground, which "usually whips the crowd into a frenzy".
- Formula: "The {Hero} is {StrongPositiveStatement}". Example: "The great resignation is growing faster than ever."

## Step 3: Gasoline plus teaser (the click to "see more")

The last line must take the frenzied reader and hook them into clicking "see more".

- "Pour gasoline on the fire" by saying he loves it: he loves seeing the enemy beaten down and the hero strengthening, as if everyone is crushing the enemy together.
- Then lob a simple teaser question. The "Why?" exists only to get readers past "see more".
- Formula: "And I {gasoline}. {TeaserQuestion}?" Example: "And I love it. Why?"
- The result tarnishes the crowd's enemy, champions the hero, and whips readers into a frenzy.

## The full reusable template

```
The {RelatableEnemy} is {Negativity}

The {Hero} is {StrongPositiveStatement}

And I {Gasoline}. {TeaserQuestion}?
```

## Turn winners into templates

Justin's repeatable process: "For every post that I create that performs well, I analyze the post, turn it into a template, and reuse it multiple times." Instead of chasing new ideas each time, he reverse-engineers what worked in a top performer, extracts the structure into fill-in-the-blank slots, and reuses it.

## LinkedIn versus Twitter writing

- Twitter content arrives as short tweets of 280 characters or less, easy to skim.
- LinkedIn posts are long-form feed items, so the opener has to earn the click.
- He notes LinkedIn has shifted from a professional "resume" site to a place where creators build 7-figure businesses, and that creators like Sahil Bloom, Nicolas Cole, Dickie Bush and Jack Butcher post there daily.
- Writing on LinkedIn differs from Twitter, and new creators often "hear crickets".
