## The core thesis: one problem, one audience, one roadmap

Justin argues that most first online businesses fail because of a stack of wrong choices: niche, messaging, ideal customer, pricing structure, product offering. His fix is narrowness at the start.

- Pick one problem that one very specific group of people share. Not "all problems" or "all people."
- Find it by looking backward: a problem he solved for himself a few years ago.
- Example: you and your friends at a local state college all wanted a job at a great tech company, but only you figured out how. That is a solution to a common problem.

## Package the solution as a roadmap (Point A to Point B)

- "We overestimate how much people want to learn at once." Most people want to solve one problem: get from point A to point B "easily, quickly, and affordably."
- Craft the offer as a roadmap or blueprint aimed at a specific audience.
- Example offer: "The 14-day roadmap that shows state college graduates how to land their dream job at Google."

## Product format and hosting: pick something simple and finish it

- Format matters much less than the problem and the roadmap. Pick whatever you are likely to complete: writers make an eBook, teachers record a video course, systems people build digital materials.
- Uses Parkinson's Law ("work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion") to justify timeboxing. Set a specific deadline; he suggests starting with something fast, like 28 days.
- For hosting, don't overcomplicate it. He recommends Stan Store or something similarly simple. "Get complicated down the road. Just make sales now."

## Pricing: $50 to $150 for a first product

- Charge between $50 and $150. That is affordable enough to get buyers without needing years of built trust.
- The goal is to deliver thousands of dollars' worth of value, if not more, relative to the price.

## Finding the first customers: test channels, then triple down

- Justin says the best place to find customers is LinkedIn.
- Other channels he lists: articles on Medium or your own site, Twitter, Twitter snapshots reposted on Instagram, Quora answers, IndieHackers and Makerpad posts, subreddits, and Facebook groups.
- It is work, but it teaches you which distribution channels lead to sales. "You want to find which of these channels leads to sales and then triple down."

## Social proof: turn customers into testimonials

- Once you have a few customers, automate testimonial collection. He uses Testimonial.to, triggered at the midway point and end of each of his courses.
- Display testimonials everywhere: website, landing page, social media, articles, blogs.
- "Social proof sells." His course pages are deliberately heavy with it.
- Result he cites for The Operating System page: 4.7% of visitors buy, leading to roughly $130k+ in sales every 30 days for that product.

## A repeatable selling system and the math behind a 6-figure product

- Every good online business has a repeatable system: know exactly where you post each day, at what time, and why.
- Track separately which content drives impressions and engagement versus which drives visits and purchases ("They are different"). Intersperse both types: attention content, then conversion content.
- Target: find 4 people per day who will buy a $75 course. 4 x $75 x 365 = $109,500, a 6-figure product.
- Work backward from the target. With a 3.5% site conversion rate, you need to drive about 114 people to your site daily. Find your best channel and get 114 people there with something that piques their interest.
- He frames the path as requiring "a long-term growth mindset, patience, and an engaged audience on a social platform of your choice."
