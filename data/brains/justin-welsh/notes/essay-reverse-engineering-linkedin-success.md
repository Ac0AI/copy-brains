## How Justin breaks a LinkedIn post into four testable parts

Justin treats LinkedIn content as a data problem rather than a guessing game. He started using data on LinkedIn in the summer of 2019, when he was about to quit his job and launch a sales consulting business. He had about 20K followers (building the presence since late 2018), posted about sales a few times a week and got decent engagement. Coming from a sales leadership background, he asked: "What if I bring this approach to LinkedIn?"

- He argues most LinkedIn content is guesswork: people post what they hope gets traction with no data suggesting it will perform. "Guessing is not a strategy."
- He breaks every post into four components: the opener (hook), the body, the call-to-conversation (CTC), and the optional image.
- For each component he tracks a specific metric, which lets him keep improving content quality, engagement and efficiency.
- His analytics tool for this is Taplio. He also uses Short.io to track clicks and click-through rates on posts that contain links.

## Optimizing the opener: sort by impressions

The opener decides whether a reader clicks "...more" or keeps scrolling, which Justin calls arguably the most important part of the post.

- Method: in Taplio, sort posts by impressions, then look at the top 10 posts and study what the openers have in common.
- Recent finding for his audience: openers made of two short, mobile-optimized lines outperform other styles.
- Caveat: what works for him may not work for others. He says to test different approaches and let the data guide you.

## Optimizing the body: sort by likes

The body is where the post delivers on the promise made in the opener.

- Method: sort Taplio data by likes. His hypothesis: "If more people like a post, then they likely read it to the end. That means I did something right."
- From the top 10 posts by likes, he studies body length, structure and content type.
- Finding for him: medium-length posts that mix short sentences with bullet points perform best.
- He suggests running the same experiment on your own account to learn what your audience cares about.

## Optimizing the call-to-conversation: sort by engagement percentage

The call-to-conversation (CTC) is the closing prompt that drives comments.

- Method: sort posts by engagement percentage, meaning what share of the people who saw the post left a comment.
- Finding: specific questions that tap into personal experience get more comments, and more thoughtful ones, than generic questions.
- Example of a strong CTC: "What decision will you make today that your future self will thank you for?" It drew far better responses than the weaker "What's your take on this advice?"

## Optimizing the image: keep it clean

- He found that Twitter screenshots or Canva images with 1-3 lines of text got higher impressions and engagement rates than posts with more complex graphics or bullet points.
- His rule: keep post images "clean, crisp, and un-busy."

## Results and takeaways

After a quarter of testing and tracking, Justin had a clear picture of what a high-performing post looks like for his audience at that time.

- Average engagement rate rose 28.7% quarter-over-quarter.
- Followers grew from 586,000 to over 645,000 in 90 days.

His takeaways:

1. Data beats guesswork. Track everything.
2. What works for him may not work for you, so run your own tests.
3. The LinkedIn algorithm evolves. Keep testing and adjust quarterly.
4. Engagement quality matters as much as quantity.

Starting point he recommends: build a simple spreadsheet tracking post performance, test different approaches for each post component, and let the data guide your strategy.
