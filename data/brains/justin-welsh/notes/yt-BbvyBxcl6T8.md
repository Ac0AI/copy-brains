## Audience data and the feedback loop behind his products

- Justin's audience data: 79.9% (roughly 8 in 10) either haven't started a business or run something part-time that doesn't make enough money.
- He concludes beginners don't need inspiration or "pie in the sky strategy." They need "what button do I press" and "what time do I do this thing" level instructions.
- His Saturday Solopreneur newsletter (101 issues at the time) is built for this: tactical and actionable, readable in under 4 minutes (about 250 seconds).
- He found most business newsletters were 15-minute philosophical reads with little tactical content.
- Every reader suggestion is a potential issue. After 101 issues he had roughly 1,000 feedback data points, which he bundled into the Creator MBA course.
- He emails buyers and subscribers asking what else they want and what's missing from the agenda, and collects data at signup.
- He filters requests: he gives people what they need, not everything they ask for. Examples he ignores: "best time to write a long-form Twitter post" (9:00 vs 9:05 doesn't matter) and "best newsletter software" (all that matters is that you start and continue).
- He adds the "unsexy" fundamentals nobody asks for, such as positioning, branding and storytelling, because people skip them and they are the foundation.
- He worked at two billion-dollar startups, joining before either had revenue, and uses that startup-style feedback process (NPS-like) in his own business.

## Writing a value statement and unique value proposition

Justin's four-step process for a USP, which ends as an extended elevator pitch:

1. **Value statement.** State who specifically you help, which specific challenge, and the specific outcome they should expect. "I help early stage companies grow" is too vague. "10 years of experience" fails because seven other coaches say the same.
2. **Pain, problems and objections.** Flip the value statement into the worries, objections and challenges prospects have. The USP language must be objection-overcoming.
3. **Future value / preferred future state.** Capture words a customer would use to describe a 10/10 experience (easy, fast, simple, step by step, better than expected).
4. **Movement statement.** Position against something, an ethos or best practice, the way politicians define themselves against the other side.

The test: a prospect who interviewed 10 providers should say "you really spoke to me, you nailed it." That is how you get chosen. He teaches this inside the Creator MBA because most people struggle to get specific.

## Building a movement without a villain

- He builds around a mission (solopreneurship), not an enemy. Tearing down others or declaring things "dead" gets short-term followers, but he calls people who react to absolutes "the most immature."
- He wants followers who hold "strong opinion loosely held," and calls that a network worth being in the middle of.
- He uses padded language ("more likely to," "perhaps," "maybe") and avoids absolutes.
- From a decade in startups, he saw two paths to the top: pushing others down privately, or being lifted on shoulders. He led about 150 people on his team, which had the highest internal NPS because there was little political jockeying.
- His goal is to grow because people cheer him on, not because they hate him.

## Starting small: pricing, trust and the service-business bridge

- Creators often package everything they know into a $1,000-1,500 product too early. He notes this is a mortgage-sized purchase for many, and that you need months of content to earn that trust.
- His example: Irwin Lear had 5,000-6,000 followers and sold a very specific $25 product. 500 buyers at $25 is $12,500, and the lessons from selling carry forward.
- On getting from $12,500 to $125,000: "you do it 10 times longer." Build the following longer and learn business as you grow.
- Growth hackers with 300-400K followers who "hack and trick" their way there often can't make more than about $4K a month because they aren't known for anything.
- A service business is the easier on-ramp: you're "getting paid to talk to people," which produces the data points needed to design a product. He suggests moving between service and product over time.
- His own start: advising and consulting for early-stage healthcare SaaS companies. He kept noticing DMs asking about LinkedIn content.
- He built a $50 course (2018) and offered buyers a chance to talk to him, which showed the topic could be bigger. About 1,500 people bought over 18 months, making roughly $75K.
- He emailed all 1,500 buyers for feedback, then rebuilt it bigger. Revenue went from 75K to 300K, then 500K and 600K, while he cut consulting clients, time and meetings. The pivot took about two years.

## Pivot or shiny penny: how Justin tells the difference

- He gets at least one new opportunity a day. His test: has he seen the same question 25, 50, 75 or 100 times from the same kind of prospect? One person asking is not a signal.
- A pivot doesn't need to be immediate. He ran the $50 course alongside consulting and shifted gradually, moving content to social media topics and finding customers through channels like VCs, networking and Slack groups.
- The second course came from a podcast with Dicky Bush and Nicholas Cole, where people's reactions to a template walkthrough made him realize there was a second course.
- He had an identity struggle during the transition (a website mixing healthcare SaaS advising with a LinkedIn course). His fix was a separate site for the course.
- He admits he misses opportunities too, for example declining a partnership to market a software product that later sold for $7 million.

## Diversifying under one umbrella and designing the product ladder

- He diversifies like a venture capitalist, with a thesis (solopreneurship) and every revenue unit under that umbrella: courses, newsletter sponsors, one-on-one coaching, a paid subscription email.
- He has not released a new product in two years. Most revenue comes from products untouched for over 24 months. Constant content creates the impression of constant new launches.
- Diversification comes from audience gaps. Buyers of his course said they liked the templates but wished he'd send 100 of them. So he sent templates in a monthly email for $9, adding monthly recurring revenue.
- Creator MBA tiers: Do It Yourself $497, Do It Together $897 (community, peer-to-peer, monthly AMA), Do It One-to-One $6,997 (limited to 5 buyers, quarterly calls, under 20 calls a year, likely an application process).
- The course is 8-10 hours and about 2,000 slides, and he says he could have charged $1,000-$2,000. He prices for accessibility so people in other countries can buy, and he doesn't want to look like he's trying to retire off the launch.
- Price history: his first course was $50 and the second $150, each meant to deliver $5,000-$150,000 of value and blow buyers away. The second has about 2,000 reviews at 4.98 stars and 23,000 students.
- Pricing lesson: he put "7" endings on prices after tweeting that you should round $147 to $150, which drew complaints. He saw it as a test and said he probably should have stuck with $500 and $900.
- He has no whisper-and-shout style launch strategy and dislikes making audiences feel part of a tactic. His priority is selling to the right person so buyers don't want refunds.
- Community lesson: his 2021 Slack community was built "me first" because everyone said to build one. It was synchronous and constant, which he didn't want. He shut it down after about 15 months. The new one is built "student first" on Circle: a place to ask questions and meet peers, not "ask Justin every question."

## Defining solopreneurship and working with AI

- Solopreneurship to him is an ethos: no team or full-time employees, but not doing everything yourself. He has a virtual assistant, a coach, a supportive wife who helps with the business, and communities.
- The hard, disliked work is front-loaded (systems, first hires, pitch, go-to-market). Once traction comes he outsources, delegates or automates what he dislikes. The goal is a life that improves a small percentage each day, not recreating a startup.
- He suggests getting to 60-70% of your take-home pay on the side before quitting, which makes scaling to 100% more likely than starting from zero. His own exit was abrupt after a panic attack.
- AI use: ad hoc, as an "anti-Justin" sounding board. He feeds in the course agenda and asks for gaps (it suggested legal/LLC formation, which he rejected), and asks ChatGPT for the alternative argument to his newsletter drafts.
- He says AI writes "garbage nonsense" for his content, and leaning on it makes you lazier. His view is that with more solopreneurs and more options for customers, you can't rely on lazy machine writing and have to be excellent.
- On being sold to: he responds to personalized, specific outreach (a Loom with three thoughts and what you'd do), then a deep discovery call. He says not everything has to scale, and growing slowly beats a rocket ship because you solve each problem as you meet it.
