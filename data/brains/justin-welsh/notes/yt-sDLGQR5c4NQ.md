## How Justin builds a content operating system for a one-person business

- Justin's business runs on content: more content pushed out means more top-of-funnel followers on Twitter and LinkedIn and more newsletter growth.
- He has no employees. His virtual assistant only handles admin, customer service and support, so he is "forced to have systems."
- His background is executive SaaS: two $50M ARR companies, teams of 150+ and $300M+ in venture capital raised. Process at that scale made systems "second nature."
- Weekly output he has to produce:
  - 1 newsletter
  - 10 LinkedIn posts
  - 10 Twitter posts
  - 1 Twitter thread
  - 1 LinkedIn carousel
  - 6 LinkedIn business-account posts
  - That is 28 pieces of content in total.
- He starts by listing the outputs, then arranges them in the order that makes sense to him.

## Hub-and-spoke: the newsletter first, everything else derived from it

- Many creators write short social posts, see what resonates, then turn that into a newsletter. That did not work for Justin. He finds the newsletter the hardest piece, so he moves it to the front of the week.
- The newsletter goes out Saturday and he aims to finish it Monday. It is the "hub" of the system.
- The other roughly 20 short-form posts come from the newsletter, which he pushes through "six to ten different lenses":
  - story
  - observation
  - contrarian take
  - listicle
  - past versus present
  - present versus future
  - analysis
  - teardown
- His line: learn to "say the same thing a thousand different ways." This also serves audiences who learn differently, since some like motivational stories and others like step-by-step guides.
- Twitter thread: copy the newsletter text, chunk it, add headers and subheaders.
- LinkedIn carousel: take the same thread, chop it up into a PDF, and post it on LinkedIn. It is the same content in a different format on a different platform.

## Building the system by removing friction

- Justin's rule: any time he hits friction, he asks how to systematize removing it. The system grows from each snag.
- Evolution of the weekly process:
  - Version one was just "newsletter, then content."
  - First Monday: he sat down and had no topic. Fix: an ideation step. Tuesday and Thursday he sets aside 30 minutes to ideate, by watching favorite creators' YouTube channels and most popular videos, scanning newsletters (including Josh's) for a topic he can add his take to, and checking his RSS feeds and Readwise.
  - Next: the newsletter needed more than opinion, so he added a research step.
  - Next: he forgot to edit, so he added an editing step with a short list of questions to run through.
- The end result is a roughly 10-step process built in Notion. Each Monday he opens step one and works through the instructions, producing a newsletter, two pieces of promotional content, 20 short-form posts, a thread, a carousel and six business posts.
- He calls it the "content operating system" and sells it as a course (an eight-step process).
- "When I don't feel motivated, creative, excited, that's where systems come in."
- Justin agrees that a good system is about formats, not topics. It works regardless of subject, and it evolves rather than being finished.
- On the objection that systems kill authenticity: his Twitter feed looks different every day because the lenses vary. Movies, music and every creative profession follow templates, and audiences don't notice.

## Why Justin shut down his paid community

- The community was a Slack-based paid membership. It was thriving at about $14k MRR, members loved it and were resubscribing. He shut it down anyway, as a lifestyle choice.
- Two things make a community work, in his view:
  - Goal alignment: it is not "the Justin Welsh community" but a community of creators and solopreneurs who all want one-person businesses and are aiming at the same target.
  - Shared context: members have been through a similar journey, such as the same self-paced course or cohort-based program, so they share the same jargon. He got the first right but did not build the second (putting people who had completed a specific program together).
- Why he closed it:
  - A commitment "drastically reduces optionality." Selling annual memberships tethered him to a 12-month commitment.
  - He wanted time for more courses, a book, possibly a cohort-based coaching program and a micro SaaS.
  - His personality didn't suit a synchronous community. Because it always existed on Slack, a free block of time (e.g. noon to three) was never really free. With members in 61 countries, someone was always awake, so "you never had a day off."
- On whether people came for him or for each other: he admits he was probably part of the draw. What keeps people in a community is connecting them with one other meaningful person.
- He gave members a 90-day notice so as not to pull the rug out. He told members they could start their own community as long as it wasn't tied to his name or brand and didn't solicit from his community.
- He warns that running one involves a lot of customer service (logins, tech issues), so leaving it open and free would still have been a near full-time support role.

## Pricing a membership community: quarterly beats monthly or annual

- He began with annual plans and enrolled three cohorts. Each cohort extended his commitment another 12 months, and he did not want to shut down and refund people.
- He moved to quarterly memberships and calls that "the sweet spot," the model he would use if he ran a community again.
- Reasoning:
  - Monthly lets people "kick tires for two seconds and then exit." He didn't want to build "that hotel environment."
  - Annual, from the creator's side, "drastically reduces that optionality."
  - Quarterly keeps him always 90 days away from stopping, while committing members long enough to learn the channels, attend events, make friends and get entrenched.

## LinkedIn versus Twitter: different content, same mechanics

- Both platforms have creators, consumers and a feed. Justin went LinkedIn to Twitter; he sees many Twitter creators doing the reverse.
- LinkedIn:
  - Highly professional. People are there to learn how to get better at their job.
  - Content that works is practical, tactical, emotional, inspirational and story-driven.
  - Pithy, ironic or philosophical one-liners (Naval-style) do not play well there.
  - 3,000 characters, and posts are truncated with a "see more" break. Writing long-form on LinkedIn is like writing a thread every day, so he teases the rest with hook lines at the top.
- Twitter (in his echo chamber):
  - Leans interesting, philosophical and unique; you have to be unique and interesting to stand out.
  - 280 characters.
- LinkedIn to Twitter: compress. He took his LinkedIn content and made it short, bulleted, with lots of white space and short sentences, and it did well.
- Twitter to LinkedIn: move threads into a LinkedIn post (educational, step-by-step content works), and bring over popular tweets. Keep it professional and tight; there is not much trolling on LinkedIn.
- LinkedIn is consumer-heavy, with everyone consuming, which is why he calls it so valuable for audience, income and business growth. He saw the opportunity in 2019 and says there was "a lot of luck."
- Repurposing a short tweet on LinkedIn: if both the brevity and the idea made it popular, keep it short. If it was the idea, expand it with extra information and a call to action or call to conversation. He often posts a 280-character piece on LinkedIn, then again three months later with content added, checks the results in Shield Analytics, and stores it for a six-month repurpose. The answer comes from the data.

## Putting links in LinkedIn posts

- The common advice is not to link in posts because it hurts impressions, and he agrees it does: his average post gets about 150,000 impressions and a post with a link gets about 70k, roughly a 50% reduction.
- But click-through rate is about 7x higher than telling people to "find it in my featured section" or spelling out a URL, which he says kills clicks.
- His reasoning: "My business doesn't run on impressions, my business runs on money." Because so few people do it, he stands out.
- He links in the post itself (not the comments) on about 5 of his 10-12 weekly LinkedIn posts, nearly half.
- The link is never an aggressive ask. Process:
  - Write a newsletter on a topic such as building an online course, then push it through the lenses.
  - Write an educational, relatively long story post, since the further a reader gets, the more likely they are to act.
  - At the bottom, offer a free guide or the newsletter issue on his site.
  - That drives about 4,000 people a day to the newsletter issue on his website. At the bottom of the issue he passively lists three ways he can help (courses and services).
- Example he gave: the morning's post had 4,000 engagements, the afternoon's was heading toward 700, and he had more website traffic right then than that morning.

## Choosing and sequencing platforms

- Pick based on where the audience plays.
  - LinkedIn: coaches in B2B SaaS, sales development, marketing, customer success, executive leadership, i.e. professionals trying to do better at their job.
  - Twitter: niche or hobby topics such as health and fitness, trainers, crypto.
- Use a tool like SparkToro to find where an audience actually hangs out. Both platforms are excellent for building businesses.
- Start with one platform. Most people start with too many and underestimate how long it takes. He agrees you grow faster focused on one.
- His own timeline:
  - Three years on LinkedIn (about two and a half years of daily posting) and he built 200,000+ followers before posting his first week of Twitter content.
  - On Twitter since 2009, but began posting daily on October 25th (the prior year).
