## Reframing a business as AI commoditizes information products

- Justin's revenue was tracking down as AI tools spread. He checks revenue at set times (8am, noon, 4pm) and saw the numbers trend down, recover after adjustments, then creep down again.
- He had a 4-6 month "dark period" of existential worry about where the business goes before accepting the shift.
- His conclusion: "I've sold information. I've sold products. All of that is being commoditized and eaten."
- He now asks "how much money do I actually need to make?" rather than how to double revenue. He runs 90%+ margins with just himself and his wife, and has grown the business year over year for seven straight years since 2018.
- He sees three things still sellable:
  - Leaning into AI (he won't be "the next AI coach").
  - Proximity: small, thoughtful in-person dinners, masterminds and events.
  - Authentic writing, which he is choosing. His edge is real experience: building two billion-dollar healthcare companies, then a business of about $12.5M to nearly $15M run by one or two people.

## Revenue mix of the one-person business

Rough breakdown of the business at its peak:
- **Information products (about 65-70%):** started from his sales background (VP of Sales, then Chief Revenue Officer at healthcare startups). He learned to grow a following by writing, then packaged that into content courses and later business and revenue courses.
- **Sponsorships (about 10-15%):** the newsletter has 180,000 subscribers, steady for about 5 years. The audience is entrepreneurs, solopreneurs, creators, makers and builders.
- **Partnerships and advising (about 10%):** companies pay him to promote and also to advise on revenue and marketing, using his sales background.
- **Membership community (about 7-8%).**
- **Affiliates and other small items:** he only recommends products he uses.

He says he doesn't have exact numbers in front of him, so these are approximate. He has never had an employee. His wife Jennifer is effectively a 50% partner: she edits the newsletter (flagging what doesn't make sense to her), and handles admin and logistics for the membership community and in-person events (she has an events background). He outsources things like website builds to an agency.

## Choose a medium, not a platform

- The most valuable asset is not the one that made the most money. Courses earned the most, but they have poor completion rates and don't bring you close to your audience. His newsletter is the most valuable because it will "stand the test of time."
- Advice: don't ask which platform (TikTok, Instagram, LinkedIn, X). Become excellent at a medium: video, audio or writing. "Great writers will always sell books. They'll always have newsletter subscribers. People who do great interviews will always have podcast listeners."
- How to pick: just try them. Write something; if the challenge draws you back for another attempt, that's a good sign, and if you hate it, it's probably not for you. Same for video: if after take one you think you can do better on take two, lean in.
- He tried YouTube shows around 2017 and the only thing that stuck was writing. He has written every day for about 10 years.
- On going where reach is: "going on a platform and doing something you dislike simply because it has reach [is] the definition of a bad life." Pick something you like and make it work.
- He doesn't use a sophisticated framework for this and dismisses most such frameworks as written to "sound complex."

## Using AI for writing without losing the craft

- He tries to stay away from AI for drafting. He worries heavy users "forget how to write."
- His process: write the draft himself, then run it through Claude as an editor. Prompts include:
  - Where are the gaps?
  - How would you challenge this argument?
  - What am I missing?
  - Is this story interesting?
  - Grade this out of 100.
  - Is there a non-obvious surprise readers will enjoy?
- He uses it for word choice like a thesaurus (for example, softening a sentence that feels too aggressive).
- Over time he learned to predict what the AI will flag, which sharpens his own self-editing.
- Idea sourcing: he and his wife turn real-life moments into newsletters. Example: a 25-year-old winery server named Hess was far better than they assumed. That became a prompt about when you doubted someone and were surprised, then pushed through an LLM for other angles and a stronger thesis.
- "The easiest way to be interesting online is to be interesting offline."
- Writing history: he is on year five of the newsletter, with 250-plus issues. His first long-form piece (about 15 minutes, weeks of work, sent to roughly 30,000 readers) covered three buckets of a well-lived life. A recent piece, "Synthetic Purpose," about inventing problems once your real ones are solved, drew about a thousand replies, a lot for him.

## What content works now, and where he expects it to go

- Value-adding how-to content has been displaced. Problem-solving is available from Claude or ChatGPT, so storytelling matters more. Compared with X in 2020 (step-by-step how-tos, guides, frameworks), X today is "bravado, production, storytelling." He wants the value but delivered with showmanship: "Ringling Brothers," knocking people's socks off.
- Being bombastic works right now because of the noise, in both video and writing. He dislikes it because it strips nuance, and he loses trust in brands and people who remove nuance from topics that need it.
- Prediction: people will return to nuance, context and hedged language, and to curation and taste.
- On volume: he thinks creators will move from machine-gunner (50 pieces a week) to sniper, like a band that releases a great album every five years and leaves the audience thirsty. He says he hopes so.
- He expects audiences to favor creators who actually do something in the world (founders, writers, operators) over pure lifestyle creators.
- Personal shift: he started ending newsletters with "write back and tell Jennifer and I what you think." Replies came in noticeably faster. Readers care about what's behind the scenes, so he is sharing more family and personal context and moving away from "how to grow 40,000 followers on X" content.

## Founder distribution and the solopreneur label

- On founders needing built-in distribution: it is "an accelerant, not a must-have." Great product-market fit can grow without it; he cites a former sales team he advised at owner.com, where the founder now amplifies launches with a produced video.
- For building distribution, "tell a unique and interesting story" and be someone people want to follow. Even Elon, he says, doesn't do much personal branding around Tesla; "he's just him."
- On the solo label: he is moving away from "solopreneur" (even the newsletter name "Saturday Solopreneur" may change eventually) because it creates silly constraints, such as having to defend hiring a VA. He remains bullish that one-person businesses, whether one person, a couple, or one plus VAs, are the future.

## Angel investing and company-building preferences

- He calls himself a hobbyist angel with a two-part thesis:
  - Tools he uses and enjoys with founders he likes (Kit, Gumroad).
  - People he would back regardless of what they build (the Patient Pop co-founders, Travis Schneider and Luke Kervin; he was VP of Sales and CRO there, and it merged with Kareo to become Tebra).
- He sells through Kajabi now.
- Preferences: bootstrapping over VC. He doesn't want a boss, and he already puts heavy pressure on himself, noting board-meeting prep was the part of his CRO job he was told he did well because he feared it. He leans toward self-publishing a book for similar reasons.
- Hiring: "fire fast." A bad hire compounds like the stock market, and culture killers hurt the most, more than a C performer among A players. The smaller the team, the more this matters.
- Risk style: ten small wins over one moonshot; being right over being early. He would only start a company again with a mentor, as an apprentice-style learner.
- Life and work rhythm: he works in roughly 5-year phases. Current routine is a workout and walk, lunch, then writing most of the afternoon. He takes sabbaticals (three months in Europe last year, California this year) because they improve his writing and let him meet new people. Next goal is testing long-form writing with a book or ebook.
