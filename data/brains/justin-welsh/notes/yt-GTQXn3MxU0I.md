## Build around a "bleeding neck" problem, offer, and attention

Justin says a business rests on three pieces, and with only two of them you have a hobby, not a business:
- **The middle: a bleeding neck problem.** Understand the target audience's most painful problem, one they will pay to solve today. "You don't give a [damn] about what color the band-aid is, how much it cost, how much the ambulance cost."
- **An offer** that solves it differently from others: "better, faster, cheaper, with a higher degree of likelihood."
- **Attention** (marketing and leads): you can understand the pain and solve it, but without a way to get attention you have no customers.
- Pain generally maps to saving time, making money or saving money, being accepted, or improving relationships.
- Test for a real problem: "If it doesn't hurt, if it doesn't keep them up at night, it's not a bleeding neck problem." Example: nobody has nightmares about "better team communication."
- Many people are experts with a skill or knowledge but lack one of the three pieces, so they are not ready to launch.

## Digging two to four levels deeper than the surface problem (sales discovery)

Justin reframes surface problems into existential ones, drawing on 20 years in sales:
- "Higher converting landing pages" or "more sales" is not what keeps people awake. "$30,000 behind in what I need to make for my family to survive" is.
- **Rule of two:** when someone gives a vague problem, offer two possible meanings (for example, conversion rate dropped from 10% to 5%, or traffic dropped from 1,000 to 500 visitors a month), then add "if it's neither, tell me the third direction." This gives them permission to correct you.
- His example chain of questions: what have you tried, what did it cost (30 hours at $500/hour plus a $10,000 landing page is $25,000 down the drain), what is the average revenue per user, what is the monthly gap ($100K to $50K), is it keeping you up at night, is it priority one, when do you need it solved, why that date (a product launch coming).
- The goal is to make the problem existential before pitching: "You're trying to stick a knife in someone's heart and twist it." Then explain how your offer solves it, what it costs, and why it is unique.
- Don't skip the questions because you "know the business." You may understand how the business is structured, but you don't know their problem.

## First action: interview 3-5 people and mine their language

Justin's one suggested first step:
- Find three to five people who have had the problem you want to solve. Ask for 15 minutes.
- Take those who solved it back in time: how did the problem feel personally, emotionally, in their family, on their bank account? Be candid. If they stay surface-level, "peel the onion back a little bit."
- Capture their emotional words (fear, nightmare, struggling) and their industry-specific terms.
- With AI, record the conversations, drop them into Claude or another LLM, and ask it to analyze the common words, phrases and language used to describe the problem.
- Then "all your content revolves around that, your offer revolves around that." The hub is knowing how customers talk about their big problems.

## The KPI Justin tracks: visitors to the intended destination

- His most valuable KPI is "visitors to intended place," tracked religiously for six years.
- When selling courses, that was visitors to the course landing page, which let him predict sales to within a few digits. Today it is traffic to partner pages or newsletter signups.
- He prefers this over impressions, which "ebb and flow."
- His logic: he believes his products are valuable, so if 3 to 5% of visitors buy and use them, they come away happy, and happy customers drive word of mouth.
- His landing page has converted at about the same percentage over the years.
- He expects to sell fewer courses than before because "information is becoming commoditized quite quickly" with AI, but he can still predict volume in a given timeframe from visitor counts.

## Why he shifted from tactical writing to storytelling

- He built his business on being tactical, on one thesis: make complicated things simple so people can take action, which benefits the customer and therefore him. "Help your customer," and it comes back to pay you.
- After five years there are only so many ways to write about landing pages, email sequences or content, and it interests him less now. He is "not interested in the perfect email sequence."
- He now writes about what he wishes he had thought about earlier: energy, outcomes, "enoughness," and the holistic side of business beyond cash and customer counts.
- Story material comes from sabbaticals (three months in France, three months in California) and conversations with interesting people. Example: a 25-year-old at a wine tasting who impressed him, which exposed his own bias. He and his wife ask each other "what did you take from that experience?" and turn those into business-relevant newsletters.
- He documents vulnerably rather than building a character, so readers feel closeness and know him as a person. He thinks keeping people at arm's length feels less authentic.

## How to leave a job and go solo: runway, pipeline, relationships

Justin left his job on August 1, 2019 and lists what he did right:
- **Runway:** he saved six months of runway, and recommends this if you can.
- **Pipeline built before quitting:** from September 2018 to August 2019 he created content aimed at founders and CEOs of healthcare startups, drawing on his experience helping build Zocdoc to a billion-dollar valuation and his time at Patient Pop. Inbound requests for help came in and he kept saying "in time," building a bench of opportunities he could turn on.
- **No burned bridges:** he gave six months' notice instead of two weeks, wrote a letter, and sat down with the board, keeping board and VC relationships. This meant he could get a VP of sales or CRO job again.
- **A risk reduction plan:** as a CRO he knew a board's first question was "what's going to go wrong, and what's the risk plan?" He applied the same logic to himself: if this fails then this. "Don't just quit your job and go all in on a whim."
- *When to Jump* by Mike Lewis was the primary book behind his decision. He also recommends Paul Millerd's *The Pathless Path* (bought 50 copies to give away) and *Good Work*.

## Operating habits and long-game principles

- **Weekly money meeting:** every Monday for almost six years (about 315 weeks) he reviews finances with his wife for roughly 20 minutes. They look at business revenue, tax set-aside, stock market investing, savings, net worth, and expenses. He fills in the data before she wakes. They do it weekly rather than monthly or quarterly to spot trends and kill bad habits early.
- **Planning formula:** "goals decades, strategy yearly, tactics quarterly, effort daily."
- **Beware survivorship bias:** stories like "left my job and made $2M in 18 months" are outliers with luck and timing involved. Don't hold up him or anyone else as the speed or route to copy.
- **Act first:** action creates momentum, not the reverse. For a goal like brewing his first batch of beer, he says "push the first domino." He notes he has the idea of a nano brewery and has not yet taken the first step.
- **Say yes and get in rooms with opportunity.** At Zocdoc he said yes to moving to San Francisco, Boston and LA, and the CEO told him he would never fire him. His advice, especially to younger people: use your energy to put yourself where you can win. "Every good thing is hard."
- **Reinvention:** he expects to reinvent himself roughly every five years and says "expertise is boring." His fear is not becoming irrelevant, which he has accepted, but "who am I after that irrelevancy."
