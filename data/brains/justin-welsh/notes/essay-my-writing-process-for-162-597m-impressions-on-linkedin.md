## The three-part structure for a LinkedIn post: meat, trailer, summary

Justin writes every LinkedIn post in three parts, and builds them in this order:

1. Write "the meat" (the information).
2. Write the "trailer" (the opening that earns the click).
3. Write the summary plus a call-to-conversation (the close).

He says he uses over 10 different processes for writing daily content, but this is the one he shares as a fast, easy way to think about creating it. His context: over 5 years on LinkedIn he generated over 162M impressions (he later updated that to 900M) that power his consulting, product, subscription and sponsorship revenue. Favikon ranked him the #1 global LinkedIn creator 5 times in a row.

## Step one: write the "meat" first

- The meat is where you teach, show or suggest something. It is the information the reader wants and that you want to convey.
- Justin starts here, before any hook, so the post is anchored in real substance.
- His example post taught readers how he built his online course business. He wrote out 2 learnings and 1 piece of advice he felt were important.
- His caveat: readers may find the suggestions useful, but "they'll never see them if they don't stop to read the content." That is why the trailer comes next.

## Step two: write the "trailer" that breaks the scroll

The trailer has 2 jobs:
- Break the scroll pattern with the first line.
- Make each line intriguing enough to pull the reader to the next one.

How he builds it, using his example post:
- Line 1 is short and consumable, and uses a big revenue number to stop the scroll. It is built around the $1.5M in course sales.
- Line 2 gives context and signals this is not "some 'get rich quick' post." It is a process that takes time.
- Line 3 is the hook line: _"Here are 2 big learnings and some advice:"_
- He starts by asking what the audience cares about. His audience wants to learn how to produce and sell digital products online, so the first line speaks to that.

## The hook line sits right before "see more"

- The hook line is the last line visible before LinkedIn's "...see more" button.
- This is where the reader commits to reading on or keeps scrolling.
- Justin says it "absolutely must be intriguing."
- He creates intrigue by promising the reader will learn a few things tied to the $1.5M in course sales.
- If the reader clicks "see more," the trailer has done its job and they move into the meat.

## Step three: close with a TL;DR and a CTC

- The ending packages the whole post into a few sentences, labeled "TL;DR" (too long, didn't read).
- Purpose: the reader shouldn't have to scroll back up and reread the whole post before joining the comments.
- After the TL;DR he adds a CTC (call-to-conversation), which makes a comment more likely and gives the post more traction.
- Three ways to do a CTC:
  - Ask a question.
  - Ask for a contrarian take.
  - Offer to answer questions.
- The TL;DR plus CTC lets readers review the key points fast and start participating in the comments right away.

## Publishing, results and the three-step checklist

- Justin schedules the finished post in Taplio, his preferred LinkedIn scheduling tool.
- Result for the example post: 232,471 views, 2,161 interactions and 461 comments. He says this is in line with a typical daily post for him.
- His recap:
  - **Step 1:** Start with the information. What are you teaching or suggesting?
  - **Step 2:** Create the trailer. Does each line get the reader to the next? Is the hook compelling?
  - **Step 3:** Make it easy to engage. Leave a short summary and a CTC.
