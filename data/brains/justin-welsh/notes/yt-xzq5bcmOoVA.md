## Anti-goals: decide what to stop doing before what to chase
- After his 2018 panic attack and 2019 exit from his job, Justin priced his consulting by dividing his prior income by 2,080 hours. He ended up with seven to ten clients and the same 40-hour life he had left.
- He defines an anti-goal as "this is the stuff I don't want to do next year."
- Process: list what gives him energy, ask clients what they want more of, and keep only the overlap. Drop everything else.
- Each anti-goal gets a concrete action plan. Example: he dropped non-healthcare clients (about 30% of customers) and updated his website language, URL and business name to a healthcare focus.
- He raised his rates. "If you double your rates and work half the amount of time you make the same money."
- Each year he reviews what he disliked the year before and does less of it. The open time ends up producing new revenue.

## Shutting down a working $15K MRR community
- He launched a year-long Slack community (61 countries) because "that's what everyone's talking about."
- About three to four months in he saw it conflicted with his goal of doing whatever he wants, whenever he wants, with whomever he wants. It was a 24/7 obligation, and every gap in his schedule turned into community work.
- He first offered three-month subscriptions to regain optionality, then shut it down. "A community is a full-time job."
- The freed time went into a newsletter that reached 70,000 subscribers in 11 months, plus a paid subscription tier. That replaced the community revenue.
- He did not try to refill all the freed time. About 30% got filled, and the rest is left for new products or vacation.

## Optimizing for time, not scale
- Justin says he doesn't need a big house or a yacht. His version of riches is "time, friends, family, experiences."
- He doesn't need to make more than he did in 2022 and doesn't need to 2x or 5x. Staying consistent for 10 to 20 years would thrill him.
- He calls envy of others' results "shiny outcome syndrome," as opposed to shiny object syndrome.
- His test: working 100 hours a week to 5x revenue is a loss, because there's nothing 5x revenue buys that he wants today.
- Revenue growth didn't make him meaningfully happier. He was happy in a small Brooklyn apartment too.
- His wife grounds him by asking him to describe the outcome he wants and why.
- Clarification: "do what you want" doesn't mean easy. He avoids things he dislikes (60-minute coaching calls) but takes on hard goals he's drawn to, such as writing a book.

## Thinking in decades and building transferable skills
- His shifts from SaaS consulting to LinkedIn to solopreneurship were "accidental." He followed the overlap of what his audience wanted and what interested him.
- Around mid-2020 to early 2021 his engagement and revenue became predictable, and engagement tied to revenue. That is when he started thinking long term.
- He treats the work as a 20-year journey. "What happens on Thursday compared to Wednesday is irrelevant to me."
- He is fine losing 50% of an audience on a pivot because he knows he can rebuild it.
- His confidence comes from skills, not a fixed path:
  - Bias to action
  - Clear written communication (copywriting plus understanding psychology)
  - Keeping his ego in check and learning from everyone
  - Curiosity and self-teaching
- He describes skill stacking as getting 1% better at many things at once.
- For planning he sketches five possible paths (three good, two bad) with check-in points.

## Course design and pricing
- Audience: most buyers are beginners going from point A to point B. Cut advanced fluff.
- Length: shorter beats longer. His 65-minute course has three times the completion rate of his 3.5-hour course. He tells buyers to book two 45-minute blocks on their calendar, because "buying it doesn't work, doing it actually does."
- Structure like a theater play:
  - Act one is exciting, high-value material.
  - Right before the "intermission" he sells the second half. This is also where he asks for testimonials and affiliate signups.
  - Act two delivers on every promise.
- Result: completion rates of 40% to 60%, against a stated industry average of 13.8%.
- Pricing is unscientific. He asked what he would fairly pay, and chose $150.
  - That is affordable enough for an impulse buy and expensive enough that buyers want their money's worth.
  - It works as a Trojan horse. A large installed base from a $150 course sets up a future $2,000 premium product.
- Illustrative math: if 10% of 50,000 buyers purchase a $2,000 product, that is a big launch.
- Purchasing power parity: he uses parityDeals.com so the course is about $30 from Pakistan or $60 from South America or South Africa instead of $150. His reasoning is that it was recorded 18 months ago, so a $30 sale costs him nothing and may change a life.

## Growing on Twitter and writing process
- Borrowing proven formats: after his LinkedIn success, he studied how Dan Koe, Dickie Bush and Sahil Bloom grew. He used Twitter advanced search with date filters and replies turned off to read each person's early tweets.
- He copied their formats for the first 12 to 18 months, then moved to his own style. He says copying his content today doesn't work because he did the groundwork first.
- Analyze, don't just post: writing daily without improving usually means not asking why a post did well or poorly. The job is to replicate what works.
- Social is multiplayer: big accounts support each other. Form a group of peers who grow together. It won't matter on day 7 but will by day 730.
- Justin reached about 70,000 followers on Twitter without that, then bigger accounts started engaging with him.
- His writing setup: coffee, an early gym workout, and about 90 minutes of uninterrupted writing from 10:30 to 11:00 on, with closed door, headphones and lo-fi music. He is useless after 2 p.m.
- Output: one newsletter on Monday or Tuesday, then 5 to 10 pieces of content on Wednesday or Thursday, which get repurposed across platforms.

## Reciprocity, scale and the one-person business
- He can't answer every message and gets hundreds a day. His rule is reciprocity. He prioritizes people who have engaged with him over time, such as course students and active followers.
- He helps them with quick tips, LinkedIn recommendations or retweets. "You can't just show up as somebody who has never been part of the ecosystem and demand a 90-minute consulting call."
- He wants to build a brand on "Midwest values" (transparent, nice, open, helpful), unlike creators who look down on people.

## Predictions and the challenge
- 2023 prediction: more high performers will see one-person internet businesses as a viable alternative to traditional work. His reasoning:
  - Burnout and the aftermath of the pandemic.
  - Layoffs leave high performers carrying heavier loads.
  - "If one customer at my company will pay me for my services, why wouldn't a thousand customers?"
  - Companies will struggle to retain high performers, so service quality will decline.
- His challenge: take one idea planned for later and take one real step on it today (buy the mic and send the first guest invite, create a Substack and write a three-minute newsletter).
- He calls waiting for perfection "procrastination dressed up as perfection."
