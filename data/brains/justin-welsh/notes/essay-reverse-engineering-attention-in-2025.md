## Attention is three things at once: pattern disruption, relevance snap, emotional accuracy

Justin argues that attention in 2025 is a bigger battle than before. People have trained their brains to ignore almost everything. "Scroll, skim, and forget" is how they consume, and with 10,000+ posts a day the brain builds an automatic filter. He says most creators are now fighting that reflex, not specific competitors.

- He used to think attention came from "hooks," then "providing value," "being consistent," and authenticity.
- After 6 years and 4,000+ unique pieces of content, he concluded that attention "isn't one thing." It is three things working together.
- If you miss any one, "you're just more noise in someone's feed." Nail all three and "they can't help but pay attention."

## The 2019 post that taught him the formula

Justin reread a 2019 LinkedIn post that opened like a hustle-and-grind morning routine post.

- Readers stopped after the opening and got furious. Comments included "Another bro-ey morning routine!", "Another productivity hack!" and "Another guy trying to make me feel lazy!"
- The post flipped completely in the second half. Readers who reached the end reacted differently: they left reactions, dropped nice comments, and 206 people shared it with friends.
- People went from hate-reading to saving and reposting.
- Lesson: the opening grabbed attention through an immediate emotional response, and the payoff turned that attention into engagement and shares.

## Part 1: Pattern disruption

Pattern disruption is "something that makes a person's brain stutter." It breaks the mental autopilot that filters out feed content.

- In the 2019 post, the first lines worked because they triggered an instant emotional reaction. Readers assumed it was a hustle post and leaned in angrily.
- He says pattern disruption alone is not enough to earn attention that lasts.
- Hypothetical example he gives of a disruptive opener: "I haven't had coffee in 97 days."

## Part 2: The relevance snap

The relevance snap is the moment a reader thinks, "Wait...this is about me." The content shifts from a general topic to the reader's specific experience.

- In the 2019 post, he moved from "hustling entrepreneurs" and "creators trying to make it happen" to something broader: "anyone who's ever felt like they're falling short of their own impossible standards." He notes that describes most people.
- Broad audience labels don't create the snap. Naming a feeling the reader recognizes does.
- Hypothetical example: a specific scene like "that 2:30 PM meeting where everyone else is caffeinated and you're running on willpower."

## Part 3: Emotional accuracy

Justin says this is "where most creators fail." They write to sound smart when they should write to make people feel seen.

- Bad (data-driven, generic, boring): "Studies show that 87% of professionals struggle with maintaining consistent morning routines."
- Good (specific, vulnerable, real): "Most days I wake up at 6 a.m., scramble to get through my Duolingo while brushing my teeth."
- He says stats make him yawn. They may be true, but they make people nod and keep scrolling.
- The good line is "the sound of someone's actual morning," so readers think "holy shit, that's literally me right now."
- Summary: "The bad example tries to prove a point, while the good example shares a real moment."
- Hypothetical example: naming an emotion others feel but can't articulate, such as "the weird pride of being the only person who doesn't need stimulants to function."

## Clickbait is only clickbait if you don't deliver

Justin says "Something isn't clickbait if you deliver," especially if you take the audience on a journey where they discover something extremely useful.

- This freed him to write content that felt "wrong" or highly polarizing, and to open with pattern disruption, because if someone pauses he can deliver something worth their time.
- Each part of a post has a job:
  - Opening: breaks their mental pattern.
  - Body: makes them see themselves in your words.
  - Payoff: gives them something they'll remember and save.
- Common failure modes, where creators get only one part right:
  - Great hooks, but no emotional appeal.
  - Helpful content, but writing so boring nobody stops to read it.
  - Strong emotion, but not useful enough to apply or share with friends.
- "The good stuff happens when you start mixing all three together successfully."

## Diagnosing why some posts get ignored and others don't

Justin says creators often post "into the void" while someone's random coffee thought gets 10,000 likes. His view is that the difference isn't the idea but "how the idea is executed."

- Diagnostic: a post that stops you almost certainly does all three (pattern break, relevance snap, emotional accuracy). A post you scroll past is missing at least one.
- For years he chased individual tactics: better hooks, stronger CTAs, more consistency, more value. His conclusion is that attention in 2025 requires "engineering the complete experience."
- The sequence runs from the brain stuttering on the opening, to engaging because the reader sees themselves in it, to sharing because it is genuinely useful.
