## Netflix's $89 cost per subscriber vs. a clipper program

- Alex's headline claim: Netflix spent $2.9 billion on marketing in 2024, including $1.7 billion in pure advertising (billboards, YouTube pre-roll, Facebook ads, the Super Bowl spot). It added roughly 32 million subscribers.
- That works out to $89 per subscriber, and "over $100" in some estimates for the US market.
- His thesis is that Netflix pays 14x more than it should, while "actively destroying their cheapest growth channel".
- The contrast case is Kick, a Twitch competitor backed by Stake. In September 2024 its clipping program generated over 30 billion views in one month, for roughly $6 million at a $0.2 CPM.
- Alex says Netflix's marketing budget could fund 40 programs that size.

## Why clips work: "commercials that don't feel like commercials"

- Alex's reading of Kick: "clips are commercials that don't feel like commercials."
- A 45-second clip of a streamer's best moment doesn't read as an ad. The viewer thinks "I need to see more of this", so the clip creates desire for the full content.
- Streamers on Kick don't need to grow their own TikTok presence, because the clipping program extracts, edits and distributes their best moments to people who have never heard of Kick.
- Alex's rule of thumb: "Every clip is a commercial. It just doesn't feel like one."

## The math for a Netflix clipper program

Alex's conservative assumptions:
- Pay clippers $0.6 per thousand views (higher than Kick, to attract quality).
- 10 billion views a month (less than Kick's 30 billion).
- Only 0.05% of viewers convert to subscribers.

Result:
- $6 million monthly spend, 5 million new subscribers a month, $1.2 per subscriber.
- That is a ~74% improvement over $89, by his own framing.
- Sensitivity check: even if his conversion rate is 10x too optimistic, cost per subscriber is $12, still far below $89.
- This ignores the added brand awareness, social proof and content discovery from Netflix clips being everywhere.

## The distribution problem: content nobody discovers

- Netflix has 7,500 titles in its US library and releases 13-14 new originals every week. Alex says most people have heard of maybe 30 of them.
- His diagnosis: "This isn't a content problem." It's a distribution problem.
- Netflix's recommendation algorithm only works on people who already subscribe. Discovery before subscribing happens on TikTok, Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts, where he cites 500 million people.
- His view is that Netflix sits on 7,500 potential viral moments and pays lawyers to make sure nobody sees them.
- What Netflix does today when someone posts a 45-second clip: a DMCA takedown and a strike on the creator's account. The creator moves on to platforms that don't attack them, the show loses organic discovery, viewership disappoints, and it is cancelled after one season.
- Example he uses: the Wednesday dance scene hit 1.5 billion views across platforms and drove major subscriber growth. Netflix paid nothing for it. Alex says it happened "despite their content protection strategy, not because of any distribution effort."
- The alternative: 10,000 clippers actively hunting moments across the whole library, paid to find and distribute the best scenes.

## Program structure Alex would build (three tiers)

Alex says he has built networks with 100,000+ creators, and lays out this structure:
- **Tier 1, open program:** anyone can join. $0.6 CPM. Clips must be 15-60 seconds with a "watch on netflix" watermark. Paid weekly.
- **Tier 2, verified partners:** creators with a 1 million view minimum. $1 CPM. Early access to new releases and dedicated support.
- **Tier 3, exclusive creators:** the top 50 performers. $2 CPM plus bonuses, premiere event access, and revenue share on attributable conversions.

Projected economics:
- Annual cost: $72 to $200 million.
- New subscribers: 26 to 60 million.
- Cost per subscriber: $1.2 to $4, which he calls "ATLEAST 32x better" than $89.
- The spend is less than 7% of Netflix's current marketing budget.

## Why incumbents won't do it

Alex lists five organizational reasons, none about whether it would work:
1. The legal team has too much power, and the content-protection infrastructure would have to reverse. "People don't dismantle their own departments."
2. It means admitting the current strategy was wrong, which is hard to tell a board after billions of traditional spend.
3. Attribution is messy. You can't pixel-track someone who saw a TikTok clip and subscribed three weeks later, and marketing teams hate what they can't measure perfectly.
4. It feels like giving content away, even though a 45-second clip of a two-hour movie gives away nothing and creates demand.
5. Someone's job depends on DMCA enforcement, and that person will fight it.

## Prediction: how the shift happens

- Alex expects a "hungry number two or three player" to figure it out, not Netflix or Disney, because they are too big and set in their ways.
- When that player launches a serious clipper program and sees subscriber growth that doesn't match its other spend, everyone will notice and copy it.
- He points to the same pattern at Netflix: ad-supported tiers, password-sharing crackdowns, live sports. The playbook is "resist the obvious move, watch a competitor prove it works, copy it two years later, pretend it was the plan all along."
- His positioning: his company (Content Rewards) already has the infrastructure, with 100,000+ creators, automatic creator payouts and quality control systems, so it will be ready when other companies "figure out what Kick already knows."
