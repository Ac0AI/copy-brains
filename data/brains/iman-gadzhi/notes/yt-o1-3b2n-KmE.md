## Two seasons: intensity first, then loosening up
- Iman compares early success to getting a plane off the ground: takeoff burns a lot of fuel, then cruising "just kind of sips fuel."
- At 16-18 he was rigidly locked in ("almost a little autistic"), with everything diligent and regimented. He says that served him up to a point.
- He then plateaued. Relaxing his schedule and adding hobbies and interests is what let him make more money again and reach the next level.
- He says the most successful people he knows eat what they want, have no 15-step morning routine and aren't "super optimized." Each has their own quirks.
- He says his videos aimed at his 16-17-year-old self make him look more monk-mode than he is now.
- On urgency: short term (today) you need agitation and urgency. Over a 1, 3 or 5 year horizon you must be patient. "The only thing stopping you from reaching your goals is time." You can shrink that time but it still has to play out.
- More success means more sacrifice and responsibility. He compares it to training: 3x a week is good, 2-3 sessions a day does more harm than good. He says people understand this for training but not for money.
- He says most advice is bad advice for any one person. Pick what applies to you rather than copying someone's whole life.

## Focus, dopamine and where a founder's time goes
- He treats a dopamine detox like fasting. It resets your baseline (caffeine, TikTok, games) so ordinary work feels rewarding again.
- Fried dopamine receptors mean you "can't sit down for even an hour and focus on one task," which makes building a career very hard.
- He says he isn't a focused person now. Deep focus was a skill he had to build for a season, when he "was basically the whole business."
- He once dismissed Gary Vee as "just doing meetings and on calls" and now owes him an apology, because that is nearly all he does. His real work is meetings, calls and decisions, sometimes thought through on a walk. He almost never sits down to build.
- Rule: use deep-work intensity while you are the business, then shift to leverage and decisions.

## Best businesses for beginners: digital products and services
- His filter is risk: choose businesses where failing for 6-12 months costs about $50-100/month in software, not $30k in physical stock or ad spend.
- He says physical product models like FBA, brand building and dropshipping can work, but they make him anxious for beginners.
- His own physical-product risk: a packaging issue at one brand cost about $80k in packaging and delayed release by 6 weeks. He can absorb that because he has other cash flow. A beginner cannot.
- He sends million-dollar wires for stock at his own brands. Customs once held stock for 3 months, even during Covid.
- Digital product examples from WAP, where the average user makes $9,000/month: a creator earning $8,000/month selling Fortnite maps, and another earning $71,000/month teaching Pokemon card trading.
- He says one $30 Excel course has made $150 million. Buyers don't check credentials, they check price and reviews.
- Other digital products he lists: Shopify themes, niche communities.
- At 12-13 he traded FIFA Ultimate Team coins and sold them to kids at school.
- Services path: his first agency client was in 2016 at about $300/month, doing photo and video for local businesses, later moving into advertising. He calls services "a foot through the door," not an instant multimillionaire path.

## Content rewards and clipping as distribution
- He says his team has run an internal clipping department for 2.5 years. At its peak he spent about $250,000/month on short-form clipping.
- Beginner version: pay about $30-100 for an editing course, then clip for companies or on a platform like WAP.
- He says content rewards let brands pay fans and creators instead of platforms. Spend goes to clippers rather than Facebook or Google, at roughly 5-10% of the CPM of Facebook ads.
- Example: $5k into content rewards instead of paying a platform for ads.
- Bounty example: pay $100/week for the thumbnail he picks. He has three full-time in-house thumbnail designers, which he says is overkill for most people.
- He says large advertisers have told WAP they want to put $10M/month of ad budget into content rewards by year end. The bottleneck is clippers, not buyers.
- He argues distribution is moving toward many accounts posting the same content with links back to the main channel or funnel. Follower and subscriber counts matter less than giving the algorithm more chances.
- He frames WAP as bigger than Shopify-style tools because it combines payments, checkout, delivery of digital products and advertising in one platform. He became a co-owner for a very large sum, and says he turned down offers from every other company in the space. He picked it partly for positioning, not wanting to be "on the wrong side of history."
- He compares digital products today to e-commerce between 2012 and 2022.

## Personal brand: be intentional about the business backend
- His top mistake is not being intentional. He says views don't equal success; they only matter if they feed the game you actually play on the back end.
- For his first 2.5 years he didn't monetize his personal brand at all. It was a vlog diary. Agency clients then came from it, including his first big client Affle (now Gemflow), who found him via a YouTube video.
- Today he says the brand's biggest payoffs are hiring, deal flow and the terms he gets when investing.
- Example mismatch: broad "millionaire mindset tips" content cannot sell high-ticket B2B services to enterprise clients.
- He recommends starting with a high-ticket, niche audience. Niche advice attracts a niche market, and broad content turns away high-level viewers.
- That's why he runs multiple channels. His main channel is for beginners, a business channel covers backend topics like thumbnail rehash SOPs and cart abandonment flows, and vlogs reach wealthy viewers who only watch vlogs. He says this is specific to his goals and not for everyone.
- Case study: Harry Stebbings' 20VC podcast has about 200k subscribers and, per Iman, raised $500M from the podcast. He cites it as proof that a niche show can be hugely valuable.
- He runs a B2B licensing program (AFL) that refuses beginners. He says broad content would be pointless if AFL were all he sold.
- Example: one AFL member makes a few hundred thousand dollars a month from a podcast, with done-for-you and coaching offers on the back end. Iman suggests a separate business-of-podcasting channel for that, rather than promoting it on the main show.
- His own monetizable broad assets: an eyewear brand, a consumables brand and a roughly $30/month mobile app.

## Spending, investing and money lessons
- Spend early money on whatever makes you more excited to work: a good chair, MacBook, desk, headphones, sleep wearables (Oura Ring, Whoop, Eight Sleep), a better gym, a cleaner, laundry service or meal prep.
- He says that at 5K-10K months, a clean space is invaluable.
- His first 6-figure purchase was a trip to Paris for his mom, which he promised at 17 and says cost a couple thousand dollars. Another promise was a dream house with a Range Rover out front, which he did at 23. He says making promises to loved ones is a strong anchor, because "people will do more for their loved ones than they will for themselves."
- His dumbest purchase: three Bored Apes in early 2022 at about $400k each (about $1.2M), an ego purchase. He sold two for $200k each and kept one.
- He put $1M into Bitcoin in November 2022 (he calls this a treacherous year). He says his net worth swung between about $5M and $15M. He says it is now over $100M, and notes illiquid assets (multi-million-dollar domains) make it hard to value.
- In the past year he has focused on investing in companies, as "a tiny little fish" in a world of sharks. He says he relies on good advisers.
- Example of why he stays humble on terms: a $60M napkin advertising company wanted him to invest, and he said he doesn't add enough value to ask for discounted equity.
- On lifestyle: spend on whatever has meaning for you, and don't shame others' spending. He says that even wealthy friends' spending sometimes shocks him until he scales it to their wealth.
- On mentors vs books: books are best for most people, but further along "mentors are more valuable."

## Finding meaning in the work, and giving
- He says money isn't an object for him, so he'd do what he's doing now. He sees WAP as reforming education and says he had "reforming the education system" in his Instagram bio in 2017.
- His eyewear brand is priced around $95-120, aimed at people who otherwise choose $30 frames or $250 Ray-Bans. He sold 4% to a strategic partner in May; the partner has done $1B in eyewear. He says the brand will do eight figures this year.
- His credit goes to his partner (Alex Hawkes) for product and WAP's engineers for building. He says his own contribution is steering: deciding where to focus.
- On mission statements: they need to be rooted in reality. His imagined knife brand's mission, "never cook again without a smile on your face," is not fully attainable but still credible. Overreaching missions demotivate teams.
- He says he can find meaning in any industry if he works with people he loves and improves the product.
- His reason for operating at scale: being "the container" that creates opportunity for others. He kept one employee for 8 years (since 2017) by setting up win-win-win deals.
- He says "the more you give, the more you get" but only with boundaries; pushovers don't serve anyone.
- His best advice: "this too shall pass," in highs and lows alike.
