## Starting from desperation, not noble reasons

- Iman began his businesses about 14 months before this 2018 interview, at age 17, out of fear for his own future. He didn't want college and said "no one else is gonna take care of me except for me."
- He started with social media marketing, built it for three to four months, then dropped out of high school with a year and a half left because he was running a business on two to three hours of sleep.
- At the time of the interview (aged 18, about nine months after dropping out) he ran social media marketing, affiliate marketing and a personal brand that sold digital products. He cites $72,000 profit in the previous month across all businesses, and moving into a $5,000-a-month dream apartment in London three months earlier.
- His family, friends and even his business mentor thought dropping out was ridiculous. He did it anyway because he believed in the idea.
- He spent large amounts on education, mentors and surrounding himself with the right people, because he wanted to get to his goal fast.

## Hunger as the engine

- Iman grew up poor in a Russian village, then lived in London in a broken house in a rich area while attending a private school. His mother was unemployed for years and money was tight.
- Because his friends were wealthy, he knew success was possible and "success was never a doubt in my mind." He argues many people from poverty stay stuck because they don't believe it's possible.
- His hunger came from two facts: he had to take care of himself, and he had to take care of his mom.
- He says that without that struggle he'd "just be a rather kid", because humans aren't built to grow: "we're meant to survive," seeking maximum reward for minimal effort.
- If your parents give you monthly money, he suggests telling them to cut you off, or to put it toward a coach or mentor instead. Comfort kills motivation.
- Some people from wealthy families still build great things, but they have to find something within themselves to light the fire, because nothing external forces it.

## Your "why" evolves; start with an ugly one

- He says you "need to go through a paradigm to understand it." His why moved through stages:
  1. Fear: if he didn't make it before turning 18, he'd have no place to live and might go back to being a village boy in Russia.
  2. Self: the dream apartment, the car, the best things in life.
  3. Becoming everything he could have been.
  4. Outside himself: he now feels he owes it to kids without fathers or role models, who get written off as he was.
- He calls an outward-facing why the strongest, but says don't start there because it's a paradigm you can't resonate with yet.
- Start with whatever lights a fire now, such as proving your mom or a friend wrong. Don't get stuck trying to find the perfect why before taking action.

## Social circle and deliberate isolation

- His first answer for what drives success is social circle: you are the sum of the five people you spend the most time with. He doesn't take advice from people who lack the result (he gives the example of a fitness coach without the body, and an economics teacher giving financial advice).
- If your friends drink or take drugs on weekends, so will you. He says he got pulled into London high society and a drug habit about a year and a half before the interview.
- Test for a bad circle: when friends talk about "what life would be like if this happened," rather than solutions and how they'll do things, "get them out now" or slowly fade them out. They may still be good people, but they won't move you forward.
- Aged 15, he brought Tupperware chicken and rice to school because he couldn't afford food off campus, read two-pound used books from a Brixton bookshop, and meditated for 30 minutes in a bathroom stall.
- He spent Sundays in a used bookstore for four to five hours reading philosophy, relationships, social circle and sales books.
- He still kept a social circle, but spent little time with it. He says every current success traces back to an action from three years earlier.
- He claims you have to be lonely first, and then once success hits, everyone wants to meet you. He stays "lonely" on purpose, turning down parties and clubbing invitations.

## Self-education over excuses

- Self-education was everything to him. If you say you can't afford a course, he says go to a used bookstore or go online, because the internet is free.
- He admits that not being able to afford a course or a event (he names 10x Growth Con) is a disadvantage.
- He'd still bet on someone making 100 cold calls a day with a stutter over someone with a light cold who makes none: action beats perfect conditions.

## Selling social media marketing like a business owner

- He rejects the common advice of charging $1,000 to $5,000 a month to manage ads. If someone pitched him $2,000 to run retargeting without explaining the process or ROI, he'd laugh and find it disrespectful.
- He wants to deal business owner to business owner, not as a worker or "slave" to the client. To be treated like one, speak and act like one.
- Sell on lifetime value (LTV):
  1. Work out the LTV of a customer in the client's industry. For a dental clinic, he says typical values run from five to fifteen thousand dollars, and for a premium London clinic (Knightsbridge Dental Care, which he closed) about £32,000, roughly $40,000.
  2. Against a $40,000 customer, a $2,000 package that brings even one customer a month makes sense.
  3. Selling on short-term returns fails. For example, 100 teeth-cleaning leads with 30 upsold at $97 gives about $3,000 and just breaks even on a $2,000 fee plus $1,000 ad spend, so it looks like a weak ROI.
  4. Selling on LTV takes pressure off the marketer, because a customer stays on average four years and refers on average 0.2 people.
- The owner of that clinic didn't know her own LTV ("what's LTV?"), which positions him as the consultant.

## Flip the buyer-seller dynamic with niche research

- Always flip the buyer-seller dynamic quickly. Know the client's industry better than the owner does.
- Start with two to three hours of niche research: for restaurants, dentists or gyms, find the best converting ads and the industry's LTV.
- Example: for gyms the best converting ads are a free week, or three sessions (or three weeks) for thirty dollars. He says he knows those "like the back of my hand."
- Once you hold the data, you have the authority.

## Sell on emotion, then justify with logic

- People buy on emotion and justify with logic, and he says most marketers skip the emotional part.
- Simple method: ask the current monthly revenue (say $50,000), the target a year out (say $100,000), and treat the gap as the stake. Then ask the motive behind the target and keep prying beyond the surface answer.
- Real motives he found:
  - A garage owner who inherited his business from his father only wanted to make his dad proud, so he cared only about profit and not brand awareness.
  - A client who was bullied as a kid and wanted the coolest brand for validation, so he said he didn't care about ROI and just wanted brand awareness.
- Understand the person you're selling to, not just the business.

## What success changes

- Money doesn't change him much; he gets acclimated to the apartment and travel. For him the reward is what it does for others: his mom, friends who used to buy him lunch, and charity (he once gave a subscriber a new iPhone to start a business).
- He says getting to six figures is the biggest life change, and going to seven figures changes little for happiness.
- He believes "there's no nobility in poverty" and that you owe it to your mom, wife or sister to earn.
