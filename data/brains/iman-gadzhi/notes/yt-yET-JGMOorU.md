## Iman's view on chaos, responsibility and the cost of operating at the top

- Iman says his year is a "season of chaos" that he chose: each year he takes on more responsibility. He rejects calling it anxiety: "when you feel bad but you know exactly why and it's a decision that comes from you... you made your bed and now you need to lay in it."
- He says his best quality of life was about 3-4 years ago, when his business was one-tenth the size, with 5-6 employees and high profit.
- He doesn't believe in the "conscious entrepreneur" idea of doing an hour of work and enjoying the wealth. Anyone performing at the very top has a "minefield" of a mind. Peace comes after selling the company, which follows decades of stress and responsibility.
- He sees himself as the "captain of the ship" (his team calls him Captain; he dislikes "boss"), responsible for the direction of about 150 employees.
- Everything has a tax, and social media hides the price. He documented 8 years on YouTube, starting at 15, including his first loss, his first hire and his first employee quitting after 3 days. He feels a duty to tell people what the life actually costs.
- He argues that someone who could stop and keeps going does so because of something innate. He wouldn't stop either ("what else would I do"), though he cites US bonds at 5-5.5% as the safe option that would let him never work again.
- He doesn't judge people who move to Southeast Asia and live on $5,000 a month instead of scaling. The test is living in alignment with who you are, which can take time to figure out.

## Seasons of life, trade-offs and how he defines success

- Success to him means "living in accordance to the season of your life." He compares his current season to a bodybuilder who only trains chest: one area gets almost all the focus.
- He admits this costs him personally: he forgets birthdays, doesn't call his mom daily, and his lifestyle isn't compatible with being the best partner or son right now. He expects a later season focused on family and says he will "shed [his] skin."
- He bought his mom a $4M dream house and sees a tax in that too: few sons do that and also call daily. They have a weekly mother-son time. He says her love language isn't lavish gifts (their worst arguments usually follow the most lavish gifts).
- Asked about his mom wanting him present over the house, he answers that she'd be asking him to give up who he wants to be, and she knows he is living true to his current life situation.
- He says he constantly asks "am I doing enough?" and the answer is never yes. He calls it an infinite game and that is the fun of it.
- He treats self-awareness as the core skill: know whether you are wired to pursue the top (like a footballer who could coast as the 100th best but chases number one) or want a quieter life, and live accordingly.

## Dropping out, Plan A and when to go all in

- He told his mom at 13-14 he would not go to college (no money, no student debt). He dropped out of school at 17 when he was making about $15,000 a month and supporting his mom. He started "massaging the idea" at 16.
- He distinguishes skipping university from dropping out of school. He believes everyone should finish school unless in a situation like his, and he has strong objections to colleges and universities.
- He didn't drop out when he signed his first client. He dropped out after making substantial money and proving to himself he wanted it.
- His framework for going all in: ask "what do I have to lose?" If you live in your mom's basement, go 100% all in. If you are 36 with a family, dropping everything would be irresponsible without a conversation with your partner about the risk of a rocky couple of years.
- He thinks you can start a business or the next career step with two hours a day first and prove it to yourself. Someone earning $80K at 25-26 has a good income and may be giving something up by leaving.
- Early on you need only Plan A (with nothing to lose, failing returns you to where you were). Later you need plans A through D, because markets change.
- On passive income: you are buying yourself time. Six months of work may front-load two years of success, but it decays and "someone's going to come in," so you need ways to pivot.

## How he built his money: agency, e-learning, software and the funnel model

- He ran his agency for 6 years. He calls agencies one of the easiest businesses in which to make about $1M a year in profit, maybe $2M, but not $10M a year.
- Near the end of the agency he launched an e-learning company (Grow Agency, late 2019/early 2020) for agency owners, leveraging his public documentation of his results (clients like oring, being flown first class to New York to consult).
- That led to his first software company, Agency Flow. He partnered with a client whose husband had spent years building influencer-marketing software; Iman had the audience of agency owners, so they retooled it for agencies.
- He cites the ClickFunnels model: reportedly zero to a billion-dollar valuation in five years, self-funded, because they got paid to acquire customers (e.g. a $12 book that pays for the acquisition) and then sold the software on the back end. Iman says he did the same: the e-learning business gets paid on the front end, and the back-end solution customers use is Agency Flow.
- Grow Agency was rebranded to Educate. It's a Masterclass-style platform with different Educators. It costs $125 a month, with the first year paid upfront, and includes a student success concierge. He is adding soft skills (e.g. a tailoring program) alongside business models.
- The "more genius aspect": every online business model needs software. For the software his students use, he owns a large equity stake in it, or has 50/50 partnerships, or recommends the best tool (e.g. Shopify for e-commerce), and says if he brings a platform all those customers, "we should be compensated for it."
- He says an agency is the only business model he'd comfortably teach a beginner. His eyewear brand shows how hard e-commerce is: 11 months into the year he hadn't taken a penny of profit out, because each time money comes in he goes back to the manufacturer to negotiate unit costs down (e.g. 8%, 3%, 15%) depending on order size.
- He doesn't talk about much of what he owns (properties, businesses) because it's too unrelatable to serve his audience.

## Colleges, loans and pricing: why prices rise when someone else pays

- His objection to universities is the student-loan mechanism. He says if the government offered a 100% loan for online education, he'd raise his prices, and every company in the space would too. He says if loans were available for tennis classes, prices would be 10x within three years.
- Colleges raised prices because loans are available to anyone, the "government's paying for it", while student outcomes keep declining. He applies the same logic to medical insurance (x-rays priced off insurance, not the patient's pocket) and the housing bubble (no-money-down loans handed out to anyone).
- He says 40 years ago everyone should have gone to college because there was a clear correlation between education and earnings; he says that correlation no longer holds in the US or Europe.
- He argues the education system was designed to produce obedient factory workers (the Prussian model), and that critical thinking matters most in life.
- He still builds education: he has built schools in Nepal for four years, for thousands of children, because a good education in that part of the world does work. Education has to fit your opportunities and where you are.
- On social media and podcasts, he says to treat his content as entertainment, not study time. He tells people to delete YouTube, and that watching podcasts isn't a substitute for doing something with your life.

## Money as a tool and benchmark, and how he handles comparison

- He treats money as a tool for making people's lives better and a benchmark of impact and personal growth. He compares it to a marathon runner proudly reporting a faster time. He says he could only honestly say "tool for better" once he was abundant, and that saying it three years earlier would have been "talking off my ass."
- Money can also reflect timing or smart investments, not impact, but making more money is still a cultivated skill and a barometer of growth.
- He intends to "die with zero" and not leave money to his children, because he knows how depressing money's downsides are even when you've earned it yourself, and worse if you never went through the trials.
- Social media warning: it can steer you off a good track. Someone making $400K a year at 26 with a wife and child would have been a king 30 years ago and now feels like a failure online. He urges people to ask whether things (dressing like him, moving to Dubai, starting a business after seeing one person do it) are true to them, and to figure out what is right for their own season.
- He notes even he gets swayed: seeing someone start a business, he thinks he should spin one off, then realizes "this thought isn't even mine."

## Security, fame and how he treats the people who follow him

- He says people have security for three reasons: they've upset someone, they carry valuables, or they're widely known. He says he fits the third: his head of security lives with him, people try to reach his house about five times a day (some have tried to swim to it, or rented villas), and he lives in a gated community with 24-hour security.
- He explains the obsession: his life is documented on YouTube for 6+ years, so people feel changed by him, and many just want to say thank you.
- His rule is to always stop for a photo and ask the person's name, because "they've given me their time and attention." Two places he won't tolerate it are his house and a place of worship.
- Security also keeps interactions good: his head of security blends in and politely says Iman wants to get back to his run, so Iman isn't the one saying no.
- He treats security as an insurance policy, especially in places like London, Cape Town and New York, which he calls dangerous, versus Dubai, which he calls very respectful. He says he has worked too hard to let his mom face unnecessary disrespect.
- He rejects the "arrogant" label: he says the arrogant thing would be to claim his life is perfect, and he has publicly shared losses (e.g. losing three clients, 40% of revenue, in one weekend) and the early months without signing a client. He bought his first car only this year despite earning tens of millions.
