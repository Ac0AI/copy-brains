## Where Iman's edge comes from (and how he invests)

- Iman builds and invests only where he has a "competitive advantage." He puts most of his portfolio into private equity and software, where he can bring users, onboarding and product feedback.
- He treats property as "park money," because he has no edge there. He dollar-cost averages into the S&P 500 every Tuesday because he has no edge in stocks either. His line: "why would I try to engage in a sport where everyone else has a gun and I have a knife?"
- On deals, he is "super aggressive" and won't take one without "ridiculous, ridiculous terms." He negotiates discounted equity up front and structures the deals himself.
- If a private bank brings him a private equity deal, he reads that as a sign it isn't a good deal. In his words, "I'm a tiny fish compared to who you guys work with."
- He asks for better terms only where he can add real value. A shoe company wanting his money isn't a place he can add much beyond a little audience, so he doesn't push.
- One software investment returned 11x in 7 months. He is the largest shareholder after the two co-founders, and the company just closed a $40M round.
- His stated reason for choosing software: "if info businesses had value... I'd be a billionaire by now." He can't recycle info profits into info businesses, so software is the closest cousin.
- He doesn't monetize being an investor, so he feels no obligation to disclose positions. He would feel one if investing were what he taught. He rolls a portfolio product out to his own programs only when it is "the best product" and ready.

## Payments as the moat at WAP

- At WAP (Iman is an investor, not the CEO) he says he is "optimized for" payments. He wants "the best payment infrastructure of any company out there."
- He says WAP is growing about 10% month on month in processing volume.
- Fees are a distraction. At scale, terms are custom, like a private bank quoting differently to someone moving $500M. Some WAP users process around $10M a month.
- He learned why payments matter from a European education owner doing about $1.4B a year (offline universities plus student lending). Without student lending, that owner said he'd lose "more than 99%" of the business overnight.
- Iman's takeaway: if info businesses had payment terms like traditional education, "our business will 100x overnight."
- WAP has exclusivity with most or all of the top payment providers. He is building an app store inside WAP, where a creator can pay a one-time fee (e.g. $5K) for a pre-built tool, or the builder takes a revenue share (e.g. 10% of $100/month).
- He is fine with platforms coexisting ("space for all of us") as long as payments are handled.

## Info products: sell transformation and ecosystems, not information

- He says the value of cohesive, organized information doesn't go to zero. He compares it to a driving instructor or professor reading from a book you could buy; people pay for the experience and transformation.
- "Build ecosystems inside of info." Example: a ~$10K tool that scrapes everything on a person and generates interview questions, with a slider for rigid versus open-ended, plus tools for limiting beliefs.
- His old $1,500 agency product had six 1-on-1 coaching calls over 6 weeks, 15 group calls, and nine courses taught by outside experts. He doesn't know another info company that offered that at the price.
- He says "I can't remember last time we just sold a course." When students wanted outreach teaching, he brought in the owner of Lemlist, a multi-million-dollar software company.
- Customers are buying the experience of him, wanting his characteristics, but not to copy him. A "faceless AI personal brand" makes no sense because the mechanism of a personal brand is you.
- Choose the model by goal. A faceless channel selling $20-$100 products suits someone wanting an extra ~$1,200/month, with "absolutely no chance" of millionaire status. For someone showing their face who wants $10K/month, he would say do coaching, the easiest route.
- Delivery works as a joint brain: about 15,000 students coached 1-on-1 over the years, and the top coaches became product developers. They feed field experience into new product versions, and he approves all content.
- Ghostwriting is normal ("99%" of recent books, in his view); what matters is that the founder corrects what he doesn't believe.

## Brand, positioning and funnels

- "The reality is you're going to piss people off no matter what you do. So, you may as well know who you're targeting."
- He follows a bell curve. Beginners think he's a genius, the moderately successful middle dislikes him, and the ultra-successful respect him. The middle "need things to sound more complicated than they are," and he won't overcomplicate. People at the top speak bluntly and use few words.
- He deliberately "dumbed down" his content because he was helping people at an earlier stage. His analogy is a running coach whose content is all about a 2:30 marathon while teaching a sub-4-hour one.
- He doesn't want to be known as an investor, so he declines suggestions to launch a fund. Public positioning should match what you sell.
- "Every hero becomes a villain," and after long enough a villain becomes a hero again, so he doesn't get emotional about hit pieces. His barometer is the revenue or main KPI of each business and the quality of his deal flow at the top.
- He says last year was his biggest growth year despite hit pieces. Views fell over two years by choice; views don't equal revenue.
- Content works as a funnel. The vlog channel feeds the eyewear business (he wanted eight figures this year and expects to miss it; next year is "definitely" eight figures), and the main channel feeds the mid-ticket offer he opens a couple of times a year (formerly a low-ticket digital launchpad).
- He expects formats to expire: "once something is named [after you], it's already not effective anymore," so be able to do any format.
- He says he'd stop making content if it had no business benefit.

## Team and leadership

- He hires a "stresser" COO for each business, who thinks only about worst cases (what if the upload fails, the partner cuts the contract). He calls them "Max" and likes German COOs for this. Creatives are not burdened with this, to protect their flow.
- He asks his team "what am I not seeing?" and plans for the worst case.
- No one-size-fits-all leadership: know each person's stress tolerance and communication style, and adjust.
- Bite your tongue and play long-term strategy: "you don't say anything until victory."
- He is very high in stress tolerance but numb to emotions, highs included. He says the trade-off is real: stoicism removes the lows and the highs together.
- Team members get a raise every year, and he frames staying as "the biggest insurance policy." He doesn't encourage personal brands inside the company, and says those who leave with his blessing often want to come back.
- Let committed people run side quests (a team member is releasing a coffee-table book). Tell people honestly when they're better suited to being entrepreneurs.
- Spend time in other industries, ask about their headaches and their competitive advantages, and you stop envying or glorifying anyone's revenue level.
- He says many entrepreneurs "are not made to make millions a month" because they lack the stress or emotional tolerance.

## Tax and geography

- His framework: "make your money in the US," protect it in the Middle East or Singapore (Switzerland "not so much anymore"), and spend it in Europe.
- He says his customers are mostly US-based. He spends about 75 days a year in the UK, and was planning to use non-dom status before the UK abolished it.
