## Iman's framing and where his observations come from

- Iman presents "17 success secrets of the top one percent" as lessons he has personally observed. He made tens of millions of dollars in his 20s and made his first million in cash at 18.
- He says he has spent time with very wealthy people, including self-made owners of large companies and multi-billionaires. He stresses that what he shares applies to the top 0.001% in any industry, and that his own results are "respectable" but "nowhere near" theirs.
- He says the ideas are conceptual life lessons that need applying, and he points viewers to his most popular video, which compares business models with an equation and formula, for concrete steps.

## Wealth: buy assets, buy A1 assets early, build bank relationships

- **Buy assets with lump sums.** After a big payout (a company sale, a huge month, a big contract), the top earners put the money into assets. In your first 10-20 years of career you are "a massive liability to yourself" because you may spend it, hit a rough patch or have a midlife crisis.
- **Favor hard-to-sell assets.** He suggests physical gold, houses and, in recent years for himself, equity in businesses. Stocks and crypto can take a share of the portfolio, but they can be sold with one click, so they don't protect you from yourself.
- **Hold A1 assets for decades.** His wealthiest friends bought them early and held them for 10-20 years: property in London, New York, Paris and LA, license plates, hotels. Returns are not the most exciting, but "you're never going to lose money" and the assets last generations.
- **Strong assets unlock leverage.** His example is a $20M jet: put $4M down and the bank lends the rest. With A1 assets in your portfolio, banks "will have absolutely zero issue giving you money."
- **Build bank relationships early.**
  - Banks are useful for creatively financing a business acquisition or buying equity in a new business.
  - As income grows you move from normal banking to premium banking to private banks.
  - He started reading finance books at 14 to understand banking.
  - He holds no mortgages and owes no bank money, but knows he could access $10M within a week and that wires of a few million won't cause trouble. Normal banks treat even clean, long-track-record clients like suspected criminals.
- **Win, then keep winning.** "If you sleep on a win you're going to wake up with a loss." He made his first million cash at 18, and says many people in that spot buy the Lambo, get the apartment and become "king of the castle." He argues it is much harder to keep and multiply money than to get it. Celebrate wins, but don't keep revisiting the past.

## Pick practical businesses over sexy ones

- Iman says the most successful people he has met did not get rich through an activewear brand or a complicated product needing $73M of investment.
- They made money in boring, traditional ways: B2B services, building software and licensing it to big corporations, import and export, logistics, timber.
- They don't chase a billion-dollar unicorn. They find market inefficiencies or do something "even three percent better" and take more market share.
- His conclusion: "there's money literally all around you and it doesn't need to be as sexy as you think it does."

## Timing and sequencing: do things in the right order

- He criticizes the social media advice "don't do what someone says, do what they do." Someone 10 years into an industry saying "if I was starting out I would do this" can't be copied by a beginner from square one.
- "It's not always about doing the right thing, it's about doing it in the right order."
- What you can do grows with time. In about 30 years, with a good career, he says you could call someone and have $5M in the bank the same evening on goodwill and reputation. You could sell $100k, $1M or $5M of a product or service before creating it, because loyal customers trust the quality.
- Some businesses cost about $4M to start, break even in year one and become $40M-a-year companies within five years. You can't start those without the $4M.
- Practical rule: if you're a real estate agent, don't study what an agent does in their 15th year. Study what they did when they started. Pick the right opportunity for your current point in your career.

## Discipline, routine and the long road to mastery

- **Discipline beats motivation.** Motivation is like runner's high in a marathon: it comes for a small part of the race. You don't quit when it fades, you "lock in" and do it whether you feel like it or not. Motivation is "fleeting," so rely on discipline.
- **Morning routine over wake-up time.** Some multi-billionaire friends wake at 10am (time zones) and some at 6am (physical offices). You don't need 4-5am. What matters is a set routine and not starting the day with a high-sugar breakfast, phone scrolling and YouTube.
  - His own: wake at 8am, wash his face, get direct sunlight, stretch and warm up for 30 minutes, train about an hour and a half, eat breakfast, start work.
  - When travelling and training less, he may just have a coffee and start working.
  - He woke at 5:30 in London, then 6:30 with a personal trainer at 7am. Now in Dubai, with 150 employees mostly in the US and UK, he wakes at 8 or 8:30.
- **Private victories lead to public victories.** The bigger the public win, the more unseen private wins behind it. A Gucci or Balenciaga hoodie takes few; a $60M jet or a boxing title takes years of sacrifice, discipline and doing what you don't want to do.
- **Mastery looks effortless after about 10 years.** After a decade or more on one thing it looks elegant and easy. Early clunkiness and being bad at it is part of the process, so it shouldn't scare beginners. He advises picking something you respect and dedicating yourself to it even on zero-motivation days.

## Communication, opinions and who you let in

- **Talk less.** Don't spill secrets. In negotiations "you should never ever be the first one to say the price." Fewer words mean fewer mistakes under stress and more attention when you do speak. The most successful people he knows listen, analyze the room and want other perspectives, not just to win.
- **Opinion vs. say.** "Everyone deserves an opinion, not everyone deserves a say."
  - He is happy to say "I don't really have an opinion on that" about topics he hasn't researched.
  - This covers parents, friends and sometimes people in your business. He takes everyone's opinion in his business, but not everyone gets a say.
  - Weigh advice from people who have done what you want to do (extremely successful people) more heavily. Always separate opinion from good advice.
- **Keep your circle small.** You can have many acquaintances and business associates, but few people can keep pace with your evolution, and "loose lips sink ships."
  - Private business should stay private.
  - His rule: never tell anyone outside the trusted inner circle anything you wouldn't want others to know. Assume it will spread, and mentally prepare for that.

## Mindset and values: responsibility, old vs. new money, small improvements

- **Radical responsibility.** Failed marriage, failed business, a knee injury that stopped tennis for six months: "it's your fault." He says that is the only way to analyze the inputs that led to the outcome. Blaming externals won't get you far.
- **Work like new money, act like old money.** He wanted old-money dress, financial literacy and values from an early age (he is 23 and says he is not perfect and talks about money more openly than old-money circles would like). New money has hunger and drive. Old money has wisdom. Combine them to make a lot of money and avoid losing it.
- **You won't change the world, but you can make it slightly better.**
  - His example: if Apple didn't exist he'd be happy with Samsung, so it didn't change the world.
  - Japanese sushi chefs spend 40-60 years on apprenticeship and perfecting rice, knife work and cuts. That doesn't change anyone's life but makes the world slightly better.
  - He calls "I'm going to change the world" grandiose and self-absorbed. Do your thing as well as you can, "I don't care if I'm a waiter I want to be the best waiter out there."
  - "If we all do that, that's how we change the world."

## Choosing your partner

- Iman calls the woman you choose "the most important decision" a man makes outside of work. He speaks specifically to ambitious men.
- Dating around and a playboy era are fine and a distraction, but the destination should be an incredible partner. Some multi-billionaires he respects have had only one partner all their life.
- An ambitious man needs home to be a safe, loving, nurturing space while he "conquers" for his future family. Many ambitious men pick partners who are too turbulent or don't understand his ambition.
- Don't choose on looks alone. "Looks fade and so does attraction," even for 10 out of 10s, and the man at home gets bored. Grand ambitions mean turbulent times, so you need a good person beside you.
- He says high-level men don't respect a partner chosen as a trophy for dinner parties. A real partner has social intelligence and represents herself and her man well.
