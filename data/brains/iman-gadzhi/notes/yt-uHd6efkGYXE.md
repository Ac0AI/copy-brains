## Starting a social media agency at 16 with no niche, no investment and no playbook

- Iman signed his first agency client in 2016, aged 16, in the UK. He was too scared to charge monthly, so he charged £95 a week (£380 a month).
- For that fee he shot, edited and posted 30 Instagram posts, 30 Facebook posts and 4 YouTube videos a month. He calls it "basically minimum wage."
- In 2016 he did everything he now tells people to avoid: long proposals, in-person meetings, and burying the point of the conversation. He signed no more clients in the second half of 2016 and was rejected a lot.
- A 2016 goal was to make £15,000 in a year. He tracked it early: £489 in the first 11 days.
- He says he never made excuses for his age, school, or lack of rich parents. He bootstrapped everything with zero investment and zero guidance.
- His mindset advice: enjoy the tedious process (making channel art, talking to clients), because happiness doesn't arrive at the first 1,000 subscribers or the first million. "If you can find a way to do that then maybe you're doing the wrong thing" if you can't.
- His daily routine was gym, steam room, sauna, meditation, then 30 to 50 pages of a book. He aimed for a book a week and kept roughly a 90% track record for about 3 years.

## 2017: $25,000 months while doing everything wrong

- By 2017 he was hitting $25,000 a month, which he calls the best period of his life. He says the first $100,000 does something "unexplainable" that later millions don't.
- He was making that while running a content creation agency with no niche. He also sold side services, did all the work himself, didn't auto-bill clients, and didn't keep cards on file for automatic monthly subscriptions.
- He hired his first employee in summer 2017. The person flew over from America and left within days because the deliverables-based work was boring. Iman says he lost about 50% of revenue around then.
- In August 2017 he lost 50% of his clients. He blames himself: he got complacent, stopped being a student, and let his ego and comfort take over.
- His lesson from that: "the problem with comfort is it breeds complacency." Staying a student and keeping your ego in check is what produced the success in the first place.
- Even with the money, he still felt young. He felt clients didn't respect him as a business owner, which became the focus for 2018.

## Selling to people far above you: don't qualify yourself

- Iman's rule when talking to millionaires, influencers or boardroom execs is "don't qualify yourself."
- Qualifying means listing accolades or how much you've made to prove your worth. He says people at that level "see right through that."
- They hear that constantly from people trying to climb up to their level, and it reveals that you know you're below them.
- Instead, act like you belong there from the first minute. He says that sets a better dynamic from the start of the meeting or conversation.
- He ties selfworth to a purpose bigger than you. He told his mom a year before that he'd take her to Paris and then did it. He argues that if you do it only for yourself you will quit, but doing it for another person gives extra will.

## 2018: locking in a niche, the first five-figure retainer, and moving up the service ladder

- In 2018 he finally locked in a niche. He signed his first five-figure-a-month client, a sleepwear brand (Oura-type product company he was already a customer of) that he calls a very cool moment. He hired his first proper full-time employee, who still works for him.
- In 2018 he stopped feeling imposter syndrome and bought his first Rolex at 18 for $155,000 with his own money. In 2016 he had set a 2017 goal of a $115,000 profit and blew past it.
- His service ladder:
  1. Social media distribution and growth plus some content: $5,000 to $6,000 a month.
  2. Higher-value services people pay more for: paid ads, funnel creation, email marketing, retargeting.
  3. Consulting: "come in, get paid a big fee, and get out."
- He charged $20,000 for a 2-day training (he says £15,000, roughly $19,500). He says he stopped selling packages and pricing should differ per client.
- Pricing analogy: if a doctor says "take this medicine" without hearing your problem, it feels like a scam. Quote per client based on their situation and the work involved.
- In a 45-day stretch he doubled his retainers to about $50,000 to $55,000 a month. In one day he signed a £4,000-a-month and a £7,800-a-month client. He did this without a website.
- He says being respected is a big perk of social media marketing, because owners tell him a workshop "literally changes everything" for their business.

## 2019 and 2020: retention, lifetime value, retainers vs performance fees

- In 2019 he was signing five-figure monthly retainers "left, right and center," but found his client retention wasn't where he wanted it.
- His realization: there's a direct conflict between how much you charge and how long you keep the client. Charging a bit less keeps them substantially longer.
- He shifted to optimizing lifetime value instead of monthly value: a client paying $25,000 a month for 10 months beats one paying $5,000 a month for 3.
- In 2020 they signed more clients than in 2019, charged them less, and kept them longer, making much more money overall.
- Structure he moved to: lower base retainers, longer minimum terms (five or six month minimums), plus performance fees.
- His agency advice: get client focused, operations focused and sales focused. Track cost per conversion and return on ad spend, not cost per click. A better service than the agency next to you wins.
- About 70 to 80% of his clients were in the info product space. He ran paid ads for them, ran webinar funnels, and said retainer plus a percentage of return on ad spend is riskier but has far higher rewards. He wishes he'd done that sooner with more clients.
- He used retainers first because they gave predictable monthly cash flow, and he wanted operations simple and organized.
- He avoids retainer-only if incentives aren't aligned. He criticizes agencies that charge a percentage of ad spend or revenue: "just leave the meeting," because incentives need to be aligned.

## Deal examples and sales approach

- Deal 1 (info product client): a £10,000 setup fee to build a case-study funnel plus a 30% performance fee on return on ad spend. He had done $35,000 to $45,000 a month in return on ad spend for the client's competitor. He estimated about $20,000 a month average over 6 months, so about £6,000 a month to the agency from the fee.
- On the sales call he wanted to close on the spot, but the client asked for a follow-up call. He chose to remove his ego and booked a 20-minute call for Tuesday. Soon after, the client emailed asking to pay £5,000 that day and £5,000 the next week.
- Deal 2: £2,500 base plus 15% on anything above 1.9x return on ad spend. He called this client "a certified whale" and expected the performance fee to be roughly £9,000 to £10,000 in the first month. The performance fee never falls below the base-level floor he described.
- His qualification bar for the agency: info product businesses doing at least $25,000 a month. He still takes sub-threshold calls because "that 45 minutes gives me reference experiences."
- He normally aims for a one-call close and rarely needs a third call. He is strict about taking only 100% fit clients, and he treats a no-show or stalling prospect as a sign they are less likely to sign.
- He uses a digital contract tool (HelloSign, or similar like PandaDoc) as part of the onboarding funnel after payment. Payment comes first, then the onboarding link and the contract.
- When he must stall for time on a first call, he uses Zoom's waiting room to pause and consult his CMO, then reconvene.
- He warns more decision-makers on the client side means slower, harder closes.

## Cash flow, investing and bandwidth (2020 to 2021)

- At the end of 2020 the agency account held $1.5 million. He decided idle money was pointless and moved $1 million into investments: 70 to 80% crypto and the remainder watches.
- He calls the agency "such an amazing cash flow business." From then on he moved $100,000 a month from the agency into his investment portfolio and still does.
- His rule: when you have cash flow, invest it and let compounding do the work.
- In 2021 he made $9 million between agency profits and portfolio gains, and said the agency hit its record month in April 2021. He was only spending 5 to 10 hours a week on it because his CMO Danny ran it.
- He says he moved to Dubai for tax reasons, rents instead of buys at this stage ("it's more important to be mobile"), and had a roughly $10 million portfolio at age 21.
- His team ran about 20 clients at one point, with one client spending about $280,000 in a month across up to 10 ad accounts, which needed load balancing. His team burned out because he was slow to hire a new performance marketer, which he admits was a leadership miss.
- Bandwidth lesson: people overestimate what they can do in a year and underestimate 10 years. He said he had four companies plus a portfolio and needed to take on less.
- Abundance mindset: many people have "your foot on the gas but also on the brake" by believing rich people are evil. He says self-made entrepreneurs understand that money is a transfer of value, and you must provide value to the market to build a business.
