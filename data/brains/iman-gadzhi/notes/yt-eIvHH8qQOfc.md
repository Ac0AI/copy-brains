## Two ways to run an SMMA in 2026: traditional vs. shadow operating

Iman says SMMA "still remains one of the best business models for 2026," but argues the way most beginners plan to do it is wrong. He gives two routes and says both work.

- **Traditional:** sell marketing services to local businesses for retainers.
- **New way (shadow operating):** partner with micro creators and take a percentage of what they make from their own audience.

He picks the agency model because it has no big upfront cost, little risk and high margins. It also doesn't require being "a dancing monkey online." He says it is the model where beginners learn the most. He notes that many of his friends with eight- and nine-figure net worths started with an agency or service business, himself included. His first agency was "IG Media," which he calls a terrible name, especially once he moved to paid traffic.

- Agency setup is an admin step you do once and move on from. He says "your agency setup doesn't make money. Prospecting and outreach do."
- Name: keep it simple and don't overthink it. "No one cares about your name as much as you do."
- Domain: if the first choice is expensive, pick a variation or a different TLD such as .io or .org.
- Logo: use Canva or an AI tool. A nice logo won't make the difference between failing and succeeding.
- Google Workspace for a professional email, Zoom for calls, and a website that is optional. He made $20,000 a month before he had one.
- For shadow operating, add a cleanup of your social profile because creators see it first. That means a clean photo, a clear bio, and at least nine posts so the grid is full. He says you don't need a Lamborghini in Dubai, just to look like "someone who has their life together."

## Traditional route: niche selection and service selection

Iman says to niche down for two reasons, specialization and scalability. His analogy is that you'd trust a brain surgeon over a general practitioner, and the specialist can charge more. With one niche, prospecting, outreach, sales style, delivery and offer all stay the same. With several niches it is "literally like running multiple different businesses."

**Example:** his client Bobby sold to authors who publish on Amazon. He scaled to $150K a month with 100+ clients by repeating the same prospecting, outreach and delivery.

**Criteria for picking a niche:**
- Profit: look for businesses making at least $10,000 a month in profit, not revenue. Otherwise paying you $2,000 feels expensive.
- Ticket size: it is easier to help a solar company that earns $10,000 per job get one more sale than to help a shop with $5 profit per t-shirt.
- Growth: avoid dying industries, such as DVD rental versus info businesses.
- Market size: home renovation has millions of businesses, while Italian wedding dress designers number maybe 50 in a country.
- Sophistication: as a beginner, avoid sophisticated markets such as e-commerce owners who already run ads. If you offer a "4 out of 10" service to someone doing nothing, it beats nothing. If they're already at 6, you must get them to 8.

He says 90% of people get stuck on niche selection and overanalyze it for weeks.

**Service selection:** services are either convenience or ROI.
- ROI services are easier to sell: paid traffic, email marketing, copywriting, SEO, lead generation.
- Convenience services save time: social media management, videography, video editing, reputation management, web design.

To pick one, ask two questions: what is the biggest problem businesses in my niche face, and what is the most efficient way to solve it? Then weigh four factors:
- Skill set: can you deliver it yourself? If so, use it. He started with a creative agency because he was good at creating content.
- Scalability: this mostly comes down to location. He switched from a content agency to an advertising agency because paid ads let you manage 20 clients from a laptop, while video production needs you on-site.
- Upfront cost: copywriting needs a laptop, while videography needs camera gear. Match the service to your current resources.
- Income variation: project-based (for example $4,000 for a website) versus recurring (for example $2,000 a month for 6 months).

## Shadow operating: why micro creators are the target

Iman says there are an estimated 150 million+ micro creators with 20,000 to 50,000 followers. They have audiences that "know, like and trust" them but earn only a few hundred dollars from brand deals or affiliate links. They are good at content but don't know how to package and sell.

He gives three tiers:
- **Nano (1,000 to 10,000 followers):** too small, with limited monetization potential.
- **Micro (10,000 to 100,000 followers):** the target. Elsewhere in the talk he gives the range as 20,000 to 100,000.
- **Macro (150,000+ followers):** avoid as a beginner, since they already have agents and managers.

**Why this lowers difficulty:**
- You skip niche selection because the creator's niche is already built.
- You don't need to be the expert, because the creator is the expert on fitness, finance, parenting or whatever it is.
- You skip the hardest part of agency work, which is getting attention for a business nobody knows. The audience already exists.
- Creators are unsophisticated buyers. They started from passion, not business, so they are easier to help.

**Example:** a divorced-single-moms account with 40,000+ followers and 10,000+ views per reel on average. The bio has a collab email and a few affiliate links but no product of her own.
- Selling a $50 product to just 1% of her audience would mean about $20,000.

**Proof of demand:** a TikTok creator with 100,000 followers (inner healing) DMed him asking for "AI shadow operators." She said the business and tech side was her challenge. He points to Congo Brands (Prime, which owns 60% of the business) and the Shahiti twins building Happy Dad with the Nelk Boys as examples of shadow operators. He says he met many creators' behind-the-scenes partners at the WAP office.

## Offer creation and pricing

**Traditional:** an offer is niche + service + pricing model. It bridges the gap between the prospect's current state and desired state. Five components:
- **Promise:** a tangible outcome plus a time frame, such as "30 qualified booked appointments in 90 days" or "four long-form YouTube videos in 60 days."
- **Methodology:** keep it simple. For example, run ads, leads fill a form, qualified ones get booked.
- **Pricing model:** monthly retainer, pay per project, pay per lead, or a percentage of ROAS, plus an optional setup fee.
- **Contract duration:** at least 3 months for recurring services, so you have time to optimize and get results.
- **Free value:** audits, resources, lead reactivation.

**Pricing:** the more money you make for clients, the more you can charge. Start at a price you're comfortable with and test it. If every prospect says yes with no pushback, raise it. If everyone says it's too high, lower it.

**Guarantees:** he is "not the biggest fan" for agency services because they send the wrong message.

**Shadow operator:** there are no fees. You earn a percentage of sales, so the more you make them the more you make.
- Starting splits: he suggests 90/10, 80/20 or 70/30 in the creator's favor, to make the partnership attractive until you gain confidence.
- Free value: a "creator audit" is a document covering who they are, who their audience is and their pain points. He suggests a 2-minute Loom walkthrough.

## Prospecting and outreach

**Traditional prospecting:**
- Manual: Google Maps, directories and social media.
- Software: Apollo, Leadswift, Leads Gorilla.
- Hire lead generators on Upwork or Fiverr.

**Traditional outreach:** cold calls, cold emails or cold DMs, chosen by where the business owner lives (calls for home renovation owners who are on the move, email for SaaS). It is a numbers game and some prospects will ghost you.

**BAM and Reach:**
- BAM ("bare ass minimum") is a daily number of outreach messages you do no matter what.
- Reach is the stretch number you strive for.
- He attributes much of his agency success to this consistency.

**Shadow prospecting:** find micro creators on Instagram, YouTube, Twitter, TikTok or LinkedIn, and DM them on Instagram, where they're most active.
1. Search niches such as fitness, personal finance, productivity, self-improvement, parenting, relationships, career and mental health.
2. Keep accounts in the 20,000 to 100,000 follower range.
3. Note content type, audience, average reel views, posting frequency, and likes and comments.
4. Check on SocialBlade for an engagement rate of at least 2.5% to 3% (the transcript is garbled here).
5. Use the "suggested accounts" button to find similar creators and repeat.

**Challenge:** build a list of 100 creators. He offers a free prospecting tracker.

**Outreach messages:** short, personalized with their name, about them and not you, and ending with a question. Examples:
- "Hey John, I notice you have a very engaged audience who follows you. Are you currently monetizing your audience?"
- "Hey Sophia, do you currently sell any coaching or mentorship?"

**Goal of outreach:** you are "selling the meeting," not the service. When someone replies, lead with value (the creator audit).

## Sales: traditional call structure vs. shadow operator calls

**Traditional call:** rapport (2 to 3 minutes), pre-frame, discovery, transition, pitch.
- Pre-frame script: "I'm going to ask you a few questions... all I ask for is transparency... if I believe we can help you, I'll make you an offer. Does that sound fair?"
- Discovery: find the current state, desired state, the gap, the opportunity cost and any doubts. Example questions:
  - "How many clients do you get on average per month?"
  - "How much profit do you want to be making in 6 months?"
  - "Where would you be today if you had a solution 6 months ago?"
  - "Why have you never tried a solution like ours before?"

**Shadow operator (two calls, no pitch):**
- **Call 1:** only gather information. Ask:
  - Their 6 to 12 month goals and income targets.
  - What holds them back.
  - Their audience's biggest problem and frequent questions.
  - What transformation the audience wants.
  - What they've sold before.
  - Their vision (coaching, course, community).
  End by saying you see opportunities and will put together a plan.
- **Call 2:** present the plan and the numbers.

**Worked math (30,000 followers, $95 product, 2.5% conversion):**
- 750 sales × $95 = $71,250.
- 50/50 split: about $35,000.
- 70/30 split: about $21,000.
- 90/10 split: about $7,000.
- All of it is pure profit with no employees.

He stresses this has no upfront cost and no risk to the creator, so there is nothing to overcome.

**Auto revenue split:** he says every recall is a reframe, meaning each time a client remembers to pay you, they reconsider. Creators manually sending a share to a partner creates friction. He says he is a co-owner of WAP, a platform processing almost $200 million a month in payments. His team built an auto revenue split feature. For a $100 sale at 70/30, the creator gets $70 and the operator gets $30 at the moment of sale, so the creator never feels like they're paying you. He compares it to Uber.

## Service delivery: contractor arbitrage vs. AI arbitrage

**Traditional:** the agency model gets paid upfront, so you stay cash-flow positive when paying contractors.
- Contractor arbitrage: hire skilled people in places like Brazil, South America or Serbia for around $500 per client per month, while you charge the client $2,000.
- Find them on Upwork, Fiverr, Facebook groups or OnlineJobs.ph.
- You still need to know the basics, so you can sell confidently, manage them and quality check.

**Shadow operating:** he says "contractor arbitrage is getting replaced by AI arbitrage." The creator is the expert, and AI tools produce the marketing assets: sales copy, landing pages and course outlines.
- Tools named: Ghostwriter OS (he says he uses it daily and that it produced the story sequence for his own launch) and Synthesize AI for program outlines.
- He says AI doesn't take sick days or raise prices, and does in minutes what took a contractor a week.

**Build on WAP:**
- Mentorship plus community: create the account, product and store page, then add the calendar bookings app and the forums app.
- Video course: same setup but with the courses app. The creator records modules and sends them to you for upload.

## Launching with the Instagram story sequence

He gives a rule to build anticipation before the product goes in front of the audience. He says there are 15 launch frameworks but recommends starting with the Instagram story sequence. He says it is the same strategy he uses, and that he has "broken the WAP records twice this year."

**Why stories work:**
- Story viewers are the most engaged followers.
- Stories feel intimate, like a conversation.
- They disappear in 24 hours, which creates urgency and FOMO.
- A first-ever product has pent-up demand.

**Format:** a 14-day sequence with three phases: warm-up, pre-launch, open cart. The creator posts 5 to 10 stories a day using a Hook, Story, CTA structure.
- Day 1: "Something's been on my mind lately," then a story about the audience's problem, then "Can you relate to this?"
- Day 7: reveal what you've been building, then "doors open tomorrow at 10:00 a.m."
- Day 14: "Last chance, cart closes in 2 hours," with final testimonials and "link in bio before it's gone."

He says you don't need to be a copywriter. The creator knows the audience's problems and language, and you structure it from what they tell you. After the first launch you can refine, repeat and launch again.
