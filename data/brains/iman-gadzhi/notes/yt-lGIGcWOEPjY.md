## Agencies get paid up front, so the bank balance is the real lever

Iman argues that an agency's finances are unusually simple. The client pays first, the team gets paid later, and software is the only real recurring cost. That leaves the owner free to put cash into the bank and into themselves.

- He compares agencies with gyms and e-commerce brands, which carry employees, payroll and ad spend that drive the business.
- He describes agency profit margins as "ridiculous" because the product is a service, not a physical product or commodity.
- His advice applies at any size: ten employees, five, or just the owner.
- Capital allocation means deciding where the money goes, and he says you first need money to allocate.

## Bill on day one, pay contractors on day 30

Iman's rule is to bill the client up front and start work only after the invoice is paid.

- Example: he signs a client on January 1, collects payment, onboards them, and the contractor bills him around day 30.
- Contractors are often paid per client per month; his example is roughly $700 per client per month for email marketing, Facebook Ads or Google Ads.
- This gives a roughly 30-day "float", meaning positive cash flow with the client's money in hand before the team cost is due.
- He compares the float to why Warren Buffett has extra capital to invest from a business like Geico, and he skips going deeper.
- Because staff get paid after the client does, team cost is never a place you need to "allocate" cash.
- He says an agency's costs boil down to software and team members. The gym owner by contrast lives with payroll stress and can't pay staff a share of memberships.

## Keep software spend small

Software is the one ever-present expense, and it stays tiny relative to revenue.

- Iman's agency was doing about $40-45K a month in profit (his figure) and about $50K a month in revenue, with software around $300 a month.
- His stack: Typeform (about $24/month, which he says does the same thing as Google Forms and he just likes the style), ClickFunnels, Loom Pro (he admits he doesn't need it), and a tool for running ads and making new ad variations.
- When bootstrapping, he suggests free options: Google Slides, Canva, Google Forms, ClickFunnels free trial.
- Upgrade software only as the agency scales.

## First money goes into the bank

Iman's first priority for profit is cash reserves, and he repeats it emphatically ("please please please put some money in the bank").

- Story: a private consulting client was making around $15-25K a month and told Iman he had only about $7,500 in the bank. Iman was "blown away" and called it "not a healthy business."
- His point: you can't decide where to put money most effectively if you've put none away.
- Cash in the bank is also a sales tool. Two equally skilled salespeople pitch the same client, one with $30K in the bank and one with $3K; the one with $3K needs the deal more, and prospects can tell.
- He cites this as one reason he keeps landing high-ticket clients when pitching $5K-10K/month retainers: his case study funnel makes it plain he doesn't care whether any single prospect signs, because he has money in the bank.

## "The hungry don't get fed"

Iman uses this phrase to explain why scarcity hurts sales.

- Example: people pitch Gary Vee all the time with free work and "value", though he is the person who least needs it. Nobody does that for an ordinary person just starting an agency.
- Prospects "smell" neediness "from a mile away"; abundance attracts more abundance. He calls this simple psychology and social proof, not mysticism.
- Desperate owners after a dry month start signing clients who aren't a fit. They chase prospects, trip over their words and handle pushback badly.
- The right posture is willingness to walk away at any moment: if you lose this client, you have tons more. Prospects then read you as the right person for the job.
- Background he shares: the only tattoo he has says "my cup runneth over", meaning "I have more than I need". He ties it to his childhood and to his mother, the youngest of seven children from the Soviet Union, who taught him to "take everything you have because you never know when you're gonna get more". His view is that in a first-world country with lots of opportunity, you need to drop that scarcity mindset.

## Invest in yourself early, then in paid traffic

Iman says the best early investment for an agency owner is self-education.

- Reasoning: an e-commerce business needs paid traffic, which needs cash in the bank. Agency lead generation (video outreach, email outreach, cold calling, LinkedIn, personal brand with inbound leads) costs almost nothing.
- So at the start, "the only person driving you forward" is you, the main lever of the agency. Improving yourself is the highest-return spend.
- Once profit is consistently around $20-40K a month, he recommends spending on paid traffic in one of two ways:
  - Growing your own social platforms with ads. He points to Gary Vee spending millions on social ads, which then lets him command retainers of $200-500K a month.
  - Building an auto-acquisition funnel, such as a case study funnel or webinar funnel. He says there are four or five ways to get agency clients through paid traffic.
- He warns that funnels are hard to get right at first, and you will probably lose a couple of thousand dollars initially.
- He uses a case study funnel for his own agency.

## The flywheel: cash makes sales easier

Iman's summary ties the points into a loop. He references Jeff Bezos's book on Amazon and Jim Collins's "Good to Great" for the flywheel idea.

- More clients lead to more money in the bank, which makes it easier to sign clients, because you walk into meetings not needing the deal.
- Priority order for agency profit: 1) put money in the bank (it improves your sales and gives security), 2) invest in yourself, 3) later, spend on paid traffic once profit is solid.
- The agency stands out from a gym or e-commerce brand because margins are high and the main driver of the business is the owner, whereas for e-commerce it is paid traffic.
