## Why SMMA is the best beginner model

- Iman started with a social media marketing agency (SMMA) in 2016 and ran it for six years, until 2022. He calls it the business that "paved the way for the rest of my career": his first $10,000 a month, moving out at 17, and supporting his mom.
- Gomez (founder of Lemlist), Sam Ovens (founder of Skool), Alex Hormozi (founder of Gym Launch) and Andrew Wilkinson (founder of Tiny) all started with service businesses or agencies.
- He judges a beginner business model on four metrics: startup capital, profit margin, demand and fulfillment. The best beginner models are low capital, high margin, high demand and easy to fulfill.
- Dropshipping scores badly:
  - Startup cost is $1,000-$5,000, mostly for testing ads.
  - Margins are 10-60% (he says 60% is too high) after ads, chargebacks and refunds.
  - Demand is low because the products are trendy gimmicks.
  - Fulfillment has lots of admin from logistics, refunds and support.
- Agencies score well:
  - Startup cost is $50-$100 (a domain and Google Workspace).
  - Margins are 65-98%.
  - Demand is high because you solve real business problems.
  - Fulfillment is easy when done the way he teaches.
- Iman says he closed his last agency program, Agency Accelerator, at the end of 2024. He made this video as a free blueprint for getting to $10,000 a month.

## Niche selection: specialize so you can charge more and scale

- There is "no such thing as the best niche". The goal is the most suitable niche for your situation.
- Specialists beat generalists on trust and price. His analogy is a brain surgeon versus a general practitioner: "people always gravitate towards specialists over generalists", and specialists charge more.
- One niche means one way to find prospects, one outreach method, one delivery process. Multiple niches mean different channels (Instagram DMs vs LinkedIn) and a new delivery setup for every client, which Iman compares to running multiple businesses. "Increased variation in your business results in increased complexity and increased complexity hinders your ability to scale."
- Example: a friend scaled to over $150,000 a month with 100+ clients at peak by working only with real estate agents.
- Niche checklist:
  - **Revenue and profit:** the business should make at least $155,000 a month in profit before it will comfortably pay $2,000 a month. A $50,000-a-month business barely notices $2,000.
  - **Product ticket:** pick businesses with expensive products. A coffee shop paying $2,000 at $2 profit per cup must sell 1,000 extra cups to break even. A roofer earning $10,000 profit per job gets a 5x return from one extra job a month. It is easier to promise one extra sale than a thousand.
  - **Growth and stability:** avoid dying industries. His example is newspaper printers (dying) versus info businesses (growing).
  - **Market size:** home renovation has millions of businesses. Asian-themed wedding dress designers are so rare you'd need a near-100% close rate to reach $10,000 a month.
  - **Competitive advantage:** past work in the niche, knowing the language, or a family or network with warm leads.
  - **Sophistication level:** as a beginner, sell to unsophisticated niches so you're "the expert in the room". E-commerce owners have often run ads for years. Real estate agents almost never have. Even 2-8 weeks of focused study puts you ahead of them.
- His difficulty key:
  - Advanced: SaaS, law firms, cybersecurity, fintech, e-commerce.
  - Intermediate: solar, accountants, chiropractors, med spas, real estate, car detailers.
  - Beginner: HVAC, pest control, roofing, painters, pool installation, landscapers.

## Service selection: find the problem first

- There are two types of service:
  - **Convenience** services save time: social media management, videography, video editing, reputation management, web design.
  - **Return-on-investment** services make money: paid traffic, email marketing, copywriting, SEO, lead generation. These are usually easier to sell because the client sees an investment with a monetary payoff.
- To avoid analysis paralysis, ask two questions: what is the biggest problem businesses in my niche face, and what is the most efficient way to solve it? Research by talking to business owners and reading online.
- Example: roofers' biggest problem is not being at client capacity, so they need more clients. SEO, paid traffic or email marketing could all deliver that.
- Choose among candidate services using four considerations:
  - **Skill set:** use skills you already have. He started as a creative agency because his background was photo and video.
  - **Scalability:** location-bound services cap your client count. Videography means one client at a time. Paid traffic lets you manage many ad accounts from home. This is why he switched from a creative agency to an advertising agency.
  - **Upfront cost:** videography needs thousands in equipment. Copywriting needs a laptop. Keep upfront costs minimal as a beginner.
  - **Income variation:** project-based work (e.g. $8,000 for a website) is lumpy. Recurring work (e.g. $2,000 a month for 6 months of paid traffic) is stable.

## Agency setup: don't overthink it

- **Name:** his was "IG Media" (his initials). It stopped making sense within a year when he became an advertising agency, and he still made hundreds of thousands a year. If you're stuck, use Namelix.
- **Domain:** buy on Namecheap. If the name is expensive, pick a new name or a variation. Prefer .com and avoid .edu and .org.
- **Logo:** make it in Canva (the free version is fine). A nice logo won't decide success or failure.
- **Personal socials:** clean up your personal profiles, because prospects will look at them. No party or drinking photos, but don't be "a corporate robot" either. "People want to do business with people who ooze authority."
- **Email:** set up Google Workspace for a professional address and for Slides, Docs, Sheets and Gmail.
- **Email warm-up:** new accounts have low deliverability and land in spam at volume. Use a warm-up tool (he names Lemwarm); it takes 3-4 weeks, so start immediately.
- **Website:** optional. He made close to $20,000 a month, if not more, before he had an agency website. He mentions his own software company, Floy, as an all-in-one agency hub (website builder, CRM, proposals, invoicing, onboarding funnels, client space), but says any tool works.
- **Other tools:**
  - Stripe for payments (Payoneer if Stripe isn't available in your country).
  - Zoom, upgrading to Pro once you start booking sales calls, because the free plan's 45-minute limit looks amateur. Google Meet is free.

## Offer creation and pricing

- An offer is your niche plus service plus pricing model, plus how you package it. Two agencies with the same niche, service and pricing can have very different offers because packaging changes perception.
- The goal of an offer is to bridge the gap between a prospect's current state (unsolved problem) and desired state (problem solved).
- Components of an offer:
  1. **Promise:** a tangible result in a time frame, e.g. "30 qualified booked appointments in 90 days" or "four long-form YouTube videos in 60 days".
  2. **Methodology:** a brief outline of the steps. Example: you run social ads to the target market, leads fill in a form, and qualified leads are booked into the client's calendar.
  3. **Pricing model:**
     - Monthly retainer.
     - Pay per project.
     - Pay per lead.
     - Percentage of ROAS (return on ad spend: what you brought in minus ad cost, taking a percentage of the difference).
     - A setup fee can be added to any model except pay per project.
  4. **Price:** research what other agencies charge, start somewhere you're comfortable with and aligned with the market, and undercut slightly. If every prospect jumps at it with no pushback, raise the price. If everyone says it's too high, lower it.
  5. **Contract duration:** a minimum term, never more than 90 days. Three months gives enough time to optimize and get results, and protects you. The client doesn't have to leave at the end; terms are renegotiated.
- Bonuses to go the extra mile: free website audits, value resources, lead reactivation.
- Guarantees: Iman is generally not a fan. They "do more bad than good" and signal that you're begging for business. The one he'd use in a tough negotiation is a pro-rata guarantee. Example: you promise 20 appointments for $2,000, so each is worth $100; if you deliver 18, refund $200 instead of the full amount. He holds it as a fallback, not a headline offer.

## Prospecting and outreach

- Prospecting means building a list of businesses in your niche with contact details. Ideally collect name, location, email, number and social media.
- Three methods:
  - **Manual:** highest quality, slow, free. Use Google Maps for local businesses, Checkatrade for blue collar, Zillow for real estate agents, LinkedIn for corporate professionals.
  - **Software:** fast and cheap, with lower quality, but worth it for the volume. He names Apollo, LeadSwift, LeadGorilla and Clay.
  - **Lead generators:** contractors on Upwork or Fiverr who build lists for you. Most expensive but hands-off. Buy a small, cheap list first to test quality before buying a bigger one.
- During outreach "you aren't selling your service". You are pitching the meeting only.
- Pick the outreach method to match how the niche works: cold calling, cold email or DMs. Home renovation businesses are on the move, so calls beat email. SaaS companies are active on email, so the opposite is likely true.
- Every message needs three elements:
  - Highlight the pain point.
  - Emotional buy-in (the result of the pain being gone).
  - A no-brainer offer: the meeting plus a bonus such as free resources, SOPs or templates.
- Outreach is a numbers game. Monthly thresholds to sign clients regularly: bronze 2,000, silver 3,000, gold 4,000 outreaches. Volume matters most early on because you have no credibility yet, and more data shows what to change in method and copy.
- Better path: inbound through a personal brand. He says he turned down about 90% of the businesses that wanted to work with his agency without running ads or doing cold outreach. He started with a micro personal brand: an audience of a few thousand where everyone is interested in what you share and sell. He pitches his company Monetize for this, which was closed to enrollment and reachable by DMing him "personal brand" on Instagram.

## Sales call structure

- Prep for calls: Zoom Pro, a clean professional background (no bed), and fast, stable Wi-Fi.
- Call structure: rapport, pre-frame, discovery, transition, pitch.
- **Rapport:** the first 2-3 minutes, an icebreaker so the call isn't awkward.
- **Pre-frame:** ask permission to move to business, then set the agenda. His script: you'll ask a few questions about the business, you ask for transparency, you'll make an offer if you believe you can help, and if not you'll try to connect them with someone who can. "Does that sound fair enough to you?" Delivered with confidence, it lets you lead the call instead of the prospect taking over.
- **Discovery** is the most important part, in five parts:
  1. **Current state:** clients per month, profit per client, how many clients they could handle, what they're doing now to solve it.
  2. **Future state:** profit they want in 6 months, extra clients needed, what life looks like if they hit it. Keep it tied to finances.
  3. **The gap:** break down the numbers and ask what concrete plan they have if they hadn't jumped on this call. Most don't have one, which they realize on the call.
  4. **Opportunity cost:** where would they be today if they'd had a solution 6 months ago, and where will they be in 6 months without one. Answering aloud raises problem awareness.
  5. **Doubt:** surface objections early. Ask why they've never tried a solution like yours, and what went wrong if they used another agency.
- **Transition:** summarize what you learned and ask what you missed. Then offer two options: answer more questions, or break down how you can help. About 99% of the time they ask for the breakdown.
- **Out-of-this-world frame:** before pitching, ask "if I break everything down and it makes sense how we're able to help you, would it be completely out of this world for you to get started today?" It weeds out financial objections early.
- **Pitch:** explain the methodology in their language, since "confused buyers never buy". Keep getting verbal agreement with "does that make sense?". Finish with "where would you like to go from here?", which leads them to ask for the price.

## Onboarding and service delivery

- Onboarding is collecting payment, getting information and granting access. Send a small onboarding gift for a good first impression.
- Avoid manual onboarding over Zoom, email or WhatsApp; it's slow and looks amateur. Use an onboarding funnel link, ideally with a video on each page. Order:
  1. **Contract and payment:** a downloadable, e-signable contract (stating payment terms and length) and the first invoice. Collect payment at the end of the sales call, then let the client continue on their own.
  2. **Welcome video:** you welcoming them, covering your mission and values, past results and expectations, with a "get access" button.
  3. **Client space access:** plus a Loom walking through how to use it.
  4. **Strategy call booking:** a call to cover anything still needed and plan the next few months.
- **Contractor arbitrage:** Iman is a Content expert, not an ads expert, when he moved to advertising, so he hired someone to run the ads. Agencies get paid upfront and pay the team at month-end, so there are no cash-flow problems. Experts in places like Brazil, South Africa and Serbia can earn well on $500 per client per month while you charge, for example, $2,000. At five clients that is $2,500 a month for them, which is a strong local salary.
- He found his first contractor in a Facebook group. Other places: Discord, Fiverr, Upwork. Run a real interview process; Upwork and Fiverr let you post specific job roles.
- Still learn the basics of the service you sell. It makes you more confident selling, helps you spot talent in hiring, and lets you manage the team better.
