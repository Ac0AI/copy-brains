## Starting out: first client, pricing, and rejection (2016)

- Iman signed his first client in August 2016, his old football club, without knowing the words "retainer" or "social media marketing". To him it was "I'm doing this service for a client in return for a monthly fee."
- He quoted weekly because it "sounded cheaper": £95 a week, about £380 a month. Deliverables were roughly 4 YouTube videos, 30 Facebook posts and 30 Instagram posts. Effective pay was about minimum wage, but he cared more about landing more retainers, at higher prices.
- In 2016 he sent proposals and took many meetings, got rejected "again and again", and treated every rejection as a reference experience. Later he strongly discourages sending proposals.
- One early proposal (Skin Yoga, around October 2016) offered Instagram distribution and account management at £150 a week, plus £150 a week for content creation. It pitched December as the busiest growth month and urged building momentum for Q4.
- He says credentials mean nothing and experience means everything, and that school isn't important for what he's doing.

## Why social media marketing and agency work compound

- By 2018 he describes social media marketing as "one of those financial vehicles that gets better over time". It doesn't get saturated, because you as a person become more valuable.
- Two years in he was signing five-figure monthly retainers, after starting at £380.
- He pitches age as an advantage: he understands the platforms better than a 30 or 40-year-old marketing graduate, since digital marketing has existed about 20 years and social media about 10.
- Acting like you belong in the room matters: "don't qualify yourself". Trying to prove you're worthy signals that you're beneath the people you're meeting, and high-level people "cut straight through that BS".
- Mindset framing: "you get what you think you deserve, not what you deserve." He uses affirmations, and prepares mentally for big meetings. He credits one multimillion-pound tech client, among his first five, to preparing his state the whole day before the meeting.
- His cut-off rule for ambition: write macro one-year goals down. In August 2016 he wrote eight, including making £15,000 in a year, which seemed crazy and then looked on track.

## 2017: the first breakthrough, and getting burned

- He grew to $20,000 a month at age 17 while doing everything wrong: a creative agency with no niche, no auto-billing, sloppy onboarding, and doing the work himself.
- His first full-time employee, a videographer, lasted about three days (the video says three days, and a week in another place) before quitting because the work was too commercial.
- By August 2017 he lost around 40% to 50% of revenue (he cites both figures), which he blamed partly on himself. He had become complacent and stopped being a student, and he says comfort breeds complacency. Some of the loss was outside his control.
- August is a "shit month" for clients in any industry.
- By end of 2017 he felt burnt out and treated like "just any other contractor" rather than a respected business owner. He wanted that changed in 2018.
- His view of entrepreneurship: not endless networking events and deal closing, but sitting for hours building systems, watching them fail, rebuilding, and "think blocks" to check whether what you're doing works.

## 2018: niching down to paid traffic and landing whale clients

- 2018 was when he gained full clarity. He moved from a creative agency, to a general full-service agency, to offering only paid traffic on Facebook and Google.
- His niche is about 80% info-product businesses and 20% e-commerce. He says the two have enough affinity to serve both. The niche locked in around April 2018, about 1 year 8 months in.
- Pricing step-ups: he describes quoting a huge agency $66,000 and realising he could have charged $10k to $15k more (the transcript is ambiguous on the exact figure). He started doing two-day trainings for $20,000 with expenses paid, including in New York.
- A "whale" client is $8,000 a month in retainer, roughly $100,000 a year in fees. He landed several in 2018.
- Valuation logic: if you're worth 100 units to someone, charge 20 units. A client explained he'd pay because it would take them about three years to build that skill in-house, and he didn't want to wait a year.
- He notes inbound beats outbound: clients came through his content and funnel (one converted six months after entering), and he gets no cold calls.
- A 2018 example: an ecommerce client (Oura-style ring brand) spent $4,700 on ads over two weeks and brought in $156,000, which he frames as about $151,000 of profit.

## 2019: ads for his own agency, whale clients, and retainer vs percentage

- Around February to March 2019 he started running ads for the agency itself, and that brought 70% to 80% of clients for the rest of the year. He was comfortable spending $2,000 to $6,000 to acquire a client, since the first month's retainer covered it.
- Minimum retainer was £4,000 a month. Minimum term was three months, but average retention ran longer. He occasionally accepted £3,600 retainers when the back end (percentage of return on ad spend) was where the money would come from.
- July 2019 was the first month he hit $100,000 profit from the agency.
- In 2018 and 2019 he mostly avoided performance-based pay to keep cash flow predictable, and says he missed out on large upside when campaigns went well. He wishes he'd added percentage of ROAS earlier.
- Focus principle: "the real power in business is traction, not addition". He rejected adding services like Instagram management, email marketing or IP protection (each could be $2k to $12k a month), and chose to stay focused on one service.
- His specialisation rule: by seven figures you need to specialise in one service offering (lead gen, funnels, webinar funnels, etc.).
- A lesson in cash flow stress: when lead flow is low you get into a scarcity mindset and make worse decisions. He once refunded an $8,000-ish month, and the client pushed back, saying they appreciated his integrity.
- Payment rule: "until the payment has come through, they are not a client."
- Client filtering: he turned down a client whose ad performance made a 4x return on ad spend target unrealistic.

## 2020: restructuring and retention

- By 2020 his agency was his most successful year. He added another media buyer to remove the capacity bottleneck that limited 2019.
- He revamped systems: new website, onboarding, custom reporting.
- Average monthly retainers were lower than 2019, but he signed five and six month minimums and made retention the priority. His logic: if clients stay 12 to 18 months, you need few of them to hit $100k to $200k months of profit.
- Nearly every deal now includes a percentage of ROAS performance component, so the agency earns more when the client does.
- His 2019 prices were high; by 2020 he deliberately had a different approach to pricing, which he says he covers in more detail elsewhere.

## What he says changes at 12 to 24 months, and the agency ladder

- He claims something shifts around month 9 to a year, and it's most pronounced at 18 to 24 months, when owners feel "so much resolve". He says he sees this across thousands of students in his education business.
- Progression he describes for agency services: social media distribution and growth ($5k to $6k a month), then paid ads, funnels, email marketing and retargeting, then consulting (come in, charge a big fee, leave).
- Agency profit margin: he says about 20% to 25% on revenue after outsourcing, though doing the work yourself raises it. Time commitment can shrink: he'd automated parts of the agency to about 4 hours a week of real work.
- His reasons for why agencies win: most competitors report vanity metrics like cost per click, whereas for e-commerce and info clients the key metrics are cost per conversion and return on ad spend. Better service than the agency next door is enough to win.
- Mindset points he repeats: "two shall pass" (good and bad both pass), pendulums swing back as far, and don't let a bad month define you.
- He ends by promoting his Agency Incubator program and a free training, and says his agency also funded retiring his mother and buying a Rolex at 18.
