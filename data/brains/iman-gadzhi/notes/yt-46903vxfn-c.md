## Why Iman went from a seven-month drought to a $100K month (2016-2018)

- Iman started with photo and video favors, then landed his first social media client in August 2016 at £95 per week. He quoted weekly because it "might sound cheaper" and was too scared to ask for £100.
- Seven months of work earned about £380 a month, which he says worked out to roughly 2 hours below minimum wage (30 Instagram posts, 30 Facebook posts, and managing Snapchat in person).
- Next client: Gymflow at £1,900 a month. Two or three days later he signed one at £500, then another at about £750. About 6 weeks after that he was at roughly $15-20K a month and dropped out of school at 17.
- Timeline he gives: about $5-5.5K a month, then $15K two months later. April 2018 was his first $40-50K month, and October 2018 his first $100K month.
- He says he did not quit during the drought because he never compared himself to others: "I was making 400 bucks a month, this is sick." Today's social media comparison would have made him feel shame, so he looked only at himself, stayed patient, and asked what his other option was.
- On the claim that he sells "get rich quick": "all I've done over the years is try to tell people hey it's going to be slow but what's your other option."
- When it rains it pours, in both directions. Multiple good (or bad) things tend to stack in a row.

## Pivoting from content to ads, and contractor arbitrage

- Clients paid £2,500 a month for full social media management and content, but some also spent £1,000 a month on Red Bull ads and kept hounding him over his fee. That frustration made him learn advertising and offer ad services to existing clients as a small add-on fee.
- It backfired at first. He was bad at ads and charged little, and clients saw him as bad at everything, so he lost about 70% of his business and got dropped for both services.
- The upside: it forced him to look outward for contractors. He realized he could use "contractor arbitrage" and the global market.
- Example: he charged UK and US clients $2,500-3,500 a month, and could pay a contractor in Brazil, South Africa, Romania or Serbia about $300 per client per month. Three clients gives them $900 a month, which for a university student is life-changing.
- Honest take on his early career: part of it was luck, and he stumbled onto business models at the right time. He says it is the same with his early fitness videos: he was early to simple, to-the-point fitness content, and the same videos would not get the same traction now.

## How he grew YouTube: thumbnail testing, the 7030 content split, and the evergreen backlog

- He took YouTube seriously from summer 2020 and went from about 400K to 3M subscribers by summer 2023. He says that growth rate was unprecedented on YouTube for self-development or fitness content.
- Thumbnails: since 2020 he has had three full-time thumbnail editors, each producing five options, so 15 options for every video, even though he uploads one video a week. He does not stage thumbnail shoots and uses whatever images exist online.
- Every day the team rehashes an old video by A/B split testing new thumbnails. He says that even if he did not upload for a year, he would still gain about 5,000 subscribers a day from the backlog (current average about 7,500 a day).
- The 7030 split: 70% of videos are to grow a new audience (he says he does not care if his existing audience watches these), and 30% are what his existing audience loves, so they do not unsubscribe. These are also the videos he enjoys making (events, investigations, vlogs).
- Result: every video gets at least 700K views in the first month, usually 1M or more.
- Failure modes he names: channels that only chase new audience bore their existing audience, and channels that only serve their existing audience never grow.
- Packaging matters most: the concept of the video, and why someone would click it in the recommended feed (what stands out or benefits them personally). He writes only 10-15 bullet points per video.
- Early on (2021) he did not care about YouTube and picked the thumbnail within minutes on upload day. He now has a chief content officer leading a content team of 40.

## Why he rarely sells or takes sponsorships

- He takes zero sponsorships. His team still sends out a rate card of $250,000 for a 30-second ad read, and dozens of companies have wanted to pay it.
- He says too many sponsors dilute and damage your brand, and that he sells his own product only two weeks a year (twice a year). The rest of the year he has no bio links and no links in YouTube videos.
- He claims he leaves about $50M a year on the table by not promoting more, and accepts that for longevity. He says top athletes and celebrities who DM him as fans would not do so if he pushed products weekly.
- When he does sell, he tries to make it entertaining.
- On sponsors in general: he criticizes gambling, crypto and Forex promoters because the sponsor's revenue is a percentage split of what the audience loses, and "for you to make money they have to lose money."
- The only brands he would consider are premium ones like Patek Philippe, and maybe Rolls-Royce.

## Risk management, pricing mistakes, and the skills he says matter most

- Five skills he names: sales, managing people (and emotions), paranoia, critical thinking, and finances and cash flow, including offense, defense and risk management.
- Paranoia means asking who is taking your spot, who is doing it better, and what the same thing for two more years would look like. He says: "there's no such thing as passive income" and that things decay: you can buy yourself time with hard work, but the market will take your spot.
- Crypto: he criticizes telling beginners with about $3,000 to trade crypto. Even with about $10M in crypto gains (he put about $1M into Bitcoin in November 2020 and rolled into Ethereum), he calls that luck, not skill, with no link to his business ability.
- His advice: work on businesses where 12 months of failure does no catastrophic damage. A $5K event or course that builds real skills will likely return more than $5K in a few years, unlike a trade that can go to zero.
- A mistake he owns: about six months earlier he made a pricing change at his main software company, which he calls "a horrible move." Growth stopped, and they are moving back to the old pricing.
- He says his biggest failures are people (someone you misjudge) and market assumptions (launches or pricing pivots), not ventures. He says he has never put money into a company and lost it.
- He says he is not a trained expert at some things but is very sharp with people. He knows who will stay 18 months and who will stay 18 years, though once in a while someone blindsides him.

## Spending, assets and a 30/70 rule

- His rule for free cash flow (dividends and investment returns taken personally): spend 30%, save and invest 70%. He recently ran the numbers and found he was not spending enough.
- He judges assets by whether the money is recoverable, not by price: "someone could spend $50 million on an asset and if they are guaranteed to return back $50 million... they're not spending money they're just parking money."
- Properties: he rents some out when he is not using them, so he gets to enjoy them while making a bit of money. He prefers not publicizing which ones, since owner identity changes who rents (he would rather host a 55-year-old billionaire family than party guests).
- Jet: he has explored buying one (about $25M). A jet broker told him that with his travel patterns it will never make sense, and a full-time charter would lose about $400K a year. He still wants one, and says it only makes sense once a $1-1.5M yearly loss does not matter.
- Cars: despite tens of millions earned, his first personal car came in March of that year. He did not buy supercars when his brand was growing because it was not authentic to him. He says he had to be his real self, not manufacture a flashy image for growth.
- Money milestones he cites: first million at 18, about $10M personal net worth at 21 (after tax), and he says he takes home tens of millions a year now. His happiest period was ages 19-20, doing a couple million a year in profit, with less stress and responsibility.
- He says that once you have around $10M, you do not need more, and that friends with $300M in their 20s can be a suicide risk because they lose purpose.

## Social media, privacy and fame

- He says he has toyed with leaving social media and is unsure how long he will stay. The reason he did not quit about 18 months before this interview: his business partner at his software company said he could drive so much business through his socials that quitting would be irresponsible.
- Most of his companies are unaffected by his social media, and some would do better if he could focus on them.
- He says it is better if people do not know your wealth or your abilities. People counting him out, apart from those who matter, is an advantage.
- He says he cannot show on social media what he now does (family office, corporate structures, investments, managing his team), because it does not help his audience. He was honest about every stage while he climbed (from £95 a week to a 40% revenue loss in one weekend), but that stopped being possible at the top.
- Boundaries: he will not take photos at his home or at a place of prayer. People have swum over to try to reach his house.
- On the risks of fame: being known for the sake of being known "is legitimately the stupidest thing anyone could ever do." He values being known for helping people.
- On content strategy (platform risk): he lost his Instagram blue tick multiple times and is shadow banned for his COVID content, and says he accepted the consequences. If a platform removed his videos, he would not complain about being deplatformed.

## Running an e-learning company and his view on university

- His e-learning company, Educate, positions itself as "10 times the impact on your income for one 100th the price of University." He describes it as MasterClass for hard skills (agency, sales, copywriting and other high-income skills). Pricing: $125 a month, with the first year paid up front, then monthly and cancelable anytime.
- Educators include himself (agency), the top sales expert who works in his company, his CMO (copywriting), and Jordan Welch.
- His case against university: 98% of people attend to appease parents or because they do not know what else to do, and only about 2% genuinely want to be a doctor or lawyer (for those people he stays quiet). He calls it theory taught by people who have never run a business.
- What he thinks should replace it: critical thinking and real mentorship, like a Roman school of philosophy you pick to match your worldview.
- He also hosted an in-person event for fans, which he describes as scratching an itch to talk operations (like handling 10,000 support tickets a day, or sending handwritten notes to 8,000 new customers a week) that he cannot discuss on social media. He does not do one-on-one meetings or paid dinners and says he has turned down $50-100K offers.
- He funds school building in Nepal through the Parar Trust: two schools in 2019, then two to four a year. It took six months to find a charity that took a large yearly donation seriously, because small charities assumed money laundering.
