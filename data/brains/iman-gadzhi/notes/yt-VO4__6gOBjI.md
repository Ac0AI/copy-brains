## Why Iman says profit and ownership beat revenue in info

- Iman calls the info space's focus on revenue "the most Preposterous ridiculous thing". He says he saw this in a Mastermind about seven years ago (2018) and left it for good, calling it a "weird cult" of people comparing revenue numbers.
- Revenue matters for software or e-commerce, where it feeds company value. In info it does not, because the founder is the service and the mechanism for getting customers, so the company is not sellable.
- His first question to any info founder is "what's your profit?" He cites the top person in the space doing $36M revenue and $1M profit.
- He would rather make $2M profit on $3M revenue than $3M on more complexity. He prefers $15M revenue with $3M profit to bloated, high-ego numbers.
- Revenue figures are often not cash collected. He gives his own example of an $800K month with about $490K collected.
- He says info is "the best business on Earth" for printing roughly $5M a year, and e-commerce friends are jealous of its margins. The mistake is applying billion-dollar-company ideas, such as "build a sellable asset", to info.
- He says he and one other person from that Mastermind each cashed out roughly $50-100M. He claims "over 100 million" total in info, with 95M+ mostly from YouTube, and says only about $2.5-3M went to Facebook ads, nearly all retargeting.

## Paid traffic vs organic customers

- Paid-traffic customers have higher refund and dispute rates and are less forgiving. Organic customers who know, like and trust him are patient.
- Example: his custom platform has had outages of 1-2 hours over four to five years. With paid traffic that triggers "scam" alarm bells. With organic customers it does not.
- Ad spend is rented: "they were renters and eventually their landlord kicked them out". A Facebook spend of $10M/year leaves nothing behind once it stops.
- He is not against ads. His rule: "if something's working you don't touch it" (profit, not revenue), but build organic in parallel.
- For events he spends about $300-400K on retargeting. Leads cost about $2 on average, and he gets 350-500K leads per event. Leads are scored from a Typeform into A/B/C/D tiers, and D leads will not buy. He says cost per qualified A-tier lead is about $1.
- He treats his short-form accounts (300M views a month) as retargeting data for events, not as a sales channel.

## Why recurring low-ticket info is a trap

- He launched a $37/month mobile app offer and says "don't recommend it", calling it "the stupidest model". He claims nearly eight figures of profit from low-ticket recurring, with the offer a year old and close to eight figures.
- Info and continuity clash: impact is highest in the first month, "imagine you bought a book today and in 4 months they're still charging you for it". Continuity works for software, meal delivery or supplies.
- Reason from LTV, not MRR. His $37 product has about 6 months average retention and $222 LTV, and he ships a new masterclass every month (one this month with a boxing expert with a 2M-subscriber channel, filmed on RED cameras).
- Worked example of the $100/month fantasy:
  - 1,000 members at $100/month looks like $1.2M a year.
  - At 4 months average retention, LTV is $400, so 1,000 customers is about $400K.
  - Getting 1,000 buyers at a 1% sales-page conversion needs about 100,000 clicks, which would cost far more than $400K in ads.
- He says he told a $1.4B-a-year education owner his e-learning acquisition's numbers before being told them: about 4-month retention, under 10% on the yearly plan, and not profitable.
- Dan's side agrees (a $97/month site that stopped being worth it), but Iman's point is that the same effort goes further at higher price points.
- He prefers B2B software, where products embed in a business and are hard to leave, such as inventory management for e-commerce firms with 30-100+ SKUs and $5M+ revenue, packages from €200 to $10K/month. He finds B2C software churn brutal.

## Reinvesting, time-for-money, and non-negotiables

- On "I need to reinvest in the business": info has no real startup cost and ad returns show within 30 days. Inventory-based businesses actually reinvest, and he has wired over $1M for stock. For info he asks "what are you reinvesting in?"
- Info is "a three out of 10 difficulty business". His path was to reach $10M liquid, then choose between retiring or placing bigger bets. He took the bets into software, physical products and investing in software companies.
- On "never sell your time": Taylor Swift and F1 drivers sell their time and earn hundreds of millions. Time is fine to sell if it is valuable enough, and customers often buy the founder, so removing yourself kills the appeal.
- Example: someone at $100K/month wanted to 2.5x with a $15K offer while fully removed. Iman's answer was to give the buyers about 2.5 hours a week of one-on-one.
- Everyone gets non-negotiables. His is no sales team. He would accept a bit less money for that.
- Life design first: ask "what do you want in life?" and "what monthly passive amount would make you chill?" His own target is about $500K/month with a team he can count on two hands and 25 days a month free.
- A lifestyle version he describes: a micro-niche YouTube channel, a course, a community and some one-on-one, 15-20 hours a week, about $60-100K/month.
- He buys the domains of failed info companies that once did $25-30M a year, "just to prove a point".

## YouTube and content strategy

- YouTube is for people who want to learn, short form is for consumers in "entertainment and consumerism" mode. He says short form is not worth it for info or services, and suits physical products.
- His short-form results: 300M views a month across his own accounts. He once had 65 full-time staff posting about 15,000 pieces a month and 150 accounts. He now has about 10 people and 70 accounts, with 3x the views. He personally spends roughly 90 minutes to 2 hours a month on it.
- A $37/month offer (Digital Launchpad) sold to the short-form audience made about $30K in month one before he took it off, because "they're non buyers" (wrong countries and mindset).
- Content team: $20K/month on a team keeps paying off, unlike ads. Editors cost about $1,000/month each, and $5K/month is enough to start. He wants the team's scripting involved for customer-facing roles but does not outsource his own ideas.
- A new YouTube channel can work in 30-90 days. He suggests sending your existing list or friends to it for retention data.
- Don't chase virality: "there's a big difference between a viewer and a lead".
- His approach is to treat YouTube like an ad platform: the thumbnail is the creative, the title is the hook, and the video concept is the targeting. Example of bad targeting: a "day in the life of a Dubai millionaire" video brings in anyone who likes Dubai, not buyers of your $20K B2B offer. A day in the life of a $5-10M infopreneur is better.
- He expanded audience in steps. He started with SMMA/agency content, took it to the top, then went to online business and later general self-help. His business content is on a separate channel (about 10K subscribers) that he never promotes, and he opens with "if you're making less than $250,000 a year, just click away".
- Production quality: high-ticket and educated audiences are put off by heavy production, so he keeps advanced content less produced. Mass-market audiences like animation.

## Emotional vs logical selling, and selling on YouTube

- A savvy, upper-end B2B audience wants about 90% logical, value-based content. Amp up emotion only in the conversion phase (a VSL or live event). A wider mass audience needs more emotion and less tactics.
- Consumer products: eyewear buyers do not want a motivational speech. He told his team to cut a monologue from a shoot as "patronizing".
- Men buy aspirational lifestyle products, with "show, don't tell". He says his female creative director and COO want relatable content, and he pushes back for male buyers.
- Where to send viewers:
  - Cheap products can be sold directly in content. He mentions a new eyewear collection in a vlog doing $150K that day.
  - Hot, tactical videos that make viewers want the system go straight to a call. His VSL of nearly 10M views made about $20M.
  - Conceptual videos (such as hiring A players) send people to Instagram to nurture.
- He sells his higher-end offer only after screening: nothing under $250K/year revenue, no crypto/trading or OnlyFans businesses, and no businesses he doesn't think he can help. Sales go through a call, and he sends people to a 3-day event.

## Personal brand as the real asset, plus physical products

- He claims his brand has brought him advisory shares worth over $10M, discounted equity in every company he invests in, his pick of talent worldwide, and access to hard-to-get products. He says "take away everything I have, leave me my personal brand, I'll make 100 million back within 3 years."
- You don't need to be famous. A small but strong brand in your niche is "the tip of a spear". Everyone already has a personal brand, and for most it works against them.
- Brand costs: his entire content team is about $250K a year, versus the multi-million-dollar ad budgets of rivals who ended up with nothing.
- Eyewear business (iWare/Eazi, with a partner who sold a billion dollars of eyewear, and 100 stores via Hawkers):
  - He started about four years ago; the first order was around $30K.
  - He says it has made close to $8M, all organic, with 80-95% gross margins. His partner bought 40% of the company 8 months before the interview.
  - Eyewear is a utility product with timeless shapes, so it needs no brand story (unlike clothing).
- E-commerce caveats: even at $30M revenue and 20% EBITDA ($6M), about $3M is held back for stock. Customs delays have held his products for 3-4 months during covid.
- He says he spent between $100K and $1M a month on lifestyle and felt no difference above $100-250K a month. He bought a Phantom for the seats, and for cars like the Maybach he suggests a 2-3K-mile high-trim S-Class and letting someone else take the depreciation.
- Take operators seriously: he has a technical co-founder or partner in every non-info business, and hired a new COO from Munich for Madrid.
