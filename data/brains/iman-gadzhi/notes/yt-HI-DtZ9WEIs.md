## Ownership over building: why Iman buys 5-20% stakes instead of building unicorns

- By 2025 Iman concluded that "for this stage of life" he does not want to build a billion-dollar company. He prefers owning large pieces of billion-dollar companies.
- His target is roughly 5 to 20% of a company. Above 20%, he says he doesn't want to be involved in "the changing of a CRM or really the minutia." He wants to be impactful where he can be impactful.
- Big Day vs. Cadence: at the end of 2023 he started Big Day, a sparkling electrolyte drink (his version had a bit of caffeine). The founders of Represent (George and Ross) then pitched him to come in as a third partner on Cadence. The choice was 70% of his own venture or about 33% of another one.
- Big Day and Cadence were neck and neck in revenue for the first 9 months, and Ross even wanted to merge them. In 2025 Cadence moved to a different level. Big Day didn't get the "labor of love" it needed because he and his business partner both had too many other ventures.
- He cites Watt (his term: "the biggest case of that") as the clearest example of the stake strategy working.
- Oura Ring lesson: at 18 he charged Oura Ring 7,800 pounds a month, which equated to $10,000 a month, his first five-figure retainer. The then-CEO Harpreet offered either the retainer or the equivalent in stock, and Iman took the retainer.
  - He worked with them for 9 months. He calculates that Oura is now worth $11 billion, and that the equity offered in 2018 would be worth about $19 million today, the equivalent of about $2 million a month.
  - His takeaway: "no one teaches you the value of ownership."
- Caveats he adds:
  - Early on you have to think short-term; when you're "drowning" you take the cash, and that's reasonable.
  - 20% of nothing is still nothing. You have to identify good companies where even 1 to 5% becomes huge in the long run.
- He says there are many ways to reach your destination. You have to pick the path that fits who you are: owning a big stake in a unicorn lets him go fishing and hunting on a random Tuesday, which running one would not.

## Keep your passion project separate from how you make money (the HEELS example)

- Iman says he has always been good at keeping passion apart from how he makes money. This doesn't mean you shouldn't enjoy your work; he loves his.
- HEELS (formerly GAGGI): GAGGI started with clothes in 2019 and moved into glasses in 2020, so he has made glasses for about 6 years. It began as a labor of love, built because he wanted a specific type of blue light blocker. His admitted bad habit: when something he wants doesn't exist, he makes it himself, "which of course is generally not the best way to run a business."
- In 2025 he brought on business partners with a strategy that "100% works if executed", but he didn't want to execute it, and it didn't work out directionally.
- Advice he got from a founder whose e-commerce company did just over a billion dollars in 2025 (he refers to him as Hudson, owner of Comfort):
  - Raise the price to $60-$70.
  - Run buy two, get one free.
  - Activate TikTok Shop affiliates.
  - Iman says this is "100% right" and still chose not to do it.
- His reasoning: even at $30-40 million a year in revenue with 20% net margins, it wouldn't change much for him. Getting there would mean doing a lot of direct-response marketing he doesn't want to do.
- HEELS currently does "low multiple seven figures" in revenue. His plan:
  - Run it for 10 years.
  - Even if revenue reaches $20-30 million organically, he doesn't want to take money out; he wants to reinvest everything in the business and brand.
  - He doesn't want it to lose money, but he'd keep funding it even if it did, because he loves it.
- Examples of financially senseless things he does on purpose: expensive packaging, one-off products, candles he isn't sure he'll sell, and money clips. He told his COO Liz that in 2025, unless they were losing money or in a "code red," she shouldn't talk to him about projections or hitting numbers.
- His analogy: like artists choosing between a mass-market role and a niche indie film, "there's certain things in life that should be left just as passion."
- Brand evidence he cites: celebrity athletes and artists (including a Latin American artist with about 37-40 million followers) wear the product without being sent it, and wealthy friends who wore other luxury eyewear now wear only his. He compares the positioning to Chrome Hearts in the '90s: "anything it touches may not make sense from a commercial perspective but you just know it's going to be the best."

## "It's not what you do, it's who you do it with"

- His big 2025 reflection: success is less about the mission and more about the people. The mission doesn't need to be world-changing.
- Example mission: a cooking knife company whose purpose is "we want to make sure that anytime you cook, you have a smile on your face." His advice is to pick even a small problem, attach meaning to it, respect it, and do it with incredible people.
- He doesn't know what he'll be doing in 10 years, but says that if he does it with people he loves, "you can keep playing this game forever."
- What you remember is the inside jokes, the hard days and the small random moments (such as driving to sort out a license for a new office), not the expensive purchases or the milestones.
- Team principles he endorses:
  - Everyone plays their role, respects each other's role, and there is no ego.
  - Acknowledge what others can do that you can't, and work as a team.
  - "Life's too short to work with people you don't love and respect."
- He says clients ask why he doesn't start his own agency; he'd rather be part of a bigger company that aligns with his values and culture, working with high performers.
- He values real-world experience (his mother worked as a receptionist at an NHS hospital and at Harrods) and says watching how people treated her is why he stays kind and respectful to everyone.

## Keeping a chip on your shoulder as a motivator

- Ten years into entrepreneurship and content, he says he feels "the most unaccomplished I've ever felt" and went to Scotland to reassess and decide next steps.
- 2025 was a "generational year" for him, with two massive moves: a stake in WSO and acquiring consulting.com. Specifics of the deals, he says, aren't all disclosed publicly.
- He first thought it was bad to still feel like an underdog, then concluded it's good and worth keeping: "you still need to keep some of that dark motivation."
- He cites Michael Jordan, who reportedly found or invented slights even 20 years into his career to stay motivated.
- He says it's easy to get comfortable after success and that the hunger of earlier years made it easier to get up in the morning. New challenges and targets bring that hunger back.
- He believes his achievements get less credit than the same moves would from others. Examples he gives: buying consulting.com and giving what people paid $80 million for to all customers going back to 2018, and the structure of his WSO stake. He says he has the data room and intimate details of the businesses in the space, and WSO is his favorite company in it.

## Why people dislike you: "hate with emotion, justify with logic"

- Iman's 2025 realization: people dislike others emotionally and then invent logical justifications. His examples are the Kendrick vs. Drake beef (accusations that stuck without being proven) and a dinner at his home.
- At that dinner, an extremely wealthy person called a business owner "a scammer" with no real basis. Iman, a customer of that business, had found it the best experience of its kind in the world. His read: the person just didn't like him but couldn't say so, because disliking someone for no reason reflects poorly on you, so you need a "logical" reason.
- His takeaway: you can't be everyone's cup of tea, and you don't need to keep disproving every claim. He says his approach is to stay gracious and humble, and notes he pays for everyone at the table, even friends wealthier than him.
- He says envy is a driver: "they never want to see you doing better than them," and winning consistently annoys people.

## Perspective lines worth keeping

- "What you're living through right now is what you look back at and you're like, these are the good old times." He includes the bad stretches, e.g. a late-2024 crisis in which he had to "take the biggest L we ever had and turn it into a W" and felt like an episode of Succession.
- Don't be so fixated on the dream life that you can't enjoy the simple version you're in now; "still strive and achieve and try to push for more."
- Competitive drive for him is intrinsic ("it's for me"), not a way of earning love or approval.
