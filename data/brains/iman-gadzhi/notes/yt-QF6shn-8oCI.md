## Pre-call setup: tech, environment and presentation

- Iman says the most important skill in an agency is selling, and "99% of beginner agency owners have absolutely no idea how to sell." The system below is what he has taught to thousands of agency owners over 6 years.
- **Zoom:**
  - Use a paid Zoom Pro account. The free plan caps meetings at 45 minutes, and a 10-minute countdown appears at the top of the screen after 35 minutes. He calls that a terrible first impression, and Pro costs roughly $16 to $20-something a month.
  - Set up meeting reminders. The call may be the most important thing in the agency owner's day, but for the prospect it could be one of nine meetings.
  - Change the display name from "Zoom user" to your full name, and add a profile picture (a headshot or the agency logo, never blank).
  - Turn on the waiting room so you can get your notes in order and let the prospect in when ready.
- **Look like a business owner:**
  - No bed in the background and nothing that looks like a bedroom.
  - A clean white wall works. So does a tidy office with books, lighting or a hobby collection.
  - No clutter. He keeps one side of his desk "clean pristine" for calls.
- **Audio:** bad audio kills professionalism. He uses a Shure SM7B-type mic (or the cheaper MV7), and used a Blue Yeti earlier. When travelling he uses $20-25 wired Apple EarPods, and notes that many of his wealthiest friends do the same.
- **Lighting and camera:**
  - Use a softbox, or face a window for natural light.
  - A good laptop camera (e.g. MacBook) is fine. You can also use an iPhone as a camera through Zoom, and later buy a dedicated camera.
- **Personal presentation:**
  - Be showered and groomed.
  - Wear a simple collared shirt or well-fitting t-shirt (smart casual or semi-formal).
  - No hoodies, oversized prints or three-piece suits.
- **Internet:** lag throws off the rhythm and raises doubts about professionalism. Upgrade your connection or take calls from a co-working space.

## Researching the prospect before the call

Iman says to prepare like for a job interview. The research lets you assume familiarity, look professional and build "ammo" for objections. Learn:

- **Scope of product or service:**
  - What they sell, who their target market is, their price points, and services in their field they don't offer.
  - This shows whether they fit your niche, and lets you show knowledge without making them explain everything.
- **Locations:** whether they are a local family-owned business, a franchise or a global e-commerce business, and how wide their market is.
- **Current marketing:**
  - Whether they run ads, are active on social media, or are on lead-gen sites (his examples: Zillow for real estate, Checkatrade for home renovation).
  - This helps you handle objections and craft the angle for why your service adds more value than what they do now.

## One-call close vs two-call close

Iman used both over the years. Early in his agency career he aimed for one call; later he used two.

- **One-call close:**
  - Discovery through close happens in a single call.
  - It suits more seasoned owners, because you must think on your feet, handle objections quickly and keep energy high.
  - Pros: saves time and removes second-call no-show risk.
  - It works best when the offer varies little between clients, e.g. a simple offer for real estate agents or gym owners.
  - Downside: you have to build the offer on the spot, with no time to crunch numbers.
- **Two-call close:**
  - Call 1 is discovery. Call 2 is the tailored pitch and close.
  - He recommends it for beginners.
  - His agency's deals were complex: performance-based, working with e-commerce and info-product owners. His CMO ran discovery, and then both joined the closing call.
- **Why two calls work better:**
  - **Energy:** prospect energy, attention and interest drop as a call runs longer, and so does close likelihood. He compares it to a big shopping day, where by hour 4 or 5 you can't be bothered to try things on. Two 30-minute calls beat one 60-minute call, and the gap resets energy.
  - **Preparation:** the break lets you review the first call and tailor the presentation to their needs.
  - **Crunching the numbers:** if the prospect isn't at the level you expected, you can tweak the offer (e.g. for a lower-level client). By call 2 he was "70% dead set" on the offer.
- **If new info forced an offer tweak on call 2:**
  - He didn't schedule a third call.
  - He put the client in the Zoom waiting room and talked to his CMO about minimum term, price, and how much should be base versus performance.

## Sales call framework: rapport and pre-frame

The order is: rapport, pre-frame, discovery, transition, pitch, close.

- **Rapport (2 to 3 minutes):**
  - Break the ice naturally and ask a question or two about them.
  - Avoid weather small talk.
  - Speak with confidence, as if you've done this a thousand times.
  - If you share a commonality (a city, a product), he doesn't mind chatting about it for 2 to 3 minutes.
- **Pre-frame:**
  - Shift tone from casual to business.
  - Every call has one person who leads by asking questions and one who follows by answering. The seller must hold that authority, otherwise "you will get interrupted continuously and the call will inevitably go nowhere."
- **Pre-frame outcome 1: gain authority.** Say you've done this many times and typically know how these calls go. Say you'll be transparent about whether you can help, and ask for the same transparency from them.
- **Pre-frame outcome 2: show no desperation.**
  - Use a line like "If I believe that we can help you at the end of the call we will make you an offer, but if not we're going to try to point you in the direction of someone that can."
  - This puts the prospect at ease and draws them in, since "people want what they can't have."
- **Pre-frame outcome 3: agree an outcome.**
  - Say you just want "a yes or a no, let's avoid the maybe."
  - This heads off "I need to think about it" and "I need to speak to my wife" later.

## Discovery: the five elements

Iman calls discovery "arguably the most important part of any call." The aim is to highlight the problem so the prospect mentally sells themselves.

1. **Current state:** what they do, what they charge, average monthly sales, profit margins and monthly profit (you can work it out), and how they currently get clients.
2. **Future state:** financial goals, how client acquisition must change to hit them, and the timeline.
3. **Gap:** how many extra sales or clients they need per month. Judge whether your service can fill it. Also ask what their plan was if you hadn't approached them, and politely challenge them if they have none.
4. **Opportunity cost:**
   - Ask whether having your promised outcome would bring them closer to their goals.
   - Ask whether what they're doing now will get them there.
   - "When you say it, it means nothing, but when they say it themselves is when your message is hitting."
5. **Doubt:**
   - Ask why they haven't tried something like this before.
   - Ask whether it's their first time hearing about it, whether they've had bad agency experiences, and whether they doubt agencies bring value.
   - Surfacing this lets you manage objections before pitching.

## Transition into the pitch

The transition is the bridge from discovery to pitch. It shows you listened and gets buy-in to hear the offer.

- **Last chance to add info.** Example wording: "Thank you for all the information. Is there anything I missed or any information you'd like to share in order for me to understand your current situation better?"
- **Confirm synergy and fit.** Example: "Now that I have a good understanding of where you're at and where you're looking to get to and the gaps that currently exist, I think we could definitely help you get to your goals."
- **Permission to pitch.**
  - Offer two options: they ask a few questions, or you run them through the whole system from A to Z.
  - Always give the A-to-Z option second, because 99% of prospects won't know what to ask and nearly all will pick it.
  - Example: "…or alternatively what I can do is just run you through the process on how we help XYZ type of client, and you can ask me questions along the way. How would you prefer to do this?"
- **Final outcome agreement.** Example: "My goal is to make sure that you have complete clarity… by the end of today's call would it be out of this world to say that we could get you started today?" This surfaces third-party decision-maker objections early.

## The pitch: system, guarantee, testimonials, price

- **Pitch a system, not a service.** Business owners see services as expenses and systems as investments, so a tangible system raises perceived value. Keep it simple: "confused buyers never buy."
- **Guarantee:** if you have one, use it. Showing you bear the risk makes the offer a no-brainer because the prospect has nothing to lose.
- **Testimonials:**
  - Get them from clients as soon as you can.
  - Other people vouching shows the offer works, and credibility is why sales get easier over time.
- **Price:**
  - State it with confidence after they understand the system, guarantee and proof.
  - Do not justify the price, because justifying "radiates desperation."
  - Use silence after stating it, whatever their reaction.
  - He generally doesn't recommend offering multiple price options.

## Closing, collecting payment and onboarding

- **Agree on the close:** get a solid yes.
- **Contract and invoice:** have both ready before the call. Send them as soon as the prospect agrees, and push them to sign on the call, giving them a few minutes to read.
- **Collect cash on the call:**
  - "A client is only a client once they have paid."
  - Push for the full first-month fee.
  - If contract terms cause confusion, take a deposit for now. A deposit shows they are on board, and without payment nothing stops them ghosting.
- **Onboarding:** once paid, send the onboarding funnel, briefly explain the process, and say you and your team will help with any issues.

## Mindset principles for agency sales

- **Sales is problem solving.** A strong discovery makes the prospect aware of their problems, so they see the solution sitting in front of them.
- **Confidence is everything.**
  - How the prospect feels talking to you drives trust.
  - Nervousness creates objections and doubts.
  - Confidence comes from experience, which comes from preparation and reps: book as many meetings as possible and do mock calls with other agency owners.
- **Businesses buy into you.**
  - Many people sell marketing services, and the biggest differentiator between agencies is the owner, not the service.
  - Present well, speak with confidence and earn trust.
- **Failure isn't final.**
  - Review calls that didn't close to find what to improve.
  - In business, unlike formal education, failing a test is a chance to improve for the next one.
- His stated target: implementing these steps and improving them should get you to a "$10,000 a month agency by the end of the year."
