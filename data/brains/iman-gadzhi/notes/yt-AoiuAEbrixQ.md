## Intentional personal brand: the purpose is attracting customers, not fame

- Iman defines a personal brand as creating content to reach a target audience and convert it into customers. He says there is no right or wrong size: 5,000 followers or millions can both work, depending on the outcome you want.
- He says fame for its own sake is not aspirational to him. For most people the point is "being able to attract rather than chase."
- His own timeline:
  - His first YouTube upload was on Boxing Day 2015.
  - From 2016 to 2018 he only documented his agency work. He did not think of himself as a business owner until he hired his first employee in 2017, at age 17, who quit after three days.
  - He later niched down to sharing what worked in his agency.
  - In 2019 he launched his first digital product.
- He became intentional at the end of 2021. He was about to delete his social accounts while launching the software company Floy. His co-founder Pier refused, saying they would not raise tens of millions to acquire customers because Iman would acquire them. Iman says that was the first time he deliberately connected his social media to his businesses.
- He had no expectations in the early years. He started sharing his journey in 2014/2015, when being an entrepreneur was not "cool," because he had no friends at school and some online friends.

## Why a personal brand is the leverage to build now

- Iman calls it "jet fuel" and a competitive advantage that sits above any business model. He says you should keep the job or business you have, and the brand makes it easier to attract clients, earn more and get noticed. Even an employee who brings in business becomes more valuable to a boss.
- He uses Naval Ravikant's capital, code and content framework:
  - Older billionaires made their money through capital.
  - The next wave made it through code (Musk, Zuckerberg, Sergey Brin).
  - The next generation will use content.
- Capital and code are not accessible to everyone. Content is "the first form of leverage ever that is accessible to anyone."
- For bootstrappers it is "the great equalizer," because it replaces VC-funded customer acquisition. It gives:
  - negative cost of acquisition;
  - discounted equity on acquisitions (he says he bought three software companies in about four weeks, with discounted equity);
  - easier hiring (more than 100,000 applications across his companies);
  - easier access to partners and DMs.
- He says his personal brand is worth more than all his investments combined, because he could rebuild everything else from it.
- He puts his career earnings at over $100 million, mostly in the last five years, not counting company valuations.
- A £1M physical product brand he launched on the back of his audience is profitable but "not a home run." He says the brand gives confidence that he will not lose money on a venture.
- His eyewear company makes a couple of million a year in profit. Eyewear has 80-90% gross margins, which lets him spend on paid acquisition beyond his own audience.
- Everyone already has a personal brand, because employers and clubs look you up. The choice is whether it helps or hurts you, so make it work for you even at 5,000-10,000 followers.
- Billionaires act on this. Ray Dalio's channel has 61 million views against Iman's roughly 10 million. Zuckerberg's image change and Elon Musk's purchase of Twitter are, in Iman's view, bids for influence. Iman says that once someone has money, they want influence.

## Micro personal brands: audience quality beats audience size

- Iman argues for a micro, niche brand: higher engagement, easier monetization, a more loyal following. He says you are "one of one" and do not need to claim expertise.
- Revenue per follower (RPF) is total revenue divided by total followers. For example, 1,000 followers generating $10,000 is $10 per follower. He says more followers who are not your people is bad.
- Examples he gives:
  - Odd Muse: a UK clothing brand whose founder started creating content 2-3 years ago. With about 172,000 followers, mainly from TikTok, it reached $20M a year.
  - George Heeden's Represent: $100M+ a year. Iman says it was struggling about five years ago until founder-led content in lockdown rebuilt it.
  - A friend who went from agency to a bootstrapped software company, worth hundreds of millions per Iman. He could not spend $600 to acquire a customer, so he built a niche LinkedIn brand instead.
  - Alexandra, a Fiverr freelancer who documented buying farmland.
  - Nick, an AI entrepreneur with 11,000 subscribers, whom Iman guesses makes $500K+ a year.
  - Alexi, a 13-year-old making about $50K a month with 2,000 subscribers.
- Iman runs his own micro channel for business owners making over $250K a year. He tells smaller owners not to watch because the advice would confuse them. He deliberately does not want it to pop off. Its offer, "Eight Figure License," packages about 50 backend systems, such as a short-form department that gets about 300 million views a month on owned accounts. He excludes forex/trading and adult businesses, anyone under $250K, and anyone he does not think he can help.
- For 2022-2024 he deliberately "dumbed down" his main channel to corner the 18-25 market. He now splits audiences across a main channel, vlogs and the business channel. He does not recommend multiple channels to most people.

## Content strategy: authenticity, documentation and storytelling

- He calls being real the "unfair advantage." He recorded his whole journey, including losing 40-50% of his agency retainers after taking his eye off the ball, and unsuccessful proposals.
- Ways to provide value:
  - document;
  - open people's eyes with a new perspective;
  - educate.
- You do not need to be an expert, only one step ahead. A level-1 person relates to a level-3, not a level-10, so being too far ahead is unrelatable.
- He cites the 7-11 rule: about 7 hours of content across 11 touchpoints. He thinks the four-platforms part does not apply to personality brands. Optimize for watch time, not views, because trust is built over time.
- His "magnetic persona" has these parts:
  - Show flaws, as a hero needs them. In 2021 he shared his experience of panic attacks and depression while still living through it.
  - State your values. During 2020 he made his own masks, then gave away the stock (about $10K cost) rather than sell it, because selling did not match his values.
  - Develop a signature style, such as film photography or red and blue lights in videos.
  - Tell stories rather than listing facts. His line: "You admire a character for trying more than their successes."
- His advice on mistakes:
  - Stop trying to please everyone.
  - Stop chasing views.
  - Do not try to be "the next Iman Gadzhi."
  - Stop waiting for the perfect time.
- He had one breakthrough video after four years. His action step is to record a 5-10 minute video daily for 7 days and not post it, just to get reps.

## Ideation, packaging and retention tactics

- Pick the platform by where your audience is and which format feels natural to you. His team repurposes TikToks to LinkedIn with a different intro written for the business audience.
- Mix broad, superficial content (reach) with in-depth content (it shows expertise and makes money). Categories he lists are actionable, narrative, theoretical and "attractor" (sharing results). Beginners should lean toward actionable and theoretical.
- Ideation:
  - Do not copy only within your niche. Look at other niches and old 1950s-60s newspaper ads for headlines.
  - Use hobbies to make the brand magnetic.
  - Use the free tool VidIQ to find outlier videos (his example is a video at 8.9x a channel's average), then study the creative and headline.
- Search traffic: "mine field farming" targets what people already search for. His example is Miles Beckler, who averages 5-10K views and makes millions a year. Also use problem farming (a problem-oriented title versus a solution-oriented one), and brand leveraging (commenting on current events, such as More Plates More Dates at $100M a year).
- Ultimate guides capture existing demand, such as a 3D printing beginner's guide.
- Structure: a big idea, three main points, three sub-points and a clear takeaway.
- Growth topics: double down on your longest-watch-time topics. His have been millionaire habits, beginner-friendly businesses and monk mode. Audiences have not seen everything, so repeat and reframe.
- Packaging: he employs three full-time people on thumbnails and has for three years. A Veritasium title and thumbnail change got about 9 million more views.
- Retention: about 80% of the drop-off happens in the first 30 seconds. Hooks come in three types:
  - a bold claim;
  - curiosity or an open loop, which his team sometimes opens in one video and closes in the next;
  - a value promise.
- Speak to one person, answer comments and run polls and lives.

## Monetization: start with coaching, sell information

- Keep your business if it works. If you want a clean slate, or your career is capped (like a one-to-one personal trainer), Iman says the one way to monetize in 2025 is information.
- He argues everyone already pays for information: lawyers, tutors, driving and tennis instructors, McKinsey. It is packaged as a book, course or coaching.
- Big names made much of their money from information:
  - Russell Brunson (a potato gun DVD course);
  - Alex Hormozi's Gym Launch (about $100M, charging $30-42K a year), by Iman's conservative estimate;
  - Alex Becker (about $75M).
- He says you pick a "unique value zone," not a niche. His example: helping successful online entrepreneurs reduce screen time, not generic productivity.
- You synthesize what is already out there. AI makes researching a market's problems easier.
- He pays for information himself. He paid $250,000 for 10 calls with one person, and says it was worth it.
- Why coaching first:
  - It charges $3,000-$20,000 per client and needs three to five clients a month.
  - It works with an audience of a few hundred to a few thousand.
  - It needs no upfront production.
- A sample calculation:
  - $2,500 for five calls is $500 a call.
  - Ten calls a week is $5,000 a week, or about $20,000 a month.
- Courses charge $100-$1,000, need thousands of customers and an audience in the tens of thousands or more, and are hard to sell. He says courses are commoditized and "people want you."
- His own Digital Launchpad app costs $37 a month and offers a money-back guarantee.
- Coaching also validates the offer and shows what your market needs.
- Group coaching is harder to sell than one-to-one. Courses are at the bottom.
- On selling time, he disagrees with "never sell time." Taylor Swift sold nine months of her time. He says it is fine if you are paid adequately and it gives leverage. Beginners should not start with e-commerce, because ads, stock and debt can wipe out savings.

## Automate and scale

- When coaching hits capacity:
  - Move to group coaching or a hybrid. For example, 3-4 hours of recorded training, then a final live hour for nuance.
  - Then turn it into a digital product.
- Trade-offs: you reach more people and get automated sales, but you lose personal connection and find premium rates harder.
- He sees one-to-one coaching as the MVP, because it shows where students get stuck, which you cannot predict.
- His five-step summary:
  1. Build an intentional personal brand.
  2. Create a micro personal brand.
  3. Implement a content strategy.
  4. Monetize.
  5. Automate and scale.
