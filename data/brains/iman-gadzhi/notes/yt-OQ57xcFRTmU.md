## Who Iman serves: four markets, two at a time

- Iman splits audiences into four markets: beginner, mid-market, advanced, and mass market. He says people often miss the fourth one.
- Beginners have "solved out all the kinks in their inner dialogue." Show them a process and they will follow it.
- Mass market is different. The process is not their problem. Mindset and limiting beliefs are, so a guaranteed process does not move them.
- Mass market language must be dumbed down. Words like "sell," "selling online" and "funnel" scare them, and his team stops filming when those words slip out.
- Mid-market hates his style. He gives consultant.com as the example: take a 2-minute idea and stretch it into 2 hours.
- Advanced people "look at the results, not how the words someone uses."
- You can hold two markets at most. For his brand that is mass market plus advanced. Moving toward one market moves you away from another.
- His rule: "You shouldn't be for everyone." Some segment will always dislike you, and that is fine.
- Mass-market content takes constant effort to stay dumb. He says dumbing it down is "so much harder than you would think" for logical people like him and his CMO Luis.
- He admits the team narrowed its content over the past 18 months, partly from ego, and now plans to go wide again.
- His barometer for the main channel: if the team would happily watch the video, it is the wrong video. Make it for "me 10 years ago," not for the team.

## Organic plus ads: short form as reach, not conversion

- His view: "I don't think in today's day and age it's organic versus ads. I think it's both."
- Short form runs about 70 owned accounts. At peak the team posted around 15,000 pieces a month, now fewer. Monthly views run 150M to 300M.
- Short form has no CTAs and no links. It exists for reach and face familiarity.
- The payoff is lower ad costs. A big organic month is followed by cheaper advertising the next month.
- True cold traffic only appears past about $2.5M of monthly ad spend. Before that, most people seeing the ads have already seen his face.
- He tried funneling short form to leads (including ManyChat flows) and found little. One month with 240M views produced roughly 5,000 to 10,000 leads. Years ago it was around 50,000. TikTok and Instagram throttle CTA-based content.
- He no longer tries to funnel it. The return comes from lower CPMs.
- The department was renamed from short form (clipping) to the narrative team, because the goal is to set the message and manage PR, not maximize views.
- He avoided outside clippers and built in-house. His reason: view-incentivized clippers hurt the brand, and "not all publicity is good publicity."
- He is never the first mover. He studies what the first mover did and avoids their mistakes.
- Source of content: internal "car podcast" and similar. About 6 minutes of chatting yields roughly 8 clips, which can reach 100M to 500M views over their life. He dislikes public podcasts and does internal ones instead.
- Interactive, lifestyle content beats a plain podcast format. He regrets not pre-scripting more scenarios and questions, such as the clip where he was asked about his bank balance, which went around half a billion views.
- Practical habit: doom-scroll TikTok 10 to 20 minutes a day to find hooks that work, then reuse them in ads.
- Acting classes or a public speaking coach help on camera.

## Running a 1.2M-registrant challenge

- The last live event got about 1.2M registrants: roughly 200,000 organic and 1,000,000 paid.
- Lead capture spend was a little under $2.5M over 3 weeks.
- The event also produced 42,000 VIPs in 3 weeks.
- Prep runs 8 to 10 weeks out. Concepts to be taught at the event are seeded into videos beforehand as warm-ups.
- The Notion task list starts at about 500 tasks per event and ends around 700 to 800. Every department is involved, with hundreds of creatives.
- His own face is the bottleneck on creatives. He says output could reach about 20,000 creatives a month without that limit, versus 600 to 700 before an event.
- Before this, offers were evergreen with 2 to 3 events a year, including pre-recorded 4-part launches that he knew converted worse but did "because it was sick." His advice: it is fine to do some things in your business just because you like them.
- Then they moved to 5-day live challenges, roughly 3 to 4 per year.
- They only sell about three weeks a year. He says this lowers revenue but also lowers regulatory exposure, since live sales are less exposed than evergreen.
- Context he stresses: this works for his objectives and does not automatically transfer to yours.

## Offer design: guardrails, licensing and objection-first

- His first step: list the objections, then reverse-engineer the offer. Give people "what they want," deliver it, then give them "what they need."
- Parts of the offer are not revealed publicly until someone is a client, because they would scare buyers.
- He treats "course" as dead: "You're not in the business of information, you're in the business of transformation." Stay undogmatic about the format.
- Tier structure at consultant.com:
  - Mass market: software, with preset guardrails so customers "can't mess it up."
  - Mid-tier: services, such as a dedicated consultant.
  - High end (Quantum): strategy, because those clients already have teams and software.
- He licenses instead of building. Example: a Brazilian company with about 50 marketing agents (Instagram story sequences, VSSLs, YouTube and more) that Luis was already using.
- Illustrative deal shape (he says the numbers are not exact): about 20,000 seats a year, a $1.5M to $2M annual check, and a roughly 90% wholesale discount. The software is white-labeled under his name and placed inside the offer stack, not resold separately.
- Leverage comes from buying volume, so he can demand aggressive discounts.
- He tells others they license too little and build too much.
- Standalone software is a trap: "my recommendation is don't ever try to sell as a standalone thing, just put it in your offer." He ran his own software to six figures of recurring revenue but only his own audience wanted it. He ended up buying out his partner and bundling it.
- The 80% failure fact: most buyers of any effortful product do not succeed. His analogy is DJ decks, where most buyers never become professional DJs. Build your numbers on that, not on the idealized picture.
- Refunds are priced in as a "get tax." His guarantee is truly no questions asked: 7 days on the front end, 7 days after the event for VIP tickets. Refund rate runs roughly 5% to 10%, last event around 9%.
- For late refund requests (even months later) the team can refund to avoid headaches, because that roughly 1% of angry customers is who triggers regulators. He says they have never had a verified negative customer review.
- He wants carrots dangled outside refund windows to reduce refunds.

## Sales team vs no sales team

- He claims to have run large volume with no sales reps, and says the live events do this at scale. He says every sales hire adds risk and liability.
- When challenged on dialers for a low-ticket funnel (an audience member's $350 CPA free-course funnel into a $52/month tool), he says that setup often gets shut down within 6 to 12 months. His reasoning: platforms pay out upfront for beginner signups, cancel rates are high, and eventually the finance team pulls the plug.
- His advice there: run it for all it is worth, but do not close your high-ticket offer, because reopening it is hard.
- He says full compliance is impossible. Study the biggest known risk items, remove the worst ones, and do not tiptoe around everything.
- Avoid phone sales on hungry low-ticket offers where reps upsell the next thing.
- For compliance he cites the FTC suit over Element's hydration claim and says to know what others have been penalized for.

## Investing, partnerships and competitive advantage

- Core filter for any opportunity: what are our competitive advantages? He turned down a Qatari hospitality family because he has no edge in hospitality.
- Build outward from the core. Amazon began with shipping, then built AWS. He prefers an ecosystem around what works over starting from scratch in a new field because of ego.
- His stance on opportunities: he never says no, he says "not right now."
- Keep the main thing until a strong operator runs it. He credits his COO Max as the backbone of the business.
- Ask founders in other industries about the worst parts of their work. Everyone hates around 80% of what they do. He passed on starting a fund because managing other people's money and 100K investors "on my case" sounded like a nightmare.
- He prefers owning 5% to 25% of great businesses that will succeed with or without him, and does not want sweat equity.
- Investing vs partnership: investing is a cheque and a lost cheque is survivable. Partnerships need slower, deeper due diligence because you are tying your brand to theirs (he gives the example of a CEO scandal). The WAP deal took about a year and a half and talks with every company in the industry. He calls it the largest part of his net worth.
- Trust and personal chemistry matter in partnerships. He is skeptical of giving equity to affiliates; instead consider letting them buy in on favorable terms and giving titles like advisor or investor, since people want to feel special.
- Advice to someone weighing an equity offer: both sides depend on each other, and conditions exist for a reason. Ask what drives the counterparty.
- His team gets phantom equity in the businesses he invests in, which has made several of them millionaires.
- Hiring: no side hustles, stated on day one. He "anti-sells" the job, asking whether working for someone is really a step forward, to find the real driver. Everyone on his core team has a team-sport background.
- He wants clarity on what you want: "biggest creator" and "monetize the most" are different goals. Know your lifestyle burn, and when your investment portfolio covers it "all the bull's gone."
- For a creator in an off cycle, he suggests using the quieter season to build skills, hire and train. He says growth is uneven: one year of 2x to 5x, then a few of 10% to 20% while foundations are built.
- Build the brand for longevity, not short-term cash. His brand stays ambiguous on purpose, and he avoids over-committing to AI even though they use it, because audiences tire of any narrative.

## Content mechanics and brand longevity

- Time on content is about 8 to 10 hours a month. Scripts are written by his team from his concepts, he gives feedback in a weekly writers' room (30 minutes to an hour), and an internal tool called "Scripty Man" holds everything he has said over 11 years.
- Casual videos use bullet points he riffs on. He says a restaurant does not need a new menu daily: audiences graduate and get replaced, so repetition with variation is fine.
- YouTube channels are split by awareness level: a main mass-market channel, a business channel, a beginner channel and a vlog. People usually graduate off the main channel after 6 to 12 months.
- Every personal brand hits face fatigue and a "handbrake phase" where views trend down. He says talking-head formats suffer most because viewers learn everything about you.
- Vlogs and multi-character content last longer because they show a team and friend group. He thinks creators should film their office culture.
- Formats degrade: his own editing style worked for 1.5 to 2 years then got stale. He says the message matters less than packaging and format, and the next move is more dynamic production.
- He runs three full-time thumbnail people, a department running for four years.
- On AI: he does not think AI personalities replace personal brands, since fans want a real person they might meet or hear rumors about. He says his team uses AI in many ads and it performs well.
- Information at the top end (10K per hour consulting for specialized insight) stays valuable. At lower and mid levels, sell transformation, not information. He has bet most of his net worth on this industry.
- Ecom, he says, works like info: spend most time on ads and marketing, find a problem, agitate it, present the product as the solution. He believes an event audience could sell a $50M physical product.
- Distribution is the most valuable asset now. Even a 30,000-subscriber channel can get millions a year from companies wanting access, so tell large companies what you have.

## Operating philosophy and pace

- Passion is overrated: "find something you're passionate about and you'll never work another day" is a "cancerous idea." Everyone hates around 80% of their work and tolerates it for the 20% they love.
- Intentionality over scale. Everything the team does is calculated even if it is not talked about.
- Focus on profit, not revenue: for an info business, "revenue is futile, just a vanity metric." Ego aside, they play dumb about their numbers when it suits the goals.
- He says the 100K-per-1M-a-month example he saw at a mastermind persuaded him revenue is not the point.
- Team size: about 75 full-time with eight-figure annual payroll. Roles named: Luis (CMO of consultant.com, started as an intern at his agency six years ago), Max (COO, German, "babysitter" for the company), Alex (head of creative).
- Core principle on pace: they treat content as a real business since 2022 (grew from about 1.5M to 4M subscribers the next year), mostly work to the same plan, and took pride in the fact that no one competes at their level of owned media.
- Founder risk: "You are always the biggest risk to the business." Burnout kills companies, so he trains, detoxes and parties hard alongside work.
- On hate videos: part of the game. His test is whether people he respects call him about it. After the 2024 wave none did, and most critics later removed their videos.
- A rule he applies when listening to others' playbooks: only apply what fits your own business objectives.
