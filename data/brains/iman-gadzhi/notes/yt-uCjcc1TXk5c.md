## Timing is roughly 80% of success, and there are two kinds

- Iman argues "80% of success is timing", on top of hard work, which is required but not the deciding factor. If it were, every hardworking person would be a millionaire.
- Timing is controllable. If you've worked hard and gotten nowhere, he says the missing piece is timing, not effort.
- He says the people who win make a few right decisions that outweigh all their wrong ones. Those big decisions share one factor: timing.
- Market timing is the external environment: getting into the right industry early (real estate, stocks, crypto, now online businesses).
- Career timing is where you currently are on the spectrum. Wrong decisions "don't matter in the long run" if you match career timing to market timing when choosing what business to start.

## Match the business to your stage, one step at a time

- Under $1,000/month trying to build a $10M/year business has odds "slim to none": you lack capital, experience and a team.
- The reverse also fails. At $10M, starting a $100K/year business will probably work but never reach the scale you want.
- "The business you should start to get to $10 million is not the same business to get to a million", and not the same as for the first $100,000 or first $10,000.
- Ladder: $0 aim for $1,000; $1,000 aim for $10,000; $10,000 aim for $100,000. "Do not try to skip steps or you will fail."
- He made his first $1,000 online in 2014. He cites starting his marketing agency at 16 and a software company a few years later as moments where market timing met career timing.
- Don't copy what a favorite entrepreneur is starting now, since it may be a $10M or $100M business.

## Don't follow your passion; pick what produces practical results

- He calls "do what you love and you'll never work a day" bad advice: "do what you love and you'll stop loving what you do."
- Passion careers often pay barely enough for bills. Add the parts of any job that suck, and over time you resent the thing you loved.
- The only question: "does it generate practical real life results?" Successful people chose something practical, not their passion.
- Example: he didn't start his agency because running Facebook ads was his passion. It's "a very boring type of business" from the outside. It was a practical way to make money for himself and his mother.
- Passion followed success: "as a man you fall in love with the process of becoming successful and the vehicle that takes you there."

## Build a negative vision of your future instead of setting goals

- Exercise, which he calls "the opposite of setting goals":
  1. Write down what you don't want (a miserable job, constant money struggle, asking others for what you want).
  2. Write down where you'll be in 5 years if you keep your current path. If unsure, observe where the masses' mindless choices lead.
  3. Write a page describing that outcome in detail, then ask if you want to be there.
- Takeaway: you either act now to change the trajectory or end up there.
- He ties success to "extreme responsibility" over your situation and actions. Successful people take responsibility; unsuccessful ones let others decide their path.

## Speed of implementation: go through more iteration cycles

- Early on, things go wrong, and speed is the best weapon. Improvement comes from trying something new and seeing the result; either outcome teaches you.
- Someone testing 20 new things in a period beats someone testing 5 "every single time", even if all 5 worked, because of the iteration count.
- Opportunity cost: starting in 1 month vs 3 means the faster person earns during the extra 2 months and runs many more improvement cycles, so you can't catch up.
- Example: after taking over Twitter, Elon looked at how often updates shipped, fired non-contributors (Iman says over 80% of the company), and pushed updates 10 times more often.
- "You either do it fast or you fail." Cutting your learning curve pays dividends.

## You don't need a massive business; start a minimum viable business

- He uses a pendulum idea: public opinion swings between extremes (his example is crypto going from scorned to embraced and back), and when it's skewed far one way, move the opposite way.
- Starting a business went from risky to trendy to a given that you "must" build a million-dollar company. He says this belief "has created a generation of failing individuals."
- A minimum viable business is simple, easy to learn, needs no money or hires, can be done solo, and makes your first $1,000 almost inevitably.
- Example: ask neighborhood businesses to improve their Google listing photos, shot on your phone, for $50 to $100. Pitch: people search for them but give up because the pictures look bad, so better photos can double online visitors.
- His favorites at the time: viral organic e-commerce using free traffic, appointment setting, and AI copywriting, because they pay the most for the least input.

## Struggle is a requirement for success

- Everyone struggles (family not supporting you, money, breakups). The reaction decides whether it makes or breaks you.
- Some use struggle as an excuse to stop believing in themselves; others use it as fuel and work twice as hard.
- "There's not a single successful person that hasn't suffered." Struggle builds the stress tolerance and endurance success needs.
- He says working with tens of thousands of students showed him this pattern.
