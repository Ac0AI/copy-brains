## Age is an advantage in online marketing, because clients look for people who grew up with it

- Iman says a common limiting belief among new agency owners is "I'm 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, even 25, why would business owners take me seriously?" He calls it "cancerous" because it stops people from building six and multi-six-figure businesses.
- His test is to assess the belief objectively rather than just assert that age doesn't matter. The analogy: if he needed a document produced on a typewriter, he would look for someone 50 to 80 who grew up with typewriters, not a teen.
- Business owners hunting for help with online marketing think the same way: they look for the generation that grew up with it. Most of them did not grow up with social media, and many did not grow up with the internet, so a young operator reads as an advantage.
- He says this plays out in practice. Corporate clients have referred him onward as a "wonder kid" or "wizkid" of marketing, and his age helped him rather than deterred him (he says he hates being introduced that way).
- He has seen 11 and 12 year olds closing a couple of grand a month in social media marketing, so he sees "genuinely no excuse." He allows that at 11 to 13 it may be a struggle, but not much.

## Why online marketing is unusually open to young people

- Iman describes online marketing as an "eighth wonder": a field businesses know they need but don't know how to do, which is where the agency comes in.
- Contrast with fields like accounting or advising on company mergers, where a 22-year-old has no track record. He doesn't think teens or people in their 20s can get away with a very successful business in those.
- Businesses are only now starting to realize how much money they leave on the table by not doing it. Once they see the opportunity cost (competitors doing it and winning), they search for who can fix it, and they look for people "almost bred" to do this.
- He started at 16 and closed his first couple of clients then. He says he didn't really struggle with it, and he didn't realize at the time that it was a real business model; he "stumbled upon" it.
- His takeaway: "there has never been a better time to get into digital marketing," because it breaks down age and experience barriers.

## The pitch to a client who says "you're young, what do you know?"

- Iman's answer was that he knows a lot because he grew up with the platforms. He is on Instagram and YouTube every single day, including the platforms he pitches on.
- He frames this as "eating your own dog food" or "eat your own cooking": practicing what he preaches.
- He notes his agency now only sells paid traffic services, so he no longer uses that exact pitch, but he calls it a "very compelling pitch" when he did.
- For age, you "play it off as an advantage." If you're young, you're the person who grew up with this, it's all you think about, and you're seen as the expert.
- If you're older (he mentions 40, 50, 60), the same lever works the other way: you have traditional marketing and business experience and you also know online marketing, SEO, ads, whatever you're selling.

## Handling the "you never went to university for marketing" objection

- Iman calls this a "very simple objection to squash."
- His answer: online marketing knowledge goes obsolete within roughly 6, 12 or 24 months. Anything he'd have learned in a marketing degree three years ago would be obsolete now.
- Because he has no certification or degree, he is learning every day, whereas a chief marketing officer may think they know everything.
- He argues a degree gives "a false sense of entitlement," a feeling of having accomplished something when you've only read about it.

## Turning any limiting belief into an advantage

- His rule: "just choose to believe whatever favors you." Objectively assess whether the belief is actually a disadvantage (most times it isn't), and if it is, find a way to spin it.
- Worked example for living somewhere with a low cost of living, such as Brazil, when a US client asks why they'd hire you:
  - Admit it plainly: "yeah you're right, I do live in Brazil, and I'm not gonna lie, there's not much to get up to here, so that's why I do this 24/7."
  - Contrast with a well-paid US hire doing it 8 hours a day because it's just a job, who has more distractions and is less passionate.
  - Make the price argument: that person might cost the client $4,000 a month because of their cost of living, whereas Iman could do it for $2,000 a month. The extra $2,000 "isn't going on increased expertise," just on their rent.
  - His illustrative figures: his dream apartment would cost about $500 a month versus about $3,000 for them.
- He says living abroad can be an advantage for exactly this reason, not a disadvantage.
