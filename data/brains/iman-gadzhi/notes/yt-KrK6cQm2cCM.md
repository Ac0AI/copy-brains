## Origin story and the "why" behind the business

- Iman was born in a small Russian village and moved to London at age 3 with his mother and stepfather. He went to expensive private schools while home life deteriorated.
- By 14 his stepdad had cut off his mom entirely (though still paying school fees). Iman describes it as "a Rolls Royce but no money to pay for petrol."
- At 14 he decided "either I step up and do something or we are [screwed]." He read a book a week from 14 to 17, buying used books at Brixton for about 3 pounds instead of 14.
- At 16-17 he drifted into nightclubs and drugs. A New Year's morning where his mother realized she was losing him made him commit to business seriously. At that point he had one client paying 300 pounds a month.
- He says his motivation was desperation first, then inspiration. The "why" of supporting his mom got him to 10-15K a month but could not carry him further.
- He stalled at roughly 50-80K a month for about six or seven months (February to September of the prior year) because he had no new reason to push. He moved on once his why became reforming the education system through the best online programs.
- When he turned 18 his stepdad filed for divorce and demanded they leave within seven days. Because of the business, he could cover a divorce legal bill he describes as costing "a couple hundred grand."
- He says he would not have grown as fast without the pain of his mother's situation, and calls it a matter of motivation, not ability.

## How he got the first clients (before the agency took off)

- Early money came from flipping Instagram accounts (2014): grow niche accounts, then sell them or use them to sell shout-outs.
- Then he did personal training (about 30 pounds an hour to friends' wealthy parents), studying the certification books he bought from trainers on Instagram.
- At 16 he bought a camera (a Canon) to move into content and videography, a higher-leverage skill.
- Client 1 was his semi-pro football club. He told the coach their social media was terrible; the owner asked "how much?" before he could say yes. Package: 75 pounds a week (300 a month) for 4 YouTube videos, 30 Facebook posts and 30 Instagram posts, all shot and edited by him on Saturdays.
- He valued the recurring retainer. Predictable cash flow stops emotionally charged short-term decisions.
- Client 2 was a fitness company, Athlete, found by cold-emailing gyms and fitness businesses with a video, then a shoot, then a management retainer. He packaged it in tiers and quoted 1,900 pounds a month (about $2,500). He got the yes while sitting in class and left.

## Dropping out and the 45-day jump to about $15K a month

- A mentor, Bob, got him a video shoot with a Bang & Olufsen owner in Holland (about 800 pounds plus paid travel). On the flight he decided he wanted freedom and to feel like "a respected business owner."
- He set a rule: if he could secure his overhead plus rent (about 2,500 pounds a month, so roughly 10K in contracts, deposits or lump sums) within two months, he would drop out of school.
- He signed Athlete, Zebra Fuel (about $3,500), Furo Systems and a personal trainer within about two and a half weeks, then went to the local council to make the case for leaving school.
- In roughly 45 days he went from about $2,500 to about $15,000 a month.

## Cold outreach: the "fake audit" strategy and other prospecting tactics

- The first clients all came from Instagram DMs and cold email. His hook was a short video story: "give me 5 seconds and I'll show you 3 ways you're leaving 10K a month on the table," followed by an offer of a coffee and a free 15-20 minute consultation.
- "Leaving money on the table" is a phrase that resonates with owners. He also tells them "your social media isn't congruent to the size of your business."
- Fake audit strategy: sending 100 personalized videos a day was too slow. Instead he took a screenshot of the prospect's homepage, added a play button in Canva, and put it in a template email saying he had made a free audit of three ways they were leaving money on the table.
- The link did not play. About 10 of 100 replied "your video wouldn't play," which showed they were interested; only then did he spend time on a real 15-20 minute audit.
- Subject lines: he never capitalized the first letter, because promotional email capitalizes everything, so lowercase looks different.
- He now uses Loom for audits, because it uploads in seconds and shows whether the prospect opened it.
- Target the CMO or founder, never the social media manager or paid traffic person, because an agency threatens their job. A CMO only cares that marketing makes money.
- Finding the decision maker: LinkedIn for company size and roles, then Facebook, then the person's previous employer's email format, since people often keep old emails.
- He says there is no single best outreach method. It depends on niche and country (cold calling is banned by a registry in Holland). He gave examples: dentists are hard to reach by phone because the owner is often practicing.
- Force versus power: early on you must use force (cold outreach, calls, networking). Later you get power (inbound and referrals) through a niche authority built via value posts in Facebook groups and LinkedIn.
- Case study funnel: he sends cold traffic to a one-hour case study page built in ClickFunnels with a pixel. Even people who bounce get retargeted aggressively for 30 to 60 days until they book a discovery call.

## Handling objections and positioning against big agencies

- Against full-service agencies he positions as a boutique that specializes in one thing. A large agency splits 100% of its effort across ten services.
- He tells clients the extra 7,500 a month a big agency charges is paying for rent, an espresso machine and payroll, not expertise, while he uses contractors.
- A one-day internship at an agency doing about half a million a month in retainers showed him their inefficiencies and that their campaigns were optimized for brand awareness and reach, not revenue.

## The pivot from social media retainers to results-based Facebook ads, and the Expedia model

- Retainers based on content stopped working around month three or four, when funded clients asked for ROI. He concluded he had to sell on tangible results.
- First attempt: he added Facebook ads to existing clients for only 500 pounds extra a month. He lost his first 4 or 5 clients, who dropped everything, not just the ads. His agency "got smashed to pieces" in late 2017.
- Lesson: you cannot learn Facebook ads in 30 days, nor email marketing or SEO. Claims that you can start at zero and sell and deliver 3K ad retainers within 30 days are false.
- He bought every Facebook ads course and coach, and says none of it worked. He compares it to learning to ride a bike by reading.
- The Expedia insight: Expedia made about 11 billion in revenue as a glorified affiliate connecting customers to services, like Uber owning no cars. He realized he was "a glorified contractor."
- He hired a Colombian contractor (Danny) at about $600 per month per client and resold the service at $2,000, then $3,000, then $5,000 and eventually a $10K a month retainer. Danny also served as his coach, so he learned by shadowing: for instance, why a retargeting campaign uses a conversion objective rather than reach.
- He had to teach Danny copywriting. Danny now works for him full time.
- Work on the business, not in it. He built systems (onboarding, scheduling) and tested them, rather than running around "like headless chickens."

## Systems, hiring and team building

- Scheduling system: Acuity Scheduling (he says Calendly works similarly) with a universal Zoom link in the confirmation email, so prospects get an iCal or Google invite. Clients get a Slack channel and email, never his phone number.
- Where to hire: Facebook groups, not Upwork or Fiverr. Upwork people often work for the hourly rate. He found Danny in a Facebook Ads group because he kept asking and answering questions with no agenda.
- A good team member takes initiative. Danny joins sales calls, vets the client, and checks their pixel data without being told.
- You don't need to deliver the service yourself. He gives the example of his girlfriend's LinkedIn agency, where she trains the team and oversees delivery.

## Facebook ads and client retention tips

- Tip 1: Sign "easy clients" with existing pixel data. A new client like Mattress.com (mattre.com), with podcast features on Tim Ferriss, Kevin Rose and Joe Rogan, lets him backdate six months of traffic and retarget initiate-checkout and add-payment-info visitors for fast sales. The same logic applies to a large Instagram, Facebook or YouTube audience. Warm audiences beat cold.
- Tip 2: Study copywriting. Copy and creative matter most; for info products and e-commerce long-form copy works. Avoid stock-photo-looking images, because if people see an ad they scroll past.
- Tip 3: The 90-day emotional journey. Clients are happy in the first weeks, then around days 20 to 40 they get anxious and make emotional decisions. He now sells a three-month minimum then month to month, so they cannot cancel at the dip. Earlier he ran month by month and "stacked the odds against" himself.
- Billing: every client is on auto-invoice with a card on file via Stripe, accepting the 2% fee. "Every invoice is a reframe," and he does not want clients re-deciding monthly.
- He ran about $2-3K a day on ads for himself and clients and says agencies waste money chasing awareness.
- Niche research matters. In real estate, seller leads beat buyer leads because sellers often buy too and the agent gets double commission. For cars, a larger purchase means you can target a radius of about 100 miles, versus about 3 miles for a gym.
- Dental offer idea: free teeth cleaning as the front-end offer, then the dentist mentions stained teeth from coffee or wine to upsell whitening or crowns in the thousands.

## Focus, routine and mindset

- "You don't have business problems, you have personal problems manifesting in your business." His stall at 50-80K was due partly to waking at 5:30 only 70% of the time: "how you do one thing is how you do everything."
- Most of what he teaches is not outreach tactics: block distractions, wake at the same time daily, and do one to two focused hours a day. In his view that gets you about 80% of the way to six figures a year.
- Tools he uses: the Alarmy app (he must scan a barcode in the kitchen to stop the alarm), Facebook News Feed Eradicator, a YouTube blocker for comments and recommendations, and Focus, which blocks email, Spotify and sites. He keeps a second phone for work that is on only eight hours a day.
- He warns that social media lets you get the validation of work without doing it, so he limits exposure to it "like a drug dealer who doesn't use his own product."
- Mentor Sam Ovens helped him from about 100K a month through focus, efficiency and routine, though he says Sam's marketing and sales material was not revolutionary.
- His vision: reform the education system with the best online learning platform, including a custom portal (about $500K to build) integrating Oura ring, QuickBooks and course content so students can see how sleep and finances affect their business.
- With about 1,500 students he saw that some get no results while others make 10K a month in weeks. He credits systems, routine and mindset, plus whether they actually take action.
- His closing advice: work on the business not in it, focus on income-producing activities, and "wherever you go, there you are": success does not remove personal problems.
