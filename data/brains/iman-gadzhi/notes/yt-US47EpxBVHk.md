## Pick a simple model and validate before building big

- Iman says most beginners fail by picking business models that are "way too advanced" and trying to build the next Amazon or manage a team of 20 before earning a first dollar online.
- His analogy: don't run a marathon before you've run a 5K. Validate the idea in a small way first, because "ideas are dime a dozen."
- His target is a $10,000/month one-person business: no employees, no overhead, no complex systems, just a laptop and the right strategy.
- He has run service, software, digital product and physical product businesses for over 10 years. His most profitable ventures are consistently the simplest: him plus a proven system.
- Leverage that used to be out of reach (AI, software, platforms) is now available to individuals. He cites predictions of a billion-dollar company with one employee.
- He frames making money online as "problem solving and using leverage." Most people fail because they don't take enough action or quit too early.

## Productize: build once, sell forever

- Digital products (courses, ebooks, templates, even a Shopify theme) are anything bought online, delivered online and built once.
- His comparison: 40 hours on one custom website for a $3,000 client, versus 40 hours on a course sold hundreds of times at $300. The result is $3,000 plus headaches versus $30,000 plus freedom. This is "working in your business versus working on your business."
- He says to build assets, not just income. Digital products work 24/7: "They never have a bad day. They never ask for a raise." You front-load the work, then sell for months or years.
- Personal proof: programs he created once became multiple six-figure-a-month income streams years later.
- Service work is still a valid start. It's how he began almost 10 years ago, and it needs no money, so you can fail for 6-12 months without going bankrupt. But with services "every dollar requires another hour of your time," and you're "essentially just buying yourself a job."
- Models change, so match the business model to the current market and your situation.

## Niche down hard and make the offer specific

- Going broad to reach a larger addressable market backfires because "your marketing message gets diluted."
- Example: don't be a fitness coach. Be the guy who helps busy dads in their 30s lose their beer belly in 90 days "without giving up weekend beers." The last clause adds specificity and attracts one demographic.
- He calls the offer his "unique value zone" (UVZ). "Specificity wins every single time." The goal is for the viewer to think, "this person is talking directly to me."
- He applies it himself. His beginner-focused channel speaks to people in his earlier position. His other business channel speaks differently, to potential advisory clients doing $500K/year at the low end, about $5M on average, with a top client at $120M last year.
- "The riches are in the niches, but the real fortune lies in the subniches."

## One funnel, one offer, one outcome

- $10K/month is "about simplicity, not complexity": one funnel, one core offer, one clear outcome. Find one thing that works and double down until revenue is consistent, then expand.
- Common mistake: five products, three funnels, ten audiences.
- He has seen people make multiple six figures with one landing page and one product, while others try 47 things and wonder why nothing works.
- He expanded to many businesses only after 10+ years, and says he started small too.
- Example: Armono, a customer of his Monetize program, was new to digital products. He sold one product on a digital product platform to one specific group (improving their marketing) and made over $100K in 30 days.
- Iman co-owns that platform (he says he "wrote a very large check" earlier in 2025). He also owns Consulting.com, which he says did $75M in revenue and which advises info, knowledge and coaching businesses.
- Claimed platform stat: the average creator makes $7,382/month. Monetize members range from $10/day to $200 and $2,500/month. His advisory clients reach $10M/month.
- Summary: "Simplicity scales, complexity fails."

## Use AI as leverage; solve problems, not passions

- In 2025, not using AI to replace work that would normally need employees puts you behind. "It's not human versus AI. It's human versus human that knows how to use AI."
- He says he could build a $10K/month business with only AI tools. He needs three things: knowing which tools fit which job (like using the right blade for sashimi versus a tree), knowing how to use them, and having access.
- "The only difference between you and multi-billionaires is they have more leverage than you."
- On what to sell: "No one gives a about your passion. People care about their problems." Monetize what people need solved, not what you love.
- Look for where the audience is "bleeding": frustrations, pain points, urgent problems. "The market does not pay for what you want to teach. It pays for what it needs to learn."

## Distribution: build an audience first, ads later

- Don't blow money on ads early. Pick one platform (YouTube, Twitter, Instagram or LinkedIn), stick to it, give massive value and build trust.
- Add paid traffic only once you have an engaged audience and a proven offer.
- Using ads to fix a broken offer, or to build an audience from scratch, is "an expensive mistake."
- Organic converts better because people already know, like and trust you.

## Ship imperfect work, stay consistent, protect early customers

- You don't need to be a genius. You need consistency and a willingness to ship imperfect work. Most people quit at 3 months and jump between ideas.
- "Your first course, your first landing page, your first sales video" will be bad. Successful people test repeatedly through that stage.
- "I would much rather have an ugly offer that's live and generating revenue than a perfect one that never sees the light of day." Done is better than perfect, and "perfect is the enemy of profitable."
- "Consistency beats intelligence every single day of the week." He says he was never the smartest kid in class, just good at taking action and learning from mistakes.
- Treat first customers "like royalty." They give testimonials, case studies, referrals and feedback. He still has early clients (he names Oura Ring, Genflow, Camden Rose) in his network referring people.

## Don't scale before product-market fit

- A common killer: people at $5K/month hiring teams and building complex operations. His analogy is getting the car to start after hours, then turning it off and pushing it yourself.
- Order of operations: perfect the offer, dial in systems, make sure unit economics work, then scale.
- "Scaling a broken business will just break it faster and cost you more money."
- His summary checklist: pick one model, niche down hard, build once and sell forever, stay consistent, ship fast, solve real problems.
- Sticking with it past 90 days already puts you ahead of about 90% of people, in his view.
