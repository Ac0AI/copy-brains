## YouTube growth: moving from paid ads to a full organic machine

- Iman's early YouTube growth was paid. In early 2018, a client wanted their channel grown with ads. He had never advertised for subscribers, so he built a small test ad to see if he could deliver the service.
- The ads brought in subscribers for roughly "half a dollar or a dollar" each in the US and European markets. He ran this personally for a while and got about 100,000 subscribers before performance trailed off.
- He admits he was "terrible at organic for years." One mistake was turning off monetization because he didn't like ads on his videos, which he calls a massive mistake for anyone trying to grow a channel.
- For years the process was minimal: record, trim the start and end, upload. He and his head of content, Tristan, would decide the thumbnail and title in a quick meeting on the day of filming.
- He nearly left social media about two years before this interview. His business partner in the software company persuaded him to stay, arguing that an audience lets a founder 10x, 20x or 30x the company in 12-24 months.
- Once he committed, the channel grew 2.6 million subscribers in 12 months and was still adding about 250,000 subscribers a month. He says this was done on a deliberate plan with set targets, not by accident, and excludes vlog-style channels from the comparison.

## Thumbnail split testing and content system

- About 18 months before the interview, Iman built a dedicated thumbnail team of three full-time people.
- The channel uploads once a week, but the thumbnail team works daily on split tests for old videos.
- Each split test comes with five thumbnail options, so the team produces 15 options per video. The team runs about 30 thumbnail "rehashes" a month on existing videos.
- He claims this alone means he could stop uploading for a year and still get about 5,000 subscribers a day.
- Each week's video is a single piece of content. He spends only about an hour or two a week creating content himself, around six hours a month.
- He declined to share some of the other tactics on camera. His view is that in hyper-competitive spaces, some things you shouldn't give away because they are what got you where you are.

## Short-form distribution built in-house

- The short-form department started around summer 2023 (about 18 months before the interview). It has roughly 18-20 full-time people.
- The team puts out about 6,000-7,000 pieces of content a month and gets around 250 million views a month. He expects roughly 3 billion short-form views for the year.
- He chose an internal team over an affiliate program. He watched what happened with Andrew Tate's affiliate clippers: the program helped him blow up but sometimes in ways that hurt his brand. Iman wanted to be omnipresent without his words being chopped up and spun the wrong way.
- Internal production gives control over how his message is cut and framed.

## Educate.io and the case against college

- Educate.io is built as a "Masterclass for hard skills." He recruits people he respects to teach their own skills, for example Jordan Welch teaching e-commerce.
- He teaches only what he has actually done (agency, and a few other things). He says his own seven-figure e-commerce income comes from having an audience, not from skill, so it would be wrong to pretend he can teach it.
- He won't teach things he considers immoral to sell to beginners, such as crypto trading, stock trading, or Amazon FBA that requires thousands in upfront stock. Dropshipping and e-commerce can start with very little money, for example validating a product on TikTok.
- The motto: "10 times the impact on someone's income for 1/100th the price of a college education."
- On college pricing, he blames student loans, not universities. He says that if the government offered everyone a loan for online education, his own prices would go up too.
- Educate reportedly received 40,000 applications in one year. The company never asks for a CV or resume.
- Law and medicine still require college. For most people, the goal order is financial freedom, then time freedom, then location freedom, and a traditional job path rarely delivers time or location freedom.

## Advice on college, parents and risk

- He told a young listener planning a computer science degree as "something to fall back on" that this is a cop out. The only real fallback is your skills: "you are the retirement fund," not a piece of paper.
- He says parents' career advice is outdated, not out of bad intent. Timeless life principles (how to carry yourself, how to keep a marriage healthy) are worth taking from parents, but "the best career vehicle" advice changes quickly. Your own children will likewise find your advice outdated in 20 years.
- He estimates that of 10-20k spent on online education over four years, about 50% is wasted, 25% teaches a thing or two, and 25% is life-changing. You usually have to buy a "basket" of things. That is still a fraction of tuition.
- He recommends working as a free intern or apprentice at the right company (not a bloated one where you get lost) and being around good people who can mentor you.
- He is not a "burn the bridges" advocate. He tells someone in their thirties with a wife and kids not to quit their job, because they have things to lose. A person with nothing to lose, like an 18-year-old, can go all in.
- He favors business models where failure doesn't ruin you. He is against telling beginners to get rich through crypto trading: you can lose your savings and have no skills afterward.
- He treats losing a few thousand dollars on a real business as acceptable, because you take lessons from it. He says "it's how you lost it" that matters.

## Timelines, expectations and disclaimers

- He says people should treat the first year of a business as a write-off and not expect to win in year one.
- His own record is public: he has uploaded to YouTube since age 15, was open about being broke, and read a book a week by buying £3 used books at a Brixton bookshop because he couldn't afford £50 a month on new ones.
- He went about seven months between landing his first agency client and his second.
- He notes that many people now feel like failures if they aren't making $10,000 a month within seven months. He suggests telling older entrepreneurs about a business that made $3,000 a month in profit after nine months; they would see it as a success.
- He says he tries to attach disclaimers to his income claims: results like his are not normal, and a more likely outcome is $2,000-$10,000 a month after a first year of little success.
- He says making money online has "never been easier" but is not guaranteed.

## Business operations and time allocation

- By this point Iman's days are mostly meetings: calls with the C-suite at his different companies, his family office, his internal finance team, reports, private banks and wealth management firms, legal and finance.
- He says he used to dismiss executives like Gary Vaynerchuk who had 12 back-to-back meetings as not doing "real" entrepreneurship, and now his own life looks like that.
- He spent most of October studying the bond market and was putting millions a week into bonds because yields were high.
- He says he is a worse business owner because of social media, since someone focused only on the business will beat someone splitting attention.
- He expects his social media relevance to last perhaps 3-5 more years and is comfortable leaving online life "when you're on top," while pushing his business career harder. He says online interest is short-lived while a business career can last decades.
- He takes long breaks from podcasts so he doesn't repeat himself.

## Personal brand, spending and gambling stance

- He buys things that are authentic to him, not for signaling. He bought watches (Patek, Rolex) from 2018, before they were widely seen as investments, and built a car collection only in the current year, because before that he had no real use for cars.
- He avoids visible logos, saying he wouldn't do business with someone in head-to-toe Gucci because they "care too much what people think." Style, he says, comes from growing up in Europe, his friends, and having nothing to prove after making money young.
- He says that in London he drives a Range Rover to avoid drawing unnecessary attention, while Dubai allows flashier choices.
- He publicly criticized gambling promotion by influencers after a trip to Vegas, comparing it to promoting drinking or drugs. He says he was offered an eight-figure deal plus bonuses earlier that year for gambling promotion and turned it down.
- He points out that hungry, smart, diligent people are "very dangerous people" when money offers come. He kept his career "slow and steady" so he "didn't have to answer" when "the devil came knocking."
- He says he does not burn out because he doesn't want a quiet life, and could have retired with eight figures net years earlier.
