## Why he turned the dial up three years ago

- At the time of the first podcast, Iman was a few years past his first million, earning a couple million a year with eight employees, "living seven out of 10" of his capability.
- He says he can't name a mindset shift or environment change (he rejects the idea that Dubai drove it): "one day I just woke up" and wanted to take things bigger. He framed it as an experiment: "what would this look like if I was to just turn up the dial on everything."
- He admits his life was "substantially better" at 7/10, because that level gets roughly 70 to 80% of life's benefits without the stress and complexity.
- His current mission is to build for the people around him ("Entourage season"). He gets his fulfillment from what the team gets to experience, not from material things.
- He says fulfillment comes from doing hard things, which is why he feels more at peace now despite having far more to run.

## Habits change by stage: don't copy what he does now

- Early on (2016 to 2019) he was obsessive: back from the gym at a set time, at his desk at a set time, no breaks. He says a beginner has to be that detail-oriented because "you are the business operator as opposed to being the business owner."
- Back then, one McDonald's lunch meant brain fog and roughly 50% less output, and since he was the whole company, the company's output fell 50%.
- With 100 employees, if he takes a day off or eats badly, net company output drops about 1%. His wealthiest friends eat clean but will have the dessert or take the day off.
- He pushes back on "don't do as I do" criticism: he is buying a roughly $750,000 physical business in Dubai, and says a beginner "shouldn't" do that, nor copy his habits, 10 years in.
- Owners who micromanage stay "80% of the company's output" because they never put leaders in place.

## Personal brand and synergistic businesses

- The biggest change since the last podcast: he now believes "anyone should build a personal brand," because it correlates with whatever their business does.
- Earlier, the content team was one or two people and he saw it as the "least profitable" department. Taking it seriously grew the YouTube channel from about 170,000 subscribers (Aug 2021) to about 5 million, with roughly 250k about two years before the recording.
- He says his team posts about 300 million views a month on its own accounts, systemized across every platform, with no affiliate program.
- He builds businesses synergistically: he only enters one if he has a competitive advantage and can funnel his audience into it. On his e-learning platform, the real money is on the back end, since he owns or invests in most of the software he promotes.
- He says he is "a business person that creates content," spending two to three hours a month on content, and runs his businesses day to day. He dumbs himself down online and avoids talking about 95% of the real behind-the-scenes business.

## Hiring, operators and playbooks

- His other big learning curve was running a real company (legal, HR, accounting, finance, department leads), with no business school or prior employer.
- He learned who is an operator and who is a thinker. A visionary CEO paired with a visionary founder "just simply doesn't work," so he sometimes needs a COO-type who actually operates.
- Playbooks don't always transfer: a competitor to his software company Floy had raised $475 million and can acquire customers at a loss, while he has raised nothing. A CEO from a $3.2B company wouldn't know how to run his self-funded company.
- Masterclass, he notes, is valued around $2.7B on about $94M in revenue and is not profitable, so he can't follow its playbook.

## Competitive advantage, uncorrelated assets and what a business is worth

- Rule: he only invests where he has a competitive advantage, but he is now also buying uncorrelated assets. The Dubai business he is buying should do roughly $1.5M a year once turned around, and he gets 50%.
- Reason: in a recession his stocks and property fall, while some businesses (cigarettes, fast food) barely move, whereas Ferrari makes less. Collector handbags (Birkins, Kellys, vintage pieces) are another uncorrelated asset he has held for years.
- GGI, his eyewear brand, does a few million dollars a year with zero advertising, but he says it is "worth absolutely [ __ ] all" because it has his name on it. He brought on a partner who sold an eyewear business for a few hundred million and is renaming it to a five-letter name to sell it.
- He says a business is worth what someone will pay, not a multiple: "I could say my e-learning company is worth $400 million... no one's going to pay that." Name risk is why Gucci is worth more than Tom Ford on identical fundamentals.
- Risk philosophy: five years ago he'd take a guaranteed few million a year over a 20% shot at $250M. Now he can take risks with "basically no risk" because he has leverage and cash flow. He says it is the reason he stresses: "get rich guaranteed" first, don't copy his risks as a beginner.
- His software company started in 2020 only worked because his business partner was an agency client already running a software in another industry; they repositioned it into AgencyFlow, later rebranded Floy.

## Digital Launchpad: positioning, pricing and delivery

- Digital Launchpad is a $37/month subscription (yearly is 40% off, about 74 cents a day, $270 a year) with a new 10 to 20 hour program launching every month, around 200 hours at launch, and about 15 coaching calls a week.
- Production: shot on Red Raptors in a roughly $100,000 setup. He says competitors could do loom and a webcam but he wants Netflix-grade quality at that price. It also has an app, allowed on the App Store.
- Teachers include Mike Thurston, Jordan Welch and a boxing YouTuber with 2 million subscribers, plus tailor, detox and relationship content, so it covers health, wealth and relationships.
- Promise: "we're here to help you make your first $1,000 online," not "become a millionaire." At $270 a year, a buyer has about 3.5 years to fail and still break even.
- His goal is to reform the education system, so he is trending toward cheaper products, not a $30,000 program. He says Masterclass is about $180 a year and doesn't teach hard practical skills.
- Why big names join: financial incentives, a few who own software that he features and who got big growth from it (one sold his company for a life-changing sum), the pull of being around his team, and his reputation as a long-term player. He says he has the pull now to call anyone, which he couldn't do three years ago.
- He only features the best solutions in a category, and brings in outside experts where he isn't the best: 75% of Agency Accelerator is taught by others, and outreach is taught by the owner of lemlist (he says it processed $118M of deals in a month).

## Why service businesses, and how the arbitrage works

- He prefers service businesses for beginners because you can fail for 8 to 18 months for about $30 to $50 a month in software, whereas e-commerce or Amazon FBA risks serious debt or dead stock. He cites his own dead stock from 2021 in one company.
- His own first client came by accident in August 2016, and his second came seven months later; he didn't quit because trying cost only about $30 a month.
- Sell to businesses, not consumers: "$1,000 to you and $1,000 charged to your business are two different things." In the UK a business can reclaim 20% VAT and reduce its tax bill, while a person would need to earn about £2,000 pre-tax. Even a small coffee shop has about $40,000 a month in expenses.
- Pick services beyond Facebook and Google ads. As an example, three weeks learning Zapier and backend automation could sell at $5,000 a project, and he says you can get up to scratch on many skills in about 14 days.
- Agencies (and creative agencies) beat solo services like personal training or videography because you can take a day off and delivery continues with contractors.
- Arbitrage model: the client pays upfront, the contractor (e.g. Facebook ads in South Africa, Zapier in Brazil, email in Slovakia) is paid after delivery, so you get positive cash flow, like insurance float or dropshipping. He says you still need to know something about the service.
- Limits: any service in any niche can reach about $10,000 a month, but not every niche reaches $100,000 a month. He says getting the first client is hard but no harder than anything else.

## Investing: know your investor type

- His biggest regret: he only started investing in 2020. He put $1M into Bitcoin around Nov/Dec 2020, held up to $8M in a hot wallet, and sold in late 2021. He says it was luck and calls most crypto gains luck.
- His style changed: he now buys bonds yielding 5 to 6% (Saudi, Apple, US government; not banks, which he says produce nothing), and does automatic weekly dollar-cost averaging into stocks every Tuesday, avoiding Monday and Friday volatility, even though he thinks the market is overvalued.
- In property, he moved from buying a property every month to about twice a year, preferring bigger assets and fewer tenants, and requires a year of rent upfront, accepting a lower price and fewer tenants. He has a family office and CFO handling investments, accounting, tax and offshore planning.
- Advice: find your risk tolerance and use competitive advantages in investing too (e.g. a family in property for 30 years should get into property). He says the best early investment is yourself, including a boring accounting or finance qualification, because as the business is you, your capability sets the business's ceiling.
- On first $10,000: have a plan for the money, or you end up blowing it.
