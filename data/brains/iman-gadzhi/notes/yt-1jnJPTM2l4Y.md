## The three "dark sides" of running an agency

Iman frames these as the realities that keep agency owners up at night. He draws on four years in the agency game: a creative agency, then social media plus advertising, then an advertising-only agency. He has handled clients from $300 a month retainers to over $20,000 in a single month of billables (base plus performance fee).

- The emotional rollercoaster of outreach, first wins, and losing clients.
- Churn is unavoidable, even when results are great.
- You are in the business of people, so you cannot hide behind a screen.

## Riding the emotional rollercoaster

- He describes the swing from zero replies to outreach, to setting a first meeting, to signing a first client (the first client's impact is "undescribable" whatever the fee), to the despair of losing one or not delivering results.
- The swings don't stop. "It just becomes a little bit more steady."
- Expect about 95% of clients to get great results only after two to three years. Early on, calibration takes time: not every business can be helped, and "some business owners can't even help themselves."
- With experience, he learns which promises and expectations to set. Some clients he knows he can add $20k to $500k a month of revenue. Others might get five extra leads a month (a realtor) or a $4-5k a month return on ad spend, which can still be big for that client.
- Example of the swings: in March 2019 he lost three clients in 48 hours, all of whom brought marketing in-house, including a five-figure-a-month base-fee retainer. They had been getting incredible results. That cost him about £25,000 to £30,000 in bottom-line profit with agency expenses unchanged, because two were "whales."
- Pattern he sees: "when it rains it pours." He signs clients in clusters (five in one week recently) and loses them in clusters too.
- His mindset fix: "this too shall pass." Treat wins and losses as seasons, stop labeling things good or bad, and accept that you need dualities (night and day) or things go stale and you never grow.
- Agency owners tend to feel enslaved by the highs and lows for the first 18 months. After that he sees maturity and resolve to weather the storm.

## Three-month minimums and living with churn

- Every client should be signed on a three-month minimum. He made this a rule from the start of 2018. Before that, clients had no minimum and could leave after month one, the most stressful month.
- Why it works: the first month is often 7 to 10 days of onboarding and getting ads live, so a client judging results at day 20 is premature. You may also need to teach them how to nurture and convert leads, or build that system. The minimum gives time for results and stops clients acting on emotion.
- He also says you must be able to stomach clients leaving. Clients can leave even with incredible results because they decide to build an in-house culture.
- When clients bring work in-house they reason that for the same fee they could employ four performance marketers full-time. He says they often come back about six months later and you start nearly fresh.
- He won't judge them: in his own education company everything is in-house with no contractors, and he doesn't want service providers there.
- Most clients leave at the three-month mark. After roughly four months they usually stay 9 to 12 months. His longest was about 16 months, just under a year and a half.

## Retention is the biggest lever

- His 2020 realization: "retention is probably the most important thing when it comes to an agency." It's much easier to keep a client than sign a new one.
- Average retention: he calculated his at about 6.8 months. He says he has never met an agency owner with average retention over 12 to 14 months. The average includes three-month-minimum leavers, so a 12-month average means some clients stay 18 to 24 months.
- Benchmarks as he sees them: 7 months is very healthy; 9 to 12 months means "you're a boss."
- His 2020 goal: lift average retention to 10 to 11 months. His math: that is about 50% more profit with no new clients, e.g. going from £400,000 a year to £600,000.
- He even cut one already-happy client's fee by £500 a month (about one-eighth of that retainer, still on his lowest retainer) when they did a six-month extension, as an experiment in incentivizing longer commitments. He was brainstorming lower monthly retainers in exchange for longer terms to hit a 9 to 11 month average.
- Levers he names: deliver better results, and incentivize longer-term contracts.

## Why you should not outsource sales

- He argues you can outsource nearly everything in an agency (ideally eventually building a full-time team and company culture rather than contractors) except sales. "Just because you can doesn't mean you should."
- His math: to sign 10 new clients a month at a 20% close rate you need 50 calls a month, about two calls a day. That's two hours of an eight-hour day driving revenue. He would not outsource unless doing around 100 sales calls.
- Revenue requires speaking to other business owners, who are often sophisticated buyers. It stretches you: you reach out, prospect, and get no's.
- Example: in 2017, at 17, he was grilled for 20 minutes by one of three co-founders ("you don't have a marketing degree, you're 17"). He snapped into the zone and handled the objections. That founder later became a good friend.
- His reframe: it's only a conversation between two business owners about what you can do for their business, a small price for how life-changing an agency can be.

## Systems, but not at the expense of relationships

- His goal is to build a machine. In 2019 he took that too far: he never joined client check-in calls and removed himself from everything except sales. About 80% of his ads ran as cold traffic on Facebook to a case study funnel.
- The lesson: "you're still dealing with humans." Late 2019 and 2020 focused on client delight and retention.
- His team delivered results that made clients "obscene amounts of money," but he personally didn't commit enough to rapport or show he cared beyond results.
- He doesn't believe in truly passive income. Systems help, but the owner still guides and shifts things.

## Agency economics versus other models

- Agencies aren't a "sexy" business model, but he says he's yet to see something that gives a better lifestyle for you and your family.
- He owns an e-commerce company and an education company, and compares margins. Revenue is far removed from what lands in your pocket.
- Education companies: friends and competitors have told him it's harder than expected with thinner margins than anyone mentioned. A real education company (not a course business with one big month) is "a blood sucking leech."
- He pairs this with encouragement for agency owners: it takes courage, and the right community, mentorship and guidance make it easier.
