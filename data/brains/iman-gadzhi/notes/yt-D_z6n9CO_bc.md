## Opportunity cost is the biggest expense
- Iman made his first million at 18 but only started investing meaningfully at 20, and only felt his investing thesis was solid around 23.
- He says the businesses never started and the side hustles procrastinated on are the biggest expenses in a person's life: "that inaction is the biggest expense you're ever going to have in your life."
- Watches were his early investment: roughly $5-6 million spent, with the portfolio up well over 50%. He bought new and direct from the brands.
- He regrets leaving cash in zero-interest bank accounts for years. Cash is fine, but it should earn something: a high-interest savings or fixed deposit (about 4-5% in his recent experience) even if he only parks it for a week or a month.

## Cash flow first, and invest in tools and yourself
- His rule: "Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, and cash flow is reality."
- His eyewear brand does millions a year in sales, but sales are not what the business keeps. Advertising and staff costs take a share.
- He started with an online marketing agency, which had minimal costs. He used geographical arbitrage: charging European and US clients while hiring remote staff in Brazil, Colombia and South Africa.
- He holds large stakes in VC-backed companies that lose millions a month. That is a deliberate enterprise-value play, not something to copy at the start. Early on, "focus on being practical first and just getting your first few wins."
- Business tools are an investment, not a cost: software, AI workflows, and similar tools. They let his businesses do with 1% of the input what competitors need 10x or 100x as long to do. Even solo operators should stack competitive advantages, since tools are "pretty much always ROI positive."
- Until a team is roughly 20 people, he says the founder is still the bottleneck. A solo operator is the company's entire output.
- So the first $2,000, $5,000, $10,000 or $20,000 should go back into skills and abilities, not stock picking. Stock investing comes later, when free cash flow is more than you know what to do with.
- A company that doesn't invest in R&D, talent, consultants, software and equipment goes backwards. He says to treat yourself the same way.

## Getting in early on AI
- He wrote a check in November 2024 for an AI company (named "LRA" in the transcript). He is the largest shareholder besides the co-founders. It was accepted into Y Combinator and is about to close an unpriced round at a $40 million valuation.
- His view: "being involved early in AI truly is like being involved early in the internet days," and an opportunity like this may not come again for a decade or two.
- He says he was too young to capitalize on the internet or crypto early, and tells viewers not to miss this one.
- He is not telling people to build multiple AI software companies. He says to give AI attention.

## Spending, depreciation and the "how much will I lose" test
- Buy used cars and let someone else take the depreciation. He buys cash but still counts depreciation mentally.
- Example: a 765LT that the prior owner bought for about $700,000 two years earlier, which he bought with low mileage for about $300,000. Losing around $200,000 a year would ruin the enjoyment for him.
- Wealthy people don't ask "how much does it cost?" They ask "how much will I lose?" He measures this as a percentage, amortized over the holding period (for example 5 years), because the first year is often the heaviest hit.
- His Ferrari was bought at a summer discount in Dubai from a motivated seller, and he expects to lose nothing on resale. He argues this is more responsible than a new Tesla, Cadillac or Jeep losing about 30%.
- A million-dollar asset that is still worth a million is better than a $200,000 asset that sells for $130,000. The same logic applies to watches and property.
- Credit cards, used responsibly, are a tool. Pay them off on time and raise limits slowly. He pays for his mother's Maldives trips with points, about 70% of the time, including business and first class flights. Good credit history leads to better lending terms, equity release against property, and Lombard-style loans against a stock portfolio. He warns they can be destructive for people who are financially irresponsible, and this is not financial advice.

## Lifestyle creep, comfort and geographic arbitrage
- Comfort is "the biggest wealth killer." Peers who ran level with him five years ago stopped early: "they hit a fast 5 km" but never wanted to finish the marathon. Staying successful is "substantially harder than getting success in the first place."
- Lifestyle creep bankrupts more entrepreneurs than failed businesses, especially younger ones. He made tens of millions before buying his first car, a Range Rover in 2023.
- He now spends about 10% of what he pays himself through salaries and distributions. Earlier in his career his target was to save and invest 70% and live on 30%. When income doubled he did not double expenses.
- Geographic arbitrage: earn in USD, GBP or EUR from Western customers and live where costs are lower. In 2021 he rented a seven-bedroom house in Cape Town for about $10,000 a month, a house he says would cost $20-40 million in Dubai.
- His eyewear company is based outside the US but sells mostly to US customers. His own bases are Dubai and London. Time zones made Bali hard when managing agency clients and team in 2018-2019, so he cautions about that.

## Mentors, creating, and outdated advice
- Mentors shorten the path to success. Some arrive naturally and some you pay for, and he has done both. A mentor drilled the buy-used-cars rule into him. Mentors pour more into you once you have shown results and will implement what they teach.
- "Stop consuming, start creating. Consumers stay broke." An economy is a value exchange: choose to be paid for value rather than only paying for it. Examples are a digital product the market wants (AI can help build it) or creating content rather than only consuming it.
- Parents' financial advice is likely about 30 years outdated: keep money in the bank, buy a home, go to college. Property and the S&P 500 are still safe, timeless choices.
- On hiring: in hundreds of hires over 10 years he has never asked for a CV. By the time a candidate reaches him their competence is established, so he looks at culture fit.

## Passive income and the real way to get rich
- Passive income isn't passive. It takes active setup and often management. Work can be frontloaded so something runs passively for 6, 12 or maybe 18 months, but "things in life always face entropy" and conditions change.
- Even stocks take mental bandwidth. The closest thing to passive is bonds, which he bought heavily in Q4 2023 when rates were high, though they can still cause worry about a US debt default.
- "You don't get rich by saving money. You get rich by raising your ceiling." Early income caps ($100 a month, then $1,000, $5,000, $10,000) are mental limits.
- The only things stopping you are your mental ceiling and time. Remove the ceiling, invest in your output, and let time work.
- Evidence of the long process: he uploaded his first video about a week before turning 16, struggled for two years before that, and keeps his old videos up to document the full 10-year journey.
