## Starting a social media marketing agency: why it works and who it suits

- Iman started his agency at 16 and, by early 2017, earned more than his principal and teachers. He then dropped out of school, though he says he doesn't recommend that for others.
- Pros: more free time, a much higher income ceiling than a nine-to-five, and location freedom. He was working from Cape Town, two hours off London time.
- Cons: more responsibility, no guaranteed income, and more mental strain because you answer only to yourself.
- Results vary with luck and timing. Some students who work hard land a client in month six, while some who work less land one in month one.
- He says many people don't want millions. They want to replace a salary or add $2,000-$5,000 a month. He cites students making $65,000, $25,000, $18,000 and $10,000 a month in profit.
- "You're one client away." One or two clients can replace a nine-to-five income. He advises keeping your job and building the agency on the side.
- Getting started is the hardest part. He describes a student who prepared mentally for three months, then on January 10th began outreach and signed his first client 24 days later. That one client paid as much as a graduate nine-to-five.

## What a social media marketing agency is, and what you don't need

- It is a service business that sells services to clients, with no hack or trick. It can be run from anywhere for clients anywhere. He holds all sales calls and client check-ins on Zoom and never meets clients in person.
- He says a small town is not a reason to hold back, because remote work removes the local-lead limit.
- You don't need an office, employees, a legal corporation, a degree, or deep marketing knowledge.
- Contractor arbitrage: he began with a creative agency (video and photo content), then moved to a Facebook Ads agency when he "really wasn't very good at Facebook ads." A contractor delivered the results while he knew just enough to sell. After about five clients, he turned a contractor into a full-time employee.
- He doesn't recommend business partners at the start, because it makes it "twice as hard to make the same amount of money."
- He operated as a sole trader from his first client (August 2016) until April 2018, tax-registered but with no company. His registered business still has a different name from his agency.
- Setup list: laptop, email, social media account. For about $1 a year (domain) plus roughly $5 a month (Google G Suite), you can email from your own domain, which looks more serious and gets better replies.
- Keep the agency simple. He has seen full-service agencies at $20,000 a month revenue take home only $3,000-$4,000 in profit because they ran ten niches and ten services plus upsells and downsells. "The best agencies on earth are the ones that are the most simple and the most streamlined."
- A website isn't required. He suggests a simple one on ClickFunnels, which he also uses for case study funnels and onboarding.

## Finding clients: niche, lead list and outreach

- A client is "anyone who needs more customers."
- Pick one niche you know and have lead ideas for. You can switch later, but don't try to serve everyone. Market size is not a problem: the US alone has about 38,000 gyms, 200,000 dentists and 600,000 restaurants.
- Build a small local lead list of 20-30 businesses in a Google Sheet, doc or on paper. Record name, phone, email, website, and Facebook and Instagram pages, so outreach can be personalized.
- Pick a channel: cold call, cold email, or Instagram DM. Most of his first five to eight clients came from cold DMs.
- His early DM approach was a short note with one to three tips: "I just saw your page and noticed a couple of places I think you're leaving money on the table." He held back the best material for a one-on-one coffee meeting. If nothing came of it, the prospect still got a free audit to use as they liked.
- Basic script: "Hey, my name is Iman and I help restaurants get new customers. I've looked at your website and noticed you're leaving a lot of money on the table. I have some great ideas. Would you like to discuss them?"
- Set a daily outreach target and book follow-up meetings.
- "You are selling the meeting, not the service." Don't pitch your service in outreach. Frame the meeting as "infinite upside with zero downside," because owners are busy and need a win-win.

## Running the sales meeting: dig, emotion, logic, close

- Meetings can be on Zoom. Sign the client there and then, send an invoice, and agree a start date.
- Structure the meeting in four phases:
  1. Dig: learn the ins and outs of their business and get numbers.
  2. Emotion: "People buy with emotion and then they justify with logic." Business owners want more money for a reason, such as a bigger house or a Rolex. Future-pace what life looks like once your service delivers.
  3. Logic: use the numbers from the dig phase to show why it's a no-brainer.
  4. Close: if the first three are done well, the close is not a hard sell.
- Tips:
  - Sync Zoom with Google Calendar so invites auto-create a Zoom link.
  - Never offer free trials. They don't convert, because going from free to $1,000-$3,000 a month feels like a new cost. He says he has trained thousands of agency owners and saw this repeatedly.
  - Best clients are independents with high margins and a small team in charge, not chains.
  - Use invoicing software and get paid immediately: "They are not a client until they have paid."
- Once you deliver, clients refer others, and referrals grow over time. Repeat the cycle.

## Choosing and delivering the service: paid social

- Convenience offers (content creation, social media management) versus results-driven offers (paid social ads, email marketing, SEO). He recommends paid social advertising: clearest ROI, easiest to learn, no software cost (you advertise directly on Facebook), and usually the best retention.
- Delivery options: outsource (easier to scale) or do it yourself (easier to start).
- Start on Facebook because it has the most users, the most targeting, is the most user-friendly, and has native lead generation. Other channels include Google, YouTube, Snapchat, LinkedIn and TikTok.
- Setup: create a Facebook business account, request access to the client's page through Business Manager, and create an ad account.
- Client pays for ad spend: "You never ever ever want to charge ad spend." You charge only your service fee. The client attaches their own card in Business Manager so Facebook bills them directly. Handling client ad money is "a nightmare."

## Building the ad campaign

- Choose the lead generation objective in Ads Manager.
- Set audience by location, age and gender. For a gym, you can target a postcode or a street plus one mile. The client supplies assets (images) and demographic info, while you write the copy and tweak targeting.
- Copy: write compelling long-form copy, which he says works better than snappy slogans.
- Images: avoid stock images and use lifestyle content. Test: would you post it on your own timeline?
- Top tip: "If the person looking at it can tell it's an ad, you've done it all wrong." It should look like an organic post from a friend, and iPhone images often outperform polished camera or stock images.
- Use a desirable offer for a strong call to action. Leads can be followed up by phone, email or in-person visits.
- Automate by exporting leads from Business Manager so the client is notified of each lead. Zapier handles this, including SMS follow-up to leads. ClickFunnels can host custom lead forms. Outsourcing saves time and can get clients better results.
