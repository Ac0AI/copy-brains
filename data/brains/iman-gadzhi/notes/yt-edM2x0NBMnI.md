## Choosing a unique value zone (UVZ)

Iman says "niche" is not specific enough. He splits a market into three layers:
- Layer 1 is the market (health and fitness).
- Layer 2 is the niche (weight loss for women).
- Layer 3 is the unique value zone, where "all of the magic happens": weight loss for postpartum moms.

A UVZ is a specific, in-demand problem that few people solve, where customers urgently need help and pay well. Coaching moves a person from point A (current pain) to point B (desired outcome), and the outcome doesn't have to be money. It can be screen time, a marriage, or video game trick shots.

- He claims you can become credible in a UVZ in 3 to 4 weeks of obsessive focus on one mechanism. You don't need 7 years of expertise.
- To find yours, he asks four questions. Have you learned a valuable lesson, overcome a challenge, built a system or habit that works, or done effortlessly what others struggle with?
- Examples: turning a 40% no-show rate into 80% attendance, fixing your health, or being very good at Excel. He says the highest-earning UVZ he knows of belonged to a guy who coaches people from beginner to advanced in Excel.
- His line: "There's a market for everything."

## Why specialists charge more

- Iman's analogy compares UK pubs with Japanese omakase restaurants. A pub serves burgers, curry, Italian and Mexican, so it struggles to charge more than about 10 to 15 pounds a dish. An omakase restaurant masters one cuisine and charges thousands for a set menu.
- Specialization creates perceived value. Clients aren't buying generic advice. They're buying one specific problem solved, and they trust a process from someone dedicated to that transformation.
- Summary: "Specialists command premium rates, generalists compete on price."
- His own path: he charged $50 to $100 an hour in 2017, when he was running his agency and sharing it on YouTube. If a call ran to 3 hours he didn't care, because early on you are building a name. Now he charges $18,000 an hour, after roughly 10 years in digital marketing and 7 to 8 years specifically advising info product businesses.
- He says that price won't happen on day one or in year one. His claimed credentials: eight figures in profit in low, mid and high ticket, and over $100 million in career earnings, 70 to 80% of it profit.
- Even with companies earning multiple eight figures, he still coaches, about 15 hours a month. He says that is $250,000 a month in net profit, over $3 million a year.

## Building the high-ticket coaching offer

He says an offer needs three parts:
- **Core problem.** A painful, expensive or frustrating problem. The client should feel "seen, heard, understood."
- **Specific result.** Measurable, with a timeframe. "People do not buy coaching. Coaching is the vehicle." They buy outcomes.
- **Unique solution (unique mechanism).** Your methodology, which gives hope that this time will be different. The simplest start is to name what you already do. Different clients will love or hate different mechanisms, and that's fine, because the ones who love it latch on.

Naming examples:
- "20-minute method" for busy parents losing weight without complicated diets.
- "Three chord system" for beginners playing their favorite songs in 2 weeks.
- "Three touch nurture system" that takes no-shows from 40% to 80% attendance in 14 days.

Offer statement template: "I help [audience with core problem] achieve [specific measurable result] through my [unique solution] without [common pain or obstacle] in [time frame]." Example: "I help busy parents who struggle to lose weight achieve sustainable fat loss of 10 to 15 lbs through my 20-minute method without complicated diets in just 6 weeks."

## Offer outline and pricing math

Iman lists what to define in the offer:
- **Overview:** program name, objective, target audience, key problems solved.
- **Structure:** step-by-step action plan, objective and actions per phase, what each call covers, timeline.
- **Delivery:** one-on-one or group, session frequency and duration, total program length, platforms and tools.
- **Support:** between-session support, follow-up after the program, channel (WhatsApp, email, Slack), response times, community access.
- **Resources:** worksheets, templates, SOPs, resource library, call recordings.
- **Expected results:** specific outcomes, realistic timeframes (for example 90 days), client transformations and past results.

Math for $10,000 a month:
- 10 clients at $1,000.
- 5 clients at $2,000.
- 2 clients at $5,000.

Why you can charge premium prices even when starting out:
- Specialization creates perceived value.
- Clients pay for transformation, not information.
- High-ticket clients are more committed and get better results.
- The "10x value rule": when clients can see 10x the value, the decision is easy.

ROI isn't always dollars. For the executive who can't sleep, the person with panic attacks, or someone whose marriage is failing, he says "you can't place a numerical value on the stuff." He gives a personal example: he'd probably pay $500 for four weekly calls to get better at FIFA.

## Getting coaching clients

He lists four ways: cold outreach, paid ads, referrals and content.
- **Cold outreach:** pick the channel by where your ideal client spends time.
  - Executives are on LinkedIn.
  - Moms are in Facebook groups.
  - Gamers and tech people are on Discord.
  - Local auto detailers are on Google Maps.
  - Phone is wrong for his own demographic: "If I don't have the number saved I ain't picking up."
  - DMs or email work better for people like him.
- **Paid ads:** he ran an agency for 6 years, 4 of them serving coaching and info course businesses, and still advises against ads early. You haven't validated the market, the offer or your messaging. His rule: stay away from paid ads until you've passed at least $10,000 a month through organic methods.
- **Referrals:** the most powerful source once you get results.
  - Over-deliver and create "wow moments."
  - Ask happy clients directly.
  - Consider an incentive: a discount, a free month, a bonus session, or cash back.
- **Content:** his personal favorite. Build a "micro personal brand" with content aimed at your UVZ. "Audience quality is far more important than audience size." The goal is not to go viral or be Mr Beast. The goal is inbound leads to message and convert.

## Being a strong coach

- Take the "doctor approach": diagnose before prescribing. On every check-in, ask where they are, where they want to go, and what's stopping them. Clients silently ask "Can this person solve my problem? Do I feel supported? Is this worth the investment?"
- Coaching is a fast way to learn a market. After 10 clients you spot patterns. In his 15 hours a month he keeps hearing about three terms, and one is always the market's biggest desire. He uses these in offers, messaging, marketing and sales, and addresses fears and pain points before they come up.
- Call setup:
  - Dress to match your clients. His are 25 to 40, so a t-shirt is fine, but not a slob and not a three-piece suit.
  - Audio matters more than an expensive mic. He uses roughly $30 earbuds.
  - Keep the background simple (his is a black wall).
  - Light your face.
- Call structure, from the framework his team used when they offered six one-on-one coaching calls to students over about 18 months (he says 15 to 20 thousand students were coached):
  1. Intro: build rapport. Ask for a 1 to 10 progress rating and one win from the week.
  2. Transition: change tone and move to business, for example "Is it cool if we jump straight into week 3?"
  3. Set the agenda: explain how the call will go, which builds authority.
  4. Coach: the longest part, working through the action plan.
  5. Actionables and wrap-up: confirm tasks before the next call and that they know where to find everything. Always check in after calls.
- Difficult clients:
  - Late or missing: book calls ahead and set rescheduling rules up front.
  - Slow progress: identify the cause openly.
  - Resistant to change: come from understanding but be clear that better results require changing their state.
  - Need hand-holding: check in more often and make sure they have resources.
  - "There's no one-size-fits-all approach."
