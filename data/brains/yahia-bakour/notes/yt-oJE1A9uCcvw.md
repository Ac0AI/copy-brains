## Origin and product evolution of context.dev

- Yahia's product started as "an API to get brand data like logos, colors, style guides." The use case: "software is very boring and we should make it less boring," so a service could personalize onboarding the moment someone signs up.
- Being part of customers' onboarding made context.dev count as infrastructure in their minds, so they began asking for more.
- His rule for adding features: if three people ask for the same thing and all are paying, it may be worth building. Some customers also wanted to migrate off other services and consolidate onto context.dev.
- Expansion path: brand data, then scraping, then entity data (companies and people).
- Positioning: infrastructure for developers to build into their apps. He describes the company as solving web access for AI agents, and says getting data from the internet "is an absolute mess" even with AI.
- Customers include multiple unicorns and Fortune 500 companies, per the interview framing.

## Growth curve and what drove the takeoff

- Yahia says every product has a different growth curve. Agencies grow fast then stall; enterprise is zero or a lot of money; product-led companies take a long time, hit critical mass, then take off, and hit new critical masses repeatedly.
- context.dev's trajectory: what it grew in the first year, it grew again in the next 3 months. What it grew in the first year and 3 months, it grew in the next 1 month. Early on it took months to land a customer; now it gets many per day.
- What changed:
  - "Extremely active on X" and online generally.
  - SEO kicked in on the target keywords.
  - The product crossed a usability threshold. His view: "it's usually further than you think" before people start using and paying.
- Churn is a separate problem, solved by making the product incredible and reliable.
- Scale: roughly 2 million API calls per month at the start of the YC batch, about 35 million later. He has talked to about 25% of the 440-plus customers; the share was higher when there were fewer.

## Customer discovery calls

- On calls he is quiet 80 to 90% of the time. "First half of a call, you just shut up and be quiet and listen."
- He has customers walk through their application, software and workflows, then looks for patterns across calls.
- Ideas come from spotting overlap. If 10 people in a month say they'd pay for something extra, he builds it. When customers say different things, he asks about their business and use case until a similarity emerges.
- The strongest signal: he floats an idea mid-call ("would you need this type of data?") and they respond "I would love that, do you have that? I will pay for it," when they hadn't known they needed it.
- Unhappy users are an opportunity: fix what they hate, email them, and a hater can become one of the best paying customers "with just one email."

## Competitors and ICP

- Competitors stop mattering in two situations: when you have very few, or when you have many and have broken through the noise to become relevant. He says context.dev is in the second case: many competitors, but only a handful matter.
- His team doesn't watch competitors. They build only from customer asks, because you can't know why a competitor built something (a "bolt of lightning" vs a real customer ask). Customers ask for odd things, so features ship in an unusual order, which he says makes intuitive sense to users and drives fast adoption.
- ICP: developers building something that needs internet data, inside businesses. Pricing is infrastructure-style. Small indie builders count as very small businesses; purely consumer tinkering is not the target.

## Scaling infrastructure and engineering

- Yahia was an engineer and tech lead at Amazon on high-performance systems, and this is his third startup. The first handled data from about 20 exchanges worldwide in the retail stock market space.
- Before the batch started he prepared the system for scale "in case" it worked, and went from about 2M to 35M monthly API calls with no scaling issues.
- On agent traffic: agents still need a human with a credit card authorizing spend, so autonomy is limited so far. He expects roughly 10x more traffic before language or architecture limits bite, and says core parts will need porting from higher-level languages, likely not for about 6 months.
- He notes you "can't vibe code" high-performance infrastructure; you need to know what you're doing.

## Bootstrap vs VC, and applying to YC

- "If you can bootstrap, you should." Raising money is "not an accomplishment," just a necessary step to do something. Many people raise for prestige, which he calls the wrong mentality.
- context.dev was bootstrapped until he realized it could be huge by "pure math." Bootstrapping would take "a decade or two longer"; raising lets you use money to grow fast.
- After VC funding, he says, the concern becomes keeping growth up.
- YC advice (he stresses he is not a partner and makes no decisions): everyone should apply since it costs about 10 minutes. Be extremely concise, show a large vision, and show clear math for how you reach it (customers times price), not "the market is this big, I'll take 5%." Get revenue if you can; apply in whatever order suits you.
- His launch video: planned in about 3 days, script written the same day, a friend's home studio, 13 takes, an editor spliced it; about 3 hours of effort.
- Final principle: the goal is to build something people want and will pay for. "Everything else is ego."

## Legal and compliance for a scraping business

- Scraping publicly available information is generally fine. He cites Supreme Court rulings and the shutdown of the Google v. SerpApi-style case, noting Google's results are public.
- His policy: stay on the green side, never veer into red, and ask lawyers about gray areas.
- Vibe check: if you can discuss it publicly and proudly without being called a bad person, you're probably fine. Talk to a lawyer for real advice.
- HIPAA: needed if selling into medical, and buyers will refuse to work with you without it.
- SOC 2: needed for bigger companies, who won't reach out without a trust page or SOC 2 mention. He says it's not hard and has value as a forced inventory of everything the company uses.

## Founder mindset

- Biggest fear: not working on something meaningful; he addresses it by building things he finds meaningful.
- Waking up anxious is real for him (email especially), but the first 20 minutes pass and then "you just got to go."
- Founding means much less freedom and higher highs and lows. He keeps relationships by surrounding himself with other founders who share the same language and co-work.
