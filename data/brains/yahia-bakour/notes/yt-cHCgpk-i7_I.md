## Selling Stock Alarm for $500k: how Yahia got in, grew it and exited

Yahia Bakour has been a developer for about four years. He spent roughly three of them on Stock Alarm, mostly as a side project, with a short full-time stint later. He did not start it. His friend Morgan launched it and posted it on LinkedIn. Yahia had built the same thing himself from Google Sheets and Python scripts, tried Morgan's app, found it barely worked and sent a list of fixes. He said he would pay about $5 a month for it. Morgan then offered a co-founder role if Yahia helped grow it, and the app was around $100 MRR at that point.

- Stock Alarm alerts you when something happens in the market. The original hook was a phone call when a price crossed a limit.
- Phone calls go through without data and can bypass Do Not Disturb. Text messages came about eight months later, and he notes most people would build them first.
- It grew into compound alerts (technical levels, upcoming earnings, combinations) and trailing stop-loss alerts. It was pure utility, not trading signals.
- It sold for $500k, with 100k+ users by his co-host's account.

## Getting the first customers

- The first few were friends, family and friends of friends. After that, it was about posting where customers already hung out, mainly Reddit and Quora. Searches like "best stock alerting app" on Quora returned tens of his answers.
- App Store optimization on target keywords "worked really nicely".
- He also did SEO, and built an in-house referral/affiliate program.
- He used growth hacks timed to specific days or times of year.
- His takeaway was that early on you have to hustle wherever your customers are, until random people start finding you on their own.

## B2C pain and the product-market fit signal

- Someone once asked him why he was building a B2C app. His view: getting people to pay $5 a month is painful, while businesses pay $40 a month easily. Getting to the first $100 MRR takes 100 customers, not one or a few.
- The payoff comes later, but the early stage is harder.
- The signal that they had something was constant positive App Store feedback and a flood of emails asking for features: other time frames, other alert types, indices.
- The pattern was that users asked for a change and said they would pay if it shipped. The team shipped it in a couple of days, accepting a bug or two.
- Eventually they had to pick what to build rather than build everything.

## Why sell, and how he chose a buyer

- Nobody was passionate about it anymore. It was taking up head space, and the product is always on call: an outage on a Friday night means calling tens of thousands of people immediately. Outages were rare over three years.
- It was still a good learning experience: SEO, referral programs, growth hacks, and owning every decision.
- They optimized for more than price: how much head space they could free up immediately, versus being on the hook for support.
- Terms that buyers proposed and the founders avoided: three months onboarding the buyer's team, and seller financing at certain percentages.
- They wanted a buyer who would give the terms they wanted after the sale, and who had a vision that made it likely the product would survive.

## Running the sale on Acquire.com

- He was hesitant to list at first and had never used the platform. The team considered going back to previous buyers, then decided selling was a pain and listed. Submitting the listing and price range was simple.
- Acquire's team gave feedback on the listing, and one person helped with the P&L.
- A P&L was a sticking point. As a three-person partnership they had spent profit at year end to reduce their tax bill, so they reworked the paperwork so it did not reflect poorly on them.
- He pitched it as: zero marketing spend, hundreds of downloads a day organically, runs on its own. Lots of interest came quickly, but finding the right buyer took a few more weeks.
- Buyers asked about the paying-user split between tiers, where users come from and how long they stay.
- Different buyers wanted different things. Some in wealth management wanted to resell it to their own customers at a much higher price point, and cared more about the tech.
- The whole process took about three and a half months from serious start to close.

## Due diligence surprises

- Mobile revenue is net of store fees. Apple and Google take 15% under $1M a year and 30% above it, and the buyer pointed that out after the MRR was advertised.
- The tier mix mattered. If most users are on the highest tier, a buyer sees less room to raise prices.
- Growth looked flat and wonky because of the GameStop surge. One month was among their best ever, which distorted the graph. Buyers wanted to separate real users from spike traffic.
- A couple hundred wealth management firms used it on individual plans, and many long-tenured high-net-worth users were a plus.
- The founders still help the buyers with occasional questions about the three-year codebase.

## What he would do differently

- Be more patient. He assumed the sale would go faster.
- Set expectations for how much effort due diligence takes, with buyers asking questions and onboarding help needed right up to closing.

## Next project: Rewardable

- He is building a SaaS affiliate tracking tool called rewardable.io. It ties into Stripe, with Paddle planned later.
- For a web app you generate an affiliate link. When the referred person pays, the referrer automatically gets a percentage, and refunds within a set period cancel the commission.
- He frames it as a B2B product that is very specific, with multiple portals for different participants. The pitch is a SaaS affiliate program set up with a couple of lines of code.
- It was nearly done at recording, with a waitlist, and he estimated one to two months to finish.
