## Deciding when to leave a job for a side product

- Yahia was an SDE II at Amazon making about $250K per year. He had wanted to go full-time on Stock Alarm since graduating college but kept talking himself out of it.
- His excuses: "I need a healthier MRR," "I need to save money," "I need industry experience," "I'll be missing out on stock vesting," "I'll never find a team as good as the one I'm on."
- His realization: "I realized that my list of reasons was growing with time." He decided it was "now or never."
- Many Amazon colleagues told him they wanted to do the same but never did, and later had too many responsibilities to take the risk.
- He went full-time at 20K MAU and $20K MRR, about 2.5 years after starting the product. He was 23, with no kids, no debt and high risk tolerance.
- His rule of thumb: in your early 20s, with a year or two of industry experience and a product with decent MRR and growth potential, going full-time makes sense. With kids, the side project should be more mature and hold a higher MRR over a longer period.

## Runway and de-risking

- Salaries from Stock Alarm cover monthly expenses in Seattle at a modest lifestyle.
- He modeled his runway in every scenario before quitting. He had about a year if things went very sideways and two years if things stayed the same.
- Going full-time was still nerve-wracking, and unsolicited opinions from people close to him caused self-doubt. After quitting he felt "more confident and calm than ever."

## Early user acquisition (0 to 10K users)

Yahia treats the first 0-10K users as a separate process from 10K-100K+. His early tactics:
- Answer relevant Quora questions about the service.
- Launch on Product Hunt, Betapage, Betalist, Reddit and Hacker News.
- Publish relevant blog content early, since SEO takes a few months to show results.
- Send a monthly newsletter about new features to existing users, asking them to spread the word.
- Reuse existing infrastructure for growth. The product automatically posts top gainers, top losers and upcoming earnings on its Twitter account.

His best channels overall are organic SEO/ASO and referrals from existing customers. Building what customers ask for gets them "hyped up" and turns them into ambassadors.

## Quora playbook

- Look for questions like "What apps do you use for stock market alerts."
- Answer honestly and disclose that you work on the app, so Quora doesn't take the answer down.
- Prioritize questions that already have thousands of views over those with hundreds, since they keep getting traffic.
- Also answer questions in the same niche without pitching the app, which helps keep answers from being removed.
- A year later, his answers still get thousands of views per week.

## SEO approach

- He targeted lower-traffic, higher-intent keywords that had worked in the App Store. The main one was "stock alerts."
- This involved heavy backlinking with other blogs and publishing content on related topics.
- He started organic SEO work a few months into the project.
- He says the tactics are "the usual suspects" found in any SEO blog post.
- A structural advantage: the web app has thousands of Google-indexed pages because every stock ticker page is public (for example, app.stockalarm.io/symbols/BTCUSDT).

## Platform economics

Stock Alarm has three clients: web app, iOS and Android.
- Customer LTV: web app > iOS > Android.
- MRR: iOS > web app > Android.

## Working habits and launch timing

- Full-time he works 4-12 hours a day. He wakes around 7:30, does about six hours of heads-down work at a coffee shop, breaks for the gym or a run, then does another 2-5 hours.
- Working for himself is more fulfilling than working for an employer: "I'm no longer selling my time to another person."
- While employed, he found waking up early on weekends, or an hour or two before his job started, more productive than working evenings.
- Launch as soon as a quality MVP is ready if the product is in an existing market. A truly novel product can be less polished.
