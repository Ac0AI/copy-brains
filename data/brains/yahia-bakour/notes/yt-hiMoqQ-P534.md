## Hiring and team: hire for skill and ethics, not friendship

- Yahia started context.dev (formerly brand.dev) as a solo founder, and now has three hires.
- He is sure he wanted to go solo. His view: co-founding is "like a marriage", with hard splits over equity and departures. But if you can find a co-founder, he says you probably should.
- Solo downside: it is easy to get lost in a maze and think something isn't working when it is. His fix is to hire people better than him at specific things, who also act as sounding boards.
- He treats hiring as picking colleagues, not friends. Hires should be friendly, but what matters is that they are "very skilled, good, and ethical" and move the company in ways he didn't expect.
- Hire 1 interned for a month at a customer's company and did great. Yahia called him the moment he got into YC, offered housing and a stipend, and he agreed on the call.
- Hire 2 is a very senior engineer from Yahia's first startup, hired in one phone call.
- He does not want hires to drop out of school. If a hire is certain, the offer stays open, but "if your whole business depends on one employee, there's a problem with the business."

## Finding product-market fit: willingness to pay and complaints

- Signal 1: someone pulls out a card and pays. The amount matters less than the fact they pay and keep the subscription. Doing this at larger scale means you are heading toward the goal.
- Signal 2: complaints. In the early days of context.dev, big-name companies signed up, complained a lot, but did not leave. For Yahia that was enough to say "there's something here"; he only had to improve the product until it was worth paying for.
- He also gets fulfilment from customers who say they love the product and rely on it, and from case studies with real photos and quotes.
- He says growth "solves all problems": customers who like the product and talk to you.

## Handling criticism, including on Hacker News

- Context.dev launched on Hacker News and got heavy criticism. He notes this is normal: hackernews.love lists Segment, Dropbox, ChatGPT and Claude Code, which all got slammed.
- Tactic: reply thoughtfully, not emotionally. He wrote something like "the real truth is this; not everything is polished, but it's heading this way, and that's why it's better". One critic became a customer the same day.
- A "silent majority" watches how you respond to angry comments. A calm, considered reply turns them into customers too.
- He says complaints and love/hate are the same thread.

## Targeting an ICP and narrowing from broad to specific

- His ICP is developers and engineers who need web data to integrate into their product as infrastructure. The landing page speaks only to them and deliberately repels everyone else ("if you're not a developer, you probably shouldn't use my product").
- For his business, "high value" means that audience, which is why SF-based views on his viral post mattered.
- Pattern across three businesses:
  - First: a consumer stock-market tool for retail traders, with meme stocks and price tracking on the homepage. It also drew semi-professional traders.
  - Second: AI customer research for product managers, turning qualitative feedback into quantitative data.
  - Third (now): developer infrastructure.
- The method: start broad, see who cares, narrow down, then tailor the language to them.

## Constant experimentation with PostHog and YC traffic

- Uses PostHog's experiments feature almost daily: tests copy changes and pricing, and measures sign-up and conversion rates.
- YC-driven traffic (plus X virality) means experiments can run every two days with fast feedback, then quick changes.
- He frames this as continuously improving the product and the business, like "the latest version of your habits".

## X (Twitter) as a distribution channel: the Yapper Leaderboard

- X is very useful for his business and personally; he has made many friends there.
- A post went viral about three days before the recording: a "Yapper Leaderboard" that ranks people's faces and companies by 7-day impressions. It got about 330,000 views.
- It was not his idea. One of his engineers built it in under a day while Yahia took two hours off for dinner with his partner. Yahia doubted it would work; the engineer was right.
- That engineer now owns growth and can run any project he wants.
- Why he thinks it worked (hindsight): ranking your face and company against others triggers a competitive instinct. Many SV tech CEOs DMed him annoyed they weren't on it, which gave him direct access to high-value people.
- Analytics showed the viewers were almost all from the SF area, which is exactly his target buyer.
- Caveat: like most side projects it may fade, and it was only three days old.

## Founder habits: intensity, fulfilment and routine

- Intensity matters more than hours. An "eight hour day" means eight hours of deep focus. During YC he works 12-14 hours; he expects to settle at around 10 later. He also works weekends and has for years.
- Fulfilment over happiness: fulfilment comes from work that is hard but necessary, while busywork is unnecessary suffering. Happiness comes and goes hourly.
- He switches in and out of work well: he takes 30-60 minute dinner breaks with friends, then returns, and can sleep right after finishing.
- His routine: coffee and something sweet on waking, meetings in the morning, engineering in the afternoon, creative work at night. He says engineering is the hardest to pause mid-task. Productivity systems like Pomodoro don't work for him; he found what works over a decade of doing this.
- Tracking: he uses a Whoop and found that sleeping at the right time (about 11pm to 7am) is better for him. He does not follow it strictly.
- Fitness: he ran a thousand miles last year to qualify for the New York Marathon, with a resting heart rate in the 40s.

## Career advice: startup vs corporate, school and degrees

- Big picture, the choice between a corporate job and a startup isn't that important. Failing at a young age is fine, and a startup with customers, users or traffic looks good. A corporate job also works if you do good work.
- He moved between both (started a company in his final college year, then Amazon for a couple of years, then back and forth) and decided he wants to run his own company now because the timing, idea and skills line up.
- If you're a venture-backed founder, "all bets are off": drop out, because you can't do it half-heartedly.
- If you're pursuing something capital-heavy like hardware, science or defense tech, fundraising takes full time. If it's software you can bootstrap, test first whether you and others like it, then go full-time.
- Degrees: he says a college degree still opens doors because of bias, so you probably need one, and a top-school name gives a "slight door opening" that skill then converts. A YC founder title opens far more doors than any college.
- Cold outreach: the worst case is being ignored, which is where you started. Being on X and viral makes reaching people easier.

## Mentors and books

- Mentorship is useful for principles, but advice is tailored to a specific person and time, so don't copy it verbatim.
- Books he recommends: "Zero to One" by Peter Thiel and "Contagious" (virality; opens with a New York bar, Please Don't Tell, where a black card made knowing the bar a social status). He says it changed how he thinks about his own products, even non-consumer ones. He also mentions "The Viral Loop" as a similar book.
