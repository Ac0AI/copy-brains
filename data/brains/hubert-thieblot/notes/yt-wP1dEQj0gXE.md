## Curse's mission: fix gaming communication, free, and own the platform layer

Hubert (CEO of Curse, ~120 employees, about 70% of whom play League of Legends) frames Curse Voice as a quality-of-life fix for a clear problem. His method: look at what players ask over and over in chat and remove the friction.
- The most repeated questions were "when is the old app up", "when is dragon" and "when is baron". He says these stop people from actually playing, so Curse fixed them.
- He says Curse was founded in World of Warcraft 9 years ago on the same approach: "we looked at problem and we looked at things that just don't make sense."
- Goal: improve quality of life and communication, not build "the ultimate timer application that does everything for you." Red and blue buff timers are "a no-brainer" later, but "we're not going to time every single thing on the map."

## Why old voice tools don't fit the new game type

Hubert argues voice software failed to keep up with how games changed.
- Six or seven years ago, in WoW and other MMOs, you played with people you knew, in a guild, so Ventrilo, TeamSpeak and Skype worked as long-term relationship tools.
- League of Legends and other MOBAs push you to play with strangers, with "one or two minutes to talk before a game". He says the old tools don't apply, and the same goes for World of Tanks and Minecraft.
- Positioning: a very easy way to chat with people. No friend list needed; share a link and they join. Friend lists, IM and chat rooms come later (the TeamSpeak-style "hang out in a room" use case within a couple of months).
- He considers TeamSpeak and Ventrilo "the old era of MMO" and not relevant long term.
- Skype critique: it became cluttered from supporting mobile and every platform, and it is peer-to-peer, so hosting a call makes your PC work hard (FPS and RAM complaints). Curse is not peer-to-peer, and he says players cannot get each other's IP through it.

## Technical choices and prioritizing stability

- V1 took about 9 months of work. It uses the latest codec and compresses "about four to five times more than on Skype", so bandwidth use is tiny.
- Servers are distributed worldwide (Taiwan, Australia, Brazil, eastern and western Europe, US east and west coast) and capacity can be raised in a region "in just a matter of seconds". Everything runs on the cloud.
- China is excluded because it already has good gamer communication apps.
- Skype is stuck on an old codec because changing it breaks every client until updated across many platforms. Curse, focused on PC, can ship better audio.
- Top priority is stability: "We don't want to crash your game. We don't want to impact your FPS." Works for 99% of users; about 1% with special drivers or graphics cards have issues, and some have headset compatibility bugs. The team was working about 14 hours a day.
- A Mac developer was hired; Mac would take a couple of months, VoIP first, overlay after.

## Beta rollout and scarcity-driven demand

- Beta grew from 0 to 7,000 to 40,000 users, then close to half a million active users, with another million who visited the site without access.
- A jump in the beta pool from 7,000 to about 50,000 about 10 days earlier raised awareness and caused the controversy.
- He says the controversy is partly because the app wasn't open yet: "if you don't have something that other people have, the interaction is always going to be a bit weird."
- Millions of accounts had registered on the website for Curse Voice. He says Curse has to represent what the community wants, and that most people want the timers.

## Handling the publisher relationship and controversy

Hubert's approach with Riot, whose rules and bans could affect the product:
- Be transparent early. Curse told Riot in February what it was doing and why, about three months into beta, rather than surprising them.
- Riot's legitimate concerns were moderation, matching random players in voice, abuse, and add-ons giving a competitive advantage or cheating. He says Curse shares those concerns.
- He expects Riot to publish a policy and believes the timers will be fine. He would not ship something the publisher is definitely against, and he would not be surprised if Riot built timers into the game.
- Commitments: work with Riot on moderation, ban people if needed, and adopt Riot's best practices on toxicity. He notes a voice account carries lower stakes than a game account, so mechanics must be designed accordingly.
- Self-positioning: "We're here to support everyone, the players, the game companies also." He says Curse understands both players and publishers and can be "a great middleman." He admits he can't predict whether Riot may later object to the in-client integration.

## Free product and monetization philosophy

- Communication and any game integration "will always be free."
- Planned experiments (no fixed plan or timeline): Asian-style communication-platform monetization such as special emoticons and premium memberships for nice-to-have features, plus other real-time services, since every user is logged in and ready to interact.
- "Monetization is not my worry." Number one objective: about 100 million hardcore PC gamers outside China, all on the voice platform.
- Next games under consideration: Hearthstone, World of Tanks or Diablo.

## Company strategy: from content to technology platforms

- Core business then was the website network: about 1.1 billion page views a month and 43 to 44 million users the previous month.
- Other platform bets: a YouTube program helping creators monetize and get known, and the voice client.
- His reasoning for moving to technology: web growth is limited without new big games, and "content has more local than technology". Curse built an app in 9 months that could launch worldwide.
- Focus is PC and Mac as open platforms. He cites a study that PC gaming had overtaken console as a worldwide market, helped by mobile games expanding the pool of players with low-spec PCs. He sees enough opportunity there that he doesn't expect to move to other platforms for a long time.
- He expects add-on ecosystems like WoW's not to repeat in League, since it's a simpler game where, for example, a DPS meter would be pointless.
