## Curse began as a World of Warcraft add-on database built to solve his own guild's problem

- In 2005 Hubert managed a hardcore MMO guild called Curse (Dark Age of Camelot, World of Warcraft, some Korean games).
- During the WoW alpha, getting information on add-ons and updates from the official forums was "a complete mess." So he built a database.
- It drew a lot of traffic and attention, and that is when he turned Curse into a business.
- His original goal was modest: get attention for his guild and "live off of advertising." He says he "would never have expected it to be this big, two years later."
- He built it without any social network. Social features were only added about six months before the interview. He uses Facebook and MySpace and watches what they do, but did not copy them.

## The add-on traffic engine: be first, biggest and best at a niche utility

- Early traffic came from the add-on section. WoW add-ons let players customize health bars, quest display and more, which "completely changes the user experience."
- Hubert credits the draw to being "the first, the biggest and the best on the net." He says Curse "surfed on that wave" for about two and a half years.
- By August 2007 the site had 3.5 million uniques (up from 1.5 million) and 80 to 100 million page views. Revenue grew in 2006.
- Early monetization was advertising: Google AdSense and other ad networks, with his brother joining as co-founder in late 2005.
- Real development (database, Wiki, forums, social networking) only began about a year before the interview.

## Product evolution: from a utility to a full community portal

- Version 2 was "really just an improved version 1." Version 3 (December 2006) was a "fully Web 2.0 approach to business."
- The team grew from 5 to 20 and the site went through several redesigns in about a year.
- Added tools: game databases, game-specific Wikis, forums, user-submitted videos, social networking.
- He argues Curse is not an editorial company. Its developers have played MMOs for nearly a decade, so they build from inside the user's experience rather than writing articles.
- Planned features: a guild package ("a full package for a guild, including all their needs"), plus a desktop application tied to the database that updates add-on files.

## Why a premium subscription: demand he already saw

- Hubert planned a monthly premium subscription to compete with GameSpot, FilePlanet and IGN, tying together the social network, add-ons and downloads.
- Evidence of demand: "tens of requests a day" for an application that ties into the database to update add-on files.
- He saw "a few big gaps of service that MMO gamers need covered" by existing sites.

## Competing with incumbents: community scale is the moat for social features

- Targets for direct competition included IGN, GameSpot and other large game sites.
- On niche social competitors such as Guild Cafe: "when you have the social networking sites, you need a huge community. We have a good advantage, because we have had a community for years."
- On the size of the opportunity: players come and go from games, and no network tracks "people you played with a year ago, or two years ago." Building that history takes a lot of players, and he believes Curse is best placed to do it.
- Hubert views Asian MMOs as a gap. Most have no official website or a nearly non-existent one, so there is nowhere for the community to share advice, videos and achievements. Curse wants to be that place. He cites Lineage II, which still had many subscribers with little coverage.

## Becoming a service company for developers and publishers

- Curse was already in the content programs for most big MMOs: interviewing developers and publishers, getting exclusive assets, running contests.
- Publishers get value because Curse supports their game and makes users play more. They also buy advertising on the site.
- Next step: building official game sites for publishers and developers. He wants Curse to position itself as "a service company" that applies what it learned building a multi-service site.
- He was also building "a huge sales force," saying "making money is a huge part of Curse, and it's what we do everyday."
- He wants to serve developers and publishers while serving the community "in the maximum possible way."

## Relocation and funding

- Moves: France to Germany (to add people), then San Francisco. He says Paris was not the place to scale.
- Reason for San Francisco: most big game-site players are in the US and it is the hub of Web 2.0 companies. He did not want to be a copycat bringing a US concept to Europe. He wanted to compete head-on with IGN and GameSpot, and found developers and media management talent there.
- Funding: an $800K angel round in December 2006, then a $5 million Series A in July 2007 from a French VC firm. He found US opportunities more plentiful than European ones. He says raising "was not hard," and the French round came mostly through strong connections there.
