## Starting Curse from a World of Warcraft guild site

- Hubert did not start Curse to make money. He says he "started Curse, not as a way to make money, but as a venture to help the community."
- Curse was the name of his World of Warcraft guild, one of the strongest in the European PvE scene, with many EU-first kills.
- The guild needed a site to organize and share add-ons, so he built Curse-Gaming.com. It turned out to be useful to players outside the guild too.
- His first taste of eSports came from that guild, which briefly played in professional tournaments. His older brother was a pro Counter-Strike gamer who competed in Korea.

## Early growth: from solo founder to ads, then funding

- At first Curse only served WoW add-ons. After moving to the U.S., Hubert found that market "too niche" and widened it.
- Expansion meant new games plus news, tips and wikis for gamers.
- At the start he was the only employee and handled servers, design and everything else.
- The site outgrew what he could manage alone and he couldn't cover its costs. His oldest brother suggested ads as a way to pay the bills, and then became the first employee.
- The company later moved to Germany and a third person helped with web development. After that they went looking for funding, and San Francisco seemed the right place to find it.
- Scale as of 2012:
  - More than 64 sites.
  - Staff grew from 3 in 2007 to over 75 full-time plus over 50 contractors or part-time workers.
  - Reach grew from nothing to over 23 million visitors a month.

## Product strategy: hire real gamers and build tools for passionate players

- Curse's strategy has always been to provide "the best tools, resources and information for passionate gamers."
- Hubert pays close attention to hiring and insists on real gamers. A team with true passion for gaming is the best way to build the best tools.
- His reasoning: "There is no better feedback than that which we receive from our own employees. If our staff uses our products, then we will make good products."

## Expansion tactics: new games, new formats, and an owned pro team

- Curse watches for new games it can serve with the best content for its audience.
- It follows how people consume information, which is why it also pushed into YouTube and video.
- The pro team is a content engine, not a branding play. It feeds LoLPro.com, and Hubert says "Real pro gamers are the best content contributors to create new guides. They know the best tips and ways to play each of the Champions."
- In 2011 Curse bought Team Curse when League of Legends was becoming prominent in pro gaming.
- Curse owned a pair of League of Legends teams, in Europe and the U.S., with Team Curse Houses in Los Angeles and Berlin. It livestreams from those houses to connect gamers with pros.

## Livestreaming: a regular schedule

- Hubert's livestreaming priority is "a regularly scheduled program."
- His reasoning: "People want to follow their favorite streamers and they don't know at what time to tune in. Scheduled programing will satisfy the audience's needs."

## Reading eSports titles: why League of Legends works and StarCraft II doesn't

- Riot's success with League of Legends: "a good game that is fun to watch and play," with "a low level of entry, but a high range to mastery," which builds a bigger audience willing to invest time.
- Riot's Finals at USC filled an arena, which he takes as evidence of the size of the North American eSports audience. The large cash prizes also helped draw attention.
- StarCraft II: he calls it "really saturated" and sees no bright future in North American eSports. It is less fun to watch than League of Legends, and hard to play at the beginning, which discourages players and fans.
- Halo 4 and Call of Duty: Black Ops II: their new spectator mode makes it easier to attract viewers and fans.

## Brand and long-term vision

- Curse was not well known to the mainstream. Hubert's plan to build the brand is to provide resources for more and more games, which widens the market and attracts more people.
- On core gamers, he says they "aren't going away" and may even grow in number as the industry goes mainstream.
- His five-year goal for Curse: "The largest gaming media property on the Web."
