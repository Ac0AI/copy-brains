## Many ideas, little depth: why AI-assisted coding can drain energy

- ThePrimeagen describes his past coding as flow: "the ideas in my head came alive and my fingers were the translation layer." He calls himself "a wizard at Vim."
- He says writing code was never the problem with AI. Bad code has always existed and is fixable. What he misses is sitting down and programming, and he accepts that this is becoming a dying activity, like horse riding.
- He still thinks the AI benefits are impossible to ignore. People can succeed without it, as some did writing code in Notepad, but the average person won't.
- He cites an old article's point: the most senior engineers can do the most junior tasks. They can plow through monotony, stay on task, and break hard problems into slog work. Learning to say no, handle the mundane and work through the slog was a big career level-up for him.
- Most programming is "put pipe A into pipe B," not building a compiler from first principles. His own card game is now mostly UI and game rules, so the hard part is generating ideas and playing it enough to see whether it is fun.

## Coding used to be design time, and AI removes that

- Before AI, an idea took little time to conceive and about 10x to 100x longer to implement, even in a basic version.
- That implementation time doubled as design time. While shaping data, he would realize "if I shape the data this way I can look at it this way," which informed the UI and interaction.
- He describes a loop where "while you code, you design and while you design, you are coding," and the product comes out cohesive and compelling because of the active brain energy spent.
- Vibe coding, light review, or only deciding which tools the AI uses shrinks that time. You get a myopic style of constant "make a decision now" prompts instead of slowly reaching conclusions through effort.
- His own example: an automation dashboard for agents testing his game. It listed trial names, work queues, personalities and test runs. He says it was "everything I asked for, but nothing I actually wanted," because he never had the time to design something good.

## Infinite possibility and the pull to work all the time

- When anything you think of can be built, ideas get no refinement. He argues the old barrier to entry was a good filter: slow decisions helped you separate the 99% "dross" from the gold.
- He praises Aaron Francis (VP of marketing and community at Laravel) for naming this. Aaron went through a "psychosis phase" of "almost like infinite possibility," ripping tokens until midnight or 2 a.m. with a wife and four kids at home.
- ThePrimeagen says he understands the "siren call." One small agent job while waiting at a kid's soccer game turns into working seven days a week with no breaks, which "is going to add up and it's going to make you feel horrible."
- He contrasts this with pre-AI sleepless nights, when a single hard problem he loved kept him up. Now he feels his brain would go crazy thinking of everything at once.
- He says sleep alone improves decision-making "by a country mile," and peace comes from not working against a ticking clock.

## Ignore the "window is closing" hype

- Aaron's realization: it is less of a gold rush than it looked. Every other developer has the same power, so building 40 products doesn't work, and "nobody's buying anything anymore cuz they're all just building it."
- Aaron's fix, which ThePrimeagen endorses: scope down to one or two things and increase "vertical ambition" on them. Move from broad (to-do lists, Screen Studio ripoffs) to deep, where you have a moat. Then you can "just go to bed" instead of fearing a launch on Twitter tomorrow.
- ThePrimeagen's observation: at Open Sauce, three of seven creators at one table were building their own video editor with Claude Code.
- He endorses George Hotz's article on LLM hype. He agrees with its rejection of "window closing," "perpetual underclass" and "falling hopelessly behind" narratives. He thinks they are designed to make you feel bad, and he doubts the claim that AI will own everything.
- He questions the logic of "the white collar jobs will be gone but you'll have tokens." If there are no jobs, he asks who the customers would be.
- He gets many emails from people who feel like they are drowning in this fear.
- His advice: build the thing deliberately. If you take your time and shape one product until it feels intuitive, others will have to chase how you did it.

## Self-awareness about speed pressure on his own game

- ThePrimeagen is building a game of about 30,000 to 35,000 lines. Each change requires more care, constant performance measurement, and a backlog of art to be made. He wants it working on a phone so he can play it a lot and make it fun.
- He admits he puts great pressure on himself to move as fast as possible, perhaps from years in corporate America. It is tempting to "slop it up," and he did, with a testing-harness UI he calls the worst interface in the universe.
- His lesson: "the point of the task is to love the thing you're creating." He says it is easy to bamboozle yourself even when you know the right approach.

## How to keep coding enjoyable while using AI

- Choose what makes you want to come back tomorrow. He compares it to working out: the ideal workout is useless if it is so hard you skip it, so pick the one you will repeat.
- If you love programming, make room for it first thing in the morning. Do an hour of it and enjoy the activity.
- Carve out a piece of the codebase that is "my feature" and hand-write it. Use AI for the surrounding parts, for example asking it to set up the interface or give you the DTS file so your hand-written code works exactly how you like it.
- This keeps control and enjoyment. He says it is not a zero-sum game, and "we don't have to become agent herders."
- He suspects most people building harnesses that build harnesses, himself included with his testing framework, mainly burn tokens and give AI providers a lot of money.
