## Burnout is multifactorial, and part of it is the individual's job to fix

- ThePrimeagen says burnout rarely has one cause. It is usually "a garden variety of all these different things pushing against you."
- He rejects the one-line explanation ("my company is bad, so I need to leave"). Bad companies exist, but if you keep landing at bad companies, "something else is happening."
- He thinks part of burnout is the individual's responsibility and part is the situation's.
- His rule: take the time to understand the whole situation before you blame one thing.
- Outside pressure counted in his own case. He names his wife's miscarriage and postpartum depression, and his own inability to help her at the time.

## His own burnout story at Netflix, roughly 2016 to 2018

- He was at Netflix on the team that built Falcor, a Netflix open-source library that competed with GraphQL.
- The team had a "non-coding architect" who proposed high-level ideas and kept changing direction ("Okay, actually that was wrong. Let's do it this way"). He felt his life was "beholden to an idea man."
- Open source added to the load. He describes demanding users and the pressure of being "the guy" who fixed everything over the weekend.
- Part of why he kept going was status. He wanted to be a speaker and "a cool kid at conferences." He later realized he hated the culture he was upholding.
- A boss, Jeff Wagner, asked him: "Are you running from Netflix or are you running to this other company?" The advice was "Don't run from something. Run to something."
- He then tried the manager track while still doing his IC (individual contributor) work. He disliked it ("doc jocking": writing docs and selling ideas in meetings). It was near his skills but gave him none of the satisfaction of turning "coffee into code."
- He tried to numb out by playing Halo 5. He says he could barely write code for months and that "every single keystroke felt like a huge amount of weight."
- Tech culture at the time also wore on him. He describes 2014 to 2018 as "austere": tone policing, a divide between conference speakers and attendees, and people being fired over jokes. He thinks this made the job less fun than it should be.

## How he recovered: more programming, not less

- He told his boss he no longer wanted to be a manager, and he disengaged for about a year to a year and a half. He still met his deliverables.
- The turn came from a side project. He built an arbitrage system between crypto exchanges and made a couple thousand dollars. It worked, though he notes "you have to have a lot of money for it to work."
- Building for himself re-sparked his interest in programming. He says "I kind of re-sparked an interest in programming by doing not less programming, but more programming."
- Side projects have no deadlines or outside pressure, and they let him become an expert in the craft again. That made the day job feel less bad.
- Streaming came next. He joined a Netflix charity stream (Extra Life), streamed Fortnite for 24 hours, then streamed programming. He found programming streams went "vastly more" successfully because he is actually good at programming.
- That became his content career, and he set out to make tech Twitter "fun and awesome" over roughly six years.

## His advice for avoiding and recovering from burnout

- Reframe obstacles as opportunities. Stop treating work as a hurdle on the way to what you want.
- Ask "how can I do this the best?" and "how can I make this awesome?" instead of "this is beneath my talent."
- Stay engaged with the craft. Find the joy in becoming an expert at it, not just in going through the motions.
- Build side projects for the joy of building. Use them to rediscover why you got into programming.
- Check back in after a period of checking out. He did his best work after he started engaging again.
- He says he also warns himself against assuming the work is "beneath me" or "boring," because he had his own list of excuses.

## How he works through hard problems and learning

- He describes himself as someone who enjoys hard problems and seeing them through. He grew up on Battletoads and says he has "a bit of a masochistic spree."
- Hard work can feel frustrating in the moment while the finish feels good.
- Example: he was learning a new programming language in a new paradigm. After two weeks he thought "I can already do all this stuff, why am I learning it in yet another thing?" He stepped back, remembered his love of learning, and got back into it.
- On starting work, he says the first minute of any session is slow ("Okay, here we go, where was I?") and then "all of a sudden you just take off and you're in the zone." He compares it to the first five minutes of a workout.
- He says work should be satisfying, not necessarily "enjoyable": "I'm not sure if work is meant to be enjoyable. It's meant to be satisfying."
- He identifies as a programmer by nature. When he tried to leave for seminary, he caught himself sketching boxes and diagrams of programs on paper. He compares the feeling to Eric Liddell in Chariots of Fire: "when I program, that's how I feel."

## How he got into programming and became good at it

- He started on a Zelda-like game called Graal. It had a level editor with code on screen. As a fifth or sixth grader he decided to understand it, and he learned while loops and if statements by moving things around.
- He failed out of college once and quit once, partly to get away from people who pulled him toward drugs.
- He later struggled in school ("Really struggled, by the way"), then got good at math through grind. He did three hours of math class and three hours in the help center, daily, over a summer, for hundreds of hours. He went "from a horrible student to effectively the best student in my class."
- He landed an early job through a career fair, around 2008, at Computers Unlimited, a C# shop. He says professional programming horrified him enough that he briefly changed his major to mechanical engineering before returning to computer stuff.
- He says a piece of feedback he got at the career fair was that he looked people in the eye, which helped him get the interview.

## Tech career lessons he draws from his own mistakes

- He overvalued his job. He puts it this way: "whatever you value the most is what you're going to be like the most."
- If he could advise his younger self: "this job stuff, it's going to come, it's going to go... you won't even be working at Netflix at one point," but family stays, so put the effort there.
- Management was not his strength. He says he is "really good at thinking through a large problem and turning it into code," and that doing adjacent work well is not the same as enjoying it.
- He took a "I'm supposed to do this because that's what grown-ups do" path (the manager track, the conference circuit). It felt wrong because it ran against his skills and interests.

## Doing the thing his strengths point at, and what he took from being a dad

- On parenting, he says he works on not acting out of anger. He takes about 15 minutes before deciding a punishment, and says the punishment changes completely in that window (from "one month no screen time and throw away your phone" to something proportionate).
- He tries to be consistent in how he responds regardless of how he feels. He compares it to extending grace to strangers while giving little to a spouse.
- He uses the word redemption in the sense of taking something broken and turning it useful: he never had a dad, but being a dad lets him live the other side of it.
- He says he has probably said "sorry" more in 11 years of parenting than in the rest of his life.
- On faith, he describes his Christian conversion at 19 as a turning point out of drug and porn addiction. He says he no longer has to prove himself ("I don't have to be cool anymore") and that this freed him from competing for status.
