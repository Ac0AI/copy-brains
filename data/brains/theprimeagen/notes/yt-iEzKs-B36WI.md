## Vim as a skill: motions, macros, and the path from Netlify-era editors to Vim

- ThePrimeagen did not start on Vim. His editor path was NetBeans (where he was fastest on the keys), then Eclipse ("dog water"), Sublime ("dog water"), VS Code, and IntelliJ, where he stayed a long time.
- A friend, Bogart Berry, said he was going to try Vim and that ThePrimeagen should too. He tried it because he already knew he was fast on the keys.
- The first week was "absolute hell." After that he got fast, saw he could get faster, and kept improving.
- What he wanted was real control of the cursor instead of up/down/left/right plus Control and Shift. Around 2016 a Netflix coworker told him to just use Vim, and he watched colleagues who were very fast in Vim (Guy Sereno) and Emacs (Anders Backen). He wanted that speed.
- He says it was "totally worth it."
- Speed: his general typing is around 110-120 WPM on stream. Switching to Dvorak and then to a Kinesis keyboard slowed him down. Typing predefined Vim motions is much faster, because it is a repeating pattern. A key monitor he built once showed over 400 on that measure.
- He compares it to actions per minute in real-time strategy games and says he modeled his workflow after StarCraft 2. That mindset is how Harpoon came about.
- Vim as a differentiator in content: his most popular shorts are him doing Vim macros, which he calls "the 360 no scope of the programming world." His fast Vim was the parlor trick that pulled people in.
- He taught Vim at Frontend Masters, and says it now sits on their backend learning path. His reasoning: motions are fundamental, especially if you work on servers. Vi is nearly always on a server, and Vim usually is too. He can still work in Vi, though less comfortably.
- Next up for him: more Vim tooling, rethinking his dotfiles, finishing a rebuild of Harpoon ("if I just had an hour of time it would be done"), and returning to developer productivity work. He wants to build "what I really want."

## Daily routine: work first, same routine every day

- ThePrimeagen follows what he calls the Alex Hormozi morning routine. He wakes around 5:30-6:00, pours coffee, and goes straight to work until about 10, 11, or 12. No breaks and no warm-up.
- His argument: people who are scrapping to make something happen wake up and go straight to work. After they have a first exit or some success, they add long routines (workouts, red light, cold plunge and hot tub cycles, vitamins, TRT). Observers then credit the routine for the success, but the routine is not what got them there.
- He says "just wake up in my hoodie with my headphones on and we go straight into it."
- He is skeptical of optimizing a morning (for example waiting 90 minutes before coffee, Huberman style). His point: whatever routine you pick, doing the same one every day is about 10 times more valuable than hunting for the optimal one. Consistency builds momentum.
- The benefit of doing deep work early is that there are no meetings. He lives in Montana, an hour ahead of Pacific time. By 10:00 his time it is 9:00 in the Valley, where nobody starts before nine. He has already done a full day of focused work before any meeting.
- Evenings: a dry sauna and running. He also works out two to three times a day in short blocks, for example 10 sets of 10 on one lift with cardio between sets (15 calories on the Peloton), finished in 13-15 minutes. He also does a Jacob's Ladder.

## Learning to focus: the Calculus 1 turnaround

- ThePrimeagen's school history was rough. He had a 2.16 in high school, with drugs, alcohol and bad decisions, and he dropped out of college twice. The dropouts and the return to school spanned years.
- The turning point was Calculus 1. He took a summer class with 3 hours of class a day, followed by 2-4 hours of tutoring right afterward. He went to the math learning center 2-4 hours a day, 4 days a week, and worked every problem.
- The lesson he draws: he was forced to work very hard, but on a single topic, every day, all day. This trained his ability to focus for long periods, and "all of a sudden" the problems he had had up to then went away.
- Results: he set the curve in Calc 1 and finished the final in under 30 minutes (the next person took 1 hour 45 minutes). He did the same in Calc 2. In Calc 4 (differential equations) he was the only one of 400-plus students to finish. In a programming languages class the top grade was around 15-20%, he scored 100% on the test, and curving gave him roughly 240%.
- His framing: "I was an overnight success after about 3 to 4 years of trying." Small changes are invisible for years, then the change looks sudden.
- He also notes he struggled with ADHD, and says the single-topic grind is what made focus possible.

## Career path: from two startups to Netflix, and what it takes to stand out

- He graduated at 24. From 22 to 24 he worked 80-100 hours a week on schoolwork and building websites, inspired by The Social Network and heavily into jQuery.
- Afterward he spent a year on a startup while his wife was still in school, programming all night many times. His friend JJ (the "micro king") finally told him it was not going to work. He already knew, gave up, and went to find a real job.
- Job 1: Schedulicity. About five months in he found the work boring and decided to "learn how to be content in every situation."
- Job 2: Web Filings in Bozeman, Montana. A man named Ivan Judson approached him at a coffee shop and offered 50% more pay. He worked there about a year and a half at 60+ hours a week, and says he learned the most he has ever learned at any job.
- Job 3: Netflix interviewed and hired him as a senior software engineer when he was about two years into his professional career. He has been there almost 10 years (his start was in November, and he cycled 12 miles to the first day).
- His advice for someone younger who wants this path:
  - Plan for overwhelming work. Anyone who says you can do it with a nice work-life balance from the start is wrong, in his view.
  - Hiring is effectively zero sum. In today's market, companies can hold a role open and wait for the best person. You will not stand out by putting in 20 hours a week of learning.
  - The first three to four years need real intensity if you want to work somewhere very good. If you prefer a slower place (he gives a medical company as an example), that is fine too. Pick your destination and accept that some journeys are hard.
  - Learn to type well, because it is a very valuable skill.
  - Learn to work long hours early, because there is a lot to learn and you need to be proficient at many things and actually "move the needle."

## Netflix then and now

- ThePrimeagen's favorite Netflix story is from about 2016, when Netflix went global and opened nearly every country it could legally operate in.
- For a full year before the announcement, all the UI teams knew. They built Dynamic ways to handle all the translations. In a building of about a thousand people, nothing leaked.
- He describes that era as the "freedom and responsibility" culture: hire adults only, and everyone took it seriously, including feedback.
- Now, he says, it is not the same Netflix. It is bigger, and in his experience he does not get feedback the way he used to. He still likes working there. He jokes that one of his code reviews once called him an idiot.

## Opinions on languages, frameworks, and tools

- JavaScript/TypeScript: TypeScript "brought all the benefits of a statically typed language with none of the benefits of a statically typed language."
- Rust: a great language to rewrite in. Go: practical. Haskell: a "white paper." C++: he finds it annoying, saying it excels at letting you make complicated things.
- OCaml: he is deeply intrigued, and worries he will love it. His view is that once someone goes down the ML family route, they cannot come back to C-style syntax, but nobody else wants to read the code.
- Algorithms: you should know them. He has a Frontend Masters algorithms course, with a second one coming that is deeper, faster, and more whiteboard-heavy.
- React and Next.js: React was an amazing iteration that let many ideas about building software get tested. Pre-rendering is the coolest idea and the biggest win; he predicts it will be the only win from React Server Components. His issue with the modern frontend approach is that it tries to have its cake and eat it too.
- A principle on abstractions: abstractions that give you full control tend to be good, while ones that wrap and repackage things tend to be difficult. He compares it to logging: a tool that tells you something is wrong is nice because you still do the debugging, but tools built to pinpoint the problem for you tend to give far too much information and are impractical.
- On ecosystems, he criticizes plugins that must be written as "react this, react that" instead of general-purpose utilities, and says Svelte does not have this problem because its ecosystem is just JavaScript. He thinks Svelte is probably the most compelling front-end framework right now.
- HTMX: he loves it, but does not consider it a front-end framework. He calls it a data-fetching library. He compares the model to real-time games: state lives on the server, a static frame is sent down once, and it only changes after an interaction produces a server change that is pushed to the client.
- HTMX tip for Frontend Masters: because the site does full page reloads, adding `hx-boost` on the body would turn link clicks into fetch-and-swap, giving single-page-app behavior with no extra programming. Gzip on larger documents helps too.

## Using AI for programming

- ThePrimeagen says AI is "both great and bad at the exact same time." He thinks it has peaked for now and describes himself as already in the "plane of disillusionment."
- He says it is great at black-box problems where inputs and outputs are fully known. His examples: a CSV with three columns and a request for a Python graph plus a histogram, or a parser that handles npm, pnpm, and yarn lockfile formats. The latter used to be a hard task two years ago. Now you ask ChatGPT, hide the result in a module, and rarely touch it again.
- For exploratory work he finds it much less effective. Answers can be made up and may not be true. For learning, he says you often get "really average information" rather than real insight, and he has seen it invent things on algorithm questions.
- He likes that it takes away the boring, average parts of development.

## Content creation: why he streams and how he balances it

- Where it came from: he streamed Fortnite first, then switched to programming because that is where his real talent lay. He says Fortnite had a huge skill gap but little meaning. He started streaming code on Twitch, where he had around 30 viewers and was one of the biggest in the Science and Tech category that was not earthquakes or chickens.
- He likes live content far more than produced YouTube video, because of the different energy. He can talk while coding because he has done it for about 20 years.
- A deliberate choice: early on he decided he was not an educational channel but an "edutainment" one. He builds things and responds live. Teaching is transactional, while building something with an audience is entertaining, and people come back to be part of it. The more people involved, the more fun.
- Platforms: YouTube loves the content, TikTok hates it (he is the only programmer TikToker and gets the most negative comments), Instagram is in the middle, and Twitter high-fives it.
- His rule for a "why": he made content because he was frustrated with tech content that had no feeling in it. He built what he himself wanted to watch rather than copying someone else.
- Balancing it with a Netflix job: he says the key is partners. His editor, Flip, matters most because he can take a vision and run with it independently. ThePrimeagen would "much rather not have the world's greatest editor but have someone … really good at just coming up with a vision and executing with no guidance." That frees him to do something else.
- His wife handles scheduling and runs other things, which lets him stay focused. He uses the "lone dancing man turns into a crowd" story: it takes one crazy person and a second to join, and she is that second person.
- On workload: recording three Frontend Masters courses at once was far too much, "emotionally painful."
- Upcoming: live reacting to conference talks (he did Next.js Conf with Vercel on October 26, and React Miami is planned with Theo). He compares it to the commentator on ESPN. It brings talks to his large Twitch audience, is good for sponsors, and lets him ask the speakers questions afterward.

## Mindset: self-direction, purpose, and parenting

- ThePrimeagen's father died when he was seven. From seven to about sixteen he did most things alone (Lego, building, exploring, video games), so he became self-directed. He says he has no mentors now and no idols, and notes that meeting your idol is often disappointing.
- Why he creates: he wants to build the thing he wanted to exist, and he wants to be a figure he lacked for others, especially at a time of loneliness and meaninglessness. He loves the process of creation itself, from A to B, calling it done and moving on, rather than maintaining a huge prestigious project for years.
- He is moved when viewers say his content brought back their love of programming. He wants people to be excited about creating.
- Fatherhood (four kids) changed his sense of what is valuable. He says he now values the current moment much more, and tries to be fully in the time he has.
- His one bucket-list goal is to be alive until his daughter is older than he was when his father died (seven).
- On staying driven: curiosity, plus a sense of having a second chance, which he credits to his faith.
