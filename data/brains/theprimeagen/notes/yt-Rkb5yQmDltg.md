## Breaking addiction by taking control instead of following feelings

- ThePrimeagen describes a childhood and college stretch of heavy addiction: pornography from age four, plus cigarettes, weed, LSD, mushrooms and methamphetamines. He says he was "fairly addicted to everything" by college.
- His diagnosis: "everyone has some sort of hole in their life in which you try to justify or fill in with something."
- The turning point was one night with a terrifying, very real sense that he was going to die if he continued. That was enough for him to stop from that day forward.
- The lesson he took from it: he had been letting whatever he felt dictate what he did. Stopping taught him he can control how he reacts based on what he thinks is the right thing to do, not how he feels.
- His timeline: weed took about three years, pornography took three to four years (he has not looked at it in 15 years), cigarettes took about a year.

## How to actually stop: say no in the moment

- He says quitting is always a "no" at the moment of temptation, "saying it all day every day."
- You don't stop at the end of a pack of cigarettes or in the middle of one. You stop in the moment.
- He distrusts "I'll be done X" plans. If you only quit when circumstances allow it (the natural separation), you aren't really winning: "it's not you who's winning it's the separation."
- He is skeptical of "I start working out tomorrow" thinking, though he admits he tends to do it too and only recently started working out again.
- He believes people have more power over their body and reactions than they realize.
- Example: after being hit by a car while biking in Alaska, he broke into a sweat and had immediate PTSD stepping onto a street the next day. He decided he could not live like that, walked across anyway while sweating profusely, and it faded in about two days.

## Head versus heart: lasting change takes time

- He quotes the idea that "the distance between your head and your heart is the greatest distance in the universe," and says he fully believes it.
- It takes a long time to match what is in your head with what is in your heart. The changes that really changed his life came from the heart to the head, not the head to the heart.
- Everyone is "only a couple decisions away from being anything." He cites the line "you're just a drink and a wink away from ruining your life."
- He tries to stay careful about how fragile this is: "my strength will only last for so long."

## Grinding until the effort changes you (his college turnaround)

- He got a 2.16 GPA in high school, "which I believe is barely passing," and later almost doubled his GPA in college.
- He took pre-calculus three times and failed. After quitting his addictions, his first semester in college was still not great.
- By Calculus 1 he was the first to finish every test, 45 minutes to an hour before everyone else, with the highest grade every time. He says he scored around 150 and the professors removed his score from the pool so it wouldn't set the curve.
- His principle: "you do something long enough there just comes a point where the fruit of that trying changes you." At first it was hard and he was still getting C's, then after about a year of trying hard every day something clicked.

## Discipline, ADHD and not being "always on"

- He is not fully disciplined: he doesn't make his bed, and cleanliness habits are still hard.
- He believes you can't win every decision. There is decision fatigue, so you need some area where you are not "on."
- He once organized shirts by color and felt he was developing actual OCD. He recognized the peak, stopped on the spot, and has "never been clean since."
- On ADHD: "ADHD plus maturity is a superpower, ADHD plus excuses is a nightmare." You have to fight it every day. Either the dog shakes its tail or the tail shakes the dog, and he sometimes has to choose a side 14,000 times in one day.
- He avoids tics and habitual fidgeting. He only does something like bouncing on the ball when the moment is right, not out of habit.
- Being a husband, father of four, full-time engineer and streamer: he lets his back brain solve about 80% of the problems, so when he sits down at work he knows exactly what to do and does it as fast as he can.
- He uses no organization system: no Jira, nothing written down. His view is that important things you will remember and less important things you will forget. He works on one thing at a time and does the most important thing he can.

## How he got hired at Netflix

- He credits preparation plus luck: "the harder I work the luckier I get."
- He went to a conference, learned RxJS, got good at it and listed it as a skill on LinkedIn. Netflix was under a big crunch hiring people good at RxJS, and a hiring manager found him there.
- Netflix's interview was social-heavy and light on technical. Asked an RxJS question, he got the interviewers talking until they wrote the whole solution themselves.
- When asked his least favorite Netflix feature, he answered forcefully and honestly, and they loved it.
- He has been at Netflix about nine years.
- Before Netflix he worked at Schedulicity for six months under a manipulative CEO. A stranger there offered him 50% more pay for a job elsewhere. It meant 60 to 70 hour weeks and was very difficult, but he calls it a very good experience that shaped who he is.

## What to learn if you want a better-paid programming job

- He first asks the goal: get hired, or understand programming. For a better job and salary, he calls the easiest path JavaScript UI.
- Reasoning: a huge and growing audience works on UI and middleware JavaScript, so it is the easiest place to get hired. Compare that with something like compilers, where maybe 500 to 1,000 people work total.
- His second pick is Java, which he says is currently the biggest industry standard with many companies hiring. Whether you will enjoy Java is a separate question.
- He notes English is very important in programming and in business, though he treats that as a business-world barrier rather than a computer science one.
