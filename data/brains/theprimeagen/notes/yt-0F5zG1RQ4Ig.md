## "You won't fall behind": the fear of being left behind by AI is mostly a feeling, not reality

- He says the experience isn't new, but today it is pressured by a new set of outcomes he didn't face when learning to program.
- His message: "You're not falling behind. And I do mean that."
- A viewer he quotes describes it well: it is the feeling of falling behind, not necessarily the reality. When models and tools change every few days, it creates a constant "I need to keep up" mentality that is "super toxic".

## Past hustle burnout: ThePrimeagen's 24-hour coding sessions at 24-25

- At about 24-25, freshly married and trying to start a company, he programmed for over 24 hours straight, in a studio apartment next to the door while his wife slept.
- He loved programming, but he also had constant fear and anxiety that he was falling behind, wasn't outputting enough and wouldn't launch successfully.
- The anxiety colored his social life. With friends he kept thinking he had to get home because it was taking too long.
- He describes it as a kind of mental breakdown over a large period of his life. It caused undue stress for his wife, probably hurt his marriage for some time, and he calls it one of his more foolish moments. He also says he wasn't a great father in his kids' early years.
- His fear then came from real competitors and a startup race, not from a generalized "everyone will be left behind" narrative.
- He admits he still struggles to step away, calling it "such a learned or ingrained problem inside of me".

## Work hard, but taking time off won't change your life

- He is not against working hard. He is a "huge proponent" of going all in, especially when young: "Two butt cheeks, whole ass that attempt."
- Becoming deeply competent and excellent at your craft is "an extremely rewarding thing".
- A good day is one where you learn and make a step forward. Taking a weekend off "is not going to dramatically change the course of your life".
- Being engaged and focused when it's time to work pays dividends. So does learning and understanding the underlying thing, rather than "pressing the I'm going to be lucky tonight button".
- His advice: choose the good things and the good work. "You don't have to keep up with everything."

## Tech hype cycles have always made newcomers anxious

- He compares AI to earlier waves. People graduating in 2020-2021 or going through bootcamps felt an onslaught of technologies they had to learn or fall behind.
- Example: he argued on Twitter that React Server Components weren't a big deal. He said you don't need to switch your company over or sprinkle them on your resume. He says he still feels justified given the vulnerabilities that have surfaced since. People quote-tweeted him saying he gives the worst advice if you're not keeping up.
- He thinks this kind of pressure causes needless swirl and anxiety in people.

## Early AI tooling advice quickly became obsolete

- When ChatGPT 3.5 came out, people said it was replacing them. During the ChatGPT 3.5 and Opus 3 / 3.5 era, the constant message was that if you weren't using these tools you'd be left behind.
- He argues that almost nothing people learned in that era is still useful. The way you set up context and interact with models today "looks almost nothing like it did in the beginning".
- Conclusion: chasing the latest and greatest workflow out of fear is wasted effort, since "every last single thing about that era is effectively void."

## Be skeptical of token-maxing and negative-valence AI hype

- He agrees with George Hotz's post that "the constant [nonsense] about the window closing or the perpetual underclass or falling hopelessly behind" is "negative valence hype". It is untrue and mostly designed to make you feel bad about yourself.
- He also agrees with Hotz that the jump from "fancy autocomplete, smart compiler, better search engine" to "it's going to own the whole light cone" is a straw man.
- On incentives: AI companies make money when you "token max". He asks: "Don't you realize that there's a little bit of a perverse incentive when the people who are selling shovels [tell] you the only thing you ever need to practice or do is dig holes?"
- He is not saying don't investigate. He keeps a loop running to test whether the approach actually works or is a waste of time, because he wants an informed opinion. But he is "not betting everything on this" and is aware of the risk of spending too much money on it.
- He notes that nobody knows what the future holds right now: "I have no idea."

## Practical advice: use AI as a search engine and debugger, have fun, and keep building skill

- He tells people to enjoy this period: you can build whatever you want and investigate whatever you want.
- He values AI as a fantastic search engine that works even with terrible English. Before, his options were Stack Overflow or reading the code.
- Example: he introduced a regression into a game he was building and asked the AI to launch `perf` and tell him which function he broke. About half an hour later it came back with exactly what he had done wrong. He calls that a big time saver.
- His closing view: learning and becoming better at your craft is still an excellent choice, as he said a couple of years ago and last year, and he doesn't think you're being left behind.
