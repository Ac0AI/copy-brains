## Betting on yourself when the odds are against you

- ThePrimeagen joined Netflix in December 2013 at age 27 as a senior software engineer, with only about 3 years out of school and about a year and a half of employment.
- He was not senior material by his own account, and the youngest person on his 40-person team was about 32.
- He felt like he had "Bamboozled" them into hiring him, but he decided to do the job as well as he knew how.
- His claim: betting that you can solve something you don't yet know pays "not just dividends that year or the next year but for decades."
- He says he can't predict whether LLMs will take jobs or the job market will improve, but he can say this with certainty.
- He applies it to people asking if they are too old to learn to code at 25: it is always worth betting on yourself.

## Taking the worst job and doing it as well as he could

- At Netflix he arrived late to a roughly 9-month project delivering an entirely new experience, so he lacked seniority and was given the worst job: a Groovy back end.
- He knew nobody at the company, didn't know Groovy and didn't know the tech stack, and decided he could figure it out and do a really good job.
- He calls Groovy "an adult shaped toddler" and "one of the worst languages of all time", and did it for nine months anyway.
- Result: no production outages, and he rolled it out to only 1% of customers in 2014 rather than everyone (the "slow clap" approach), joking that CrowdStrike did not learn from him.
- No UI engineer wanted back-end work, so his boss then asked him to rewrite a data structure with a two-year-old bug that nobody wanted. He took it, since he now knew Groovy, and it went well.

## How unglamorous work compounded into a career

- The back-end success led to being asked to help with Falcor, where he did data fetching for Netflix and gave open source talks.
- Those connections led to Guy Sereno inviting him to do an extra life event on Twitch, which led to streaming, then YouTube videos, then speaking at a PHP conference.
- He traces it all "all the way back to learning groovy."
- Takeaway he draws: tackle the hard, unwanted problem in an unfamiliar stack, because the opportunities it opens are unpredictable.

## Where the belief came from: failing math, then summer calculus

- In high school a teacher wrote him a letter saying he was the worst student she'd had in 30 years, and at 18 his grandfather told him he was out of the family will.
- In 2007 he took pre-calculus three times: failed twice, then got a C.
- That summer he took calculus: 2 hours in class, 2 hours at the math learning center, then 6 to 8 hours of studying alone, for 5 weeks.
- He started convinced he was the worst student and ended objectively the best.
- That changed something in him: with determination he could solve things he didn't know, though he says he wasn't a "gal smart" kid solving things.
- He later struggled for two months to understand recursion and thought he wasn't a very good programmer, but knew he could figure it out, and did.

## Bet on yourself, but not at the cost of what matters

- After the calculus win he was convinced he could accomplish any problem and built a startup in PHP 5.2, working 80 to 100 hours a week.
- The first and second years of his marriage were the worst years of it, because he couldn't tell what was valuable. Now, 13 years in, he says this is the best year.
- His rule: "always bet on yourself but don't bet against something that's good in value don't cut Corners."
- On overwork: "an extra 10 hours has never made a startup succeed or fail ever."
- He says excellent developers must be willing to go the distance and also know what is valuable.
- Framing: "intelligence is the ability to solve hard problems but wisdom is knowing which problems to solve."

## What keeps him going

- He provides for his family: he told his wife that if she helps with the kids in their younger years, she can work or do whatever she wants afterward with no pressure to make money, and he must always be the final provider.
- He simply enjoys building and discovering how things work, and the "magical" feeling of seeing a problem from a perspective you've never seen before.
- He is also driven by messages from people asking whether LLMs will take their jobs, whether coding is worth it, or whether they are too old to learn at 25. He wants to tell them it's always worth betting on yourself.

## Making content: do what you love, not what the analytics say

- To people afraid to start making content, he says analytics (the first 15 seconds, the music) can help, but people would rather see you doing something you really love.
- There may be no audience, but there could also be a huge number excited to see it.
- He would rather make things the way he wants than keep trying to please one person or guess what everyone wants.
- Reason: chasing what others want leads to burnout, sadness and dejection when it fails, whereas making what you like means you only ask "does anyone want to see what I like?"
