## Why ThePrimeagen felt his value was in question

- ThePrimeagen gets messages from engineers (1, 2, 5, 10 years in, or still in college) asking if their past work is now worthless. He says some messages, multiple times a month, include people saying they will kill themselves over it.
- He admits he felt the same sense of loss and confusion. For about 6 months he was in a funk and called TJ several times saying he was confused.
- His background: about 6,000 days of programming over 20 years, 14 of them using Vim motions (IntelliJ, VS Code for 6 months, then Neovim). He knew Go, JavaScript, C, Rust, Zig, and some Jai.
- He saw himself as the "ultimate generalist" who could build anything. One example: building something like GStreamer to measure latency from JavaScript to audio hitting HDMI, to check for pops in live video games.
- The question he kept asking: "Have I invested poorly in my future?" and "What is my value?"
- He suggests X is not a healthy platform for perspective. It has an aggressive "AGI achieved daily" strain, and he recommends 24 to 48 hours of social distancing from it.

## Two answers he tested and rejected: taste and lines of code

- Taste: he considered whether an engineer's value is now just "taste" (the VC buzzword), meaning how nice a site looks or how nice CLI flags are for an agent to consume. He says he hopes not, though it seems that way in much of the current writing.
- Lines of code: at his peak he could write about 15,000 lines in a week without Adderall. He notes Gary Tan reportedly does 37,000 in a day, 7 days a week.
- His conclusion: he cannot compete on lines of code, so his value cannot live there.

## The 2x4 moment: worry builds imaginary dragons

- Earlier story: he bought a house in Bozeman, Montana for 205 (on a handshake deal) and then took a job at WebFilings (now Workiva), which he says shaped who he is as an engineer.
- He was so deep in anxiety about whether he would be good enough, and about the debt, that he walked into a 2x4 sticking out of a truck and was knocked to the ground.
- His lesson: he was so focused on a future built from a made-up narrative that he missed the real, obvious danger in front of him. "I made up dragons that didn't even exist until I was laying on the ground."
- He says the same thing happened again over the last 6 months of worrying about his value.

## The tweet that reset his thinking: engineering decisions compound

- The second "2x4 moment" was a tweet where someone asked about a "really trivial issue" and attached a screenshot of an AI's internal monologue deciding "forking Chromium is a really interesting question. We should pursue".
- His reaction: forking Chromium is "the worst decision I've ever seen" and not trivial. On 2023 to 2025 hardware, compiling the project takes about 6 hours. His advice: "Do not fork Chromium for any reason."
- His realization: if AI is a true multiplier, every small decision matters, because without the knowledge you end up forking Chromium.
- Examples of decisions the AI will not make well for you unless you know them:
  - Getting a Boyce-Codd normalized database, and knowing when to break it.
  - Using standard in and standard out versus a web server.
  - Choosing a data format, and whether and how to serialize.
- He says decisions compound: "It's not some sort of like, 'Oh, you have a five end problem.' No, you have a two to the end problem."

## Code generation is great, but the right line of code is now worth more

- He still likes his Neovim plus OpenCode setup ("Trad coder") and sees why generation is great for well-understood, heavily documented problems (his example: a parser, "documented 5,000 times"), such as quickly producing a Macchiato theme.
- Core claim: "If the cost of a line of code has dramatically dropped, then the cost of the right line of code has dramatically increased."
- Pizza analogy: a first-time pizza maker adds every topping (onions, sausage, pineapple) and gets the worst pizza ever. When every option is free, picking the right one is dramatically harder.
- He disputes the "coding will be gone in 12 months" prediction: he doesn't see how you get from A to B without good decision-making. By that he means "actual engineering decision-making," not taste (for example, which data format to pick, and why).

## Advice for newer engineers: skip "toxic productivity"

- He says you don't need to be productive at all times: "earning experience is more valuable than completing something instantaneously."
- He rejects the idea that you haven't been productive unless you have a clickable end result.
- His biggest and most influential moments came from failing repeatedly to understand why something is done a certain way, not from shipping clickable things. He says he sometimes chose to skip 5 minutes of reading the friendly manual and then spent 6 hours debugging, and learned a lot from it.
- He still sees hope and fun for newcomers: you can now ask for something like an OCaml parser and get it, even if you wouldn't want to maintain it.
- He ends on a DHH quote: "It's fun to be competent."
