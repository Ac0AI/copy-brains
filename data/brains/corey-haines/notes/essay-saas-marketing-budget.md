## Two methods for setting a SaaS marketing budget

- Corey says he used to pull marketing budget numbers "out of a hat". Founders asking for "enough" avoid scrutiny by not asking for too much.
- He rejects the line "We can find the budget for the right opportunities as long as the ROI is there". He calls it "a lazy and unacceptable excuse".
- He argues project-by-project budgeting slows progress, discourages experimentation and pushes teams to think too small and conservatively.
- He claims 99% of SaaS startups don't know how marketing expenses relate to revenue growth. Without that link there is no scientific way to set a budget.
- He offers two methods:
  - Method 1 starts with the budget (5-40% of annual revenue) and ends with the revenue goal.
  - Method 2 starts with the revenue goal and ends with the budget.

## Method 1: allocate 5-40% of annual revenue, then forecast the outcome

- The budget is what the company can comfortably devote to marketing. The revenue goal is the last piece and is largely determined by that budget.
- A conservative 5% suits an established, profit-focused business that wants to preserve profit for shareholders.
  - Example: $1M ARR gives a $50,000 budget. At $100 CAC that is 500 new customers. At $50 average monthly revenue per customer (ARPC), that adds $300k ARR, for $1.3M ARR at year end.
  - With 15% annual churn, only 85% of the $300k counts ($255k), for a $1.255M ARR goal.
- An aggressive 40% suits a business that has raised funding and has ambitious goals.
  - Example: $1M ARR gives a $400,000 budget. At $100 CAC that is 4,000 new customers. At $50 ARPC, that adds $2.4M ARR, for $3.4M ARR at year end.
- He says "You can't be afraid to aggressively invest capital toward marketing when you need to hit your goal."
  - A sales leader at a previous startup, behind on the revenue goal, said "We need to figure out how to spend a million dollars this quarter." Corey thought he was joking, and says the moment changed his mindset.
- He notes Zoom's investor reports show sales and marketing at as little as 20% and as much as 55% of revenue. He says other public and private SaaS companies show a similar range.
- Process: slide the percentage between 5% and 40% until everyone agrees on how aggressive to be and how much money to deploy from the start.
- If someone pushes you to raise the revenue goal, push back by asking them to raise the marketing budget too. He calls it "a symbiotic relationship".

## Knowing and calculating CAC

- Method 1 depends on knowing CAC and on matching your ambition to the percentage you allocate.
- With no past CAC data, use a baseline of one year of customer revenue on your smallest paid plan. Compare it to real data as it arrives, then set a new benchmark.
- You don't need to run ads to have a CAC.
- CAC should be blended. Include salaries, advertising, technology, content creation and everything else related to acquiring customers. Divide the total by the new customers acquired in the last year.
- Be objective about CAC. Work on reducing it later, because for now you need a real number.
- Starting with an inflated CAC is better, because it leaves more room for error.

## Method 2: reverse-engineer the budget from a revenue goal

- Formula: [(New ARR / (ARPC x 12)) x CAC] / annual retention rate.
- He says it took many iterations and a lot of Excel. He calls it a "foolproof way" to propose a budget you can explain with confidence.
- It fits best when:
  - The SaaS is just starting up.
  - Outside capital is expected and you need to find money to hit ambitious goals.
  - You have revenue-based financing or non-dilutive capital such as Pipe, Capchase or Founderpath.
- Worked example, going from $1M to $2M ARR (so $1M new ARR):
  1. ARPC of $50 x 12 gives $600 of first-year ARR per customer.
  2. $1,000,000 / $600 means about 1,667 additional customers.
  3. At $100 CAC, 1,667 x 100 is about $166,700.
  4. Divide by 85% annual retention: 166,700 / 0.85 is about $196,118.
  5. Round up to about $200k.
- When someone asks where the number came from, walk them through the formula step by step.

## Growth targets for the revenue goal

- Corey says that once a company reaches $1M ARR, VCs generally want to see revenue triple for two consecutive years, then double for at least three consecutive years. Some call it the "3 3 2 2 2 Rule".
- His example after a Series A at $1M ARR: $3M ARR at +1 year, $9M at +2, $18M at +3, $36M at +4, $72M at +5, $144M at +6, $288M at +7.
- He says you may want to grow 50% or 5,000%, and the formula doesn't care.

## Build in buffers and an experimental budget

- CAC is the main dependency in Method 2. If CAC comes in 50% higher than estimated, the effects cascade and you risk missing the revenue goal.
- He says it is far better to overestimate CAC than to underestimate it.
- Rule of thumb: add 10-20% to the marketing budget and label it "experimental budget".
  - It gives room to experiment and be wrong, while keeping enough budget to make up the difference through other experiments.
- Once you have a spreadsheet formula, or a tool like Summit, you are just plugging in numbers.
- Talking the math through helps you understand it from first principles. How to deploy the budget is a separate topic.
