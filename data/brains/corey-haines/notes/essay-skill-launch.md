## Simple, Lovable, Complete as the gate before any launch

Corey's launch playbook starts with a readiness check, run before any launch mechanics. He says products don't market themselves, but a product that isn't ready won't market either. He names two failure modes that kill launches from opposite ends:
- **Stealth Mode:** launching too late. He calls it "procrastination in a fancy suit": polishing in private, waiting for perfect, never shipping, and nobody learns you exist.
- **"Just One More Feature":** every launch date gets pushed for one more thing, the scope creeps, and the launch never comes.

His middle path is SLC (Simple, Lovable, Complete, from Jason Cohen), the alternative to a bare MVP that is minimal but unlovable.
- **Simple:** it does one clearly defined job, done well.
- **Lovable:** people would choose to use it, not just tolerate it. If nobody would be sad to lose it, it isn't lovable yet.
- **Complete:** a whole experience for that one thing, with no glaring stubs. Complete at its chosen scope, not a teaser of a bigger promise.

Quick check before launching:
- Does it do one defined thing?
- Would a target user choose it?
- Is that one thing a whole experience?
- Still polishing past this bar? That is Stealth Mode, so ship.
- Still adding scope? That is "Just One More Feature", so cut back to SLC.

The advice is to cut scope until one thing is lovable and complete, then launch that. SLC gives a real launch now instead of a perfect launch never.

## The ORB framework: owned, rented and borrowed channels

Corey structures launch marketing across three channel types, with everything ultimately leading back to owned channels.

**Owned** (you own the channel, not the audience): email list, blog, podcast, branded community (Slack, Discord), website and product.
- They get more effective over time, with no algorithm changes or pay-to-play, and give a direct relationship with the audience.
- Start with 1-2 based on audience. If the industry lacks quality content, start a blog. If people want direct updates, focus on email. If engagement matters, build a community.
- Example, Superhuman: demand came from an invite-only waitlist and one-on-one onboarding. Every new user got a 30-minute live demo, creating exclusivity, FOMO and word of mouth. Years later the original onboarding materials still drive engagement.

**Rented** (visibility on platforms you don't control): social media (Twitter/X, LinkedIn, Instagram), app stores and marketplaces, YouTube, Reddit.
- Pick 1-2 platforms where the audience is active and use them to drive traffic to owned channels. Never make them the only strategy.
- Tactics: on Twitter/X, threads that spark conversation, linking to a newsletter. On LinkedIn, high-value posts leading to gated content or email signup. On marketplaces (Shopify, Slack), optimize the listing and drive people to the site.
- Example, Notion: grew through Twitter, YouTube and Reddit where productivity enthusiasts were, and encouraged sharing templates and workflows. Every viral post led to signups, then targeted email onboarding.
- His summary: "Rented channels give speed, not stability."

**Borrowed** (someone else's audience): guest content, collaborations (webinars, co-marketing), speaking, influencer partnerships.
- Be proactive: list industry leaders the audience follows, pitch win-win collaborations, use SparkToro or Listen Notes to find audience overlap, and set up affiliate or referral incentives.
- Example, TRMNL: sent a free e-ink display to YouTuber Snazzy Labs, not a paid sponsorship. His in-depth review got 500K+ views and drove $500K+ in sales. They also set up an affiliate program for ongoing promotion.
- Borrowed channels give instant credibility, but only work if borrowed attention is converted into owned relationships.

## The five-phase launch approach

Corey treats launching as a phased process that builds momentum, not a one-day event.

1. **Internal launch:** recruit early users one-on-one to test for free, collect feedback on usability gaps and missing features. The prototype only needs to be demo-able, not production-ready. Goal: validate core functionality with friendly users.
2. **Alpha launch:** build a landing page with an early access signup form, announce the product exists, and invite users individually. The MVP should work in production even if still evolving. Goal: first external validation and an initial waitlist.
3. **Beta launch:** work through the early access list (some free, some paid), tease the problems you solve, and recruit friends, investors and influencers to test and share. Options include a coming-soon page or waitlist, a "Beta" sticker in dashboard navigation, email invites, and an early access toggle for experimental features.
4. **Early access launch:** leak screenshots, feature GIFs and demos, gather quantitative usage data and qualitative feedback, and run user research with engaged users (incentivized with credits). Optionally run a product/market fit survey to refine messaging. Expand either by throttling invites in batches of 5-10% at a time, or by inviting everyone at once under "early access" framing.
5. **Full launch:** open self-serve signups, start charging if not already, and announce general availability everywhere. Touchpoints include customer emails, in-app popups and tours, a website banner, a "New" sticker in the dashboard nav, a blog post, social posts, and Product Hunt, BetaList and Hacker News.

## Running a Product Hunt launch

Corey calls Product Hunt powerful for reaching early adopters, "but it's not magic", and says it requires preparation.
- **Pros:** exposure to a tech-savvy early adopter audience, a credibility bump (especially Product of the Day), and potential PR coverage and backlinks.
- **Cons:** very competitive, short-lived traffic spikes, and significant pre-launch planning.

**Before launch day:**
1. Build relationships with influential supporters, content hubs and communities.
2. Optimize the listing: compelling tagline, polished visuals, short demo video.
3. Study successful launches to see what worked.
4. Engage in relevant communities and provide value before pitching.
5. Prepare the team for all-day engagement.

**Launch day:** treat it as an all-day event, respond to every comment in real time, answer questions and spark discussion, encourage the existing audience to engage, and direct traffic back to the site to capture signups.

**After:** follow up with everyone who engaged, convert Product Hunt traffic into owned relationships (email signups), and keep momentum with post-launch content.

**Case studies:**
- SavvyCal (scheduling tool): optimized the landing page and onboarding beforehand, built relationships with productivity/SaaS influencers in advance, and responded to every comment on launch day. Result: #2 Product of the Month.
- Reform (form builder): studied successful launches, crafted a clear tagline, polished visuals and a demo video, engaged communities and gave value first, treated launch as an all-day event, and directed traffic to capture signups. Result: #1 Product of the Day.

## Post-launch product marketing

Corey says the launch isn't over when the announcement goes live; now comes adoption and retention work.
- **Educate new users:** set up an automated onboarding email sequence introducing key features and use cases.
- **Reinforce the launch:** include the announcement in the weekly, biweekly or monthly roundup email to catch people who missed it.
- **Differentiate against competitors:** publish comparison pages showing why you're the obvious choice.
- **Update web pages:** add dedicated sections about the new feature or product across the site.
- **Offer a hands-on preview:** build a no-code interactive demo (with tools like Navattic) so visitors can explore before signing up.
- "It's easier to build on existing momentum than start from scratch," so every touchpoint should reinforce the launch.

## Launching continuously: how much marketing each update deserves

Corey's core philosophy is that the best companies "don't just launch once" but launch again and again. Every feature, improvement and update is a chance to capture attention. A strong launch gets the product into users' hands early, learns from real feedback, makes a splash at every stage, and builds compounding momentum.

He sizes marketing effort to the update:
- **Major** (new features, overhauls): a full multi-channel campaign: blog post, email campaign, in-app messages, social media.
- **Medium** (new integrations, UI enhancements): a targeted announcement, an email to relevant segments, an in-app banner. No full fanfare.
- **Minor** (bug fixes, small tweaks): changelog and release notes only. They signal the product is improving without dominating marketing.

Tactics:
- Space out releases instead of shipping everything at once, to maintain momentum.
- Reuse tactics from previous announcements that performed well.
- Keep using email, social and in-app messaging to highlight improvements.
- Signal active development: even small changelog updates remind customers the product is evolving, which builds retention and word of mouth because customers feel confident you'll be around.
- If you can read the product repo, generate the week's changelog, announcement drafts and pages-to-update list straight from merged PRs.

## Launch checklist and questions to ask first

**Pre-launch:** landing page with a clear value proposition, email capture or waitlist, early access list built, owned channels established (email, blog, community), optimized social profiles, borrowed opportunities identified (podcasts, influencers), Product Hunt listing prepared if used, launch assets (screenshots, demo video, GIFs), onboarding flow ready, analytics and tracking in place, and site QA done if the launch includes a new site, redesign or migration.

**Launch day:** announcement email to the list, blog post, social posts, Product Hunt listing live if used, in-app announcement for existing users, website banner, team ready to respond, and monitoring for issues and feedback.

**Post-launch:** onboarding email sequence active, follow-up with engaged prospects, roundup email includes the announcement, comparison pages published, interactive demo created, feedback gathered and acted on, and the next launch moment planned.

**Questions he asks before planning a launch:**
1. What are you launching (new product, major feature, minor update)?
2. What is the current audience size and engagement?
3. What owned channels exist (email list size, blog traffic, community)?
4. What is the timeline?
5. Have you launched before, and what worked or didn't?
6. Is Product Hunt being considered, and how prepared are you?
