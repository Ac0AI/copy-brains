## Marketing is the work of earning trust, and doing it at scale

- Corey defines marketing as "earning trust at scale." He borrows the framing from Alex Hillman: earning trust is the privilege of asking for the sale, and that is hard work.
- Trust is belief in someone's or something's reliability, truth or ability. It answers three questions: does this person know what they're talking about, will this product do what it says, will this solve my problem.
- Every purchase, signed contract or clicked link is a demonstration of trust, because business and personal relationships are continuous exchanges.
- Corey's view: trust can't be bought, only earned. It is "hard-won and easily lost", a brand's most valuable asset, and "must be protected at all costs".
- Once you have someone's trust, "you have it": they'll listen, buy, refer and come back tomorrow.
- His three framing questions: whose trust are you trying to earn, how will you earn it, and which levers will you pull.

## Why most people hate marketing: effort separates good from bad

- Corey says most people's connotations of the word "marketing" are negative.
- Part of the reason is negativity bias. He cites evolutionary and neuroscience research that we process negative stimuli more strongly, and he adds that it's easier to be a critic than a creator.
- His simpler explanation: good marketing, the kind you don't mind or even enjoy, takes hard work. Bad marketing, the kind people remember and complain about online, takes little to no work.
- He sees a direct relationship between the amount of work you put in and how effective your marketing is.
- People remember marketing they dislike and forget marketing they don't mind, so the bad examples shape the word's reputation.

## Marketing is sales, asynchronously and at scale

- Corey's distinction: "Marketing is sales, but asynchronously and at scale."
  - Sales is 1:1.
  - Marketing is 1:many.
- Without leverage, effort and impact are linear: one connection, one relationship, one sale at a time.
- With leverage, the relationship becomes nonlinear: many connections, relationships and sales at once.
- He points to the root of the word "leverage": levers are force multipliers.

## Internet leverage: media creation and distribution are both democratized

- Corey calls this "an age of infinite leverage."
- Media creation is democratized. Anyone can start a Twitter account, blog, podcast or YouTube channel and reach people worldwide instantly.
- Media distribution is democratized too:
  - Anyone can buy ads.
  - Anyone's content can rank on the first page of Google.
  - Anyone's tweet can go viral.
- The practical takeaway is that the levers for earning trust at scale are open to any founder, so the open question is which ones you commit to pulling.

## Compounding: small consistent actions produce outsized results

- Leverage brings compounding, which lets small, seemingly insignificant actions produce huge rewards.
- Example on measuring conversion: improving a website conversion rate from 1% to 3% is "not a 2% improvement, it's a 300% improvement."
- Example on content: write an article every week and traffic may start small, but over time "a rising tide lifts all boats" and the accumulated traffic becomes remarkable.
- He quotes Seth Godin: "We can definitely spend time worrying about/building the tsunami, but it's the drip, drip, drip that will change everything in the long run."
- His formula: small choices + consistency + time = significant results.

## "Gradually, then suddenly": the payoff curve of trust-building

- Corey cites the line from Hemingway's *The Sun Also Rises* on going bankrupt: "Two ways... Gradually, then suddenly." He says it applies to both success and failure.
- For marketing, it describes what happens when you do the hard work of earning trust at scale every day. Then one day you look up and sales are flooding in, your email open rate is up, and you may be "kinda-internet-famous."
- The implication is to expect a slow, unremarkable start and to keep going, since the results arrive late and all at once.
