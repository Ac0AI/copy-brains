## The ORB Framework: owned, rented and borrowed audiences

Corey (essay published 5/5/21) says marketing strategies and tactics fall into three categories, based on who controls the line of communication to an audience:
- **Owned:** a line you own.
- **Rented:** a platform you use but don't control.
- **Borrowed:** someone else's audience.

He says all three are viable and each has a place in the overall plan. He calls the combination a "handy framework to create a winning marketing plan" for any business or campaign. It works for a first strategy for a new app, or for deciding where to put $1 million of extra budget.

How the framework is drawn:
- It is concentric circles showing depth with the audience. The core is owned, the closest and most private. Going outward reaches more people but with less depth and connection.
- Lay out all marketing experiments and channels, and have everything point back to owned platforms.
- Borrowing an audience is "all for naught" if you can't lead those people back to a rented or owned platform.
- Rented platforms in particular should send people back to an owned platform, to cut the risk of lost reach or access.
- The finished map shows which owned platforms to invest in, where to engage people to grow them, and how to grow the audience by borrowing.
- Corey says it doesn't need to be complex: "Don't worry if it looks fairly simplistic."

## Owned platforms: what they are and how to choose

Corey's point: you don't own your audience, you own your platform. "You own the _line of communication_."

- Owned platforms give more control, more reach and more predictability.
- They get more effective over time, while borrowed and rented platforms usually get less effective.
- He says ask any successful creator or brand for their best channel and they'll probably name an owned one: blog, email, community, podcast or text message list.
- Video is an emerging owned platform. Wistia Channels lets you turn your website into a video destination, "like creating your own Netflix".

How to pick:
- Find the intersection of what you love doing and what your audience wants.
- You don't need a blog, newsletter, community, podcast and text list all at once. Start with the one or two that best match what you can deliver and what the audience wants.
- If your industry lacks quality blog content, blog. If you can make your audience's next favorite podcast, podcast.

Why he favors them:
- Owned platforms aren't sexy, but they've been around forever, and "the longer something has been around, the more likely it is to keep sticking around."
- **Blogs:** companies and people will keep building websites with valuable information.
- **Email:** a fundamental identity and communication asset for everyone online.
- **Community:** "just getting started" and the ultimate owned platform. You set the design, membership, what is shared and how members talk. It's an intimate medium for members to connect with each other and with the managers.
- **Podcasts:** built on RSS. Downloads and subscriber numbers are ambiguous, but you still have a direct line to the audience.
- **Texting:** the most intimate channel on the list. It's increasingly used for updates, group or 1:1 chat and content subscriptions.

## Rented platforms: tradeoffs and how many to commit to

Corey defines rented platforms as ones where you can call the account yours, but "everything is subject to change". Like a landlord, the platform controls the end user's whole experience: algorithms, monetization, reach, discovery and profiles.

Rented platforms he lists:
- Social: Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, LinkedIn, Snapchat, YouTube, TikTok, Quora, Reddit, Pinterest, Medium.
- App stores: Apple App Store and Google Play.
- Browser extensions: Chrome, Safari, Firefox.
- SaaS plugin platforms: Shopify, Salesforce, HubSpot, Intercom, Figma, Intuit.
- Messenger apps: WhatsApp, WeChat, Messenger, Telegram.

Downsides:
- **Reduced reach:** Facebook's organic reach fell from 16% to 2% between 2012 and 2016.
- **Ambiguous algorithms:** they change constantly. When Facebook launched video support, video was favored in the feed.
- **Censorship:** rules apply to categories such as NSFW and cannabis content.
- **Pay-to-play:** reaching the right people means paying for ads, such as per click.
- **Ephemeral content:** tweets and posts carry far less weight than a newsletter or a community comment.

Upsides:
- **Discoverability:** TikTok's For You Page, YouTube as the second largest search engine with a recommendation sidebar, and App Store ranking (ASO) based on installs, ratings and growth trends.
- **Shareability:** a retweet from a large account puts you in front of a sizable part of its audience.
- **Where people already consume content:** 4 billion people use social media (~51% of the global population), averaging ~2.5 hours a day.

Common mistakes:
- Putting more emphasis on building a following on a rented platform than on an owned one (Twitter vs a newsletter).
- Spreading thin trying to "be everywhere".

His advice:
- Meet your audience where they already are, and commit to a few platforms, or even just one.
- Corey calls himself a Twitter power-user. Others are YouTube-first or Instagram-first.
- Pick a platform your audience uses and perhaps expects you to be on. His oversimplified examples: Twitter for 25-43 year-old tech bros, and not LinkedIn for stay-at-home moms.

## Borrowed platforms: why borrowing an audience kickstarts growth

Corey says publishing on owned and rented platforms with no existing audience is "like speaking into the void". It's a chicken-or-egg problem, because you need people to share your content and nobody is there. Borrowing other people's audiences is "the key to kickstarting your growth" on owned and rented platforms.

What it looks like:
- Guest posting on a popular blog.
- YouTube collabs.
- Instagram shoutouts.
- Speaking at conferences.
- In general, using another person's or brand's owned platform to reach their audience.

Why it works:
- They have already attracted the right people, earned trust and kept attention. You show up and provide something valuable.
- It can give an instant boost, "a step-change".
- His examples: writing a "takeover" for a newsletter with 50,000+ subscribers, or collabing with a YouTuber with 1M+ subscribers, instead of waiting for your own channel to grow.
- As owned and rented platforms get more competitive, borrowing becomes "increasingly paramount".

Be active, not passive:
- He calls it a drastically overlooked strategy. Most people wait for others to reach out, which only works if you're already notable.
- "Doing anything _passively_ isn't much of a strategy. Progress takes intention."
- You have to make it happen.

## How to build a borrowed-audience list and win-win collaborations

Corey sums up the borrowing strategy in two steps:
1. Make a list of everyone and everything that has the attention of your intended audience.
2. Work out a win-win collaboration as a guest, partner, participant, source or sponsor.

Step 1: ask your audience, by short survey or call:
- Which social channels are you active on?
- Who do you pay attention to and learn from?
- What blogs, podcasts or newsletters do you subscribe to?
- What publications, magazines or news outlets do you subscribe to?
- Where do you find new content to consume?

He says: "If you don't walk away with a list of 50+ names, I'd be shocked."

Then add your own audience research. Tools he names to start finding borrowed platforms:
- SparkToro
- Audiense
- Listen Notes
- Agent (growthtools)
- Ahrefs

Borrowed platforms depend entirely on the audience you want to reach, so there is no plug-and-play list. You build it from scratch, and he says it's "more art than science".

## Putting the ORB Framework into a marketing plan

Corey's summary is that strategy comes down to three components:
- **Owned (the priority):** where you have a direct relationship with the audience.
- **Rented:** offers discoverability and interaction, but carries platform risk.
- **Borrowed:** the under-utilized strategy for growing owned and rented platforms through win-win collaborations with people who have already built an audience of the people you want to reach.

His order of operations:
1. Choose one or two owned platforms that match your strengths and audience demand.
2. Commit to one or a few rented platforms where your audience already is, and use them to drive people to your owned platforms.
3. Actively borrow audiences to grow both, always pointing back toward owned.

He says the framework helps you decide where to invest time and money, and helps you build a cohesive strategy. "The rest is details."
