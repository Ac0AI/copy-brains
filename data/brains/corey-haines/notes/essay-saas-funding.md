## Raising money: who you take it from matters more than whether you can

- Corey says capital is now abundant, so the real questions are who you take funding from and why.
- Once money is easy to get, he argues the differentiators are (1) terms and (2) "value add" perks.
- He lists four kinds of value add:
  - **Advice:** feedback, practical guidance, and learnings from other portfolio companies.
  - **Network:** connections to hires, partners, advisors, and other investors.
  - **Infrastructure:** discounts, credits, content assets, and playbooks.
  - **Exposure:** intros to customers, featured placements, free marketing, and shoutouts.
- Being a marketer at heart, he finds exposure the most interesting. A business's primary goal is to grow revenue, so he asks why you wouldn't pick investors who can help you acquire customers, not just write a check.
- He says to think of investors as "partners" rather than check-writers.
- Some companies (consumer, marketplace, hardware) need far more capital than others, and not every startup needs traditional VC.
- His rule of thumb: "More ownership = better. Capital efficiency should always be the goal."

## Non-VC options for bootstrapped SaaS: Calm Company Fund and TinySeed

**Calm Company Fund** (formerly Earnest Capital, run by Tyler Tringas)
- Tyler built and sold Storemapper, then worked with the bootstrapped SaaS community to create a Shared Earnings Agreement (SEAL).
- It substitutes for equity-like instruments (SAFE, convertible note, equity). It is not debt, has no fixed repayment schedule, and needs no personal guarantee.
- A "Shared Earnings" percentage is agreed. Once a Shared Earnings Cap (2-5x the initial investment) is met, payments stop and what remains is a residual Equity Basis.
- In plain English, you "buy back" equity from the fund with capped payments from profits.
- Corey's view: you get early-stage capital without giving up a large amount of equity long-term. If you eventually exit through a sale, buying back equity with profits puts more money in your pocket.
- He suggests it if you're between $2-50k MRR.
- Terms vary and are negotiable, but he cites $30-200K for 5-15% equity as a fairly standard range.
- It has an active mentor and advisor community, and buys secondary if you have a large, profitable business and want to take some chips off the table.

**TinySeed** (remote accelerator for early-stage SaaS, run by Rob Walling and Einar Vollset)
- It invests $120,000 for the first founder and up to $60,000 per additional founder.
- It takes a permanent 10%-12% equity stake, with no board seat and no voting rights.
- TinySeed gets paid when founders get paid, through an exit or dividends.
- It has a large mentor and advisor network. Rob hosts the Startups For The Rest Of Us podcast, so Corey expects some exposure there.

## Non-VC options: crowdfunding, angel vehicles, and revenue-based financing

**WeFunder and Republic (equity crowdfunding)**
- You can raise from friends, customers, partners, and fans, accredited or not.
- Corey's argument is that you build an army of investors who also act as advocates and evangelists.
  - Customers who invest are far more likely to stick around.
  - Partners who invest are more incentivized to do co-marketing and integrations.
- After recent regulation changes, startups can raise up to $5M.
- He points to Gumroad and Customer.io as case studies.
- Another advantage: the founder sets the terms and the valuation, based on their own goals and benchmarks, instead of a VC or institution setting it.

**AngelList Roll Up Vehicles (RUVs) and Syndicates**
- With an RUV, you get a single link where investors commit and send funds online, and AngelList handles the rest.
- Syndicates let fund managers raise deal by deal. Corey says this works best if you have a lead angel who can recruit and raise an angel round for you.
- He calls an angel round a great first round if you're not keen on Calm Fund or TinySeed, and a great second round if you've already taken money from them.

**Revenue-based financing (Founderpath, Capchase)**
- You get capital upfront in exchange for future MRR from customers. This is non-dilutive growth capital you can use instead of raising a Series A or B.
- Pipe works more like a marketplace that securitizes customer contracts. Capchase works more like a true lender and has specialized offerings for large expenses.
- Corey's take: the main benefit is not giving up more equity to grow, and "when used properly, it's an amazing tool for growth."

## VC and studio options: High Alpha, Collab Capital, accelerators

**High Alpha Studio**
- High Alpha is mainly a VC firm with a Studio arm that conceives, launches, and scales new SaaS businesses with founders.
- Corey says it has an exceptional team and track record.
- Because it acts essentially as a co-founder, it takes considerable equity but also provides considerable capital, personal help, and resources.

**Collab Capital**
- It invests in early-stage Black-owned companies building for the future of work, learning, and care.
- Like Calm Fund, it created its own financing vehicle, a SPACE agreement (Shared Profits and Collaborative Endorsement).
- First checks run $500-750K, with follow-ons up to $2M.
- If the business doesn't warrant a follow-on but can become sustainably profitable, the fund takes dividends instead.
- For every multiple on investment returned, the fund's equity ownership drops 1 percentage point, down to an agreed floor over an agreed period.
- Corey loves this structure because founders can take a big swing early without having to shut down or force an exit if the company isn't the next billion-dollar unicorn.

**Other names he respects**
- Mainstay accelerators: Y Combinator and Hyper.
- B2B SaaS venture investors: Craft Ventures and OpenView Ventures.
- Mercury Raise, which he's heard great things about for getting intros during fundraising.

## Why Corey prefers solo capitalists to traditional VC firms

- He names a long list of solo capitalists: Julian, Sahil, Pomp, Packy, Shaan, Andrew, Ryan, Harry, Turner, Li, Nikhail, Lenny, Josh, Elad, and Lachy.
- What they share is "big followings and even bigger networks," which is the exposure value-add applied to fundraising.
- He matches investors to the sector:
  - Crypto or DeFi: Pomp, Packy, and Shaan on the cap table.
  - Enterprise SaaS: Harry, Turner, Elad, and Lenny.
- He gives examples of their reach: Shaan has a top 10 business podcast, Lenny has the #1 business Substack, and Sahil has 200K+ Twitter followers. His claim: "they can move the needle for your business."

## Syndicates and how Corey helps founders raise

- Popular syndicates can be a good option, especially at seed stage. He points to Seed Checks and The SaaS Syndicate.
- Corey says he is a scout for a few of these funds, a friend of several, and a fan of them all.
- Before a founder contacts any of them, he offers to:
  1. Review the pitch deck, memo, thesis, or MVP.
  2. Make a personal connection to any of them and put the founder at the top of the queue.
  3. Help the founder decide which funding option best fits their business.
