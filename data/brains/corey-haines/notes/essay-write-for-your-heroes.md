## Writing for heroes: the core rule

Corey's principle is to write as if your marketing heroes were reading every post. In 2018 he was about to send his curated newsletter, *The TL;DR On SaaS Marketing*, when Ann Handley replied to his tweet about it. He felt fear ("What if she doesn't like the commentary on her quote and blog post? What if she unsubscribes?") and then insecurity ("Should I beef up my commentary... to make it sound like the most wonderful piece of content ever written?").

He then realized the insecurity was the real problem. In his words: "Shouldn't I always hold myself to a high enough standard that I would be proud of any of my marketing heroes to read my writing at any time?" He reviewed the newsletter, changed nothing, and kept the lesson: "Write as if your heroes were reading."

- The test is whether you would be proud for a hero to read the piece right now, not whether you would polish it once you knew they were watching.
- It took a hero's reply to make him question his standards, and he concluded he should have been writing for them all along.

## Why writing drifts when you write alone

Corey describes the risk of working behind a screen for 8+ hours a day with "no faces, minimal interaction, and almost zero feedback from your readers."

- It is easy to forget that real people read your work.
- If you are not careful, "your writing will become completely detached from the very audience you serve."
- Corey was writing regularly for the Cordial blog, his weekly newsletter *The TL;DR On SaaS Marketing*, and his own blog, so the drift risk applied across several outlets at once.

## Three audiences and the quality each produces

Corey ranks the audience you imagine by the quality it yields:

- **Writing for no one:** you write haphazardly.
- **Writing for the internet** (its "entire and vast expanse"): you write lazily.
- **Writing for your heroes:** you write your best work, "the work you wouldn't have written otherwise. The work you'll be proud of."

A specific, named reader gives you a standard to hold. A vague crowd or no one gives you none.

## Heroes as the people you learned from

Corey's heroes are the marketers he spent two years reading and learning from "religiously": Seth Godin, Ann Handley, Rand Fishkin, Jay Baer, Gary Vee, Joe Pulizzi, Nir Eyal, Malcolm Gladwell, Ryan Holiday, Sean Ellis, David Ogilvy, Robert Cialdini, Marc Benioff, David Cancel, Dave Gerhardt, Brian Balfour, Jeff Bullas, Louis Grenier, Noah Kagan, Claire Suellentrop, Gia Laudi, Benji Hyam, and many others.

- He wants them to be proud that his writing is "a byproduct of all that I've learned from them."
- The exercise: name your heroes, then ask what you would think of your work if you knew they were reading it. He ends the post by asking readers exactly that.
