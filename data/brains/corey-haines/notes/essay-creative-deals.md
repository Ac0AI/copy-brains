## Equity-for-services and fee-discount structures

Corey's core stance: "Everything is negotiable." He treats creative deal structures as some of the most rewarding deals, and learned them from experience plus studying other people's deals. His agency, Conversion Factory, is not cheap (he frames it as three people for the price of one), so for early-stage startups without the cash he offers "equity based partnerships": discounted fees in exchange for future upside. Options he presented to a startup doing about $350k ARR:

- **Cash + equity:** $4.5k/mo cash plus $4.5k/mo worth of equity at an agreed valuation (e.g. 4x ARR). At a ~$1.5M valuation that is ~0.3% per month, ~3.6% over 12 months, worth $180k at a $5M exit valuation.
- **Cash + delayed revenue share:** $3k/mo cash plus 15% of gross revenue, paid for as many months as the agency worked, starting 12 months after the engagement begins. Example: work July '24 to June '25, July '25 MRR of $60,000 means $9,000/mo for 12 months, ~$108k total, depending on MRR growth.
- **Cash + capped profit share:** $4k/mo cash plus 25% of profit, capped at 3x the amount discounted. Discounting $5k/mo for 12 months is $60k, so the profit share caps at $180k.

A friend running a product studio that builds MVPs uses a similar model. Normal price is $2,500/week; the equity option is $1,250/week plus 1% equity per week. After 8 to 12 weeks the founder has a working app for $10-15k and 8-12% equity.

## Revenue share instead of equity for side projects and contributors

- Corey wanted a friend to work on a side project that would likely never earn revenue itself but would drive revenue for its parent business. He couldn't justify the cash to pay fairly.
- He avoided offering parent-company equity because the friend would only be rewarded with a substantial exit.
- He offered a fixed percentage of gross revenue share instead, so the friend is guaranteed some reward.
- He added an option to buy out the revenue share at a multiple of expected revenues, so it doesn't become a liability for a future buyer.
- Milestone-based upside is a structure he has "always been fond of". Example: a CEO comes in at $1M annual revenue on salary plus 10% of profits, and the profit share ratchets up 5% for each additional $1M in revenue, reaching 25% at $4M ARR. "There are no rules": it depends on what you think is fair and what incentives you want to create.
- Steve Ballmer's pay is his example: $50,000 salary plus "10% of all extra profits", later converted to 8% equity when Microsoft became a corporation. It became worth billions.

## Financing a buyout without touching personal cash

- A cofounder of a software business wanted to step back. Corey agreed to buy him out at a fair valuation. The business made a small amount of revenue, but the equity was still a meaningful sum.
- Instead of using savings, a loan, or an investor, he worked with his accountant on a structure: Conversion Factory loaned the money to the software entity, which used it to buy out the cofounder and repays the agency over time.
- He calls this "one perk of owning a cash generating machine like an agency".
- A friend made a handshake deal to buy a house, had it appraised, spent months and $100k+ renovating before actually buying, then opened escrow. The reappraisal let him pull out new equity to pay for the renovations: a free makeover and a more valuable home.
- The same friend used a HELOC against home equity to buy a business. The business comfortably covers the repayments with profit left over.

## Buying a business: seller financing and Boopos

- Corey calls seller-side financing "one of the best (if not _the_ best) ways to buy a business."
- Ryan Kulp bought the SaaS Fomo this way: a deposit, monthly installments based on revenue, and a claw-back clause returning the business to the seller if payments fail.
- The key advantage: the business's own revenue pays the seller. Sometimes no deposit is needed. In theory you could buy a business by putting it on autopilot until the installments are paid off.
- Traditional lenders mostly don't know how to underwrite software businesses. Debt that is siloed from the business's unit economics has terms that don't fit its profit, forcing the buyer to race to grow profit to service it.
- He likes Boopos because it vets, underwrites, and finances up to 80% of the purchase price, with terms tailored to the business, non-dilutive and no personal guarantee.

## Creative sale, spin-out and fundraising structures he highlights

- **Retained equity after a sale:** Nick Huber bought controlling interest in Shepherd (now Somewhere) for $29.7M, with the business valued at $50M+. Founder Marshall kept a small equity stake, so he still gets dividends and future valuation upside. Corey calls it "a very untraditional 'sale' that resulted in a win-win."
- **Spin-out with a step-up:** Jason Fried spun Know Your Company (now Canopy) out of Basecamp. An operating partner gets 50%, switching to 75% partner and 25% Basecamp once the business hits $1M cumulative revenue.
- **Calm Fund's Shared Earnings Agreement:** invests a low six-figure amount for a low double-digit equity stake. It is repaid through shared earnings up to a cap (e.g. 2-5x), and the founder can pay down Calm's stake to a minimum. This gives the founder optionality to optimize for profitability, growth, or ownership at a sale.
- **SparkToro's round:** about $1.3M raised, with 100% of the original capital returned to investors before profits are distributed; after that, profits go pro rata to all stakeholders.
- **Cash-distribution clause in an angel round Corey invested in:** investors can compel distributions if business cash exceeds $30,000 and monthly operating expenses are below $10,000, or if cash exceeds $10,000 and monthly operating expenses are no more than 30% of available cash. In plain English: plenty of cash and low costs means profits get distributed.

## Perks as deal terms and how to judge fairness

- Examples of non-cash terms: Papa John's founder negotiated lifetime free pizza when selling control; Dollar Shave Club's Michael Dubin got a lifetime supply of grooming products in the Unilever deal; Yelp's Jeremy Stoppelman negotiated lifetime free use of premium features.
- Corey's fairness test: "Like any deal, it all comes down to risk vs reward." Fair is what both sides agree is an acceptable amount of both risk and reward.
- If a deal doesn't feel fair, he says to work out why. Those reasons are clues for how to renegotiate creatively.
- He closes with Charlie Munger: "Show me the incentive, and I will show you the outcome."
