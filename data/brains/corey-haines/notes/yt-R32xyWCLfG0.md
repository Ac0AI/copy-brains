## Programmatic SEO from content you already own

Corey ran programmatic SEO for Swipewell (his SaaS Chrome extension for one-click screenshots of landing pages, emails and ads) by starting from content rather than keywords.
- In July he spent a month swiping marketing samples into his own Swipewell account, to be "our biggest power user". The result was 200+ collections and over 6,000 items: vintage ads, SaaS competitor comparison pages, onboarding emails, landing pages and more.
- He then realized the library could be published as pages. His co-founder Connor confirmed this was easy because the marketing site is built in Next.js inside the app itself.
- He mapped the collections to keyword variations, using head terms (landing pages, ads, emails, popups) crossed with variants (SaaS, DTC, marketing agency). URLs follow a head/variant pattern such as landing-pages/saas, ads/saas and emails/saas, all under a "marketing examples library".
- Over 200 pages were generated at once from structured data, not written by hand. He had to retitle, reformat and reorganize categories first.
- A content-first start avoids the common failure of programmatic SEO, which is thin, undifferentiated pages built on top of a keyword opportunity.

## Technical foundations and domain authority for programmatic SEO

Corey says on-page and technical basics must be tight before programmatic pages can rank.
- After launch Swipewell had to fix many issues: a solid sitemap, submitting the XML file multiple times, requesting indexing multiple times, and removing 404s.
- Swipewell's domain rating was about 10 at launch, which made it a deliberate experiment in how far a low-authority site could get. It later rose to roughly 35 after active link-building campaigns.
- He says a low domain rating caps results unless competition is nonexistent and the content is garbage. "SaaS landing pages" is a competitive keyword where others already curate well, so authority and backlinks matter more.
- Rankings were "slowly creeping up" at the time of the interview.

## Which programmatic SEO playbooks fit which companies

Corey argues Zapier's integration pages are the worst example to copy for B2B SaaS. That pattern comes from how Zapier's product works, and nobody searches for integrations with your product.
- Companies like Zillow and Airbnb, and product curation and review sites, have used programmatic SEO for ages.
- He lists categories that transfer to many companies:
  - **Templates**: form builders, website builders, Canva-style design tools.
  - **Conversions**: time zone conversions (an experiment he ran with SavvyCal), Wise's currency conversions, Duolingo-style language conversions.
  - **Comparisons**: competitor pages built from reusable strengths and weaknesses modules, mixed into "us vs. them" or "competitor 1 vs. 2 vs. 3" pages.
  - **Curation**: the playbook Swipewell follows. Pick a content type (ads, landing pages, screenshots, pitch decks, videos), then split and organize it for search.

## Building programmatic pages without duplicate content

Corey designs the page first, then splits it into reusable and unique parts.
- Wireframe a page that fully serves the reader and hits on-page SEO needs, then mark which blocks repeat across pages and which are unique.
- Add customizable blocks too. He cites the Drift vs. Intercom page, which opens with a custom blurb of the company's approach ("we think Intercom is great, but if you're this type of person..."). The checklist-style comparison below it is reusable across competitors.
- Webflow's CMS lets you turn blocks on or off per page with a checkbox and relate multiple collections together (for example tags and filters), so pages are customized without manual edits.
- He uses generative AI mainly to rewrite and vary short blurbs per page, not to originate the content.

## Generative AI and the future of programmatic vs. editorial SEO

- Corey expects AI to dilute everything and raise the bar, so genuinely unique work stands out more.
- He thinks editorial thought-leadership content is more at risk from bland AI copycats than programmatic content. Programmatic content is "cut and dry and factual", based on data you already have, so there is no point using AI to generate it.
- The differentiator for programmatic pages is the execution and customization of the content.

## Market like a media company, don't become one

Corey was careful to phrase it as "marketing like a media company, not make a media company". The common mistake is hiring journalists and YouTubers and spinning up a separate brand.
- **Content is the product.** A media company sells ad slots, but its content has to be good enough to earn eyeballs. He calls content "the cornerstone of every marketing strategy", covering entertainment, education, brand voice and look. Sales is the one-to-one representation of the company; marketing is the one-to-many representation.
- **People are the core.** Media companies are built on personalities. Outsourcing everything to freelance writers misses the point, which is who the content comes from, to build authority and trust and become a destination.
- His examples are HubSpot acquiring The Hustle, Wistia, ProfitWell, Coinbase, and Drift. Drift's Seeking Wisdom podcast began as an outlet for David Cancel, who had no time to write blog posts. It elevated Dave Gerhardt as a marketing thought leader, and Maggie Crawley, a product manager turned host, reached the technical audience.

## Running a media-style content team: editor, stages, retention

- **Editor role.** To avoid message chaos with several voices, route content through one editor who keeps brand voice and content guidelines. This could be the director of marketing or a content marketing manager. Corey has had to step into this role himself.
- **Crawl, walk, run.** Crawl: one person as the face of content, plus one main outlet. Walk: one to three people across one to three channels. Run: many people, which is when the editor role becomes essential. Most teams lack the resources even to walk.
- **If a personality leaves**, the value mostly leaves too. Retention is the answer: ask why they want to leave and whether you can offer something that keeps them incentivized. Build a pipeline of "next people in line" where possible. His example is The Hustle crew (Steph Smith, Trung Phan, Alex Garcia), who later moved on.
- **Best option:** build up someone from the founding or senior leadership team (founder, CEO, CTO, VP). They are less likely to leave and hold the implicit point of view that new hires lack.
- A CEO who gets content, has opinions and adds their own "special magic dust" (not just a ghostwritten byline) lifts the whole program.

## Why Corey builds so many projects, and how he manages his time

- His long-term goal since about age 19 is to start and run a growing, profitable SaaS company that employs people. Swipe Files, the newsletter, courses, book and side projects are "reps" and outputs of learning toward that.
- He is an Enneagram 3 (achiever) with some 1. The weakness is that when he is not completing things he can burn out, procrastinate and juggle too many projects, so he tries to enjoy the process, not just the finish.
- He works in deep-work stretches with minimal meetings, email and Slack. He batches meetings on Wednesdays, keeps Fridays free, and writes a weekly list of high-leverage projects on paper, pulled from a Notion master board using the Eisenhower matrix.
- Day to day he works on whatever he has the most energy for first. He finds zero-to-one work easy and energizing, and admin and operational minutiae the hardest.
- He moved from consulting (including SavvyCal, working with founder Derek) back to a full-time head of marketing job, because client admin and tracking hours took more time than a regular job.

## Swipe Files vs. Swipewell, and the book

- Swipe Files started as a weekly marketing sample teardown and became his SaaS marketing newsletter and membership. It has 130+ editions, three courses, sponsorship and affiliate revenue, and a job board, and it is largely on autopilot after about three years.
- Swipewell has been in development for just over a year. The first six months were product development; the latest six focus on getting users. Tactics include content, programmatic SEO, affiliates, partnerships, podcasts and cross-promotion with Swipe Files.
- Swipewell's main target audience is agency owners and freelance copywriters, the power users of inspiration. This is broader than Swipe Files' SaaS marketing audience, so the synergy is partial.
- His book, "Founding Marketing", is a practical guide to early SaaS growth (foundingmarketing.com), about 80% drafted at the time.
