## Marketing like a media company: what it means and where it sits on a spectrum
- Corey spent a few months researching the idea, studying companies that exemplify it and pulling out patterns. The result was a course released in January 2022 as an added perk of his Swipe Files membership. The motivation was that people like Dave Gerhardt kept saying the best marketing looks like building a media company, and he wanted to know what that means tactically.
- His definition: a media company is a content outlet people come to for the content, and it earns their trust so they keep coming back. For a product or service company, content is a means to an end. It earns trust and educates so people later buy.
- He says there is no right or wrong answer and it is a spectrum. At one extreme, the media brand is the front and the product is sold quietly on the back end. At the other, a normal product company simply markets a bit more like a media company than a traditional marketing function would.
- Separate the media brand from the main product, but keep the two connected. If they are completely unrelated, audiences notice the constant pitching and think "why are they always sending me over to this product."

## Stop arbitraging platforms and become the platform
- In early digital marketing there were only a few dominant forces, such as Google, Yahoo, TechCrunch and Hacker News. Marketing meant piggybacking on them through pay-per-click, PR or banner ads, then converting a small percentage right away. He calls it direct response through arbitrage.
- Today content is cheap to create. There are millions of sites, people with hundreds of thousands of followers, newsletters, and communities like Product Hunt, Circle and Slack groups. Arbitrage plays are competitive and expensive. Even when they work, they work briefly, and you must rinse and repeat on the next opportunity.
- His fix is to flip the model: "how can I become the platform and then arbitrage it myself." Produce the content, don't just buy an ad on someone else's. Use the content to earn trust and attention, then sell the product or service directly or indirectly.

## Funnel everything to owned distribution
- Platforms bring censorship, algorithm changes and rule changes, and distribution can vanish overnight. His line: "you don't want to build your castle on a big plot of sand."
- He lists the only channels you fully own: a newsletter, an RSS feed, a personal website, a community, and a text message list.
- Social profiles such as Twitter are a means to an end, which is to drive people back to an owned channel like a newsletter or community.
- Email is "sort of like this indestructible distribution channel." A send always gets delivered to the inbox, even if it's later put in spam or promotions.
- He names five formats a media play can take: websites, newsletters, podcasts (which can be video shows), communities, and text message lists.

## Examples he cites of media as a growth asset
- **HubSpot and The Hustle:** HubSpot bought the newsletter, which had a couple million subscribers. That gave it direct access to a new audience it wasn't reaching. HubSpot later launched a Hustle Daily podcast. Corey relays that Kieran, the VP of marketing who championed the deal, said it was a simple build-versus-buy decision: could they build it cheaply and quickly enough, or buy it?
- **Drift:** His first introduction to Drift was the Seeking Wisdom podcast, which was about leadership and career growth, not chatbots or marketing automation. He calls this content that is inherently valuable on its own.
- **Coinbase:** It built its own news arm and covers all of crypto, including competitors, as objectively as it can. It became the publication rather than seeking placements in others.
- **Other acquisitions:** Semrush bought Backlinko from Brian Dean (SEO trust plus his content). Pendo bought a large marketing community, which gives distribution into a private place plus built-in trust. Sales Hacker was bought by Outreach, and Zapier bought MakerPad. Stripe has Indie Hackers, and he also points to MailChimp and a16z as doing this well.
- His takeaway: it is a defensible moat, because a media property is hard to replicate or compete with.

## People are the common thread: build media personalities, including employees
- After months of study, the common thread between HubSpot, Coinbase and Stripe and the solo creators was the people behind the brand. Big media brands usually have a few trusted personalities, and the host becomes part of the show.
- Founder-led marketing makes the founder a thought leader worth following. He says that is going mainstream with the build-in-public movement. He cites Bolt's founder passing 100,000 followers on Twitter.
- He thinks the larger opportunity in the next couple of years is in-house media personalities among employees, contractors, agencies and partners.
- Drift example: much of its growth was catapulted by Dave Gerhardt posting videos on LinkedIn, which helped Seeking Wisdom and raised CEO David Cancel's profile. Then podcast hosts and regular employees began making LinkedIn videos too. In his words, "you're building an army of marketers internally within the company even if their job title doesn't include marketing at all."

## Making it work inside a company
- It has to be driven from the top. If the CEO or founder isn't bought in, or doesn't know what they want, and just tells a hire to "turn us into a media brand," it won't work.
- It needs buy-in at the cultural level: everyone knows the idea and mission and will try whatever it takes.
- The people being built into personalities must also be bought in. Many don't want to be micro-famous or fear putting themselves out there. Companies also fear investing in someone who then leaves.
- He calls it a new playbook, and says marketers who podcast, write, teach and speak to represent a company are "a dime a dozen" to find, so more need to be cultivated.

## How Corey applies it to Swipe Files
- The Swipe Files homepage has no product or membership on it. It only asks people to sign up for his newsletter, content that is valuable on its own. He then moves subscribers into the membership.
- He shifted the membership's pitch from community-driven to content-driven. Community quality is partly out of an organizer's control, and he wants a high signal-to-noise ratio rather than a noisy community. The emphasis is now on courses and monthly live office hours. The community handles questions, support, accountability and nuances the courses don't cover.
- His courses are bundled into one membership. He rates the production quality "seven or eight out of 10."
- Sales are a slow evergreen drip rather than a launch spike. They are driven by his tweets, newsletter signups, and calls to action in an evergreen ConvertKit sequence. His job is to keep the right inputs going: the calls to action and a steady cadence of tweets that mention the membership and course.
- He started a marketing job board, Hey Marketers, in 2019 and sold a majority stake to a job-board operator. He bought it back in January 2020, rebranded it as the Swipe Files job board, and added a matchmaking service. Marketers and companies already asked him for referrals, and this lets him run introductions from a list instead of ad hoc through his inbox.
