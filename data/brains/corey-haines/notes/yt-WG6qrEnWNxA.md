## Product marketing is where early-stage growth starts

- Corey argues most indie hackers struggle with product marketing because they never built around a specific problem. The product started as a cool idea or a tech experiment.
- Without a named problem, there is no value proposition to write ("this product helps you solve X so you unlock Y").
- Product marketing is narrative, copywriting and positioning. The goal is to turn the product from a "vitamin" (nice to have) into a "painkiller" (a "bleeding neck" problem, an urgent need that makes buying a no-brainer).
- He says it is a learnable skill. Developers can train it like coding: "get inside the head and walk in the shoes of your customers", and build it as a habit.
- His first move with early-stage SaaS companies on a small budget is product marketing: brand, copywriting, positioning, narrative, how features are talked about. Without it, "nothing else really matters" because traffic sent to the site won't convert.

## Why marketing feels harder than code, and how to keep going

- Corey says code is a closed loop: input gives the expected output. Marketing has no one-to-one relationship. One blog post might bring 10 customers and the next zero, and often you can't tell why.
- After six years as a first marketing hire at multiple startups, he says nothing is guaranteed even when you think the playbook is nailed down.
- The answer is to keep shipping and keep marketing even when it doesn't work. It is like working on a non-functional app for a year until it works, and the earlier work then pays off.
- Gray-area results are the hardest part. A post gets 100 views and no customers, so do you repeat it? The hard part is deciding what to double down on, not whether to keep trying.
- His programming analogy: an error is a result, just not the one you wanted. A blog post that "threw an error" didn't fail; keep debugging and fiddling.

## Swipe files: don't start from a blank canvas

- A swipe file is a library of examples used for inspiration in your own work. He cites Page Flows (by Rami), which collects app screens like paywalls and expired-trial screens, so you can see how Spotify or Calm does it.
- The same idea applies to marketing assets: landing pages, competitor comparison pages, welcome emails, pricing modules.
- Working derivatively from others' work gives structure and avoids the blank-screen dread every artist or writer feels.
- He often tells indie hackers about tactics their competitors already use that they hadn't considered: "all of your competitors are doing that", and that is how those competitors grew. Studying what others do is a shortcut.

## When to hire your first marketer

Corey describes two approaches and says there's no fixed trigger.

- **Do it yourself first (the better option if bootstrapping).** Delegating dev and design is easier because it's more deterministic ("build this feature, squash this bug"). Hiring "get me 100 customers" is not guaranteed, so you can't tell whose fault a miss is. Adopt a student mindset, use first principles, learn from others, and use swipe files.
- **Hire or partner early.** He sees himself as a good non-technical co-founder type who is happy to leave design and development to someone else. There are "two really big jobs in SaaS: build the software, sell the software."
- His example: he joined Derrick Reimer's SavvyCal as an early freelancer, right after an early access launch, to do all the marketing while Derrick focused on design, development and company building.
- Other routes are an agency or a technical co-founder. In every case, "hire the right person and then nothing else matters." The right person will figure out the right things to do, and he says they need patience and autonomy.
- On timing: he has seen companies hire at a few hundred dollars MRR and others wait until mid-seven-figure ARR. He says earlier is better because marketing is a compounding growth lever, but you shouldn't feel obligated to delegate right away.

## Conversion Factory: productized product marketing and design

- Corey co-founded Conversion Factory with two friends (Zach Stevens and Nick Lowden). It is a subscription ("productized") service for product marketing and design, aimed at early-stage SaaS.
- The gap he saw: there are content marketing agencies and ad agencies, but no product marketing agencies, even though product marketing is where he always starts. The usual options are a full-time hire or doing it yourself, and neither suits early-stage companies.
- Scope: brand, website development, copywriting, sales enablement, onboarding, email marketing, retention and activation. He positions it as "Design Joy, but for product marketing" (Design Joy being Brett Williams's productized design service).
- Operating model: one request at a time, with unlimited requests and revisions queued. They aim to return each deliverable in 24 to 72 hours, sometimes in minutes. A conference signage request came back in about 20 minutes.
- He contrasts this with traditional retainers and projects, where billable hours incentivize agencies to drag on work and clients to squeeze scope. His example: a first startup job where a new website (copy, graphics, web development) cost about $134,000 and took 14 months.
- The Conversion Factory site took about a week, including a day for Corey's first copy draft, Zach's Figma design and Nick's Webflow build. The longest delay was waiting on the Mercury and Stripe accounts after incorporating.
- They are "dogfooding" their own expertise. He finds it baffling when agencies outsource their own site, comparing it to an email software company using a competitor for its own automated emails.

## Pricing a productized service

- He says cost-plus pricing breaks down for services, where each deliverable has different value. Hourly billing misaligns incentives.
- Flat subscription pricing: the premium comes from "paying for access". Clients pay for the work delivered "in our way with our opinions and with our secret sauce", like wanting a Gucci bag rather than any bag. A flat rate also avoids being a commodity next to undifferentiated services like Design Pickle or copywriting subscriptions.
- Two tiers: an async base plan where work is thrown over the fence, and a sync plan with weekly calls, strategic input and a roadmap of what to request next. The sync tier carries about a 50% premium. He says there isn't "a lot of magical math" in it: it is a standard premium markup.
- Plans run roughly $6,000 to $9,000 per month. His anchor: $6,000 is about half the cost of a full-time product marketing manager, and the team also covers a part-time designer, product designer and copywriter, so $6,000 to $9,000 is about a third of what hiring that team would cost.
- Early observation, flagged as not yet conclusive: smaller clients with tight budgets tend to keep the queue full and squeeze the most value, while larger-budget clients use it less.

## Why a productized service de-risks an agency

- Corey says the subscription model gives diversification: many similar-sized clients instead of one whale. He retells a mentor's story of an integrated marketing agency that lost a $7 million-a-year client after the financial crisis and had to lay off about half its team.
- It also removes the pressure to hire based on variable project inflow.
- A narrow service avoids the full-service agency problem of needing different specialists per client (demand generation, content, product marketing), which wrecks utilization.
- Control: the three founders cover copy, design and Webflow build, so no deliverable depends on outside parties. He stopped advisory coaching because clients struggled to implement the advice, and he hated "telling you the right things but you're just not doing them."
- No platform dependency, in contrast to indie businesses built on Shopify or the Twitter API.
- Scaling: they capped intake with a waitlist and chose about five concurrent clients as the point of profitability. Then they will raise capacity as they learn what work they can handle. They plan to add contractors with specific roles if needed, and later to sell products such as Webflow templates.

## Building a portfolio with one cash-flowing business

- Corey treats Conversion Factory as a main thing for now, not a throwaway bet. His reasoning: "people like hiring people", and finding product-market fit for a service is easier than for software.
- He calls himself an entrepreneur-tinkerer who likes starting things. His view is that you need one thing that works as a cash cow so the other projects don't have to pan out for you to be financially stable.
- Swipe Files: he paired the community down to essentials, launched a new course, and shifted toward an async model. He saw people losing enthusiasm for online communities after earlier waves of creator economy, newsletters, communities and podcasting.
- He blocked July and August as 100% Conversion Factory time and left Swipe Files on autopilot with his co-founder until September.
- On new ventures, he looks for synergy with his existing B2B SaaS audience, and prefers channels that fit what he already likes creating over chasing every trend.
