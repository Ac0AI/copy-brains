## Multi-step launch plan for early-stage products (cold start)

- Corey says every product faces a cold start and has to build its own momentum: first users, attention, followers and subscribers. He likes working on this stage and mostly advises early-stage B2B SaaS companies.
- He borrows a multi-part launch plan from April Dunford (author of "Obviously Awesome"), who described it from her time at IBM. A launch is a series of moments, not one day. He notes Brian Chesky's tweet that Airbnb launched three times.
- The layers he lists:
  - **Pre-announcement:** what you're launching, what it is, and where to learn more.
  - **Building anticipation:** tell the story, share sneak peeks, and talk about the problems people have.
  - **Pre-launch:** an early-access list, beta or trial. He cites Maven running cohort courses with creators before launch, and Superhuman staying in early access for a long time.
  - **Actual launch:** make noise and run a PR push. Corey spends little time here because it's the one step most people already focus on. Post where your audience is (Product Hunt, Reddit, Facebook groups, Twitter, LinkedIn). He says the specific place matters less than where your customers are.
  - **Post-launch follow-up:** offer something new after launch.
  - **Momentum launch:** share how the launch went, such as "what I learned from my Product Hunt launch", customer counts or a rising revenue chart.
  - **Relaunch:** stagger platforms on different days (one platform one day, another the next, then Hacker News). This isolates each platform's impact and extends the attention window.
  - **Big update:** a notable feature, or a funding announcement a week after going public.
  - **Launch celebration:** go back to supporters to thank them, set up calls, or review who helped. This is common with book authors.
- He personally prefers pre-launch tactics such as early-access lists and waitlists. They build anticipation and engage people before they use the product.

## Pre-launch tactics: customer development calls and engineering as marketing

- Corey likes customer development calls when building a startup, feature or product. Talk to customers and check the data points that support the need.
- On those calls you can tell people you're launching and invite them onto the list. The personal relationships help later when you need a retweet or a Product Hunt comment.
- He treats engineering as marketing. He points to a company that shipped several free mini-products, each with its own launch plan, building toward its main product.
- Looking at Savvy, he calls the "pre-launch" push mostly a matter of getting people onto a list and warmed up before launch day.

## Savvy launch case study (Product Hunt, username reservation, Calendly buyout)

Corey advises Savvy (Savvycal), a scheduling-link product. The founder, Derek, launched it in September 2020 and Corey joined in November.

- **Product Hunt launch:** he calls it the real kickoff. Doing it well lifts you up the homepage list, which gives the most visibility and credibility. It produced thousands of visitors and thousands of new signups.
- **Username reservation campaign (just before launch):** only a limited number of usernames were available. Corey told people the launch was the following week, that a flood of people would arrive, and that they could sign up now to claim a username. The company held names for 60 days. This drew many people in before the official launch.
- **Calendly subscription buyout (right after launch):** many interested people said they loved the product but were locked into a year-long Calendly subscription. Savvy credited their account for the remaining months of the old plan, modeled on what T-Mobile, Verizon and AT&T do. It created extra post-launch buzz.
- **Messaging product (another launch):** email marketing is a big decision that takes research. For the first 25 to 50 users, Corey's team offered a done-for-you onboarding service: they set up the campaigns and copied everything over, so people could try it with no effort. He says this worked well for people who were interested but hadn't acted. He adds that the product ultimately didn't work out as it should have.
- **Public thank-you pages:** he cites another onboarding-tool startup that built public pages thanking and promoting its early users. This prompted them to share it on Twitter or Facebook and introduced the product to new people.
- His recommendation: multi-step launches, lots of guerrilla marketing before launch (early access, customer development calls), engineering as marketing, and "triggers" that put you in front of many people and use existing attention.

## Build trust and an audience before monetizing

- Corey cites Gary Vaynerchuk's principle "jab, jab, jab, right hook." Attention is hard to get, many people are skeptical, and you must give a lot first.
- It's tempting to go straight to selling and launching. His view: the longer you wait before launching and monetizing, the more successful you are long-term.
- His reasoning is that "marketing really comes down to earning trust." You earn it by giving a lot away free, showing credibility, and proving you can help. He ties this to the "rule of seven": people need to see you repeatedly before they act and trust you.
- Applied to Swipe Files, he was eager to launch and bring in members. Looking back, he says that for creators, and for SaaS content marketing too, the longer you defer revenue, the better your position when you do turn on monetization.
- He points to Superhuman, which stayed in pre-launch and early access for about 3 years. Entry required an invite from an existing user. This created FOMO and exclusivity, and they onboarded users one by one. If you've delivered a lot of value and people like and trust you, they are happy to pay.

## The five growth factors for SaaS

- Corey built this framework at Baremetrics after founders kept asking how to grow. He calls the old "build it and they will come" idea the "Field of Dreams fallacy": launching and expecting people to spread it no longer works because competition is intense.
- The five factors, in order: **market, product, business model (including activation and pricing model), messaging and positioning, channels.**
  - Channels come last on purpose. Everyone jumps straight to channels and growth hacks, but no channel works if the other four aren't working.
  - The factors are interconnected and must work together. The strength of each sets your growth potential.
  - Example: market 3, product 3, model 5, messaging 5, channels 1 would waste a lot of opportunity. Doing all five well gives big growth potential.
- He teaches this in his course "Rethinking Growth" (about the five factors) and the "Mental Models for Marketing" course (40 models).

## Market and product: how Corey evaluates them

**Market**
- Ideally you build a product for a type of market, rather than build a product and then look for a market.
- Look at three things: TAM (market size), the market's growth rate, and excitement (is it a category people talk about and urgently need).
- A shrinking market, even a large one, pressures everyone: more competition, higher churn, a race to the bottom. In a growing market you can grow with it.
- Gumroad example: it launched around 2011, had to do a big layoff roughly three years later (he says he thinks it was three years), and wasn't growing fast enough for a VC-backed business because the market wasn't big enough yet. Revenue kept growing month after month anyway. You can't see the layoffs or the TechCrunch hit in its revenue chart, because it was riding the market's growth.
- Remote work: after the lockdowns the TAM for remote-work tools grew roughly 100x overnight. An unnamed startup he works with, described as a Zoom for Hollywood production, had just over $1M ARR before COVID. Within a few months its business grew 10x and it reached eight-figure ARR, because producers needed high-fidelity, low-latency collaboration that Zoom couldn't provide.
- The creator economy also boomed (side income, newsletters, communities). He calls it a rising tide lifting all boats.

**Product**
- It's hard to be objective about your own product ("there are a lot of ugly babies in the world").
- First criterion: how big the problem is and how frequently it occurs. Big, frequent problems make a good product.
  - A big problem that occurs once a year (accounting, tax season) works, but the buying cycle is longer and people procrastinate.
  - A small problem that occurs very often (for example, can't search your Twitter favorites) caps pricing. You might get $5 or $10 a month, not $100.
- Second tool: a differentiation vs. demand matrix for features.
  - Unique and highly demanded features are the "killer" features.
  - Highly demanded but not unique means you compete on brand, customer service or price, like an incumbent.
  - Avoid unique features with little demand, since they shrink the addressable market.
- Other advantages: IP, brand, patents, design and UX, partnerships and integrations, and hard-to-copy features. Examples he gives: Ahrefs' backlink data, which he says competitors matched only recently, and Riverside's local recording, which was long the deciding factor for him.
- The question underneath all of this is "how marketable is my product?"

## Positioning and messaging (April Dunford's process plus Corey's copy rules)

**Positioning steps (credited to Dunford's book)**
1. List your competitive alternatives, meaning what customers compare you against. Corey uses jobs-to-be-done to separate direct competitors (same job, same way), indirect (same job, different way) and secondary (different job, different way). Alternatives can include spreadsheets, specialists such as data scientists, or manual work.
2. Identify your unique attributes against those alternatives. These can be features, brand, customer service, way of working, or an opinionated method.
3. Work out the value those attributes unlock for customers.
4. Decide who cares most, which is the target market. Example he recalls from Dunford: a database company repositioned as a database for banks and grew rapidly, because banks cared most.
5. Choose the category you place yourself in.

**Category**
- Corey doesn't think new categories are easy to create, and says people are confused about what creating one means.
- Praising Drift's conversational marketing, he nonetheless questions whether "revenue acceleration" is a category. Your category should add accurate context. Calling yourself revenue acceleration when you're a live chat tool confuses customers and can cost you them.
- For Savvy: "It's a scheduling tool. It's a scheduling link", because those are the words people use. They didn't invent an "AI-powered magic tool" label.

**Messaging**
- Use the pain-pain-dream-solution copy framework: the big pain, the ideal outcome, then how you get people there.
- Use customer words, and be specific rather than vague or buzzword-heavy.
- Use social proof directly on the page (testimonials, videos, quotes).
- Address objections upfront (competitors, features, how it works, barriers, time and money).
- Savvy example: in customer research Corey saw a viral tweet from Brett Goldstein about how sending a scheduling link still feels weird. Savvy's homepage headline became "Sending your scheduling link shouldn't be weird." The landing page worked well and helped Savvy position itself as a credible competitor to Calendly.

## GROWS framework for prioritizing and running growth experiments

- **G, gather:** collect all experiments, data and research in one place. On research, he says to build a "customer insights bank": customer feedback, Intercom conversations, tweets. Also write down all your own ideas to get them out of your head.
- **R, rank:** he still prefers ICE (impact, confidence, ease). Ease accounts for resources and timing: some ideas are out of budget, take too long or clash with the roadmap.
- **O, outline:** answer who, what, why and how. Depending on the idea this might be wireframes, a prototype or a plain description.
- **W, work:** execute. He tries to run one to three things at a time. A large growth team of 50 could run 10 at once, but a one-person marketing team can handle one or two.
- **S, study:** review the data, judge how well it worked, and decide on tweaks or iterations.
- Keep it simple. Many people feel guilty or ashamed about moving slowly. He argues you should be deliberate and work on only a couple of things at once. Running fewer experiments at a time makes it easier to isolate and trust the results.
