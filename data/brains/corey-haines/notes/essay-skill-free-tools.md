## Free tools as an acquisition channel: build a free version of what others monetize

Corey frames engineering-as-marketing with a twist on Bezos: "your product is my marketing opportunity" (Bezos: "your margin is my opportunity"). Take a capability others charge for and build a free version as an acquisition channel.
- Example he cites: Unsplash gave away the stock photos Getty sold, and Getty ended up acquiring it.
- His goal with free tools: generate leads, attract organic traffic, and build brand awareness.
- He cautions that sourced cases (Unsplash, HubSpot Website Grader, Buffer, Shopify) have measurement limits, so evidence should be checked before copying them.

## Four principles for a tool worth building

Corey says a free tool should:
- **Solve a real problem**: provide genuine value, address something the audience actually has, and be useful even without the main product.
- **Sit adjacent to the core product**: related to what you sell, with a natural path from tool to product, and it educates on the problem you solve.
- **Be simple and focused**: do one thing well, with low friction and immediate value.
- **Be worth the investment**: lead value × expected leads must exceed build cost + maintenance.

## Tool types and how to generate ideas

Corey's types and their best uses:
- **Calculators** (ROI, savings, pricing estimators): decisions involving numbers.
- **Generators** (templates, policies, names): creating something quickly.
- **Analyzers** (website graders, SEO auditors): evaluating existing work.
- **Testers** (meta tag preview, speed tests): checking whether something works.
- **Libraries** (icon sets, templates, snippets): reference material.
- **Interactive** (tutorials, playgrounds, quizzes): learning and understanding.

To find ideas, start from pain points:
1. What problems does the audience Google?
2. What manual processes are tedious (spreadsheet tasks, repetitive calculations)?
3. What do they need before buying (assessments, planning, comparisons)?
4. What information do they wish they had (benchmarks, hard-to-access data)?

Validate by checking search demand and competitiveness, uniqueness (how can you be "10x better"), whether the audience matches buyers, and build feasibility (can you scope an MVP).

## Lead capture and gating

Corey lays out four gating approaches:
- **Fully gated**: maximum capture, lower usage.
- **Partially gated**: balances both; the common pattern.
- **Ungated with optional capture**: maximum reach, lower capture.
- **Ungated entirely**: pure SEO/brand, no direct leads.

Best practices:
- Make the value exchange clear, e.g. "Get your full report".
- Keep friction minimal: email only.
- Show a preview of what they'll get.
- Optionally segment by asking one qualifying question.

## SEO and link building for tools

- **Tool landing page keywords**: "[thing] calculator", "[thing] generator", "free [tool type]".
- **Supporting content**: "How to [use case]" and "What is [concept]".
- Tools attract links because they are genuinely useful (people reference them), unique (you can't link to just any page), and shareable (social amplification).

## Build vs. buy and MVP scope

- **Build custom** when the concept is unique, core to the brand, high strategic value, and you have dev capacity.
- **Use no-code tools** (Outgrow, Involve.me, Typeform, Tally, Bubble, Webflow) for speed to market, limited dev resources, or testing a concept.
- **Embed an existing tool** when something good exists, white-label is available, and it's not a core differentiator.

Minimum viable tool:
1. Core functionality only: it does the one thing and works reliably.
2. Essential UX: clear input, obvious output, works on mobile.
3. Basic lead capture: email collection, with leads going somewhere useful.

Skip initially: account creation, saving results, advanced features, perfect design, every edge case.

## Evaluation scorecard

Corey rates eight factors 1-5: search demand, audience match to buyers, uniqueness vs. existing, natural path to product, build feasibility, maintenance burden (inverse), link-building potential, and share-worthiness.
- **25+**: strong candidate.
- **15-24**: promising.
- **Under 15**: reconsider.

## Common pitfalls

- **Over-engineering**: shipping a bloated tool before validating demand. Unsplash started as a Tumblr blog with Dropbox links and only invested in a custom site after demand emerged. Scope to one useful job and test it with your audience.
- **Poor product integration**: a tool with no natural path to the product earns traffic but not pipeline. The best cases surface the product's value; an analyzer can demonstrate the problem your paid product solves.
- **Maintenance and security debt**: tools that scrape, call APIs, or take user input rot and become attack surfaces. Budget for upkeep before building.
- **Vanity metrics**: visitors and usage don't pay. Track leads, qualification rate, and trial/signup conversion in your own funnel.
