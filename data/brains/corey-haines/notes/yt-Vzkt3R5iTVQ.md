## Why a portfolio instead of one focused product

- Corey says the question "should I just focus on one thing?" has followed him for years, about a newsletter, a course and a job as much as about products.
- As a marketer, he argues his time stretches further across many projects than a builder's would. A builder owns code and uptime for each one. Marketing often means planting a seed, waiting for it to prove out, and circling back.
- His structure: a personal media business (Twitter, LinkedIn, newsletter, soon YouTube), the cash-flowing services business Conversion Factory, and products as "moonshots, experimental software, scratching my own itch."
- The personal brand drives traffic to a couple of the projects, so "a rising tide lifts all boats." He does not market every product through his audience. Most of the portfolio is quiet and grows on SEO.
- His portfolio: True List (email validation, with co-founder Grant), Magister (AI marketing agent with Elliot, born out of the Marketing Skills repo), three SaaS apps for churches, Mac OS apps, mobile apps and content sites. The first eight or so have customers; roughly 20 others do not.
- He says it is "basically impossible to vibe code" True List because it is infrastructure and DevOps heavy. He keeps technical co-founders on two SaaS products because it is "above my pay grade."
- He describes his own tension: always asking "what's next," so constraining himself would feel like "a straight jacket."

## From non-technical marketer to builder

- Corey started Conversion Factory in mid-2023, tired of working for companies and of trying to recruit people to build his ideas.
- In August 2023 he did a coding boot camp at Ryan Culp's house in rural Georgia: nine strangers, taught in a garage over screen share.
- He found Cursor there and realized ChatGPT-style AI could live in the IDE. He built his first two apps from scratch without AI, then "button mashed" Claude after that.
- His view: he learned the hard way once, and every time after that he did it the easy way.
- The Marketing Skills repo is, by his account, the 481st most popular repo on GitHub of all time.
- On the "technical co-founder" meme, he notes that a technical co-founder used to act as validation: they would ask whether you had customers and revenue before spending ten years on it. Now non-technical people build anything, so he asks what validation looks like. He says he tries to avoid that trap.

## Where he draws the line on portfolios, and why products that rise then fall are the real risk

- He rejects both "10 startups at $1k MRR each" and "one at $10k MRR" as the right frame.
- He is fine with a portfolio where one product reaches 100k MRR, one 10k, a couple 1k, a few zero, as long as it is sustainable and trending up.
- His objection is the treadmill: reaching 50k MRR, watching it decline, then starting the next one to 10k and watching that decline too, so you "never really make forward progress."
- His example of the pattern is Marc Lou: Shipfast hit about 120k per month and then dropped, while DataFast does about 30k MRR.
- His rule: "the numbers should kind of always be going up or at least plateau." Things that keep growing even slowly are a win because they are "ever increasing in value."

## Automating across 30+ repos without drowning

- He runs 30 to 40 repos, so doing maintenance manually is not an option.
- Example: a new end-to-end testing library came out (agents click through the UI like a real user). He tried it on one app, then had Claude implement it across about 30 other repos in one day, roughly 500 commits.
- He also closed about 130 PRs in a week on the open-source Marketing Skills repo using Clawdbot-style agents with hooks and loops for bug fixes. He is making a video on this.
- He is always hunting for what is next, which is why automation fits his style.

## Running marketing experiments on a loop with agents

- Corey's model is to install jobs into bots that run on a loop: agents propose experiments, he approves by email, they post and optimize, track metrics, report learnings, and the cycle repeats.
- On the risk of volume without impact, Corey says the approach works because he has too many repos to do it any other way.

## AEO (answer engine optimization): what works and the case study

- Corey puts AEO alongside SEO as his default first channel for any new product, then Google Ads, then down the list.
- His framing: there is a dam of demand piling up in people searching ChatGPT and Claude, and basic good AEO "unleashes that dam."
- His three pillars:
  1. Good AEO is first good SEO.
  2. Good AEO is good product marketing: positioning plus many good pages on your own site. He cites studies saying third-party citations alone moved recommendation odds by about 20 to 30%, while a third-party citation plus a page on your site pushed it to about 90%. The LLM wants your site as a primary source and a third party to verify it.
  3. Good AEO is good brand: organic web mentions through PR blasts, backlinks, YouTube, LinkedIn. He notes SEO now wants only high-quality links, while AEO is where SEO was 10 to 15 years ago, so any backlink counts.
- Case study: a very niche trade-software client with Conversion Factory SEO, ads and a new website. After three months of AEO work tracked in Magister (his own AI visibility tool), their AI visibility score went from 14% to 28%, traffic doubled and booked calls tripled.
- His caveat: it is new vocabulary and he is not sure how meaningful the score itself is; he points to the traffic and calls.
- He agrees with a point on "footprint": a business needs many places on the web, not one site or one Twitter account.

## Paid channels and how he orders them

- His order of operations: SEO and AEO first, then Google Ads (same intent, but paying to play), then keep working down the list. He stops moving down once a channel resists.
- Google Ads is getting squeezed. When AI Overviews launched on major terms (he dates the shift to around May), clicks fell and costs rose. Customer acquisition on True List went from about $100 to $1,000 and he turned ads off.
- He expects Google to build ad products inside AI Overviews eventually, but says we are in a weird transition.
- He sees a resurgence in Meta ads: the Andromeda algorithm handles targeting, so you "just make good ads" and it finds the right people at decent prices.
- For a minor project like his open-source invoicing tool, he would start with SEO and not spin up LinkedIn ads.
- He says marketing software to other software companies is incredibly hard right now because everyone is doing it, and more niche buyers are easier and faster to grow.

## Finding and winning a niche from zero

- His church apps came from a friend at volleyball who needed check-in software for an inner-city teen center and faced a roughly $50k quote. Corey offered to build it for a small monthly subscription.
- That customer texts feature requests and FaceTimes while sharing his screen. Word of mouth has brought more churches.
- Step two is learning buyer psychology through jobs-to-be-done and secret-shopper questions: where they looked first (TikTok, Google), what tool they tried, why it failed, where it led.
- He uses AI to learn a niche's vocabulary (for example, what church administrative pastors call things, how they search, what budgets look like) to find keywords he would not know otherwise. He takes the output "with a grain of salt" but says it gives a big head start on deciding which channels are viable.

## Productized services and the "harder is better" principle

- On Josh Pigford's SEO service ($2,000 per month, Slack only, no calls), Corey calls the dream of no calls appealing but warns it will attract headache clients; most businesses want five minutes with a human before buying.
- He says SEO productizes well because the output is clear (for example, four blog posts a month). Outcome-driven work like ads or branding is harder to productize. About half of marketing is output-driven and easy to productize; half is outcome-driven and hard.
- Conversion Factory's productized offers have clear, transparent pricing by tasks at a time, and he is working on a highly automated, lower-priced AEO service.
- His closing principle: "the harder it is, the better," because a higher barrier to entry protects you. He now defaults to the harder thing, even for marketing channels, and wants to double down on channels most people do not know exist.
- On product ideas, he says the best inspiration comes from industries you did not know existed before meeting someone who works in one. That is harder than scratching your own itch, which is why it is more defensible.
