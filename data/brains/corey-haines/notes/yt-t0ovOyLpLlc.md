## Mental models as the fix for "imitation marketing"

Corey became the first marketing hire at Cordial, an early-stage startup, after starting as an intern. He spent four to five hours a day on blog posts, podcasts, books and Udemy courses. He still felt like an imposter because he could not apply tactics to his own company.

- He argues you can't copy and paste marketing: companies differ in persona, industry and product.
- Hacks and tricks only show the end result (the growth hack, the quadrupled conversion rate), not the decision process behind it.
- A coworker at Cordial said "we're just going to market our way through it", which Corey took as code for improvising. He built the "Mental Models for Marketing" course (roughly 40 models) to avoid that.
- He defines a mental model as a simple thought process or framework that helps you interpret the world, a "mental toolbox". Good decisions mean removing blind spots, and changing perspective is how you do that.
- Without models, marketers default to three bad options: do what's easiest (copy), go with gut instinct, or use an incorrect subconscious model.
- The most common bad model is "what would I want?". It fails because you usually aren't the customer, and two marketers using it will reach different conclusions with no way to settle who's right.

## First principles: deconstruct, then rebuild

Corey calls first principles "the mental model of mental models".

- Example: Elon Musk found rockets cost $65 million to buy. Breaking down what a rocket costs to build, he found it was about 2% of retail, just over $1 million. So he built them, and SpaceX followed.
- Marketers rarely deconstruct. They rush to launch the campaign instead of asking how it works.
- Process: (1) disassemble an idea into its fundamental parts, (2) reassemble them into something better, (3) test and repeat until you reach a conclusion.
- Strip out assumptions and conventions ("this is how everyone does it"). Conventions produce mediocre campaigns because everyone does the same thing.
- He contrasts the chef, who invents recipes from ingredients and flavor profiles, with the cook, who follows or lightly tweaks a recipe. The same split applies to a playmaker versus a coach.
- The "rule of five whys": ask why at least five times about any plan.
  - Example with SEO: "Why SEO?" Because of keyword volume. Why those keywords? Traffic. Why traffic? Leads. Why leads? They're qualified. Why does that matter? They become customers and revenue.
  - If you look under the hood and the people searching aren't qualified, you might skip SEO or target only a specific keyword set.
  - Use the same exercise for Product Hunt launches, podcasts or any channel or tactic.

## Jobs to be done and the four forces of switching

- Origin story: a fast-food chain tried segmenting by product and demographics and testing flavors, but milkshake sales didn't improve. Clayton Christensen's researcher spent a day in a restaurant and found 40% of milkshakes were bought first thing in the morning by commuters taking them to go.
  - Their job: keep a free hand busy on a boring commute, stave off hunger until lunch, and cope with constraints (one hand, work clothes, no mess). A milkshake beat a bagel or donut because it is clean, fits a cup holder and lasts.
  - The chain made a thicker morning shake with fruit chunks and a kids' size, and sales rose.
- Principle: people "hire" products to do a job. They want outcomes (a clean apartment, not a vacuum cleaner), not features.
- Personas (age, gender, race, weekend habits) don't explain causality. Corey's own In-N-Out milkshake jobs: nostalgia, tolerating mall shopping with his mom, satisfying an ice cream craving, and being cheap enough ($2-3) to not feel guilty.
- Run a jobs-to-be-done interview focused on emotional triggers and pain points that led someone to switch (hire or fire a product).
- Four forces behind switching. Teams usually underestimate the cost of switching. Treat the forces as an acquisition strategy:
  1. Increase the push away from the existing product (show it's clunky, neglected, mismatched).
  2. Increase the pull of your product (social proof, testimonials, examples, tutorials).
  3. Decrease the fear and anxiety of change (assure switching is quick). Example: he told someone worried about leaving Calendly for SavvyCal that it takes about 15 minutes, and they signed up immediately.
  4. Decrease attachment to the status quo (sentiment, relationships, brand affinity).
- The goal is to make the attraction to the new product outweigh the anxiety of change and the status quo.

## Competitors via jobs, plus a job template

Ignore product categories and ask who else is fighting for the same job. There are probably more competitors than you think.

- Direct: same job, same way (Salesforce vs HubSpot, Nike vs Adidas, Toptal vs Upwork).
- Secondary: same job, different way (Baremetrics vs a spreadsheet, Buffer vs an intern, a job board vs a recruiter).
- Indirect: a different job with a conflicting outcome (six-pack goals vs ice cream and pizza, Drift vs form builders, Ben & Jerry's vs 24 Hour Fitness).
- At Baremetrics, many customers switched away from spreadsheets. Buffer replaced the intern who scheduled social posts manually.
- Template to fill in: "When [situation/trigger], the customer wants to [motivation], so they can [expected outcome]."
  - Triggers include landing on your site, reading an email, signing up, requesting a demo or chatting.
  - Listen for these patterns in customer conversations to formulate the job.

## Stages of awareness and mere exposure

Corey draws on Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966). Prospects sit in five stages, from least to most aware:

1. Unaware: don't know they have a problem.
2. Problem aware: sense a problem, don't know a solution exists (e.g. "I spend 20 hours a week filling in a spreadsheet").
3. Solution aware: know the result they want, unsure your product delivers it.
4. Product aware: know what you sell, unsure it's right for them.
5. Most aware: know your product, only need the deal.

- Car dealership analogy: pushing a special deal on a different car to someone who has decided on one model, or pushing any deal on someone just starting to learn, both frustrate buyers. Meet people where they are and move them to the next stage.
- Marketing gets easier further to the right. The marketer's job is to engineer a machine that moves people right.
- Mere exposure effect (Robert Zajonc): the more people see something, the more they like it. Corey went from finding the Cybertruck ugly to wanting one after repeated exposure on Twitter, YouTube and in the news. Takeaways: don't be afraid to repeat your message, and get your reps in to build awareness.

## The action model and persuasion levers (loss aversion, scarcity, social proof)

**Human action model** (Ludwig von Mises). Three ingredients are required for someone to act (the pain, dream, fix model):
1. Discomfort with the current state.
2. A vision of a better future.
3. Belief that your product is the path from here to there.

- Mario analogy: the mushroom is the product, but you sell the superpower (what it lets you do).
- Most copy and sales pitches explain the product fine but fail to say who it's for and why they should care.
- Corey now runs a checklist on everything he publishes: dissatisfaction with the present, vision of the future, and a plan of action. He also checks what he is anchoring against, and how to create commitment and consistency and avoid loss.

**Loss aversion.** People feel losses about twice as strongly as gains. Corey's own example: his stolen guitar hurt more than the replacement felt good. Applications:
- Frame offers around what people lose by not buying.
- Lazy registration (let people use the app, then require an account to save their work).
- Newsletters and fear of missing out.
- Pre-filled carts: pre-loaded discounted upsells are removed less often. He cites roughly three times the upsell take rate versus requiring users to add them, with the reverse being about one third.

**Scarcity.** Less available means more valuable ("would the Mona Lisa be more valuable if there were 100?").
- Tactics: limited stock, only a few spots (e.g. coaching), invites, and seeing other buyers grab items.
- Deadlines: he recommends against fake countdown timers and for legitimate ones, like Tiny Seed's batch application deadlines, and shipping cutoffs like Amazon's. Follow through on any limit you state.

**Social proof.** People assume others' behavior is correct. Types: testimonials, share and like counts, subscriber counts, reviews and ratings (his wife picks restaurants by best rating and most ratings), influencer endorsements, in-depth case studies, media mentions, raw numbers (Basecamp showing 6,000 signups in a week), video testimonials, real-time stats, verified accounts, trust badges (Visa, PayPal), studies, awards and milestones (Shopify's 1 million merchants), and brand advocates.

## Practical decision frameworks: effort vs impact, good-cheap-fast, CRIBS

- **Effort vs impact:** "no one talks about opportunity costs" in marketing. Map each campaign by effort and reward.
  - Avoid high effort, low impact. Low effort, low impact needs volume to matter.
  - Favor low effort, high impact, or high effort, high impact.
  - At Cordial he asked who social media scheduling was for. Marketing executives rarely look at brand accounts, so the time wasn't worth it.
- **Good, cheap, fast:** his Cordial boss wrote "good, cheap or fast, choose two" on the whiteboard. Good and fast costs top dollar, good and cheap takes time, and cheap and fast gives lower quality. Pick two for each project.
- **CRIBS (feedback framework):** most "feedback" requests really seek approval. Ask reviewers whether parts are Confusing, Repeated, Insightful, Boring or Surprising, positive or negative.
  - Give reviewers this structure and the quality of feedback changes.
  - Dan Murphy (Privy, formerly Drift) credited constant DM feedback with this framework for the team's success at Drift.

## Q&A: learning customer pain, early social proof, mediocre product vs marketing

- **Finding customer pain and motivation:**
  1. Interviews or calls, asking about biggest problems, bottlenecks, frustrations and workarounds.
  2. Surveys with questions like "what takes the most time in your day-to-day?"
  3. Online research: keyword research, forums, reviews on Amazon, G2 and app stores.
  - He collects customers' exact words into a "customer insights bank" he can word-search and categorize.
- **Getting social proof early:** ask a few friends, influencers or strangers for advice on what you're building. Give them free access, get feedback with CRIBS, then ask for a testimonial. He did this with *Refactoring Growth* and *Mental Models for Marketing*.
  - Collect proof diligently as you go.
  - Borrow credibility from other things you've built. People may endorse you as a person ("whatever Corey works on is usually insightful").
- **Mediocre product with great marketing, or great product with no marketing?** Corey picks the mediocre product with great marketing.
  - No product is ever done, and marketing gets people in the door, builds proof and brings the feedback needed to improve.
  - A great product with no marketing is a huge uphill battle.
  - His mantra: "start marketing before you even start building", selling the vision first.
- His course sells in an open enrollment window every three months (next one that Cyber Monday); the Teachable listing was a discounted way in between windows. It is split into problem solving, persuasion and process models.
