## Why pricing is the crux of a SaaS business

- Corey calls the price you charge, and the way you charge it, "the Crux of the business" and one of the major keys to growth.
- You can have all the users and traffic in the world, but if you can't monetize it well you don't have a business (or only a weak one).
- B2B SaaS is utilitarian: people pay because they get value. Pricing is how you capture a proportion of that value.
- SaaS pricing is far more complex than a one-time sticker price. It involves subscriptions, monthly vs annual, per user, per feature, and add-ons.
- Corey says most founders treat pricing as an afterthought: they copy competitors or add margin on top of server and delivery costs (a Cost Plus model).
- Two failure modes:
  - Growth plateaus because customers get more value but you don't capture more of it.
  - Pricing hinders usage. You want people to use the product more, but the price discourages it or pushes them to cancel or find a competitor.
- His framing: there are only a few things to do right, but many ways to get SaaS pricing wrong.

## Early-stage pricing: don't over-engineer, just pick a price

- Corey says new founders should not spend much time on pricing early on. They don't yet know their customers, the product isn't feature complete, and just acquiring users is hard.
- Over-engineering a "perfect" model you'll keep for years is counterproductive to growth.
- His advice: pick one single monthly or annual price that is:
  - Low enough that about 75% of your users wouldn't have a problem paying.
  - High enough that they have to think about it and weigh it against the value they expect.
- Intercom example: they were modeling pricing in spreadsheets, asked Jason Fried (Basecamp co-founder), and he said "just charge 50 bucks see what happens." They did, and $50 was low enough that people tried it.
- Don't base the price on competitors, but make it competitive. If you're the new product with half the features and competitors charge $100/month, charging $200 makes no sense.
- Even small gaps matter: $80/month feels very different from $10/month, because $80 is still eight times more.

## The main SaaS pricing models

- **Flat rate:** one price for everyone. Corey notes a long-time flat-rate company recently switched to user-based pricing.
- **User-based (per seat):** common for CRMs and scheduling apps.
- **Usage-based:** charge a variable price on one usage metric, such as API calls or pay-as-you-go.
- **Feature/tier-based:** bundle features or usage into multiple price points, e.g. a $9/month Basic plan, a $49/month Premium plan, Enterprise, and possibly a free plan.
- **Hybrid:** any combination. Examples: pay-as-you-go early, then usage-based later; per-user pricing plus feature tiers ($9/user for X, Y, Z features, $12/user for A, B, C features).
- How to choose: work from data on how customers actually get value, rather than closing your eyes and pointing at one.

## Matching the model to how customers get value

- Key question for per-user pricing: is the product experience different for each user on a team account? If so, per-user is a good starting place.
  - CRMs and scheduling apps suit it, because of per-user settings, contacts, and views.
- Stripe is the counter-example. Per-user pricing would make no sense because value comes from end customers paying. So Stripe charges per transaction (a flat fee plus a percentage; Corey cites 2.9% plus 30 cents) rather than per user or per subscription.
- Feature-based tiers fit sophisticated products that serve customers with different needs.
  - Example: an early-stage startup may not need the advanced automations, compliance, and security of a big ESP like customer.io, and may prefer something simpler like userlist.com at roughly half the price.
- The underlying pattern is finding your **value metrics**:
  - Identify one primary value metric (number of users, API calls, contacts in a CRM or email list) and one or two secondary ones.
  - Anchor price to the value metric, then decide between add-ons, bundles, a cart, or strict plans.
- A more scientific version of this takes a lot of data analysis and modeling. Corey's core point is to figure out the value metric and match it to the standard models.

## Passive vs active usage-based pricing (the ahrefs example)

- Context: ahrefs recently moved to usage-based pricing, with limits on reports (for example, about 10,000 reports a month at $99), and drew backlash. Corey says he is a fan and was only speculating about their reasoning.
- Likely thinking: some users ran reports far out of proportion to what they paid (a $100/month user acting like a $1,000/month user), so ahrefs wanted to extract more value from heavy users.
- Corey's distinction:
  - **Passive usage-based pricing:** Stripe's per-transaction fees, or ConvertKit's price rising with each additional 500 subscribers. Charges happen automatically in the background and track value the customer wants more of. Customers accept it because they want more payments or more subscribers.
  - **Active usage-based pricing:** closer to the ahrefs case. Customers must actively think "to get this I have to pay for this" every time they use the product.
- He is "not a huge fan" of credit-based pricing, because each action is a conscious spend, usage becomes a running tally, and the link between value received and charge is less clear.
- Customers may also confuse the two types.

## Pricing strategy: testing new pricing and migrating customers

- Corey treats pricing strategy as partly the pricing page itself (presentation, social proof, packaging), but says a bigger part is how to test pricing and migrate people off legacy models.
- **Test on new customers only**, for roughly three to six months:
  - New customers give an unbiased read because they don't have the old price in mind.
  - Existing customers are anchored to what they've paid, so testing on them is "not a great idea."
  - Run multiple experiments, then look at the whole funnel: retention, ARPU, expansion revenue, and activation rates.
- **Migrate everyone to the winner**, but don't "flip a switch" and double or halve someone's bill overnight. Instead:
  - Announce the change well ahead, with three to six months of lead time.
  - Explain why you're making the change and how it affects them.
  - Sweeten early switching with incentives, such as 25% off the first year, 20% off for a couple of months, or half off the next month for those who move within the window.
  - Actually complete the move when the window ends.
- **Avoid grandfathering and legacy plans:**
  - Customers stuck on old plans (Model A here, Model B there, Experiment C elsewhere) make it hard to generate reports and measure results.
  - Never promise a customer they can keep their price forever. The product, your understanding of customers, how people consume data, and your costs all change.
- His summary: give people a lot of notice, communicate very clearly, always move them to the latest pricing, and roll it out seamlessly with no double charging.

## Corey's productivity approach and reading recommendations

- Corey plans in months (big rocks), then breaks them into weekly focus and daily work.
- He batches meetings on Mondays or Wednesdays, so about 80% of his week is open for deep, creative work. He spends roughly two hours a week on email, and says "my email is a dumpster fire" and that's the compromise to stay productive.
- Core idea: relentless focus on work that moves the needle or is customer facing, and cutting out the rest.
- Background: his career has been SaaS marketing, often as the first marketing hire with a tiny budget and a blank canvas, where he has had creative freedom to experiment and ship.
- Books he recommends: *The Mom Test* by Rob Fitzpatrick and *Obviously Awesome* by April Dunford.
- He also endorses *Super Thinking* by Gabriel Weinberg (the DuckDuckGo CEO) as a fantastic book on mental models for decision making.
