## Firsthand vs. secondhand expertise: astronomers vs. astronauts

Corey draws on a line from Jurassic Park III, where Dr. Alan Grant says there are two kinds of boys: those who want to be astronomers and those who want to be astronauts. The astronomer "gets to study these amazing things from a place of complete safety." When it's pointed out that astronomers can't go into space, Grant replies: "That's the difference between imagining and seeing: to be able to touch them."

- Corey uses this to separate expertise gained by observing from expertise gained by doing.
- Both astronomers and astronauts are experts in space, but they gain and apply that expertise in two totally different ways.
- His closing test: when push comes to shove, whose expertise would you trust more, the astronomer's or the astronaut's?

## Firsthand and secondhand expertise defined

Corey argues that today anyone can be called an "expert" because we lack good vocabulary for how someone developed their expertise.

- **Firsthand expertise** is knowledge gained through doing.
- **Secondhand expertise** is knowledge gained through observing.
- Example in cooking: a firsthand expert is someone who spent decades as a chef in a Michelin star restaurant. A secondhand expert is a food critic who blogs about meals from top restaurants around the world.
- Secondhand expertise can still offer valuable insights to relay to others, but it "lacks the depth and nuance that comes from actually doing the work."

## Why observing is easy and why that matters

Corey points out that building expertise through observation is attractive because it is safe.

- Observing is safe, and it's easy to point out what others are doing right or wrong.
- He warns about the "countless 'experts' on social media who observe from afar, collecting case studies and repackaging knowledge learned from the firsthand experience of others."
- His position: "Let's not throw 'expert' around so lightly."
- Practical takeaway for evaluating advice: ask whether the person has done the work themselves or is relaying what others did.

## Questions that test whether someone is a real expert

Corey frames the test as a set of rhetorical questions about hands-on experience:

- How much of an expert can you be in copywriting if you haven't written copy for clients?
- How much of an expert can you be in wildlife preservation if you haven't been in the field protecting animals?
- How much of an expert can you be in software engineering if you haven't built apps from scratch?
- How much of an expert can you be in politics if you haven't held office?

He applies the same standard across fields: expertise in a craft should come from having practiced the craft.

## Nuance: levels of expertise, and why doers advance knowledge

Corey does not claim secondhand experts are worthless or that hands-on experience is an absolute requirement.

- He acknowledges "there are exceptions" and says he is "simply pointing out that there are levels to this."
- His framing: "Secondhand experts stand on the shoulders of firsthand experts."
- In a world where everyone has unprecedented access to information, he argues "it's the doers, those who transform theory into practice, who advance our knowledge."
- The implication for his own work and for choosing whom to learn from: favor people who have shipped, run, or built the thing, and treat observers as useful relayers rather than primary sources.
