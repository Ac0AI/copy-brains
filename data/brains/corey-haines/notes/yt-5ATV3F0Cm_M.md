## Corey's read on the 2022 SaaS reset: longer cycles, tighter budgets, and a good moment for bootstrappers

- Corey calls late 2022 "a big reset." Companies had raised huge sums and chased growth at all costs. Now everyone is asking what actually works, cutting what doesn't, and trimming their tech stack.
- Sales cycles are longer. Growth is slowing, and it can go negative if a company has churn or retention problems.
- VC-backed startups now have to think about ROI in a quantifiable way. That means knowing how much of a raise turns into net new ARR, on a trajectory they can sustain.
- He thinks this is a great time for bootstrapped or minimally funded startups. "If you're profitable, you rule right now." Bootstrappers have always scrutinized ROI from day one.
- Some bootstrapped founders he has seen are doubling down on marketing. Acquisition has become cheaper and more efficient against their competitors.
- Early-stage startups caught in the middle are at a critical point. They either find an inflection point and grow, or they quietly shut down. He declines to predict the economy. He expects the already-solid companies to look even more solid.
- The metrics that matter now are cash flow and retention, since fundraising is no longer a "never-ending faucet."

## Bundling and the move toward all-in-one products

- Corey says startups keep calling themselves a "platform" even when they are a single product. Everyone wants to become multi-product.
- Reasons to bundle: you can replace another tool, charge more, solve more use cases and win the sale. It also creates vertically integrated growth, where you make more money from existing customers (land and expand, negative churn).
- Categories now overlap and compete with each other. Website builders are becoming email tools, email tools are becoming selling tools, and selling tools are becoming community tools.
- He separates bundling from being a platform. Bundling is multiple products in one suite. A platform lets other developers build on top, as Shopify does.
- Customers want one place, one license and one bill. His examples are ClickUp, Basecamp, Shopify, Salesforce, Adobe Creative Suite, AWS and Stripe (which started with one tool and now has many).
- A single-product, narrowly focused niche SaaS is now attacked from several angles. As he puts it: "we're not just competing in our little subcategory now, categories are competing with each other."

## Free tools and freemium as a way to pull users off paid competitors

- Corey sees two routes. You can create or double down on freemium and make it more generous. You can also build micro tools, standalone mini products or "drops" that are free. These work as a ramp from another tool onto yours, even in a limited capacity, and as a loss leader into the product.
- His example is SavvyCal's Meeting Polls, launched in the months before this talk. It is a completely free, ad-free Doodle alternative that he calls vastly superior.
- Adoption was slow. The product has to be pushed out and the viral loops need time. He says it won't be "a viral success overnight like so many people say."
- He frames the product as a marketing opportunity: "your margin is my opportunity."
- Doodle and Calendly were first movers in an uncontested market, and that advantage is very hard to displace. Corey's competing pool is narrower. It is people unhappy with Doodle who are actively searching, plus newcomers such as new graduates, career switchers and late adopters. He says "we have to scrape and claw a lot more."
- The most promising use case SavvyCal is exploring is scheduling for sales. Sales is closest to the money, has the most urgency, and any advantage there is easy to quantify.

## Positioning, picking your best customer, and a one-line value prop

- Corey says many companies are re-examining their positioning right now. They are asking who their best customer is and why, rather than spraying and praying.
- Generic horizontal tools could pivot toward a sales positioning, because that is where the money and urgency are. He says to check competition and feature gaps to decide whether you should be there.
- Marketing strategy should start from who you are targeting and what the value proposition is. It shouldn't start from the noise you will make or the ads you will run.
- He likes ClickUp's "save one day every week" as a model value prop. It doesn't have to be literally true for every customer. What matters is that it is so short and memorable that people can't get it out of their heads.
- He tells every company to find its own "save one day a week" line. Avoid listing all your features, and avoid a fancy tagline nobody understands.

## Underpriced channel: paid advertising is resetting

- Ad costs rose because many advertisers competed for finite platforms, audiences and ad slots. Now many companies are halving or cutting their ad budgets. Corey thinks some are cutting the wrong channels and may be cutting their own growth in half.
- He says to treat this as an arbitrage window. He expects "a year or two" of cheaper CAC and CPCs than in the last 10 years.
- He compares it to the Facebook ads golden era (roughly 2008 to 2014), when businesses that poured money in grew exponentially. After that, costs rose until the unit economics broke.
- Channels he would revisit:
  - Emerging ad platforms: TikTok, and Reddit, which he thinks is finally getting its ad platform together.
  - Sponsoring podcasts and newsletters.
  - Influencer marketing, including white-labeling and whitelisting influencer content.
- The goal is cost per click that makes sense for your unit economics.

## SEO as the compounding channel to start now

- Corey says channels are declared dead every year and then come back, but "none of these channels actually go away." He considers "SEO is dead" and "Google screwed me" complaints to be victim mentalities.
- He expects many people to revisit SEO, and says they should. It is the only source of free recurring traffic, and it compounds. You invest once and benefit for as long as you rank.
- He likes the current state of SEO because it is the least hacky and gameable it has ever been. Google's updates are removing loopholes, so it comes back to fundamentals.
- People put it off because it takes too long, feels like black magic or agencies are expensive. His advice: "The best time to plant a tree was 20 years ago, second best time is right now."
- When the market is slower, he wants to be in demand-capture mode and make the most of every at-bat. Inbound is fundamentally easier than outbound because the people are already interested. He wants to cast the net "right at the head of the river" so qualified people flow into the funnel.

## Early-stage advice: do whatever it takes, no silver bullet

- Corey's mantra for early stage is "just do whatever it takes." There is no silver bullet and no little pool to tap into.
- Early on, you can't afford to double down on one channel and scale it. Hustle for people to sign up, get their feedback, talk to them and be their friends.
- He says it's "not rocket science." The underpriced opportunity is actually going out and doing the work.
- He agrees that the right channel depends on context: who your customer is and where they hang out.
