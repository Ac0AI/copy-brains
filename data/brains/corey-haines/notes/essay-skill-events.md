## Portfolio-first selection for events

Corey argues that events are the most expensive, riskiest and hardest-to-measure channel, so the leverage is in choosing which events to invest in, not in executing any single one. He says never to write off "events" because of one bad conference: each event is its own ecosystem, and judging all events on one conference "is like judging all paid media on a single Google Ads test."

- In-person earns its cost mainly for high-trust, high-ACV motions: enterprise or multi-stakeholder deals, regulated buyers (health, finance, government), heavy customization, conservative industries, and 6+ month sales cycles. If the ICP isn't there, spend on digital first.
- A handful of events generate most event pipeline. Find them and double down (speaking slots, side events, more people, better placement), then cut the tail.
- Bigger isn't better. Mega-conferences bring more noise, higher cost and audience dilution (students, press, vendors, tourists). Niche or regional events with 50-200 attendees often deliver more qualified leads per dollar.
- He sorts events into three types with different risk profiles:
  - Owned (roadshows, summits, user conferences): maximum control, maximum risk.
  - Trade shows: someone else's arena, where 120 days of prep beats the 4 days on the floor.
  - Community: small regular gatherings that compound and spawn more, measured by the "Saturday Test."

## Setting up an event: role, outcome and audience

Before planning, Corey says to settle four things in one batch:

1. Role: hosting, sponsoring/exhibiting, speaking, or attending. Mixed roles are normal (sponsor + speak, attend + host a dinner), and each role's motion should be planned separately while sharing one follow-up system.
2. Outcome: pipeline/meetings, authority/brand, community, or content production. He insists on picking one primary, because "events that try to do everything measure nothing."
3. Who must be in the room: the ICP segment, and roughly how many of them exist at this event.
4. Budget and team: money, and who can actually work the event.

He checks for existing product marketing context (`.agents/product-marketing.md`) first and only asks for what isn't covered.

## The universal arc: 20% event, 80% before-and-after

Corey holds that the event itself is the smallest part of event marketing, and most failures are arc failures, not event failures.

**Before (where pipeline is made)**
- Build a target list of attendees matching the ICP, using attendee lists, speaker lists, "who's going" posts and past-year attendees.
- Book meetings before arriving. "A meeting booked two weeks out is worth ten hopeful hallway collisions." The outreach should be specific, low-friction and time-boxed, such as "15 min at the coffee bar Tuesday."
- Announce presence where the audience already is, with a reason to find you, not just "we'll be at booth 402."

**During**
- Optimize for qualified conversations. One real conversation with an ICP buyer beats fifty badge scans.
- Capture context, not just contact: what they said, what they care about, and the agreed next step. Without it, follow-up is generic and dies.
- Create content while there, since the event is a recording studio you already paid for.

**After (where pipeline is won or lost)**
- Follow up within 24-48 hours, referencing what was actually discussed. He says each day of delay roughly halves response rates (directional, not a law).
- Tier the follow-up: hot conversations get a personal note and concrete next step; warm ones get a relevant asset tied to their stated problem; scans with no conversation get one light touch or nothing, so you don't burn your domain.
- Route leads to the CRM with event source tagging and to nurture for the not-nows.

## Hosting: choosing the format

| Format | Best for | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| Webinar | Lead gen and education at scale | Low-mid |
| Workshop | Product-qualified leads, activation | Mid |
| Dinner / small gathering | Exec relationships, ABM accounts | Mid |
| Meetup series | Local community, recurring presence | Mid |
| Virtual summit | List building via partner audiences | High |
| User conference | Retention, expansion, category authority | Very high |

- Workshops convert better than presentations, and they are smaller and deeper than a webinar.
- Dinners run 8-14 seats, with no pitch and a curated guest mix. He calls this the highest meetings-per-dollar format in B2B.
- Meetups: consistency beats production value, and hosting should be handed to community members over time.
- Virtual summits have built-in distribution because every speaker promotes.
- User conferences should not be attempted until there's a community that would attend "without being begged."
- Two rules outrank format choice. First, "the topic is the targeting": "State of [category] 2026" attracts the ICP, while "All about [your product]" attracts only existing customers, so pick topics the buyer would attend even if they'd never buy. Second, recurring beats one-off, since a monthly webinar or quarterly dinner compounds audiences, promotion muscle and content libraries, while a single big event evaporates.

## Attending without a booth or stage

Corey calls this the zero-budget motion and often the best ROI in the building.

1. Build a target list of 15-30 named people from the attendee and speaker lists and social chatter.
2. Pre-book 1-3 weeks ahead; the ask is 15 minutes anchored to a specific time and place.
3. Run a side-event play: host a dinner or breakfast adjacent to the conference for 8-12 target accounts. You get host status without sponsor pricing, and it often out-generates a booth at a tenth of the cost.
4. Work sessions strategically: go where targets are speaking, ask a real question, and follow up on it.
5. Apply the same 24-48 hour follow-up discipline as every other role.

## Treating every event as a content engine

Corey says events produce the highest-proof content, so capture it deliberately.

- Record everything you're allowed to record (talks, webinars, panels). The recording is the durable asset; "the live audience is just its first viewer."
- Published recordings, transcripts and show notes get crawled and cited by AI assistants, the same logic as podcast guesting and YouTube text. Say quotable lines cleanly, with the company name next to the category and numbers said out loud.
- Slice the recording into clips, a recap post per session, social pull-quotes, and proof points for sales.
- Collect social proof on site, because testimonials captured at an event are the most natural you'll get.

## Measuring events: pipeline, not applause

Corey splits metrics into three tiers.

- Vanity: registrations, badge scans, foot traffic, impressions. Track them, never optimize for them, never report them as success.
- Real: qualified conversations, meetings booked, opportunities created, pipeline influenced. This is the actual scoreboard.
- Decisive: cost per qualified meeting, cost per opportunity, closed-won influenced. These decide whether you run the event again.

Further rules:
- Compare cost-per-qualified-meeting against other channels such as ads and outbound for the go/no-go math.
- Events are multi-touch, so use source tagging, self-reported attribution ("heard us at X") and influence windows. Never claim last-click credit for a deal the event merely touched.
- Judge a recurring program on a 2-3 event trend, because the first run of anything underperforms its steady state.

## Common event-marketing mistakes

- Sponsoring for "brand awareness" with no conversation target: "If nobody owns a meetings number, the booth is décor."
- The follow-up gap: leads captured, then a first touch two weeks later from a generic sequence. "The event was fine; the follow-up killed it."
- Optimizing show-up rate after picking a topic nobody wants. Reminder cadence can't save weak demand, so fix topic and promise first.
- One-off thinking: budget for the third instance before running the first.
- Doing the event but skipping the recording, which means full production effort and zero durable assets.
- Counting badge scans as leads. "A scan is a person who walked slowly." Qualify before it enters the pipeline.
- Writing off "events" after one bad conference.
- Chasing the biggest conferences: size correlates with noise and audience dilution, not ROI.
