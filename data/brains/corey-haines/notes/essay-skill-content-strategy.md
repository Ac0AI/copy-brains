## Content as a product: brand surface area, not overhead

- Corey treats every piece of content as its own launch. He says content isn't overhead, it's "brand surface area": each published piece is a new entry point where a stranger can discover you, and hundreds of pieces compound into hundreds of doorways working 24/7.
- He plans, ships and promotes each piece with the same intent as a product release. A post that is written and forgotten has almost no surface area; a distributed one multiplies it.
- Every piece must be searchable, shareable, or both, and he prioritizes in that order because search traffic is the foundation.
- Searchable content captures existing demand (people actively looking for answers). Shareable content creates demand (spreads ideas, gets people talking).

## Information-gain gate and searchable content rules

- Before committing to a searchable piece, read the current top results for its query and write one sentence on what yours adds that none of them have: original data, a real product example, a working tool or template, a clearer answer, or a stronger opinion.
- If you can't write that sentence, don't write the piece. It will be "the eleventh copy of the same article," and neither Google nor AI assistants have a reason to prefer it.
- Searchable execution checklist:
  - Target a specific keyword or question and match search intent exactly.
  - Use titles that match search queries and headings that mirror search patterns.
  - Put keywords in the title, headings, first paragraph and URL.
  - Cover the topic comprehensively, with data, examples and authoritative links.
  - Optimize for AI/LLM discovery: clear positioning, structured content, brand consistency across the web.
- Shareable content leads with a novel insight, original data or a counterintuitive take; tells stories that make people feel something; connects to trends; and shares honest, vulnerable experiences.

## Content types and link-earning formats

- Searchable types:
  - Use-case content: "[persona] + [use-case]" for long-tail keywords (e.g. "Project management for designers").
  - Hub and spoke: build the comprehensive hub first, then spokes, and interlink. Most content works fine under `/blog`; dedicated hub/spoke URLs are only for major topics with layered depth (his example: Atlassian's `/agile` guide).
  - Template libraries: high-intent keywords plus product adoption, giving standalone value.
- Shareable types: thought leadership, data-driven content (product data, public data, original research), expert roundups (15-30 experts on one question), case studies (Challenge, Solution, Results, Key learnings), and meta content like "How We Got Our First $5k MRR" or "Why We Chose Debt Over VC."
- For backlinks specifically, he cites Foundation Inc.'s B2B Backlink Intelligence Report (March 2026, a single-vendor study, so directional). Share of backlinks relative to share of pages:
  - Statistics/data roundups 4.25x; glossary pages 1.47x; interactive tools/calculators 1.38x; how-tos 1.36x.
  - Original research 0.80x; ultimate guides 0.77x; thought leadership 0.74x; templates 0.68x.
- His read: curating statistics earns about 5x the links of producing original research, because writers link to whatever makes citation easiest. A maintained stat-roundup page is "citation infrastructure."
- Implications he draws: publish and keep fresh a stats page for your category (cheap, compounds, and citable one-liners are what LLMs lift); when you run original research, pair it with your own stat-roundup page so you capture the links your data generates.
- Lower-ranked formats aren't dead; guides, templates and thought leadership still earn their keep on rankings, conversions and brand. Judge each piece by its job.

## Pillars, topic clusters and buyer-stage keywords

- Pillars are the 3-5 core topics a brand will own, each spawning a cluster of related content. Identify them four ways: product-led, audience-led, search-led, competitor-led.
- Good pillars align with the product, match what the audience cares about, have search or social interest, and are broad enough for many subtopics.
- Structure: pillar (hub), then 3 subtopic clusters, each with several articles, interlinked.
- Map topics to buyer stage using keyword modifiers:
  - Awareness: "what is," "how to," "guide to," "introduction to."
  - Consideration: "best," "top," "vs," "alternatives," "comparison."
  - Decision: "pricing," "reviews," "demo," "trial," "buy."
  - Implementation: "templates," "examples," "tutorial," "how to use," "setup."
- Keyword exports (Ahrefs, SEMrush, GSC) get analyzed for clusters, buyer stage, intent, quick wins (low competition, decent volume, high relevance) and competitor gaps, output as a prioritized table.

## Ideation sources: customer research first

- Context to gather before planning: what the company does, the ideal customer, the content goal, the questions customers ask before buying, sales objections, support-ticket themes, and the customer's own language.
- Call transcripts: extract questions (FAQ or posts), pain points, objections, exact phrases (voice of customer) and competitor mentions, with supporting quotes.
- Surveys: mine open-ended responses; themes mentioned by 30%+ are high priority; also note resource requests and format preferences.
- Forum research: `site:reddit.com [topic]`, `site:quora.com [topic]`, plus Indie Hackers, Hacker News, Product Hunt and industry Slack/Discord. Look for FAQs, misconceptions, debates and terminology.
- Competitor analysis: `site:competitor.com/blog`; look at top-performing posts, repeated topics, gaps, case studies and outdated content you can beat.
- Sales and support input: objections, repeated questions, ticket patterns, success stories, feature requests.

## Prioritization scoring and the 60/30/10 calendar

- Score each idea 1-10 per factor, multiply by weight, sum, rank, and make the top scorers first:
  - Customer impact 40% (frequency in research, share of customers affected, emotional charge, potential LTV).
  - Content-market fit 30% (aligns with problems the product solves, unique insight, customer stories, natural path to product interest).
  - Search potential 20% (volume, competitiveness, long-tail, trend direction).
  - Resource requirements 10% (expertise, extra research, assets needed).
- Example from his template: Topic A scores 8/9/7/6 for a total of 8.0; Topic B scores 6/7/9/8 for 7.1.
- Calendar split:
  - 60% searchable: the foundation, predictable captured demand.
  - 30% shareable: thought leadership, original data, opinion; earns links and mentions.
  - 10% experimental: new formats, channels or bets, "cheap insurance against a stale mix."
- He calls this a starting ratio, not a rule: a new blog may over-index on searchable; an established brand chasing category leadership may push shareable higher.

## Per-format execution discipline

- Blog post: write 10 title options before drafting (the title does most of the work) and plan about 5 editing passes: structure, clarity, evidence, line edit, headline/SEO.
- Long-form guide: the flagship of a pillar; comprehensive enough to be "the" resource, with a table of contents and internal links to spokes. Build the hub before the spokes.
- Video: script the hook first and front-load the payoff; plan short-form clip repurposing at creation time.
- Podcast: one interview yields a transcript, quote graphics, short clips and a written recap; design the episode knowing it will be atomized.
- Email: one idea per send; the subject line is the title, so write several and pick.

## Create once, distribute twice

- Creating is half the job; distribution is the other half and most teams skip it. His philosophy: one exceptional piece, reformatted and repurposed across every channel, beats thin effort spread across many mediocre platform-native posts.
- Build distribution hooks in at creation time: subheads that stand alone as social posts, modular sections that can be lifted out, and pull quotes or stats you already plan to turn into graphics. "A well-designed guide is a distribution kit in disguise."
- ORB as a funnel, routing attention from borrowed to rented to owned (discovery, engagement, conversion):
  - Borrowed: other people's audiences (podcasts, guest posts, partnerships) for breakthrough reach.
  - Rented: social platforms and ad networks; engagement, but you don't own the audience or the algorithm.
  - Owned: email list, blog, community; the only durable asset, and everything upstream should funnel here.
- Failure modes he names:
  - Spray-and-pray: posting everywhere with no flagship or repurposing plan.
  - Platform dependency: Facebook organic reach fell from roughly 20% to under 2%.
  - The ownership paradox: teams spend about 90% of effort on channels they don't control and neglect the owned assets that convert.

## Output format for a content strategy

- Corey's deliverable has three parts:
  - Content pillars: 3-5 with rationale, subtopic clusters, and how each connects to the product.
  - Priority topics: for each piece, the title, searchable/shareable/both, content type, target keyword and buyer stage, and the customer research backing it.
  - A topic cluster map showing how content interconnects.
- Questions he uses to start: what patterns emerge from the last 10 customer conversations, what questions keep coming up on sales calls, where competitors' content falls short, which unique customer insights aren't shared elsewhere, and which existing content drives the most conversions and why.
