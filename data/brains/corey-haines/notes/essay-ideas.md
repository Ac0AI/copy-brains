## Corey keeps a business-idea folder to train his opportunity-spotting

- Since age 19, Corey has kept a folder of random business ideas. He says there are hundreds and "most are laughably bad."
- His reasoning: the practice of identifying opportunities and thinking through what you'd do trains your brain to think a certain way, so you can find ideas actually worth pursuing.
- He also reviews how other people executed ideas he passed on, to compare his assumptions against real-life application and have better ideas and assumptions later.

## Ideas from his Baremetrics experience that others built

- **Demo-without-signup (no-code demo builder, now Navattic and others):**
  - One of the biggest drivers of traffic and conversions for Baremetrics was the public demo, plus other Open Startups making their accounts publicly viewable.
  - He believed every product should be demoable without signing up, and that companies doing so would earn more trust and a competitive advantage.
  - Everyone told him it was too hard technically and that companies would eventually bring it in house. His takeaway: "I shouldn't have listened to them."
- **Calendar scheduling (SavvyCal, by Derrick Reimer):**
  - From doing many demos at Baremetrics, he found Calendly's flaws obvious: switching tabs to schedule, no multiple durations, no quick personalization of links.
  - He credits Derrick Reimer with executing far better than he could have.
- **Integrations as a service (Prismatic, Paragon):**
  - As a marketer he saw integrations drive his own buying decisions; he wanted all tools to work together, and some were critical to core function. Baremetrics depends on partners like Stripe to ingest data.
  - One growth strategy he led was forging new partnerships to expand the addressable market. That led him to think of SaaS that lets companies add integrations faster.
- **Marketing attribution SaaS (HockeyStack):**
  - In 2020 he and a friend were set on building attribution for SaaS companies. Attribution was his biggest pain point at Baremetrics.
  - His thesis: web analytics must be tied to Stripe or pipeline CRM data.
  - They decided not to build it because of privacy headwinds, browsers phasing out cookies, and Apple obscuring referral data. He is glad he didn't, and still thinks attribution tech will get harder over time.

## Ideas he prototyped but killed (and the reasoning)

- **Job board SaaS (Job Boardly):** In 2019 he started a marketing job board, inspired by no-code and his love of Webflow. He built a hacky version with Webflow, Typeform and Zapier, quickly hit the limits of that stack, and concluded a dedicated platform for building and managing job boards would "kill it." He says he should have built it, and is now a Job Boardly customer.
- **HARO replacement (Featured, Help a B2B Writer):**
  - He used HARO off and on for backlinks; its email-only system made it hard to know if a submission would pay off.
  - He learned HARO's founder did millions in revenue in the first year and later sold it for a large sum, so it looked ripe for disruption.
  - He built a prototype on Circle called "Help a Creator Out" (HACO).
  - He killed it after realizing a two-sided marketplace that couldn't rely on SEO would take massive effort and time.

## Ideas born from personal annoyance with a product or process

- **Creatine tablets/pills/gummies (Create):** He had worked out consistently since college and hated making protein shakes. Even with premade shakes he had to add creatine powder, which made the texture grainy and clumpy. It seemed crazy that creatine only came as powder. He was one of Create's earliest customers and asked to invest.
- **Car insurance quote aggregator (The Zebra):** Each new car meant visiting every insurer's site, re-entering his info, getting bombarded with texts, emails and calls, then sorting out the best deal. He wanted to enter info once, get instantly comparable quotes, and check out. He says he wouldn't have known how to execute it.
- **Google Maps for indoors (HyperAR):** He hates shopping and dislikes store trips that require backtracking or asking a worker to escort him to products. He thought route planning and in-store navigation for finding items was a great idea.
- **Programmable desk display (TRMNL):** He saw a photo of a desk device showing that day's Shopify sales and wondered how to do it for Stripe, MRR or anything else. He had given up on hardware long ago, which is why he was "stoked" to invest in TRMNL, which he says has been hugely successful.

## Ideas spotted from market and technology shifts

- **AI call recording (Grain, Fathom):** When OpenAI's API launched and he saw the transcription demo, he "immediately knew" AI call recording would be a huge category. His logic: why ever take notes again if AI does it 10x more accurately, thoroughly and quickly. He calls it "a no-brainer."
- **Web book SaaS (Basecamp Workbook):**
  - Self-publishing has exploded in the last decade. EPUB and MOBI still dominate, but many books are gated on a website, such as Make by Pieter Levels.
  - Publishing a free book on a website is easy, but gating or charging for it requires a lot of custom code. That gap was his idea.

## Watch rentals: a lesson on capital and inventory

- Around 2015-2016, in college, he discovered MVMT watches and noticed the booming "subscription box" category. His equation: watches + subscription box = watch rentals (others now include Watch Lending Club).
- The problem he missed: you need a lot of capital to build rental inventory.
- The insight he only saw later: rental companies have a unique opportunity to decide what to buy by making items available to rent before buying them.
- He recalls even thinking about how to build the courage to tell his dad this was the company he'd start.
