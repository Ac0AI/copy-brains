## Community is built around member identity, with value flowing to members first

Corey's core strategic principles for community marketing:

- Build around a shared identity, not just the product. Members join because of the product but "stay because of the people and identity." Examples he gives: Indie Hackers (bootstrapped founders), r/homelab (tinkerers who self-host), the Figma community (designers who care about craft).
- Always define what identity the community reinforces for its members.
- Value must flow to members first. Every touchpoint should answer what the member gets: exclusive knowledge or early access, peer connections they can't get elsewhere, recognition and status, direct influence on the roadmap, and career opportunities, visibility or credibility.
- Design for the community flywheel from day one: members join, get value, engage, create content or help others, and that content helps new members discover the community. Every decision should be tested against whether it accelerates or slows the loop.

## Launching a community from zero

Corey's five-step playbook:

1. Recruit 20-50 founding members manually. DM the most engaged users, beta testers or fans. Don't open publicly until there is baseline activity.
2. Set the culture explicitly. Write guidelines that describe the vibe, not just the rules, including what great participation looks like.
3. Seed conversations before launch. Pre-populate channels with 5-10 posts that model the desired behavior: questions, wins, resources.
4. Do things that don't scale. Reply to every post, welcome every new member by name, host a weekly call. "You are buying social proof."
5. Define the core loop: the one action members should take weekly. Make it easy and reward it publicly.

## Growing an existing community

Corey's five-step approach:

1. Audit where members drop off. Are people joining but not posting, or posting once and vanishing? Find the leaky stage.
2. Build a new member journey: a pinned welcome post, an #introduce-yourself channel, a DM or email from a community manager, and a clear "start here" path.
3. Surface member wins publicly (projects, testimonials, milestones). This reinforces identity and shows participation is rewarded.
4. Run recurring rituals: weekly threads like "What are you working on?", monthly AMAs, seasonal challenges. "Rituals create habit."
5. Identify and invest in power users. He states that "1% of members generate 90% of value." Give them recognition, early access, moderator roles or product input.

## Brand ambassador and advocate programs

Corey's steps:

1. Identify candidates: people who already recommend the brand unprompted. Check reviews, social mentions and community posts.
2. Make the ask personal. Reach out 1:1 and explain why you chose them, not a generic form.
3. Offer meaningful benefits: exclusive access, swag, revenue share or public recognition, "not just 'early access to features.'"
4. Give them tools: referral links, shareable assets, key talking points, a private Slack channel.
5. Measure and iterate: track referral traffic, signups and engagement driven by advocates, then double down on what works.

## Community-led support and community models

Support deflection and retention playbook:

- Build a searchable knowledge base from the top community questions.
- Recognize helpers with "Community Expert" badges, leaderboards and shoutouts.
- Close the loop with product: when feedback drives a change, announce it publicly and credit the members who raised it.
- Monitor sentiment weekly to catch complaints or confusion before they become churn signals.

Choosing the shape of the community:

- Corey's reference material defines five community models, with examples: Support-Driven (GreenPal), Product-Development (Ydata), Education/Enablement (LiveAgent), and Founder-Led (Bento, Postaga). Each has a "best when" fit test tied to a primary goal.
- The Notion benchmark he cites as the north star for community-led growth at scale: 300+ ambassadors, 1M+ template downloads, and 25% of new users from community referrals.
- The role shifts with scale: Community Architect (0-100 members), Manager (100-1,000), Enabler (1,000+).

## Platform selection

Corey's guide to picking a platform:

- **Discord:** best for developer, gaming and creator communities with real-time chat. Watch for high noise, poor searchability and onboarding friction.
- **Slack:** best for B2B and professional communities familiar to SaaS buyers. Watch for free-tier history limits and a "feels like work" vibe.
- **Circle:** best for creator or course-based communities with clean UX. Less organic discovery; you must drive traffic.
- **Reddit:** best for high-volume public communities with SEO benefit. You don't own it and moderation is hard.
- **Facebook Groups:** best for consumer brands and older demographics. Organic reach is declining and algorithm dependent.
- **Forum (Discourse):** best for long-form technical communities that are SEO-rich. Slower velocity and higher effort to post.

## Community health metrics and warning signs

Metrics Corey tracks weekly:

- DAU/MAU ratio (stickiness): above 20% is healthy for most communities.
- New member post rate: % of new members who post within 7 days of joining.
- Thread reply rate: % of posts that get at least one reply.
- Churn/lurker ratio: members who joined but haven't posted in 30+ days.
- Content created by non-staff: % of posts not written by the company team.

Warning signs:

- Most posts come from the company team, not members.
- Questions go unanswered for more than 24 hours.
- The same 5 people account for 80%+ of engagement.
- New members stop posting after their intro message.

## Deliverables and how Corey wants community advice delivered

Output formats he defines: a Community Strategy Doc (platform, identity, core loop, 90-day launch plan), Channel Architecture, New Member Journey (pinned post, DM template, first-week prompts), Community Ritual Calendar, Ambassador Program Brief (criteria, benefits, outreach template, tracking plan), and a Health Audit Report (metrics, diagnosis, top 3 fixes).

His standard for advice: "Always be specific. Generic advice ('be consistent,' 'provide value') is not useful." Give something the user can act on today.

Intake questions he asks first: platform, stage (pre-launch, early, growing, established), primary business goal (retention, activation, word-of-mouth, support deflection), ideal member and motivation, existing users to seed from, and weekly time available. He pairs this skill with referrals, churn-prevention, social and customer-research.
