## Leaving a job to go full-time creator: the timing logic

Corey left Baremetrics in September 2020 to go full-time on his own projects. His reasoning for "why now" was risk, not readiness:
- He argues there's no perfect time to start a business, but the ideal time is before having kids and a mortgage.
- "I'd rather risk a few months of savings than my house or ability to put food on the table for my kids."
- He calls this playing on "easy mode."

His runway plan was to stay afloat with light consulting while building Swipe Files to pay his bills, with the eventual goal of bootstrapping a B2B SaaS company.

## The stairstep approach to bootstrapping, as Corey applied it

Corey loosely followed Rob Walling's "stairstep approach to bootstrapping": take entrepreneurship one step at a time, each step a bit harder and more rewarding than the last, because some business models demand bigger skill sets than others. His own steps:
- Step 1: Hey Marketers (heymarketers.com).
- Step 2: Refactoring Growth and Mental Models For Marketing.
- Step 3: Swipe Files (swipefiles.co).
- Step 4: SaaS (planned, details withheld at the time).

Takeaway: he built smaller, lower-risk products first, in order of difficulty, and used them to fund and prepare for a SaaS rather than jumping straight to it.

## Using Daniel Pink's Drive framework to justify entrepreneurship

Corey has been "obsessed" with the framework from Daniel Pink's *Drive*: motivation has three elements, autonomy, mastery and purpose. He maps entrepreneurship onto each:
- **Autonomy:** "Ownership is the ultimate vessel to direct your own life. Make up the rules. Set your schedule. Do things your way."
- **Mastery:** Double down on your strengths and delegate weaknesses. Choose which skills to improve, what to work on and how much.
- **Purpose:** Build a lifestyle aligned with your values, use profits to give charitably, and provide value to customers.

He says he's "just not that motivated by extrinsic rewards" (money, status, titles, recognition). Intrinsic rewards drive him.

## Regret minimization and "only intrinsic motivation lasts"

- Corey uses Jeff Bezos's regret-minimization framework: ask "In X years, will I regret not doing this?" His answer was "an unequivocal and resounding YES."
- He cites Daniel Vassallo's question, "What kind of work would I do if I had to do it forever?", from the post "Only Intrinsic Motivation Lasts."
- His conclusion: "I don't want to be an employee forever. I want to be a creator." He frames it as a long game: "It's going to be a lot of work, but I'm playing the long game."

## Entrepreneurs are normal people who take initiative

- Corey says hours of podcasts (Mixergy) and books (Rich Dad Poor Dad, The 4-Hour Workweek) led him to see that "Entrepreneurs are just normal people who take initiative," and that if he wanted to bring something into existence, "there's nothing stopping me."
- He endorses Joshua Levy's (Holloway) view: everything around us exists because someone put in the work, so if you want something to exist you can make it happen.
- He calls the "entrepreneurial itch" a curse: once you've seen people like you build things, "you can't unsee it."

## Starting a business is launching into space, not building a plane

- He cites the common analogy "jumping off a cliff and building a plane on the way down," but prefers his own: "launching into space and then figuring out where you want to go from there."
- He endorses his friend Corey Gwin's description of building Blurt: building it after work was like "building an escape pod," and after ejecting, "I've escaped, but where to?"
- Implication: leaving a job comes before having a fully fixed destination, and the direction gets figured out after launch.

## From "safe" accounting path to unconventional career

- Corey originally planned: graduate college, get a good job in accounting or finance, work hard, retire. He had wanted to be an inventor as a kid and chose accounting out of insecurity.
- A road-trip conversation with a friend who planned a non-traditional path (sales, a business, real estate) made him question "safe."
- He describes himself as an "architect" (vision, blueprint, meticulous execution) rather than a "tinkerer."
- Path afterward: graduated college, broke into tech, worked at two companies (including Baremetrics), then went full-time creator.
