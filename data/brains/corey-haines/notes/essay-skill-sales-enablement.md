## Sales enablement assets should be built for reps to use, not for marketing to admire

Corey's "sales-enablement" skill is a playbook for creating decks, one-pagers, objection docs, battle cards, win-loss reports, demo scripts, ROI calculators, proposals, playbooks and persona cards. It is designed to produce collateral that helps a B2B sales team close deals. Before writing anything, Corey has the agent check for existing product marketing context and gather four things: value proposition and differentiators, sales motion (deal size, cycle length, personas), the specific assets needed, and what exists today.

## Core principles: trusted, situation-specific, scannable, outcome-tied, defensible

- **Sales uses what sales trusts.** Involve reps in creation and use their language, not marketing's. "If reps rewrite your deck before sending it, you wrote the wrong deck." Test drafts with top performers first.
- **Situation-specific, not generic.** Tailor to persona, deal stage and use case. A CTO deck differs from a VP of Sales deck; a post-meeting one-pager differs from a trade show handout.
- **Scannable over comprehensive.** Reps need information in 3 seconds, not 30. Use bold headers, short bullets and visual hierarchy. If a rep can't find the answer mid-call, the doc has failed.
- **Tie to business outcomes.** Every claim connects to revenue, efficiency or risk reduction. Replace "AI-powered analytics" with "cut reporting time by 80%."
- **Claims reps can defend.** Reps repeat what is written to buyers who can check it, so anything disprovable in a minute costs more than it gains.
  - "Why we win" needs evidence: win/loss interviews, call recordings, customer quotes, or CRM reasons backed by one of those. Without evidence, write advantages as hypotheses and state what would confirm them.
  - Competitor claims are dated and sourced, say "not listed on their site" rather than "they don't have it," and never state a competitor's motives as fact (reasoning about motives is fine if labeled a guess).
  - Include where you lose. A competitive doc that admits when the competitor is the better fit is one reps will trust in front of a buyer.

## Sales deck: story arc, one idea per slide, tailored by buyer

Corey's 10-12 slide framework:
1. Current world problem
2. Cost of the problem (time, money, risk)
3. The shift creating urgency
4. Your approach
5. Product walkthrough (3-4 key workflows, "not a feature tour")
6. Proof points (metrics, logos, analyst recognition)
7. One case study told well
8. Implementation and timeline
9. ROI and payback
10. Transparent pricing
11. Next steps with a timeline

Principles: tell a story arc (the world has a problem, there's a better way, here's proof, here's how to get there); one idea per slide; design for presenting, not reading, with minimal text.

Customize by buyer:
- **Technical buyer:** emphasize architecture, security, integrations, API; de-emphasize ROI math.
- **Economic buyer:** emphasize ROI, payback, total cost, risk; de-emphasize technical detail.
- **Champion:** emphasize internal selling points, quick wins, peer proof; de-emphasize deep technical or financial detail.

## One-pagers, leave-behinds and case study briefs

Use cases: post-meeting recap, champion internal selling ("arm your champion to sell for you"), trade show handout.

One-pager structure: problem in one sentence, your solution, 3 differentiators, one strong proof point, and a CTA with next step and contact info. Design rules:
- One page literally (front only, or front and back at most).
- Scannable in 30 seconds.
- Include logo, website and a specific contact, "not info@."
- Clean and brand-matched; "a sales tool, not a brand piece."

Sales case study briefs differ from marketing ones: short, outcome-focused and tagged for retrieval. Structure: customer profile, challenge (2-3 sentences), solution (1-2 sentences), 3 before/after metrics, one pull quote, and tags (industry, use case, size, persona). Organize by industry, use case and company size so reps can find the right one instantly.

## Objection handling: five-part entries, two formats, and a live-deal mode

Objection categories: price, timing, competition, authority, status quo, technical.

For each objection, document: the exact statement as reps hear it, why they say it (the real concern), the response approach (acknowledge and redirect), a specific proof point, and a follow-up question to keep the conversation moving. Maintain two formats: a one-screen quick-reference table for live calls (objection, one-line response, proof point) and a detailed doc for prep and training (context, talk tracks, role-plays).

**Live deal objection** (e.g. a CFO says a competitor is half the price and the call is in an hour). Answer that deal, not a generic doc:
1. Pin down who said it, their role, exact words, deal stage and what happens next. If time is short, state assumptions and ask only what would change the answer.
2. Name the likely concern behind it and what in the deal points to it. If two readings fit, give a question that tells them apart.
3. Give one response the rep can say as written: acknowledge, address the real concern, one proof point, end with a question.
4. Use only proof the rep can stand behind. Don't invent a stat, customer or competitor number, and don't repeat the buyer's competitor claim as fact. "Half the price" may not cover the same scope, so have the rep ask what's in their number.
5. Say when not to fight: if the objection reveals a real fit gap, suggest qualifying out or narrowing scope.

Afterward, the rep should log the objection and outcome; live objections feed the library and win-loss analysis.

## Battle cards and win-loss analysis

A battle card is a reusable one-competitor guide used across many deals. Corey's rule: if the request doesn't name a competitor, ask in one short question before drafting, because "a polished card about the wrong competitor looks finished, so nobody questions it."

Battle card structure:
1. When you'll see them (segments, deal sizes, triggers)
2. Why buyers consider them, in the buyer's words
3. Where they're strong (honest, so reps aren't blindsided)
4. Where we win, and for whom, tied to evidence
5. Discovery questions that surface fit criteria without trashing them
6. Top objections with one-line responses and proof
7. When to walk away
8. Last verified date and what changed

Keep it current: re-verify pricing and packaging quarterly and whenever reps hear something new; tag every competitor fact with source and date; keep it to one page.

Win-loss analysis workflow:
1. Inventory sources and their bias: CRM loss reasons (rep-entered, skewed to price and timing), call transcripts, buyer interviews (most candid, smallest sample).
2. Include wins, since a loss reason means little without deals won against the same competitor.
3. Segment before counting (competitor, segment, deal size, stage lost). "We lose on price" often hides "we lose mid-market deals to one competitor on onboarding."
4. Code each deal with one primary reason, any secondary ones, a supporting quote and the source; use one coder or double-code a sample.
5. Report counts with sample sizes: "6 of 9 losses to Acme cite onboarding time" beats "onboarding is a top theme." Under about 5 deals is a signal to watch, not a finding.
6. Turn findings into assets. A deal with only a one-word CRM reason is low-confidence until confirmed by transcripts or interviews. Then update battle cards, the objection library and discovery questions, and send product gaps to product.

## ROI calculators and value props by persona

Calculator design:
- **Inputs** (prospect-provided current state): time on manual processes, current tool costs, error rates, team size.
- **Calculations:** time saved, cost reduction (tools, headcount, errors), revenue impact (faster deals, higher conversion).
- **Outputs:** annual ROI percentage, payback period in months, total 3-year value.

Implementation options: spreadsheet (fastest, easy to customize per deal, fits inside sales), web tool (polished, captures leads, worth it at high deal volume), or slide-based ROI embedded in the deck (good for executives).

Lead with the right value prop by persona:
- **CTO / VP Eng:** technical superiority and integration depth.
- **VP Sales:** revenue impact and time savings per rep.
- **CFO:** ROI, cost reduction, financial predictability.
- **End user:** time saved and frustration eliminated.

## Demo scripts, proposals, playbooks and persona cards

**Demo script structure:**
- Opening (2 min): context, agenda, confirm goals.
- Discovery recap (3 min): summarize learnings, confirm priorities.
- Solution walkthrough (15-20 min): 3-4 workflows mapped to their pain.
- Interaction points throughout, not only at the end.
- Close (5 min): summarize value, propose next steps with a timeline.

Talk track types: discovery call (30 min), first demo (30-45), technical deep-dive (45-60), executive overview (20-30). Principles: demo after discovery ("if you don't know their pain, you're guessing which features matter"), customize to their terminology, data and workflow, and leave time for questions: "A demo where the prospect doesn't talk is a demo that doesn't close."

**Proposals:** executive summary (1 page max), proposed solution mapped to requirements, implementation plan, investment, next steps. Mirror discovery-call language, reference pains they named, include only same-industry or same-use-case case studies, and name stakeholders spoken with. Mistakes: too long (aim for 5-7 pages; over 10 won't be read), too generic (customize the exec summary at minimum), and burying the price.

**Playbooks:** include buyer profile, qualification criteria (BANT, MEDDIC or your own), discovery questions by topic, top 10 objections, competitive positioning, demo flow per persona, and email templates (follow-up, proposal, check-in, breakup). Build one for a new product launch, new market segment or new-hire ramp. Playbooks die when not updated: review quarterly, get input from top reps, remove outdated content, and assign an owner, since "if nobody owns it, it rots."

**Buyer persona cards:** fields are role/title, goals, pains, top 3-5 objections, evaluation criteria, buying process, and the one messaging angle that resonates most. Persona types: economic buyer, technical buyer, end user, champion, and blocker (understand their concern to neutralize it).

## Output formats and how the skill scopes itself

Deliverable per asset:
- **Sales deck:** slide-by-slide outline with headline, body copy and speaker notes.
- **One-pager:** full copy with layout guidance.
- **Objection doc:** table of objection, response, proof point, follow-up.
- **Live objection:** likely concern, a say-as-written response, proof, follow-up question.
- **Win-loss report:** scope, findings with counts and quotes, by-competitor breakdown, asset updates.
- **Demo script:** scene-by-scene with timing, talk track and interaction points.
- **ROI calculator:** inputs, formulas, output display with sample data.
- **Playbook:** structured doc with table of contents.
- **Persona card:** one page per persona.
- **Proposal:** section-by-section copy with customization notes.

If context is missing, ask: which collateral, who uses it (AE, SDR, champion, prospect), which sales stage, the target persona, and the top 3 objections. Adjacent work is routed elsewhere: competitor comparison pages to the competitors skill, buyer interviews to customer-research, website copy to copywriting, cold outreach to cold-email, offer structure to offers, pricing to pricing, and positioning to product-marketing. For partner sales enablement it points to the Introw integration (partner engagement tracking, deal registration, mutual action plans).
