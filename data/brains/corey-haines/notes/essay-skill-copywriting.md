## Situation context: "Copywriting" skill as a Corey-brain source

## Corey's rule for diagnosing copy before writing it
- Before drafting, Corey sorts the job into three situations, because "most bad AI copy comes from imposing the model's taste on a marketer who already made good choices."
  - **A. Blank or slop:** a one-line ask or generic company-speak. Build the argument from the brief and product context. Latitude on angle, never on facts: no invented stats, customers, or results.
  - **B. Real voice present:** a draft with an angle or recognizable voice, even if rough. Restore it. Keep the argument and vocabulary; fix the buried lead, vague lines, AI tells, and structure.
  - **C. Point edit:** "fix this headline." Change only what was asked, plus the minimum needed to make it fit.
- He states the diagnosis in one line with evidence before writing.
- Reading taste from the input (B and C): vocabulary is a voice choice (if they write "stuff" and "ship," don't upgrade to "solutions" and "deploy"), where they spent words is a priority, what they left out is a scope choice, and their angle is the positioning.
- A writing sample beats the default voice: match its sentence length, punctuation habits, paragraph openings, and a few words you wouldn't normally use.

## Corey's drafting protocol: decompose, lock, gate
- Don't write a page in one pass. Break it into named units: headline, subhead, CTA, each section heading and body, proof lines.
- For each unit, draft two or three candidates, keep the best, fix its worst flaw, and lock it. Then assemble and read the whole page once for flow. In a voice-present job, lines that already work are locked from the start.
- **Three-question gate** for every line written or kept:
  1. Can the reader picture it?
  2. Can it be proven true or false?
  3. Could a competitor write this exact sentence? (the swap test; if yes, it fails)
- Scoring: three passes, keep it. Two, sharpen the weak one. One or none, rewrite from the facts or flag what's missing with `[NEED: ...]`.
- **Ration the punch:** most lines plain, specific, declarative. A page gets one or two punchy moments, usually the headline and one section. When every line tries to land, none do.
- **Leave-it-alone test:** for each change, ask if it's measurably better or just different. Revert changes you can't justify.

## Corey's core copywriting principles
- **Clarity over cleverness.** If forced to choose, choose clear. He cites clearer positioning and copy as associated with +81% conversions, a 38% shorter sales cycle, 28% lower CAC, and 175% more referrals.
- **Benefits over features.** Features are what it does; benefits are what that means for the customer.
- **Specificity over vagueness.** Vague: "Save time on your workflow." Specific: "Cut your weekly reporting from 4 hours to 15 minutes."
- **Customer language over company language.** Mirror voice-of-customer from reviews, interviews, and support tickets.
- **One idea per section,** building a logical flow down the page.
- **Claims must be supportable, and declines must name their unlock.** Write only claims the source material supports. For an unsupported claim, name it, say why it falls short, offer the closest supportable alternative, and state what evidence would unlock it (a customer-review export, a written guarantee policy, a certification, a citable study). A decline with an unlock path is an action item; without one it's a dead end.
- Style rules: simple over complex ("use" not "utilize"), specific over vague (avoid "streamline," "optimize," "innovative"), active over passive, confident over qualified (cut "almost," "very," "really"), show outcomes instead of using adverbs, and honest over sensational (fabricated stats or testimonials erode trust and create legal liability). Remove exclamation points.

## Corey's "no AI tells" list
He says buyers reject copy that reads as AI-written, and a reader who spots a tell doubts the claims around it. Write like a senior copywriter: specific, uneven in rhythm, built from facts.
- **Never write:**
  1. Contrast reveals: "It's not X, it's Y," "Not just X, but Y." State Y directly, with the reason.
  2. Negation lists: "No setup call, no templates, no waiting on IT." Say what does happen. State a real absence once, near the CTA ("No card required").
  3. Trailing pile-ons: a full claim followed by more comma clauses. End the sentence at the claim.
  4. Self-answered questions and colon reveals: "The result? 3x faster." "The best part: it learns." FAQ questions and a reader's own question ("Need to share a screenshot?") are fine.
  5. Stock openers: "In today's fast-paced world," "Whether you're X or Y," "Here's the thing," "Say goodbye to," "Unlock the power of," "Take it to the next level."
  6. Em dashes in short copy (headlines, subheads, ads, social posts, subject lines).
- **Keep rare:** at most one fragment and one list of three per section; words like seamless, robust, powerful, unlock, streamline (replace with the fact they stand in for).
- If a line would work unchanged on a competitor's site, rewrite it, or flag `[NEED: differentiator]` if the differentiator is missing.
- Before delivering: search for the banned patterns, read every sentence over 20 words, read it aloud. Fix from the facts, because swapping synonyms creates new tells. For a full rewrite, build a writer first (who's writing, what they know cold, what they'd cut, what they think) and check for second-generation tells such as wisdom-shaped sentences and tidy closers.

## Headlines, CTAs, and above-the-fold structure
- **Headline:** the single most important message; communicate the core value proposition; specific beats generic.
- Formulas: "{Achieve outcome} without {pain point}", "The {category} for {audience}", "Never {unpleasant event} again", "{Question highlighting main pain point}".
- Structure the hero as a transformation (current discomfort → better vision → path to action, the Human Action Model), and run every headline through the "Now you can" test.
- **Subheadline:** expands the headline, adds specificity, 1-2 sentences max.
- **Be direct.** Weak: "Slack lets you share files instantly, from documents to images, directly in your conversations." Better: "Need to share a screenshot? Send as many documents, images, and audio files as your heart desires."
- A rhetorical question that names the reader's pain can open a section well ("Hate returning stuff to Amazon?", "Tired of chasing approvals?"). Analogies and humor help when they fit the brand and don't cost clarity.
- **CTA copy:** avoid Submit, Sign Up, Learn More, Click Here, Get Started. Use Start Free Trial, Get [Specific Thing], See [Product] in Action, Create Your First [Thing], Download the Guide.
- CTA formula: [Action Verb] + [What They Get] + [Qualifier if needed]. Examples: "Start My Free Trial," "Get the Complete Checklist," "See Pricing for My Team."
- CTAs should be action-oriented, body copy clearer, headlines bolder, while keeping voice consistent.

## Page structure and page-specific guidance
- **Core sections:**
  - Social proof: logos, stats, testimonials.
  - Problem/pain: show you understand their situation.
  - Solution/benefits: 3-5 key benefits connected to outcomes.
  - How it works: 3-4 steps to reduce perceived complexity.
  - Objection handling: FAQ, comparisons, guarantees.
  - Final CTA: recap value, repeat the CTA, add risk reversal.
- **Homepage:** serve multiple audiences without being generic, lead with the broadest value proposition, and give clear paths for different visitor intents.
- **Landing page:** single message, single CTA, headline matched to the ad or traffic source, complete argument on one page.
- **Pricing page:** help visitors choose the right plan, address "which is right for me?" anxiety, make the recommended plan obvious.
- **Feature page:** connect feature → benefit → outcome, show use cases and examples, give a clear path to try or buy.
- **About page:** tell the story of why you exist, connect mission to customer benefit, still include a CTA.

## What Corey gathers before writing any page copy
- **Check existing context first:** read `.agents/product-marketing.md` (or `.claude/product-marketing.md`) before asking questions, and check its Learnings section so already-tested angles and claims aren't re-proposed.
- **Page purpose:** what type of page, and the ONE primary action visitors should take.
- **Audience:** the ideal customer, the problem, objections or hesitations, and the language they use to describe the problem.
- **Product/offer:** what's being sold, what makes it different, the key transformation or outcome, and proof points (numbers, testimonials, case studies).
- **Context:** where traffic comes from (ads, organic, email) and what visitors already know on arrival.
- **Voice before writing:** formality (casual, professional but friendly, formal), and personality (playful or serious, bold or understated, technical or accessible).
- **Output format:** page copy organized by section, annotations explaining each key choice, 2-3 alternatives with rationale for headlines and CTAs, and meta title and description if relevant. For a point edit, return only the changed lines with a one-line reason each.
