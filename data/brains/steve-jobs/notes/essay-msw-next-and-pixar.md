## NeXT's origin came from a conversation with a biochemist

- Weeks after founding NeXT, Steve told Newsweek the original insight came from reading biochemistry and recombinant DNA literature.
- He called Paul Berg, a pioneer of recombinant DNA techniques, said "I'm ignorant about this stuff, but I've got a bunch of questions about how it works," and asked him to lunch at Stanford.
- Berg showed him how gene repair experiments ran in a wet laboratory, taking a week or two or three each.
- Steve noticed the work "smells a lot like some of the concepts you find in computer science."
- He asked, "Why don't you simulate these on a computer?" His reasoning: experiments would run faster, and "someday every freshman microbiology student in the country can play with the Paul Berg recombinant software."
- He called it "a landmark lunch" because it got his "wheels turning again."
- The pattern: he cold-called an expert in a field he was ignorant of, asked naive questions, and found where computing could transform their workflow.

## What drew Steve to Pixar: graphics well beyond anything he had seen

- Steve met Ed Catmull, who ran the computer division of Lucasfilm, in 1985.
- He had been involved in graphics most of his life:
  - The Apple II was "the first real color computer that you could get your hands on."
  - The Macintosh was graphics.
  - The LaserWriter was graphics.
- All of that was 2-D. Apple had done some 3-D work and he knew the field.
- Even so, what Catmull's team was doing was "way ahead of anything I'd ever seen anyone do."
- He was drawn by a team clearly ahead of the state of the art in a field he already knew well.

## Pixar's early strategy failed: pay the bills with products

- In 2003 Steve told filmmaker Leslie Iwerks the early strategy was "find a way to pay the bills."
- In the background, Pixar developed animation software, and John Lasseter made a succession of short films on the way to Toy Story.
- The company tried to fund itself with other products, such as the Pixar Image Computer and software, to "just buy time."
- Steve's verdict: "That strategy really turned out not to work."
- In hindsight: "we would have been better off just funding the animation efforts and not trying to pay the bills through these other products."
- In the end, "I just ended up writing checks to keep the company going," which went on for ten years.
- The lesson: funding the core creative work directly beats distracting the company with side products to cover costs.

## Technology should disappear into the experience

- Steve saw "magic" in Pixar's short films from the beginning.
- Most Pixar technology required expertise to appreciate, but "you didn't have to know anything about the technology to enjoy the film."
- He called this "incredibly refreshing" and said it "pointed the way to where we wanted to go."
- The principle: "We didn't want to have to convince people that our technology was great — we knew it was great."
- The goal was "to use our technology to make something where nobody needed to know anything about the technology to love it."
- That is what Pixar ended up doing.

## Celebrating Tin Toy: team credit and an industry milestone (1989)

- On March 30, 1989, Steve emailed all NeXT employees after the Academy Awards under the subject "A great day and a half."
- He congratulated everyone "for a real TEAM effort" at NeXT, and noted Pixar had won too.
- Pixar won the short animated film Oscar for Tin Toy, the first computer-generated film ever to win an Academy Award.
- It beat several very good traditionally animated films.
- His framing: "The computer graphics industry just achieved a major milestone, and Pixar led the way!"
- He shared a win from a sister company with his whole NeXT team, treating it as a shared achievement and a proof point for his belief in computer graphics.
