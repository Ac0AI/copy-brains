## Steve and Bill as partners, not rivals

- Steve credits Bill with building the first software company before most of the industry knew what one was, and with a business model that worked well for the whole industry. He says the biggest thing was that Bill "was really focused on software before almost anybody else had a clue."
- Steve says the two of them stand in for the people behind their companies: "everything that's been done at Microsoft and at Apple has been done by just remarkable people. None of which are sitting up here."
- On the 1997 deal, he says Apple was in serious trouble, and "if the game was a zero sum game, where for Apple to win, Microsoft had to lose, then Apple was going to lose."
- His view was that "Apple wasn't going to beat Microsoft. Apple didn't have to beat Microsoft. Apple had to remember who Apple was, because it'd forgotten who Apple was."
- He argues that too many people at Apple and in its customer base were stuck in that win/lose mindset. He wanted to break it, partly because Microsoft was the biggest outside developer for the Mac.
- He calls the Mac development team at Microsoft one of Apple's best developer relationships.
- On the "I'm a Mac" ads, he says their art "is not to be mean, but it's actually for the guys to like each other." He says "PC guy's great. He's got a big heart."

## Apple as a software company

- Steve says the iPod exists because the Japanese consumer electronics companies that invented and owned portable music "couldn't conceive of and implement the appropriate software."
- He says "an iPod's really just software." That means software in the device, software on the PC or Mac, and software in the cloud for the store, all "in a beautiful box."
- He applies the same view to the Mac ("it's OS X. It's in a beautiful box") and to the iPhone: "it's software."
- His summary: "Apple views itself as a software company. And there aren't very many software companies left."
- He does not expect the Mac to take 80% of the PC market. Apple is "really happy when our market share goes up a point."
- He quotes Alan Kay: "people that love software want to do their own hardware."
- On hardware and software coupling, he says that in the consumer market, outside of Windows on PCs, it's hard to find examples of decoupled software and hardware working super well. Examples of coupling working well are more common. He admits "nobody knows the answer" yet, and that both approaches may work.

## Rich clients, the cloud and the iPhone Maps example

- Steve's example is Google Maps on the iPhone. Google provided the back-end data through an API, and Apple wrote the client app.
- The result was "the same set of data coming off the server, but the experience you have using it is unbelievable." He says it blows away any Google Maps client and "you can't do that stuff in a browser."
- His conclusion: "the marriage of some really great client apps with some really great cloud services is incredibly powerful," and right now it can be far more powerful than a browser alone.
- He says the browser is improving at persistent state and offline use, but "it's happening fairly slowly."
- He also notes that hardware is getting good enough to run rich clients on cheaper, lower-power devices.

## Partnering and focus

- Steve says "we don't think one company can do everything. So you've got to partner with people that are really good at stuff."
- Apple partners on search ("we're not trying to be great at search") and on map back ends, and focuses on being "that consumer's device, and that consumer's experience wrapped around all of this information... in a coherent product."
- He builds a service itself only when nothing good exists. For iTunes, "there wasn't a music delivery service that was any good. And we had to do one, so we'll do one."
- He says "life's complex," and that it's "really hard for one company to do everything."
- He concedes that Apple was not good at partnering early on because Woz and he "started the company based on doing the whole banana."
- By contrast, he says Bill and Microsoft "learned how to partner with people really well." Steve says Apple would have been well served by more of that, and didn't learn it until a few decades later.

## Where PCs and post-PC devices are heading

- Steve describes the PC as resilient and as having been reborn several times: first as the productivity machine, then as the internet machine, then as the "digital hub" for cameras, camcorders and sharing.
- He expects the PC, as a general-purpose device, to keep morphing (tablet, notebook, desktop) and to be tied more tightly to back-end internet services.
- He expects an explosion of "post-PC devices," meaning focused, less general-purpose devices such as phones and iPods, and says that category will keep being very innovative.
- He says the really radical rethinking will happen on post-PC devices, because they are a clean slate without a legacy of "zillions of apps."
- On PCs, he says innovation has to be tempered because users are familiar with what they have: "they don't want a car with six wheels, they like the car with four wheels. They don't want to drive with a joystick, they like the steering wheel."
- On the phone, he says that five years out he doesn't know what will be on it, because he wouldn't have predicted maps five years earlier. "The art of it is balancing what's on there, and what's not on there. It's the editing function."
- He calls the phone primarily a communications device.
- He says "everything's a computer in a different form factor. So what?" What matters is how consumers use it: "who cares what's inside it anymore?"

## Letting go of the past

- Asked about regrets, Steve says he could have done many things better in his first stint at Apple, and that things after he left "were wrong turns."
- He says "it doesn't matter... you kind of gotta let go of that stuff."
- When he returned to Apple ten years earlier, he gave the museum and the old papers and machines to Stanford and "cleared out the cobwebs," saying "let's stop looking backwards here. It's all about what happens tomorrow."
- His summary: "let's go invent tomorrow, rather than worrying about what happened yesterday."

## Passion, talent scouting and legacy

- Steve's advice to a founder: "you have to have a lot of passion for what you're doing and it's totally true. And the reason is, because it's so hard that if you don't any rational person would give up."
- He says the people who end up successful in society's eyes are usually those who loved what they did and so could persevere. Those who didn't love it "quit, because they're sane."
- He calls passion "the high order bit."
- His second point: "you've got to be a really good talent scout. Because no matter how smart you are, you need a team of great people."
- He says that means sizing people up quickly, making decisions without knowing them too well, hiring, refining your intuition, and helping build an organization that "can eventually just build itself."
- On legacy: "I don't think about legacy much. I just think about being able to get up every day and go in and hang around these great people and hopefully create something that other people will love as much as we do."
- He says he and Bill are "two of the luckiest guys on the planet" because they found what they loved to do and went to work every day "with super bright people for 30 years."
- He says he never cared much about money, having grown up lower middle class. Apple's early success meant he didn't have to care about it, so he could focus on work and later his family.
- He says the world is better because Bill's goal "isn't to be the richest guy in the cemetery."

## Products, services and retail

- Steve says the most interesting internet developments are new services that help people "navigate through life a little more efficiently."
- He says the best case is when "you don't have to convince them they have a problem that this solves... they go, Oh my God, I need this."
- On .Mac, he agrees it hasn't been developed much: "we'll make up for lost time in the near future."
- On entertainment, he says people want to enjoy content "when they want it, and how they want it, on the device that they want it on." He sees that as good for content owners ("more people wanting to enjoy your content more often in more different ways, that's why you're in business"), though transitions are hard.
- On older users, he says built-in video cameras sold strongly to seniors, who buy them to stay in touch with grandchildren and video conference more than younger people.
- Apple's retail "One to One" personal training had reached a run rate of a million one-hour sessions a year, about a year after starting. Members pay about $99 a year, and many are seniors learning things like Office and video conferencing.
- Steve says Apple had a little under 200 retail stores at the time.
