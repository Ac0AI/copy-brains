## The iPhone: why Apple built it and how the team decided it was possible

- Steve says everyone at Apple owned a cellphone and hated it: "The software was terrible. The hardware wasn't very good." Friends outside Apple hated theirs too.
- The market was huge. About a billion phones shipped per year: almost an order of magnitude more than music players and four times the number of PCs.
- The challenge as he framed it: "Let's make a great phone that we fall in love with."
- Apple already had the pieces: miniaturization from the iPod and the sophisticated OS X operating system from the Mac.
- Putting an OS as sophisticated as OS X inside a phone had never been tried. There was a big internal debate over whether it could be done.
- Steve adjudicated: "We're going to do it. Let's try." His reasoning was that the smartest software people said they could, so they should get a shot. "And they did."
- He saw a wave coming: nearly all future consumer electronics would have software as the primary technology. Apple could write the operating system, desktop apps like iTunes, device software and cloud back-end software, and make them work seamlessly.
- Few companies can do all of that. He saw no one who could make that contribution to phones: "None of the handset manufacturers really are strong in software."
- Rule he states: don't enter a business where you don't own or control the primary technology, "because you'll get your head handed to you."

## Building products you want yourself, with no market research

- Steve says Apple built iTunes because "we all love music," then wanted to carry whole libraries around, which led to the iPod. "The first few hundred customers were us."
- "It's not about pop culture, and it's not about fooling people, and it's not about convincing people that they want something they don't. We figure out what we want."
- The discipline is thinking through whether many other people will want it too: "That's what we get paid to do."
- He cites Henry Ford: asked what they wanted, customers "would have told me 'A faster horse.'" You can't ask people what the next big thing is.
- "We do no market research. We don't hire consultants." The only consultancy he hired in about 10 years was one firm to analyze Gateway's retail strategy so Apple would not repeat its mistakes when launching stores.
- The iTunes Music Store came from the view that buying music electronically would be great, not from a plan to redefine the music industry. All music eventually going electronic seemed like "writing on the wall."

## Focus means saying no

- "Apple is a $30 billion company, yet we've got less than 30 major products." Past great consumer electronics companies had thousands.
- Steve's definition: "People think focus means saying yes to the thing you've got to focus on. But that's not what it means at all. It means saying no to the hundred other good ideas that there are. You have to pick carefully."
- He is "as proud of many of the things we haven't done as the things we have done."
- Example: the PDA. Apple was pressured for years to build one. He realized 90% of PDA users only took information out of it on the road and did not put information in. Cellphones would soon do that, shrinking the PDA market to a fraction of its size.
- Apple passed. Had it entered, it would not have had the resources for the iPod, and "we probably wouldn't have seen it coming."
- "We don't get a chance to do that many things, and every one should be really excellent. Because this is our life. Life is brief, and then you die." He notes the team could be in a monastery in Japan, sailing, playing golf or running other companies, but chose this: "So it better be damn good. It better be worth it."

## Hiring and why great people join Apple

- For senior hires, "competence is the ante. They have to be really smart." The real question: will they fall in love with Apple? If so, "everything else will take care of itself" and they will do what's best for Apple, not for themselves or for Steve.
- "Recruiting is hard. It's just finding the needles in the haystack." Apple does it itself and spends a lot of time on it. Steve has taken part in hiring maybe 5,000-plus people.
- A one-hour interview can't tell you enough, so it comes down to gut: how does he feel about the person, and what are they like when challenged?
- He asks everyone "Why are you here?" The answers themselves don't matter: "It's the meta-data."
- Why people want to work at Apple: you can't make those products anywhere else. PC companies lost their engineering, and consumer electronics companies don't understand software. "Apple's the only company that has everything under one roof."
- Example: no one else could make a MacBook Air, because Apple controls both hardware and OS. "There is no intimate interaction between Windows and a Dell notebook."

## How Steve runs the company: the top 100, Monday meetings, and being demanding

- Apple has 25,000 people, about 10,000 of them in stores. Steve's job is to work with roughly the top 100 people, some of them key individual contributors rather than vice presidents.
- He moves good ideas around that group, gets people debating them, and has different people quietly explore different aspects.
- Delegation: "When you hire really good people you have to give them a piece of the business and let them run with it." He still kibitzes, but wants them making decisions as good as or better than his own. To do that they must know every part of the business, not just their own.
- Every Monday he reviews the whole business: last week's sales, every product in development, products with trouble, and products where demand exceeds supply. The agenda is 80% the same each week, and they walk down it.
- "We don't have a lot of process at Apple, but that's one of the few things we do just to all stay on the same page."
- On being demanding: "My job is to not be easy on people. My job is to make them better." That means pulling things together across the company, clearing the way, getting resources for key projects, and pushing great people toward "more aggressive visions."
- On succession: he made Tim Cook COO and gave him the Mac division, and "he's done brilliantly." Apple has capable people and the board would have good CEO choices. His job is "to make the whole executive team good enough to be successors."

## Resetting when the work isn't great: Toy Story and the iPhone enclosure

- At Pixar, during Toy Story, the team admitted the story wasn't great and stopped production for five months. They paid everyone to "twiddle their thumbs" while the story was perfected. Without that courage, there would have been no Toy Story as it is, "and there probably would have never been a Pixar."
- Pixar called it the "story crisis." He says there has been one on every film since. They no longer stop for five months, but a moment always comes when it isn't working, and "it's so easy to fool yourself."
- The same happened with the iPhone. The enclosure design stayed until very close to the introduction. One Monday he said: "I just don't love this. I can't convince myself to fall in love with this. And this is the most important product we've ever done."
- Apple pushed the reset button, went back through the many models and ideas, and ended up with the shipped iPhone, "dramatically better."
- It was hell: the team had to discard a year of work and work twice as hard. Their response: "Sign us up."
- "This is not just engineering and science. There is art, too." In a crisis you may not be sure you'll make it, but Apple always has, which builds some confidence. The key: "we're not all terrified at the same time."

## Owning the whole stack, the iPod turnaround, and Apple retail

- Owning the OS lets Apple innovate faster than Dell or HP, who wait on Microsoft's timetable. Vista took "seven or eight years." Apple sets its own priorities, thinks holistically from the customer's view, and can adapt the OS for the iPhone and iPod.
- Apple's DNA is as a consumer company, focused on the individual customer "voting thumbs up or thumbs down." Its job is to take responsibility for the complete user experience: "if it's not up to par, it's our fault, plain and simply."
- The iPod broke the OS "glass ceiling." Mac share had sat at 4% to 5% for years, which made him wonder whether people cared. The iPod captured 70% share and proved Apple innovation, engineering and design mattered: "a great shot in the arm."
- Next step: "We made more. We worked harder." He says Mac share was rising every quarter, growing four times faster than the industry. Intel processors and running PC apps alongside Mac apps helped, and people realized they didn't have to put up with Windows.
- Retail logic: Mac loyalists will drive to a destination, but Windows owners won't take a 20-minute drive on the risk they won't like a Mac. Put stores in malls or on streets they walk by, cutting the risk "from a 20-minute drive to 20 footsteps." So Apple chose high-traffic locations, and "it works."
- On technology waves: they can be seen years ahead, unfold slowly, and the work is choosing wisely which to surf.

## Apple TV as a "hobby" and investing through downturns

- On Apple TV: everyone has tried a great living room product and failed, including Apple. That's why he calls it a hobby, "not a business yet."
- Lesson from the first version: it helped people view iTunes content from a Mac or PC on a widescreen TV, but photos on the big screen were "frosting on the cake, but it's not the cake." What people wanted was movies.
- Take 2: Apple got all the major studios to license movies for rental. About 600 movies were then in iTunes, with thousands planned later that year. The price dropped to $229. His verdict: "I think it's got a shot."
- On downturns, he recalls the dot-com bust. He told the company Apple would "invest our way through the downturn" with no layoffs, because so much effort went into hiring people in the first place.
- Apple kept funding and raised its R&D budget so it would be ahead of competitors when the downturn ended. "It worked," and he said Apple would do the same again.
