## Steve's reaction to Avie's "sell the company" email: Apple is worth saving

When Avie Tevanian (ran software engineering at NeXT, then at Apple) said the bad Apple culture was returning and suggested it might be time to sell the company, Steve answered the same night, August 14, 1997. He said he had felt the same way lately: that Apple employees "don't deserve to be saved."

- Steve's complaint was about missing accountability. Employees "think they work so hard", but he does not see it.
- They also "think they have no responsibility for any of the mess that Apple's in."
- He lists who made the mess: the people who "engineered and shipped such bad products," who "created and authorized such bad marketing," and who "caused the support to be so bad."
- He says they "just don't want to share any responsibility for the current state of the company."

## Why Steve still thought Apple was worth saving

Steve did not accept selling the company, even after agreeing with Avie's low opinion of the employees. He wrote: "there is something good here worth saving."

- He admitted he could not fully express it: "I don't quite know how to express it."
- His reason was strategic. Apple is "the ONLY alternative to Windows."
- He argued Apple "can still inject some new thinking into the equation."
- The case for saving the company rested on what Apple could still contribute to the industry. He did not argue that the people deserved saving.

## Separating the mission from the people who work there

The exchange shows Steve holding two views at once during the 1997 turnaround.

- He judged Apple's employees harshly, as a group that would not own the company's failures in products, marketing and support.
- He still believed the company was worth saving, because it was the only real alternative to Windows.
- Avie made the same split. He said the company "isn't worth saving. At least not for the sake of the employees," while still feeling for customers, shareholders and the ex-NeXT people brought in.
- Steve treated bad culture as something to fix by acting, not a reason to give up. Avie described applying a "new chemo treatment" against the "cancer" and expected another wave of resignations.

## Steve on being challenged

On August 25, 1997, Avie wrote that he was unmotivated and, for the first time in 10 years, no longer felt like challenging Steve's ideas when he disagreed. Avie said this scared him, because "as a 'team' we work best when we challenge each other and come out all-the-better for it."

- Steve replied in a single line, 21 minutes after the email: "Please continue to challenge me."
- His reasoning: "It's the way we get to the right decisions, and I enjoy it too."
- He treated disagreement from a trusted lieutenant as the way the team reaches good decisions, and said he enjoyed it.
- He wanted Avie's pushback back at a moment when Avie was close to giving up.

## How Steve handled a discouraged lieutenant

Avie was worn down in August 1997. He described falling revenue, losing quarters, ongoing restructuring, resignations, and poor engineering reaction to the new sabbatical program, with threats of a "sick-out." Steve's responses in the exchange:

- He replied within about 30 minutes, the same evening.
- He agreed with the frustration without piling on.
- He gave a reason to keep going, which was Apple's unique role as the alternative to Windows.
- He proposed talking in person: "Let's go for a walk and talk about this sometime soon." Avie had also asked for a face-to-face, one-on-one conversation, not a phone call.
- He asked Avie to keep arguing with him, which kept the working relationship honest.
