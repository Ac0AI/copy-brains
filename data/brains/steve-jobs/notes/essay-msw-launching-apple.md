## Friendship and the origin of Apple: Woz and Steve

- Steve met Woz at thirteen in a friend's garage; Woz was about eighteen. They met through Bill Fernandez, who lived near Woz, while working late on a project.
- They talked for hours. Steve was "real impressed" with him and thought Woz was great, with a good sense of humor.
- Their shared interest in electronics bound them "even though we were totally different in every other way possible."
- Steve's 1984 description of the bond: "We're sort of like two planets in our own orbits that every so often intersect each other. There's a bond there that will last as long as we both live."

## Why Steve and Woz built a computer: wanting one they couldn't afford

- They built the Apple I because they wanted a computer and couldn't afford one. Computers cost thousands of dollars then, and they were "just two teenagers."
- They scrounged parts around Silicon Valley. After a few attempts they put together what became the Apple I.
- Friends wanted them too. Each took about fifty hours to build by hand, and the friends weren't skilled enough to build their own, so Steve and Woz built them, using up all their spare time.
- The business started as a hobby solving their own problem, then a friends' problem.

## Cutting assembly from fifty hours to one with a printed circuit board

- To fix the time sink, they wanted a printed circuit board where parts plug in, instead of hand-wiring everything. Steve says it would cut assembly from about fifty hours to about an hour.
- They funded it personally: Woz sold his HP calculator and Steve sold his VW Microbus, to pay someone to design the board.
- The goal was modest: sell raw boards to friends and recoup the calculator and the Microbus.

## The first order: a twist they hadn't thought of

- The first computer store in the world, in Mountain View, said it would take fifty computers, but fully assembled. Steve calls this "a twist that we'd never thought of."
- They bought parts for one hundred computers, built fifty, and delivered them. They were paid in cash and ran back to pay their parts suppliers.
- Then came what Steve calls "the classic Marxian profit realization crisis": the profit wasn't liquid, it was fifty computers sitting on the floor.
- Lesson he draws: they had to learn sales and distribution to sell the remaining units and get their money back. "That's how we got in the business."

## Starting a company after being turned down

- Steve and Woz took the computer idea to a few companies: Hewlett-Packard, where Woz worked, and Atari, where Steve worked at the time.
- Neither was interested, so they started their own company.

## The 1977 New Yorker interview: Steve's pitch for the personal computer

- At the First Annual Personal Computing Expo at the New York Coliseum, 22-year-old Steve, Apple's vice-president for operations, was in charge of the Apple booth. Most people at the time had never seen a personal computer.
- His case for personal computers: for ten years the media said computers controlled parts of people's lives, yet most adults had no idea what a computer was or what it could or couldn't do.
- "Now, for the first time, people can actually buy a computer for the price of a good stereo, interact with it, and find out all about it."
- He compared it to taking apart 1955 Chevys, and to photography courses: thousands take them without becoming professionals, "They just want to understand what the photographic process is all about. Same with computers."
- He wished such machines had existed when he was growing up.

## Positioning Apple: "the Rolls-Royce" and "a domesticated computer"

- Steve said Apple started in a garage in Los Altos in 1976 and was now "the largest personal-computer company in the world."
- Positioning: "We make what we think of as the Rolls-Royce of personal computers. It's a domesticated computer."
- Contrast with expectations: people expect blinking lights, but find something that looks like a portable typewriter and, connected to a suitable readout screen, can display in color.
- On value: users respond with tremendous enthusiasm, and the feedback the machine gives people matters. Asked what it can do, he says it can do many things, but "the real thing it is doing right now is to teach people how to program the computer."
