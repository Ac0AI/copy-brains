## Pixar year-end report card: grading 1997 against the plan

In his December 21, 1997 email to Pixar's senior leadership, Steve graded the company against the milestones he had written down in October 1996.

- Four A's, one B, and two F's.
- "Make BUGS Great": A.
- "Ship Two Strong Interactive Products": F, but with an A "for getting out" of the interactive business.
- "Enter Sequel Business": A, "and almost exit sequel business."
- "Greenlight Film #3": F, but a B "for great progress."
- "Manage Growth": A. Pixar hired more than 100 people, started "Pixar U," and expanded its infrastructure.
- Long-term goals: keeping a larger percentage of the profits earned an A, and increasing Pixar's brand awareness earned a B, "mostly due to Apple goings on."
- He graded the work honestly and gave partial credit when an F came from a sensible decision to exit rather than a failure of effort.
- He set the next year's goals right after the holidays, so the grading led straight into planning.

## Keeping the "forest" in view: Pixar's 1998 milestones

Steve proposed nine milestones for 1998 on December 26, 1997. He said the list existed "to remind us of the 'forest' from time to time, and to help prod us into taking corrective action when we get off track."

- A Bug's Life opens in November to "very strong critical and box office success."
- PR efforts win Pixar branding and creative credit for A Bug's Life.
- Toy Story 2 becomes "a great feature film."
- Hidden City, the film that became Monsters, Inc., begins animation by April 1998.
- Film #5 development begins by April 1.
- Emeryville groundbreaking happens in May.
- A "breakthrough new animation system" is demonstrated by summer.
- Shorts projects are secured to cover the carrying costs of the A Bug's Life crew through spring 1999.
- Equity financing is executed in spring or summer if necessary.
- The list mixes creative, brand, facilities, technology, and financing goals, and it has dates and checkable outcomes.
- He invited his team to "shoot full of holes" the list, so the plan was open to challenge.

## Handling the Newton protest: welcome the critics, stand by the decision

On March 6, 1998, Steve emailed all Apple employees about a protest over the end of Newton OS development, especially the MessagePad. Newton was one of the product lines he cut after returning to Apple to focus the company's offerings.

- Newton customers and developers were coming to campus to protest.
- He told employees the protest was "OK."
- Apple reserved a space for the protesters and provided coffee and hot drinks, since it might be cold.
- The decision "was not taken lightly, and is unlikely to be reversed."
- He asked staff to welcome the protesters anyway.
- His hope was that they would feel Apple's enthusiasm about its future and "leave more settled than they arrived."
- He combined firmness on the decision with courtesy toward upset customers.

## Celebrating A Bug's Life: the record Thanksgiving weekend

On November 30, 1998, Steve emailed Pixar with the box office results for A Bug's Life.

- It earned a record $46.5 million over the five-day Thanksgiving weekend, beating the previous record of $45 million set by Disney's live-action 101 Dalmatians in 1996.
- Its three-day (Friday to Sunday) take of $33.6 million was also a Thanksgiving record.
- Exit polls were "exceptionally positive."
- The audience was split equally between males and females.
- 16% of the audience was the non-family "date" audience, which Toy Story did not reach until two weeks after its opening.
- Competitors for the weekend: Rugrats took $27.6 million, Babe: Pig in the City $8.5 million, and Antz $1.2 million.
- He credited John, Andrew, Darla, Kevin, and the whole A Bug's Life crew, and also Jan, Karen, and the Geri's Game crew for the short.
- He turned the numbers into people: $46.5 million divided by an average ticket price of around $6 meant about 7.5 million people saw the film that weekend.
- In his words: "Few people ever get the chance to make 7.5 million people laugh so much and feel so good. Or 450 people at Pixar feel so proud."

## Telling Apple employees "We did it!": iMac at number one

On December 23, 1998, Steve emailed Apple employees that the iMac had been the number one selling computer in the U.S. in November, in retail and mail order channels.

- iMac captured 7.1% of unit volume and 8.2% of dollar volume.
- Apple's overall retail and mail order market share doubled, from about 5% in August to 10% in November.
- The news ran on Apple's website and on pages A1 and B7 of the Wall Street Journal.
- He credited "our hard work" and asked employees to reflect on how far the company had come "in the last 18 months."
- He said he was proud of the team and excited to be at Apple.

## Macintosh at fifteen: Apple's job is to bridge technology and "the rest of us"

On January 24, 1999, the fifteenth anniversary of the Macintosh launch at Flint Center, Steve wrote to Apple employees about Apple's purpose.

- The Mac's "revolutionary ease of use" made computing accessible to "the rest of us."
- Its graphics and typography put it "at the intersection of computer science and liberal arts."
- He called it the second revolution in personal computing, after the Apple II.
- He said he was "not normally one to look back," but that today was a good day to remember Apple's legacy.
- That legacy is to bridge "the gap between sophisticated technology and 'the rest of us' who make up most of humanity."
- "It's our job to make complex technology easy to use and fun to use."
- The need was greater than in 1984, because users now faced networks, the Internet, email setup, device drivers, and init files, "all of the other things that drive today's computer users mad."
- Computing was not getting simpler, and no other company had taken Apple's place as "the bridge builder."
- He described Apple's return to its roots as delivering "simpler and better ways to use computers," and said that made its future look "both bright and secure."
- His closing line: "It's been an amazing journey so far, yet we have barely begun."
