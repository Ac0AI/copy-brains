## Building a studio around talent, not contracts

- Steve says he is "not a filmmaker" and doesn't direct Pixar's films. His job is to help "create the environment where all these incredible people can make films."
- Pixar's edge: the very best creative people will only work at a few places (Disney, Pixar, possibly DreamWorks), and the very best computer graphics scientists likewise. He argues Pixar is "the only place in the world that can hire the best from both of these areas."
- It took ten years to get both groups working together, because Hollywood culture and Silicon Valley culture are really different. Pixar tried to pick "the best from both."
- His role, as he describes it: understand the pieces needed to build a pure-play feature animation studio, attract and retain the people, get a clear strategy in place, and help with the relationship with Disney.
- He says his greatest joy is working with people much better than him at something, so he can stop worrying about it and move on to where he can help.
- He says Pixar is small and "we don't want to let any of this go to our heads."

## No contracts: retention through stock and daily effort

- Steve contrasts two retention models. Hollywood uses the stick, the contract. Silicon Valley uses the carrot, the stock option. Pixar examined both economically, psychologically and culturally, and chose the Silicon Valley model.
- Stock aligns everyone on one goal: creating shareholder value.
- Lack of contracts forces constant effort: "Every single day we worry about how we can make Pixar a better company so that nobody will ever want to leave, and so we don't take anybody for granted."
- He criticizes the contract mindset: a sophisticated manager sets a database reminder six months before a contract ends, treats the person as "the most important person in the world for six months," and after they re-sign, "you put them in a drawer."
- His view: if someone doesn't want to be at Pixar, they should probably leave anyway, contract or not.

## Inverted hierarchy: management serves the talent

- In technology and in Pixar's creative side, talented people are rare and in demand, and "if you don't treat them right, they can go get another job in ten minutes."
- As a result, "the hierarchy of power sort of inverts, and the CEO is actually at the bottom." He says he feels he works for most of these people because they do the brilliant work.
- Same in software: the best people are very hard to come by, so it's management's job to support them on the front lines.

## Editing the film before you make it

- In live action, a director typically shoots ten to twenty-five times as much footage as ends up on screen, and edits afterward.
- Animation is too expensive for that. Disney realized decades ago that you can't afford to animate ten times what you need, "you don't want to animate even 10 percent more than what you need." The conclusion: "you have to edit your film before you make it."
- Disney pioneered and refined techniques for this over sixty years. Working with Disney gave Pixar access to "wisdom that you can't buy for love or money," from having made tens of feature animated films.

## Technology grows ambition, but story is the art

- Render times: on the short film Luxo Jr. ten years earlier, each frame took about three hours on average. Computers are now a hundred times faster, yet Toy Story still took about three hours per frame, because frames were up to a hundred times more complex.
- For the second feature, code name Bugs, Pixar is throwing five to ten times more computer power than Toy Story at it, and it will still take about three hours per frame. "Our ambitions visually are growing as fast as the technology can feed them."
- On the creative side: "no amount of technology can turn a bad story into a good story." Storytelling is a very old art, he doesn't think it has changed in a long time, and it has nothing to do with the technology. Pixar will keep working on it "very, very hard."

## Why stories outlast technology

- Technology products have short lives: an Apple II is hard to find outside schools, and it's unclear a Macintosh could be booted five years later. A year or two is typical, five is extraordinary, ten to fifteen is rare. They become "sedimentary layer" for new innovation.
- By contrast, Disney released Snow White in 1937. A few years before this interview, a video rerelease sold 28 million copies, probably around a quarter billion dollars of profit, sixty years after release.
- Watching his young son watch Snow White thirty or forty times, he saw that these stories "renew themselves with each generation of young children." He cites Joseph Campbell: these are our myths.
- His prediction: people will watch Toy Story in sixty years, "not because of the computer graphics, but because of the story about friendship."
- He calls the chance to "put these stories into the culture" a rare opportunity, and says everyone at Pixar feels privileged.

## Internal emails: culture and the NeXT deal (December 1996)

- **Holiday Waltz (Dec 12, 1996):** About 40 people felt uncomfortable attending because they lacked a guest or thought it too formal. Steve's pitch: that's exactly why it will be fun, a chance to see colleagues "in a strange costume (tuxedo) doing a totally foreign activity (waltzing)." He notes his own last waltz was ten years earlier, says cutting in during a waltz is "proper and flattering" for singles, and closes with "We will miss you if you don't come. What do you have to lose?"
- **Apple acquires NeXT (Dec 20, 1996):** He tells Pixar staff the deal is good for Apple (a very advanced object-oriented OS, OpenStep, that can "leapfrog Microsoft Windows"), for NeXT (OpenStep going mainstream, a ten-year dream), for Pixar (it frees him from running two companies so he can devote more energy to Pixar, with possible future Apple-Pixar collaboration "if creatively driven"), and for himself. He will advise Apple on product strategy and spend even more time at Pixar.
