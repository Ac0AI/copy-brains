## Why Steve bets on Toy Story: the milestone email to Pixar (January 1996)

- On Jan 2, 1996, Steve emailed Pixar that Toy Story had crossed $150M domestic box office, "only 13 days after crossing $100M".
- He called it "the undisputed blockbuster of the holiday season," topping Jumanji, Father of the Bride II, Waiting to Exhale and Balto.
- He flagged it could become the top film of 1995, passing Batman Forever ($184M) and Apollo 13 ($172M).
- It was already the third most successful animated feature ever, behind only Aladdin ($217M) and The Lion King ($312M).
- He closed with a rallying call to the team: "To $175M and beyond!" and "Congratulations team."

## The web as a leveling, direct-to-customer channel

- Steve sees the web as "the ultimate direct-to-customer distribution chain, bypassing all middlemen," going straight from supplier to consumer.
- A small company that invests heavily in its website "can look just as formidable and just as solid on the web as a very large company." Some small companies were hipper to the web sooner and looked better than large ones.
- He calls it "a very leveling phenomenon": goods, services and demand creation would increasingly happen over the web.
- It is also shopping for information. He checks local movie listings on a web page rather than the newspaper or calling theaters.
- Example: he bought a Sony camcorder by researching Sony's web page first. "The demand to get me to buy that thing was created from Sony's web page."
- He expects people to make more life and purchase decisions from the web.
- Special-interest groups can form around information that never existed before, such as web pages for stroke victims covering the latest treatments and avoidance advice.

## Software distribution and post-PC devices

- Steve says there is "no question" software will be downloaded from the web, and he expected it within twenty-four months.
- The bottleneck is the last mile: corporate on-ramps are fast, but off-ramps to consumers' homes are slow. Large CD-ROM games would stay on physical media for a while, until faster links such as cable modems arrive.
- PCs will be around for quite some time, but he asks whether the first successful post-PC devices are near. PDAs tried and failed.
- His next candidate is a low-cost consumer internet appliance: a three-hundred-dollar box that connects to the TV on one side and ISDN or a cable modem on the other, giving a web browser in the living room.
- He wants innovative marketing and distribution around such devices so many people suddenly have a browser at home, and calls it "the beginning of the first real post-PC market."

## Not being afraid to fail

- Being forced out of Apple, the company he created, was "a very painful time," but "you just march forward, and you try to learn from it."
- His self-coaching: "not being afraid to fail."
- After a failure, people get protective and never want to fall on their face again. He calls that "a big mistake, because you never achieve what you want without falling on your face a few times in the process of getting there."
- He says he has "failed quite a bit since leaving Apple."

## Why Apple lost its lead: the failure to keep innovating

- When he left Apple, it had a ten-year lead over Microsoft, which he says comes along maybe once every few decades (Xerox, IBM with mainframes). Apple's was the graphical user interface.
- "The problem at Apple was that they stopped innovating." The Mac shipping in 1996 was "25 percent different than the day I left," not enough for ten years and billions in R&D.
- Microsoft wasn't brilliant in copying; "the Mac was a sitting duck for ten years." Apple's differentiation evaporated.
- Compaq and other Intel-Microsoft makers need only be 5 percent better to win. Apple, being outside the mainstream, must be 50 or 100 percent better, because buyers take a risk and want a much bigger reward.
- Cost-cutting is not the cure. "The cure for Apple is to innovate its way out," and good people remain who can do it "with the proper leadership, which is what's been missing."
- He tries not to talk about Apple much. He is happy every time a Mac ships and compares it to a first love: you can't always stay in love with your first girlfriend.

## The liberal arts point of view in computing

- Steve says he expects another group would have achieved similar things eventually, but Apple was in the right place, at the right time, with the right team.
- Apple's major contribution: "bringing a liberal arts point of view to the use of computers."
- The Mac's driving motivation was ease of use for many more people on nontraditional tasks, beautiful fonts and typography, and graphics for seeing photographs and artwork, "not for plotting laminar flow calculations."
- The goal was "a liberal arts perspective and a liberal arts audience" for what had been a "very geeky technology and a very geeky audience."
- Based on how he was raised, he believes "science and computer science is a liberal art": something everyone should know how to use, "not something that should be relegated to 5 percent of the population over in the corner."
- "That's the seed of Apple: computers for the rest of us." He thinks the liberal arts view still lives at Apple but not many other places, and says Microsoft took ten years to copy the Mac because "they didn't really get it at its core."

## Company culture: great ideas from anywhere, hire people to tell you what to do

- Apple was a corporation, driven to make money "so that we could continue to invest in the things we loved."
- Its big difference: "a real belief that there wasn't a hierarchy of ideas that mapped onto the hierarchy of the organization." Great ideas could come from anywhere, and people were treated in an egalitarian way regarding where ideas came from.
- Apple was "a very bottoms-up company" for many great ideas.
- "We hired truly great people and gave them the room to do great work." Many companies "hire people to tell them what to do. We hired people to tell us what to do."
- The result was a culture "much more collegial than hierarchical."

## Work and life

- Early Apple was young and mostly unmarried, so people worked fifteen-hour days. "Your work was your life, in many cases."
- Steve says work is still his life but not all of it. "I've never been able to think of my work and my life as different things. They're the same thing."
- Work was 99 percent of his life then and is maybe 50 percent now.
