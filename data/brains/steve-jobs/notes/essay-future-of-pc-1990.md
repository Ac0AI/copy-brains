## The computer as a "bicycle of the mind"

- Steve recalls a Scientific American article he read at about age twelve that ranked species by the energy (kilocalories) spent to travel from point A to point B. The condor was the most efficient. Humans placed about a third of the way down the list.
- Someone also tested a human on a bicycle, and the human on the bicycle beat the condor, going "all the way off the top of the list."
- His lesson: "we humans are tool builders," and we can build tools that amplify our inherent abilities "to spectacular magnitudes." For him a computer is "a bicycle of the mind," something that "takes us far beyond our inherent abilities."
- He says the computer is still at a very early stage ("we've come only a very short distance") and that what happens in the next hundred years will dwarf what has happened so far.

## What actually drives the industry: three "explosions"

- Steve says only two things so far have really caused masses of people to buy computers.
  - **Spreadsheets (1977):** Dan Fylstra walked into his office at Apple with a disk from his vest pocket and called it a "visual calculator," which became VisiCalc. He says it propelled the Apple II more than any other single event. Lotus 123 (around 1982) did the same for the IBM PC.
  - **Desktop publishing (1985):** the Macintosh plus the LaserWriter let people do on their desktops what only typesetters and printers could do before.
- The third explosion, which he says is starting in 1990: "let's do for human to human communication what spreadsheets did for financial planning and what desktop publishing did for publishing."
- He calls this **interpersonal computing**. The 1980s were personal computing; networking extends it.
- Multimedia, in his view, is "the means" and not "the end." People will not buy a computer for multimedia. They will buy it for training or interpersonal communication, and voice or video clips will serve that goal. He says computers will stay computers, "just like your toaster isn't your radio," while absorbing the capabilities of other devices.

## Networked organizations and how Steve ran NeXT

- His argument: business conditions change faster each year, but management hierarchies and geographic organizations cannot. "We can't have somebody working for a new boss every week," or move people around the country every week. An electronic organization can change that fast.
- Networked computers let a cluster of people form around a task with "literally fifteen minutes worth of setup." They can work efficiently regardless of location or reporting line, and the cluster disappears when no longer needed.
- NeXT put a NeXT machine on every employee's desk about 18 months before this interview, linked by high-speed networking. Steve says the revolution he saw there was "larger than the first two."
- His example: for a special project with a company (WGBH), NeXT creates a shared mailbox with about 20 people from marketing, sales, engineering and manufacturing.
  - A message to the mailbox reaches everyone instantly, and replies copy the whole mailbox so everyone reads the "intellectual content going back and forth."
  - Each person gets around 30 messages a day and spends 20 to 30 minutes on them. He says it is "like a beehive."
  - Steve adds his own name to important projects to watch the "disagreements and the arguments and the thoughts and the decisions" fly by. He does background coaching with people he thinks are off track, jumps in on the network if he wants, and takes his name off after a month or so once it is going well.
- Results he claims: at least 50 percent fewer meetings, far more managers and individual contributors involved in decisions, higher-quality decisions, and a "window for management to look into the process of this organism we call our company."
- He wants the computer to work fully standalone too (his example: two weeks in a cabin in Yosemite), then "dock" back into the network and catch up on what happened. He names the seamless transition between standalone and networked use as NeXT's five-year goal.
- He predicts the network will define the home computer market, driving a computer into every house "just like we have a telephone in every house."

## The vision behind NeXT and platform software

- Steve says everything he has done with computers has been "along pretty much a single vector," and NeXT is another point on it: if computers are the most incredible tools we have built, then "the more powerful tool we can give to people, the more they can do with it."
- He lists three breakthroughs at NeXT:
  - A much more powerful computer for about the same price as a PC.
  - Networking integrated into the computer.
  - A whole new software architecture built from the ground up. He claims it is about ten times as powerful as any PC system and lets applications be built in 25 percent of the time (a quarter of what a PC takes).
- NeXT spent four years with 50 to 100 of the best software people he knew building it.
- On platforms: the industry creates platform software first, then persuades others to write applications on top, and "the height that these new applications can soar is enabled or limited by the platform software." He says only three platforms have ever succeeded in desktop computing (Apple II, IBM PC, Macintosh) and NeXT is attempting the fourth.
- He also frames the personal computer like the automobile versus the passenger train. PCs let millions of people use computers decades earlier and control their own destiny. The cost is that people are "islands," so the challenge is connecting them to get the benefit of both train and car.

## Xerox PARC and the Macintosh: complete the research, then make it affordable

- Steve first visited Xerox PARC in 1979. He saw larger screens, proportionally spaced text and the mouse (invented by Doug Engelbart at SRI), and it was "instantly obvious to anyone that this was the way things should be." He came back to Apple thinking "our future has just changed."
- The gap: the PARC group had never made a commercial computer and cared more about looking out "fifteen years than ... fifteen months." Menus and other issues were unsolved.
- Apple had to do two things: finish the research ("only about fifty percent complete") and implement it at a cost low enough that people would buy it.
- The Mac was "the computer for the rest of us." The computer is "not an end in itself. It was a means to an end," and most people "didn't want to learn how to use the computer. They just wanted to use it."
- The paradox he describes: to make a computer easier to use you need a more powerful one, because you burn many cycles on ease of use. The easy computer ended up able to do more, and "it took people a few years to figure that out."
- He gives a young-versus-old observation (he mentions Sean Lennon's birthday): older people want to know how it does what it does, while young people just want to know what it can do.
- On the philosophy behind the Mac: it was built by people who felt there was no strict division between science and art, that "mathematics is really a liberal art," and who asked why not bring typography into computers and have computers talk in English. He says the battles to push this out the door "were very large."
- On user interface as a topic: to him it was a natural thing that had to happen, "like automatic transmissions."

## Thinkers and doers, and why people are artists

- His observation after about 15 years in the industry: "the doers are the major thinkers." The people who change the industry are "both the thinker and doer in one person."
- His example is Leonardo: he did not have someone off to the side thinking about what to paint. Leonardo mixed his own paints, was a good chemist, and knew pigments and human anatomy. Combining art and science, thinking and doing, "was what resulted in the exceptional result."
- He warns it is easy to claim credit for the thinking ("I thought of this three years ago"), but digging deeper usually shows the people who did it also worked through the hard intellectual problems.
- Why the Mac team signed the inside of the case: he considered them artists, "painters and poets" in another era, who now express themselves to their fellow species through the medium of computing.
- The Mac introduction at the shareholders meeting: about 100 to 150 people who made it were in the first rows and cried when the 2,500-person hall gave a standing ovation. He bit his tongue because he had more to present. He says it was emotional because "from that day forward it was no longer ours. We couldn't change it ... It belonged to the world."
- On deadlines: Apple wanted the Mac out a year earlier than it shipped and missed internal deadlines. By the time the Super Bowl spots were bought it was "basically in the bag," but the team worked twenty-four hours a day for the last six months, "on the bomb run."

## Early Apple: shipping assembled, the disk drive, memory, and market research

- Steve and Woz started Apple because it was the only alternative left: they showed prototypes to Atari (where Steve worked for Nolan Bushnell) and HP (where Woz worked), and both turned them down. "We started the company because it was the only alternative left, not because we wanted to."
- Division of labor: Woz was the brilliant hardware engineer on the core design. Steve worried about which parts to use and how to build and buy them.
- Key decision: sell the main board fully assembled instead of as a kit, which Steve says Apple was first to do. Their reasoning: for every hardware hobbyist there were about a thousand potential software hobbyists, so skipping the soldering opened the product to an order of magnitude more people.
- Three design decisions he credits for the Apple II beating its competition:
  - A reliable, inexpensive floppy disk drive, two to three years ahead of competitors.
  - Up to 48 kilobytes of memory, about three times its competitors, which is why VisiCalc was written for the Apple II. His line: "if Visicalc had been written for some other computer you'd be interviewing somebody else right now."
  - The fully assembled board.
- The moment he knew it would be big: seeing people who could never design a computer, build a kit, assemble a keyboard or write software using them anyway.
- He found Regis McKenna through Intel's advertising ("He told us to go away about four or five times, but eventually he agreed to help us out") and Mike Markkula through a venture capitalist, a month or so before the Apple II was introduced.
- On the 1976 Atlantic City show: a sweltering hotel basement, a hotel table with a tablecloth, a few Apple Is and a homemade poster. The West Coast Computer Faire came about nine months later, with about a hundred companies, and is where the Apple II was introduced.
- **Market research:** In the early days the whole market was in the room at a computer club meeting, and products were simple enough to change in a few months. Later, "market research can tell you what your customers think of something you show them" or want as an incremental improvement, but "very rarely can your customers predict something that they don't even quite know they want yet." No market research could have led to the Macintosh or the personal computer. Once the non-incremental jump has been made, it is a good time to check your instincts with the market.

## Manufacturing, education, and generational change

- NeXT's automated factory is there mainly to raise quality and shorten time to market, though it also lowers cost. Steve says NeXT was already the lowest-cost producer of its class of product and one of the highest in quality. His view is that to survive in the nineties a company must be world class at both, competing with "Europe Inc. and Japan Inc. and IBM Inc."; he also says the US had forgotten about manufacturing.
- On education: higher education led business by about five years in using networks. ARPANET (funded by DARPA) was handed to universities to "bang on" and improve, and it has tied together the US research community for about a decade. This is why NeXT started out focused exclusively on higher education.
- On K-12: computers there were mostly Apple IIs used for computer literacy, held back by a lack of sophisticated courseware. He wished Macintosh upgrades had come faster.
- On getting from 20 million to 100 million users: "death is the best invention of life" because it brings constant turnover. The best place to make social change is the educational system, so that generations grow up with computers as second nature, just as nearly everyone now drives. In his words: "It's a harsh way of saying it."
