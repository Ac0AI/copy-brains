## Why Steve built the iPhone: everyone hated their phones

- The iPhone project began because Apple was "getting better and better at iPods" and saw a chance to do "the next thing."
- It was not driven by market research or spreadsheets on market size. "It was driven by the fact that we all hated our phones."
- He and the team asked friends and acquaintances, and all of them hated their phones too.
- His reasoning: this is a really important device, everybody hates it, and people "don't know how to use even 10 percent of the features."
- His summary of the motive: "We thought we could make a contribution by making something truly great that we loved."
- The test of success is whether other people love it as much as the team does. He told employees they would find out the next day at 6 p.m. (the on-sale launch).

## Apple's core competence: complex technology that is easy and self-discoverable

- Steve defines Apple's competence as "making complex technology easy to use and self-discoverable."
- Example: nearly every phone can set up a conference call, but "nobody knows it." It sits on "page 93 of the manual they didn't read" behind "seven, eight cryptic keys."
- He says this is true of "feature after feature after feature."
- On the iPhone, setting up a conference call is slick and obvious. He says Apple did this "in spades."
- The implied design rule is that a feature people cannot find or figure out might as well not exist.

## Positioning: leapfrog the smart-versus-easy trade-off

- At Macworld, Steve framed the market on two axes, "smart" and "easy to use," in a "Business School 101" graph.
- Regular cellphones are "not so smart, and … not so easy to use."
- Smartphones are "a little smarter, but they actually are harder to use." They are "really complicated," and people struggle even with the basics.
- Apple did not want to settle for either position. The goal was "a leapfrog product that is way smarter than any mobile device has ever been and super easy to use."
- His list of smartphones (Motorola Q, BlackBerry, Palm Treo, Nokia E62) was "the usual suspects."

## Introducing a revolutionary product: framing and showmanship

- Steve opened with: "Every once in a while, a revolutionary product comes along that changes everything." He added that you are very fortunate to work on even one in your career.
- He placed the iPhone alongside the Macintosh (1984), which changed the computer industry, and the first iPod (2001), which changed the music industry.
- He announced "three revolutionary products": a widescreen iPod with touch controls, a revolutionary mobile phone, and a breakthrough internet communications device.
- He repeated the three items, then revealed they are "not three separate devices; this is one device." Its name is iPhone.
- He staged a fake reveal first (an iPod with a rotary dial), then showed the real iPhone pulled from his pocket, then put it away to keep tension.
- He said the day was one he had been "looking forward to for two and a half years."

## Fixed buttons are the problem; a giant screen is the answer

- Steve argues the flaw in smartphones is "in the bottom forty" keys: a physical keyboard that is there "whether you need [it] or not," plus control buttons "fixed in plastic" that are the same for every application.
- Each application wants "a slightly optimized set of buttons, just for it."
- Hardware buttons cannot change after shipping. If you think of a great idea six months later, "you can't run around and add a button to these things."
- Apple had already solved this in computers twenty years earlier with a bitmapped screen that can display any interface, plus a pointing device (the mouse).
- For the phone, the plan was to "get rid of all these buttons and just make a giant screen."
- He rejected the stylus: "Nobody wants a stylus. You have to get them and put them away, and you lose them. Yuck."

## Multitouch: use the pointing device we are all born with

- Steve's alternative to the stylus: "the best pointing device in the world," our fingers. "We're born with ten of them."
- He introduced multitouch as a new technology that "works like magic."
- Claimed properties: no stylus needed, far more accurate than any touch display shipped before, ignores unintended touches, supports multi-finger gestures.
- He noted that Apple had patented it: "boy, have we patented it."
- He listed Apple's revolutionary interfaces: the mouse (Mac), the click wheel (iPod), and now multitouch (iPhone). Each new interface made a revolutionary product possible.

## Software: OS X on a phone, and why Apple makes its own hardware

- Steve called software on mobile phones "baby software" and said iPhone's software is "at least five years ahead of what's on any other phone."
- The foundation is that "iPhone runs OS X."
- Reasons he gave for running a sophisticated OS on a mobile device: multitasking, "the best networking," power management (Apple had done this on mobile computers for years), "awesome security," Cocoa, graphics, Core Animation, and OS X's audio and video.
- The result is "desktop-class applications and networking," not "the crippled stuff that you find on most phones."
- He quoted Alan Kay: "People who are really serious about software should make their own hardware." He said Alan said this thirty years ago and that "this is how we feel about it."
- The reason Apple does things its way, he says, is "'cause we love software."

## Customer support: Steve's 2005 iPod reset reply

- A customer in Venezuela emailed Steve in July 2005 saying his father's iPod, about 11 months old, died after charging overnight, and asked where to get it repaired.
- Steve replied personally at 7:19 a.m., about eight hours after the email arrived (11:16 p.m.).
- His answer was brief and practical: reset the iPod by holding the center button and the Menu button together for at least 5 seconds, which "should work."
- The reply shows him engaging directly with an ordinary customer problem and giving the simplest fix first.
