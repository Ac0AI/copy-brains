## The iTunes Music Store bet: why subscriptions fail and a paid carrot beats a stick

- Steve argues the music industry's problem is "technological innocence": record companies are good at editorial (picking one successful artist out of 5,000 candidates, "an intuitive process") but not at technology, so they are vulnerable to people selling technical fixes that won't work.
- About eighteen months before the interview, Apple told record companies: "None of this technology that you're talking about's gonna work. We have Ph.D.s here who know the stuff cold, and we don't believe it's possible to protect digital content."
- His framing: "You only have to pick one lock to open every door." One stolen copy on the Internet is enough, and "no one's gonna shut down the Internet."
- Apple was kicked out at first and kept going back. Warner understood first, then Universal. Credibility came from making predictions and being right.
- Predictions that proved right: MusicNet and Pressplay (subscription services) would fail, because people don't want to buy music as a subscription.
- His evidence: people bought 45s, LPs, cassettes, 8-tracks, then CDs, so they will want to buy downloads. Subscription services had around 50,000 subscribers combined (Rhapsody, old Pressplay, old Musicmatch).
- His verdict: "The subscription model of buying music is bankrupt. I think you could make available the Second Coming in a subscription model, and it might not be successful."

## Beating piracy with a better experience at a dollar a song

- Steve believes about eighty percent of people stealing music online "don't really want to be thieves," but piracy is compelling: instant gratification, no trip to the record store, music already digitized.
- His position: you cannot tell people to stop stealing "without a legal alternative that offers those same benefits." Apple's pitch to labels: "We don't see how you convince people to stop being thieves unless you can offer them a carrot, not just a stick."
- The carrot: "We're gonna offer you a better experience... and it's only gonna cost you a dollar a song."
- He attacked the pirate experience on specifics (Kazaa): searching returns a hundred copies on a hundred computers, slow connections crap out, a song may have four seconds cut off because it was encoded by a ten-year-old, and a download can take fifteen minutes, or about four songs an hour.
- Stance on competition: "We're never going to top the illegal downloading services, but our message is: Let's compete and win."
- Scale at the time: about 16 million songs distributed on iTunes as of October (the interview also cites about 20 million tracks) versus roughly 10 billion songs distributed legally per year in the U.S. on CDs. He calls it "the very beginning."

## Intellectual property, copyright and the ethics of stealing

- Steve disagreed with David Bowie's prediction that copyright would be dead in ten years: "If copyright dies, if patents die, if the protection of intellectual property is eroded, then people will stop investing. That hurts everyone."
- He wants creators who invest and succeed to be able to make a fair profit.
- He also makes a moral point: "It is corrosive to one's character to steal."
- He empathizes with the labels because Apple also gets upset when people steal its software, so he says they are within their rights to protect their product.
- On teenagers copying music, he notes a legal alternative was only six months old, so "maybe there's been a generation of kids lost, and maybe not."

## Taking iTunes and the iPod to Windows

- The iPod and iTunes launched on the Mac first. The iTunes Music Store arrived in April 2003 and came to Windows in October.
- The main risk: people were buying Macs just to get their hands on iPods. Taking iPods to Windows was "the big decision," and once made, "we were going to go all the way."
- He accepted that some Mac sales were being lost; half of iPod sales were already to the Windows world.
- Apple's credibility with the music industry came from being "perceived as the most creative technology company": nearly every song and CD was recorded, mixed and had its artwork done on a Mac, and most artists and music execs had iPods.
- He argued the music store is "non-trivial to copy," and that saying Microsoft could copy it in six months "may not be so easy."

## Competing with Dell, Microsoft and the limits of the store

- On Dell's rival player, the Dell DJ: Apple would "ship way more digital-music players than Dell this quarter," and its online store was better.
- His distribution argument: Apple has retail channels, and "most people don't want to buy one of these things through the mail." Dell's direct model works against it in consumer electronics. His example: "Would you ever buy a plasma TV without seeing it? No way."
- On Microsoft bundling a clone into Windows, he points to Amazon, eBay, Google and AOL, which have done well against Microsoft with Internet services.
- On movies, he saw no iTunes movie store coming: a movie "takes forever to download," so there is no instant gratification, and he did not think it was what people wanted.
- Store principle: it is "about giving the users choice." Apple declined artists who would only allow whole albums and not individual tracks. Getting top artists meant approaching them one by one, since many control their own online distribution.

## Why artists feel ripped off and the fix: no advances, pay on gross revenue

- Steve says Apple wouldn't easily become a record label: signing a musician is easy, but signing a young musician who is successful is what the record companies do well.
- His analysis of why artists resent labels: a young artist gets a large advance (a million dollars or more). Only one or two of the ten signed are successful, so most never earn back the advance.
- The winners effectively pay for the losers, and they are "not seeing rewards commensurate with their success."
- His remedy: stop paying advances. Pay on gross revenues, not profits, with simple accounting. Example: "We'll give you twenty cents on every dollar we get, but we're not gonna give you an advance." The label risks some marketing money.
- The logic: "The more successful you are, the more you'll earn. But if you're not successful, you will not earn a dime." He adds, "That's the way the rest of the world works."
- He was skeptical the industry would adopt it: "Whether the patient will swallow the medicine is another question."
