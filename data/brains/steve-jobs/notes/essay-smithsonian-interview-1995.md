## Building products: craft, humanism, and hands-on learning

- Steve credits his father Paul, a machinist with no high school diploma, for giving him his own workbench at about five or six and teaching him to build things, take them apart and put them back together.
- A neighbor, Larry Lang (an HP engineer and ham radio operator), taught him electronics. Steve says Larry used Heathkits, which came with detailed color-coded manuals and a theory of operation.
- Steve says Heathkits gave "the sense that one could build the things that one saw around oneself in the universe." Things stopped being mysteries and became "the results of human creation."
- He says exploration and learning give the confidence that seemingly very complex things can be understood.
- At Apple he was proudest of where "the technical and the humanistic came together." His example is publishing: typographic artistry combined with technical excellence let people use a computer without arcane commands. He says this happened on the Apple II, the Lisa and "big time" on the Macintosh.
- He sees little distinction between an artist and a top-calibre scientist or engineer. Both pursue the same goal: to "express something of what they perceive to be the truth around them so that others can benefit by it."
- He describes the artistry as having an insight into what one sees and putting things together in a way no one else has. In the 70s and 80s, he says, the best people in computers would otherwise have been poets, writers and musicians, because computing was "a new medium of expression for their creative talents."
- He says "people put a lot of love into these products."
- He frames the Macintosh as "collective works of art much like twentieth century physics": a core group of fewer than a hundred people, more than ten million Macs shipped, and hundreds of millions of copies by the whole industry. He calls this a million-to-one amplification of values.
- His image for leverage is that changing the course of a vector near its origin makes it radically different a few miles out.

## Hiring only A players and the cost of keeping the wrong people

- Steve says one of the few things he can contribute individually is to "instill in the organization the goal of only having 'A' players."
- His reasoning is the range of talent in each field:
  - Taxi drivers: best versus worst is about two to one, fifteen minutes versus thirty minutes across Manhattan.
  - Cooks: maybe three to one.
  - His field: the best versus the worst is "about a hundred to one or more."
  - Software: a good versus a great person is twenty-five to fifty to one.
- He concludes it "really pays to go after the best people in the world," in everything he's done.
- He says that sometimes his job has been to get rid of people who didn't measure up. He tries to do it humanely, but it "has to be done and it is never fun." He calls it the hardest, most painful part of managing a company.
- He admits that at times he was "pretty hard about it" and gave people no choices.
- He accepts that the people he fired may hate him and that this comes with the world he chose to live in.
- On teams, he says "one person can't do anything anymore." You build a team and owe it integrity of work, and everyone tries to turn out their best.

## Why Apple lost its way: values, not growth

- Steve says Apple grew from nothing to two billion dollars while he was there (a billion-dollar company when the Mac launched, Fortune 300-something when he left), and growth did not ruin it.
- He says "What ruined Apple was values." He blames John Sculley for bringing corrupt values to the top, which corrupted some executives, drove out incorruptible ones and brought in more corrupt ones. They paid themselves tens of millions of dollars and cared more about glory and wealth than about "making great computers for people to use."
- The original vision was to make the Mac an appliance and get it to as many people as possible. Instead they went for outlandish profits for about four years, which "cost them the future."
- He says they should have taken reasonable profits and gone for market share. By his estimate the Mac would have had a thirty-three percent share or more, against a single-digit and falling share in 1995.
- His diagnosis: no one running any part of Apple was there when the Macintosh was made, so no one knows how to create the next one. The Newton was the last attempt.
- He compares Apple at its best to Sony, "the place that made the coolest stuff."
- He predicts "The Macintosh will die in another few years" and that market share, once below a threshold, spirals down, especially once developers stop writing applications.
- Apple stood for taking on IBM, as in the 1984 ad: "your way is wrong... this is not what we want our kids to be learning," and "we are going to show you the right way to do it."
- He says Apple never knew how to sell to corporate America and its attempts were "bungled." Macs sold to people who bought on merit rather than company name, against the era's "You never get fired for buying IBM."

## The kids can't wait: putting a computer in every school

- Steve's 1979 view was that if there were just one computer in every school, some kids would find it and it would change their lives. Only about 10% of schools had even one.
- Apple wanted to donate a computer to every school in America: about a hundred thousand schools, roughly ten thousand high schools and ninety thousand K through 8.
- The company couldn't afford it outright. An existing law gave an extra tax deduction for donating scientific instrumentation or computers to universities for education and research. Extending it to K-8 and removing the research requirement would cut the cost to about ten million dollars, instead of over a hundred million.
- With Rep. Pete Stark, Steve drafted the bill, called "the kids can't wait bill." Senator Danforth introduced it in the Senate. He refused to hire lobbyists and walked the halls of Congress himself for about two weeks, meeting about two-thirds of the House and over half of the Senate.
- It passed the House with the largest favorable majority of any tax bill in history. It then died in Carter's lame-duck session when Bob Dole refused to bring it to the floor.
- California then passed its own version, and Apple gave away ten thousand computers in the state. Software companies donated software and Apple trained teachers for free.
- Steve calls it one of his great experiences and one of his biggest regrets that it failed nationally.

## Education: people over technology, and vouchers

- Steve says he believes in equal opportunity, not equal outcome, and equal opportunity "more than anything means a great education."
- He credits fourth-grade teacher Mrs. Hill with saving him. She bribed him back into learning with a math workbook, five dollars and a giant sucker, then got him camera-making kits. He ground his own lens. He says he learned more that year than any other and that without her and a few others he'd "absolutely have ended up in jail."
- Despite helping put more computers in more schools than anyone, he says computers are not the most important thing. The most important thing is "a person who incites your curiosity and feeds your curiosity."
- His reason: computers are reactive, not proactive. Children need a guide, not an assistant. A week playing with gravity and why things fall needs no computer.
- He supports a full voucher system. In his view the real customers of education are the parents, who stopped paying attention, which let a monopoly take over. He cites a bumper sticker about the phone monopoly, "We don't care. We don't have to," and compares IBM in its day.
- His numbers: a $20,000 car lasts about eight years (about $2,000 a year), while California spent about $4,400 per pupil per year in public school.
- He expects a $4,400 voucher to do three things:
  - Schools would market themselves like crazy.
  - New schools would start. Idealistic twenty-five-year-olds could start schools instead of Silicon Valley companies.
  - Quality would rise under competition. Many public schools would go broke, which he calls painful at first but less painful than the current system.
- He wants teachers good enough to earn $100,000 at his company. He blames unions for turning education into a bureaucracy rather than a meritocracy.
- He says he once thought technology was the solution to most of the world's problems and now says "it just ain't so." Problems that are human, organizational and political must be attacked at the root.
- On TV: the programming is bad because the majority of people want to "turn on a television and turn off their brain," which he calls more depressing than a conspiracy.

## Lessons from Xerox PARC and NeXT

- In 1979 he visited Xerox PARC and saw three things working: graphical user interfaces, object-oriented computing (Smalltalk) and networking (Ethernet, with about two hundred Altos on a LAN doing email).
- He says the GUI was so incredible that it "blinded me to see the other two." It took years to rediscover objects and networking, which he later brought into NeXTStep.
- He remembers knowing within ten minutes of seeing the GUI that every computer would work that way someday: "It didn't require tremendous intellect. It was so clear." He says the same holds for objects: all software will eventually be written with object-oriented technology.
- NeXT's mistake was repeating the Apple formula of making the whole widget while the industry was changing. He says NeXT would be "either the last company to make it or the first to not make it" and turned out to be the first that didn't. He says they should have gone straight to being a software company.
- Details he cites for the NeXT machine: totally plug and play, workstation power, and fit and finish in operation. Soft power on and off prevented data loss. It was the first computer with built-in CD-quality sound.
- NeXTStep recorded its first profit in nine years, with fifty million dollars in software sold. He expects it could become a few-hundred-million-dollar software company within three or four years.

## Pixar, the Internet, and why new companies keep winning

- Steve says George Lucas's Lucasfilm group built digital compositing to avoid the noise that accumulates when you copy film optically. Star Wars composited up to thirteen film elements per frame.
- When Lucas stopped funding the group, Steve bought it and incorporated it as Pixar. He says eight of the ten most important revolutions in high-end graphics of the last ten years came out of Pixar, including software behind Terminator and Jurassic Park.
- Pixar's vision was to tell stories: the first completely computer-synthetic animated feature, Toy Story, due out November 1995 as Disney's Christmas picture, with Tom Hanks and Tim Allen. He calls Pixar the first digital studio, combining art and technology, and says it was probably close to ten years ahead of anybody else.
- On the Internet and Web, he gives three reasons for excitement:
  - Computers are becoming communications devices.
  - A three-person company can look as good as IBM or the Gap, with direct distribution cutting out middlemen, a levelling of big and small and near and distant.
  - Microsoft doesn't own it and probably can't.
- He argues the government should keep funding the Internet as a public trust. He puts the cost at about fifty to seventy-five million dollars a year, and says it should stay free.
- He argues that new companies always get a chance because "human minds settle into fixed ways of looking at the world." Large companies also have poor communication paths from the bottom to the top, with IBM and the PC as his example.
- He says "death is the greatest invention of life" because it makes room for the young, who see the world without preconceptions.

## Perseverance, passion, and how to spend a life

- When people say they want to be an entrepreneur but have no idea, he tells them to "go get a job as a busboy or something until you find something you're really passionate about because it's a lot of work."
- He is convinced about half of what separates successful entrepreneurs from unsuccessful ones is pure perseverance. It is "pretty much an eighteen hour day job, seven days a week for awhile."
- You need an idea, a problem or "a wrong that you want to right" that you're passionate about to have the perseverance.
- On responsibility, he says he doesn't think of it as an individual's duty beyond doing "really good stuff" and getting it out for others to build on: "the work speaks for itself."
- He quotes the advice to live each day as if it were your last. His view is that what you leave behind is "your kids, a few friends and your work."
