## Macintosh as the "telephone" of computing: the telegraph analogy

- Steve compares personal computing in 1983 to communications in the 19th century.
- The telegraph (1844) was an amazing breakthrough: messages could go from New York to San Francisco in an afternoon. Some people talked about putting a telegraph on every desk in America to improve productivity.
- He says "it wouldn't have worked." Users had to learn "strange incantations": Morse code, dots and dashes, about forty hours of study. A majority of people would never have learned it.
- The telephone (Bell's patents, 1870s) did basically the same function, but people already knew how to use it.
- The parallel today: some say an IBM PC belongs on every desk in America. It won't work, because the incantations are things like "slash-qz's," and most people won't learn those any more than Morse code.
- Macintosh is "the first 'telephone' of our industry."
- Principle: a tool that demands specialized training before it is useful will not reach most people. Ease of use is the precondition for mass adoption.

## Letting people "sing": expressiveness as the point of the Mac

- Steve's favorite thing about the telephone over the telegraph is that it let you sing and "intone your words with meaning beyond the simple linguistics."
- Macintosh does the same: "Macintosh lets you sing."
- Concrete examples he gives:
  - Memos in Times Roman or Helvetica, a serious font for something serious, or an Old English font for fun (a volleyball announcement).
  - Drawings and pictures, including other people's, incorporated into documents.
- "And you can express yourself."
- He sees the computer as a medium of expression, not only a productivity tool.

## Better communication and more fun at work

- In business, five-page memos are compressed into one-page memos because a picture expresses the key concept.
- Result: "less paper flying around and more quality of communication."
- He calls Macintosh "more fun" and rejects the myth that fun people at home become "dull and boring and serious" at work: "it's simply not true."
- His goal: inject "that liberal-arts spirit into this very serious realm of business," which he thinks "would be a worthwhile contribution."
- Principle: technology joined with the liberal arts, aimed at ordinary people rather than specialists.

## The IBM story: how Steve framed the competitive landscape (Oct 1983 sales meeting)

Steve told Apple's sales force a short history of IBM repeatedly dismissing new categories:
- 1958: IBM passed up the chance to buy a young company that had invented xerography. Two years later, Xerox was born. "IBM has been kicking themselves ever since."
- Late 1960s: DEC and others invented the minicomputer. IBM dismissed it as too small for serious computing. DEC grew into a multi-hundred-million-dollar corporation before IBM entered.
- Late 1970s: Apple, a young company, invented the Apple II in 1977, "the first personal computer as we know it today." IBM again dismissed it as too small and unimportant.
- 1981: Apple II became the world's most popular computer. Apple had grown to a $300 million company, the fastest-growing corporation in American business history, with over fifty competitors. IBM entered in November 1981 with the IBM PC.

## The 1983 market numbers and the stakes

- In 1983, Apple and IBM each sold roughly one billion dollars' worth of personal computers.
- In 1984, each would invest more than $50 million in R&D and another $50 million in TV advertising, almost a quarter of a billion dollars combined.
- The industry shakeout was in full swing: the first major firm went bankrupt, others teetered, and total industry losses for 1983 outweighed even the combined PC profits of Apple and IBM.
- His framing of 1984: IBM "wants it all" and is aiming at its last obstacle, Apple. Dealers who welcomed IBM now fear an IBM-dominated future and are "desperately turning back to Apple" as the force that can ensure their freedom.
- He rallies the room with questions: will Big Blue dominate the entire computer industry, the entire information age? "Was George Orwell right about 1984?"

## The "1984" commercial and its rollout

- Steve introduced the ad at an Apple sales meeting in October 1983, before showing it to the room.
- Directed by Ridley Scott: gray, shaven-headed people watch a dictator on a giant screen; a woman in bright red shorts and a Macintosh shirt hurls a hammer through it.
- The closing line: "On January 24th, Apple Computer will introduce Macintosh. And you'll see why 1984 won't be like '1984.'"
- It was set to run one week before the Macintosh introduction (it ran during the 1984 Super Bowl).
- The audience responded with tumultuous applause and shouting.
- Steve credited the ad agency Chiat/Day, who were in the room: Jay Chiat (principal), and Lee Clow and Steve Hayden, who wrote the copy and did the creative.
- Takeaway: he cast Apple's launch as a cause (freedom from a dominant incumbent) rather than a feature list, and he gave public credit to the creative team.
