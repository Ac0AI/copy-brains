## Killing technologies early: Steve's "horses to ride" test

- Apple isn't the company with the most resources, so it succeeds "by choosing what horses to ride really carefully, technically."
- Steve looks for technical vectors that have a future. Technologies go through "springs and summers and autumns" before the "graveyard of technology," and Apple tries to pick ones in spring.
- Choosing wisely saves enormous work and lets the team pour energy into making emerging technologies great on its platform, instead of spreading too thin.
- Apple's track record of cutting early:
  - Moved from the 5.25-inch floppy to the 3.5-inch floppy with the Mac (Sony invented it; Apple put it in the first products and made it popular).
  - Dropped the floppy entirely with the first iMac in 1998.
  - Dropped serial and parallel ports and was first to adopt USB, even though Intel invented it.
  - Was one of the first to drop optical drives, with the MacBook Air.
- People called Apple "crazy" for removing the floppy drive on the original iMac. Steve's answer: "sometimes you just have to pick the things that look like they're going to be the right horses to ride going forward."

## Why the iPhone and iPad skip Flash

- Steve calls Flash a technology "that had its day but is really on... waning," while HTML5 is "on the ascendancy."
- Technical objections he cites: a lot of work to incorporate, battery life, and security. No smartphone was shipping with Flash at the time.
- Evidence the shift was real: an "avalanche" of sites moving to HTML5 video, which "looks better and works better and you don't need a plug-in." About 75% of web video was in Flash, with 25% heading toward 50% in HTML5.
- Consumers outside the industry were mostly unaffected, he says, apart from "holes" where ads sit, and those were closing fast.
- On developers, he points to over 200,000 apps on the App Store as proof the platform attracts developers.
- Sequence of events: Apple told Adobe to come back and show Flash running fast on the iPhone, and Adobe never did. The iPhone shipped without Flash. Adobe only "raised a stink" after the iPad shipped without it, and kept criticizing Apple in the press for months.
- Apple stayed professional and silent until it wrote "Thoughts on Flash," explaining its technical reasons. He says they were "just as true today as... 6 months or a year before."
- He insists "we weren't trying to have a fight," and says Apple likes Adobe and shares many Creative Suite customers.

## Product choices: what to emphasize, what to leave out

- Steve's framing: products are "packages of emphasis." Some things are emphasized, some done less well, and some are chosen not to be done at all. Different people make different choices.
- On critics who call the iPad crippled without Flash: "if the market tells us we're making the wrong choices, we listen to the market."
- Apple has "the courage of our convictions" to leave out what it doesn't think makes a great product, and will "take the heat" because "we want to make the best product in the world for customers."
- Customers "are paying us to make those choices." If Apple succeeds they'll buy, and if not they won't. It "all works itself out."
- Proof point he offers: an iPad sold "every 3 seconds since we launched it."

## What keeps Apple's mission constant as it grows

- Surpassing Microsoft in market value felt "surreal," but "it doesn't matter very much... It's not what makes you come to work in the morning. It's not why any of our customers buy our products."
- He wants Apple to "just remember what we're doing and why we're doing it."
- The motivation hasn't changed in 10 years: "there's nothing that makes my day more than getting an email from some random person in the universe who just bought an iPad over in the UK and tells me the story about how it's the coolest product they've ever brought home." That kept him going 5 years ago and 10 years ago "when the doors were almost closed."
- On the return to Apple: it was "about 90 days away from going bankrupt," and "much worse than I thought." He expected the good people to have left, but found "miraculous people."
- Asked why they stayed, many said "I bleed in six colors" (the old six-color logo), which he reads as "code for... I love what this place stands for." That made everyone work harder to make the company and its values survive.

## Doing the thing in the platform: why Apple built iAd

- Insight from data: on phones, people spend their time in apps, not searching. They go to Yelp or a flight-status app rather than a search engine. Steve thinks this is because there was never "one place with 200,000 apps" where many are free and the rest very cheap.
- Purpose: "We're going into the ad business because we want to help our developers make some money so that they can keep providing free or really low-cost apps to customers." He says Apple won't make much money from it.
- Problem with existing mobile ads: tapping a banner throws you out to a web page, you can't easily get back to the exact place in the app, and in a game you won't make it back to the same spot.
- iAd's design: the ad takes over the screen with an interactive experience that "combin[es] the emotion of television with video," and a button returns you right where you left off.
- Why Apple does it: it owns the OS and can build it "in the bowels of the system," so a developer can add interactive ads with about 30 minutes of work, rather than custom work with each advertiser. Others could have done it, "but they weren't, so we did."

## Privacy: "plain English and repeatedly"

- Steve says Silicon Valley isn't monolithic and Apple has "always had a very different view of privacy," taking it "extremely seriously."
- Example: location data. Apple worries a 14-year-old could get stalked because of a phone. Instead of relying on apps to follow a rule, the system itself shows a panel ("this app wants to use your location data. Is that okay with you?") every time an app asks.
- The curated App Store lets Apple reject apps that "want to take a lot of your personal data and suck it up into the cloud." He admits many in the Valley find this old-fashioned.
- His definition: "Privacy means people know what they're signing up for, in plain English, and repeatedly."
- Philosophy: "I'm an optimist. I believe people are smart and some people want to share more data than other people do. Ask them. Ask them every time. Make them tell you to stop asking them if they get tired of you asking them."
- Analytics rule: Apple restricted third-party analytics (the Flurry case) because developers' software was sending device and geolocation data to an outside firm without asking customers, which "violat[ed] every rule in our privacy policy." Analytics solely for advertising stay allowed, and he says Apple would talk to analytics firms later, "after we calm down."

## Content owners should know their customers

- Steve's advice to Hollywood: technology offers "huge" opportunities for content owners.
- Marketing is shifting from expensive TV trailers to the web: Facebook, targeting, and direct communication with loyalty groups. It is becoming "more precise, cheaper, efficient."
- Lesson from music: record labels thought Best Buy and Target (or Tower Records) were their customer. "Their customer is the listener." What changed in music was the front end (distribution and marketing), not the back end of finding and developing artists, which "is just as important now as it ever was."
- The same will happen in film and TV: "The content is going to be just as valuable if not more valuable in the future."
- What studios should do: embrace the new front end, start knowing who their customers are, build ways to communicate with them, tell them when new product is out, and "let them watch it wherever they want, whenever they want."
- He expects this to move quickly in TV (some shows are free or sold the day after broadcast) and predicts big changes within about 24 months.
- On copy protection: content owners don't want a Napster repeat and "they're not in the business of copy protection, they're in the business of content creation." Apple sets no such rules itself. If it wants to sell their content, it follows their rules, tries to persuade them, and otherwise faces a simple choice.

## Why TV is stuck: the go-to-market problem

- Steve's diagnosis: the TV industry's subsidized set-top box model (free or about $10 a month) "squashes any opportunity for innovation" because nobody will buy a set-top box. He cites TiVo, ReplayTV, Roku, Vudu, Sony, Apple, and a coming Google attempt.
- The result is a table full of remotes and boxes with different UIs. Fixing it would mean tearing up the set-top box and redesigning it "from scratch with a consistent UI across all these different functions," delivered in a way consumers will pay for.
- It isn't a problem of technology or vision; "it's a fundamental go-to-market problem."
- Why the phone worked: Apple partnered with carriers, but there's no national cable operator and every country has different standards and approvals (he calls it "Balkanized").
- Apple chose between a better TV, a tablet, or a better phone. Phone and tablet won because they could actually reach market.
- Hence Apple TV is a "hobby." Without a viable go-to-market strategy, "you're just making another TiVo."

## On life and fragility

- Asked whether he'd add anything to his Stanford commencement speech: he'd "just turn up the volume on it because the last few years have reminded me that life is fragile."
