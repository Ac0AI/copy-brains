## The Macintosh as the "telephone" of computing

- Steve argues the first computers asked users to learn "strange incantations," like the telegraph's Morse code, which took about 40 hours to learn. The telephone won because people already knew how to talk.
- His example: WordStar's manual is 400 pages. "To write a novel, you have to read a novel." People will not learn "slash q-z" any more than Morse code.
- He calls Macintosh "the first 'telephone' of our industry." It lets you "sing" as the telephone did: print styles, drawing, and pictures add expression beyond plain words.
- The mouse rests on a familiar metaphor. To flag a spot on a shirt you don't give coordinates, you point. Apple's tests found pointing faster for cutting and pasting, so it is easier and more efficient.
- Users do not need to understand how the machine works, just as drivers don't need to know how an automatic transmission works.
- Business payoff: five-page memos compress to one page when a picture carries the key concept, with less paper and better communication.
- He wants to inject "that liberal-arts spirit into the very serious realm of business." He rejects the myth that fun people must turn dull at work.

## Why Mac broke compatibility, and why it was built for ourselves

- Steve gives two reasons for not being IBM-compatible:
  - He expected IBM to "fold its umbrella" and crush the clone makers.
  - The product vision: make computers "into appliances" for tens of millions of people, which the existing IBM-generation technology could not do.
- Reaching mainstream users needed a technology "radically easier to use and more powerful at the same time." The early adopters who stay up all night learning a machine were already served by the Apple II and IBM PC. He said it "may be the last chance" for a clean break.
- He denies it was ego: "It wasn't that we had to express our manhood by being different." The technology could not be this good if it were compatible.
- Apple II and IBM PC are "rivers carved in the rock bed of a canyon," taking seven and four years to carve. Mac blasted a third channel through in under a year.
- On picking standards: insisting on one standard now is like demanding one for automobiles in 1920, which would have blocked automatic transmission, power steering and independent suspension. "The last thing we want to do is freeze technology."
- Pricing: launch prices are high because low volume costs more, and Apple cuts prices as soon as it can. "I'd love it if Macintosh cost $1000."

## Building for yourself, and craft all the way through

- The Mac team "built it for ourselves. We were the group of people who were going to judge whether it was great or not. We weren't going to go out and do market research."
- He contrasts Mac with IBM's PCjr, which he says was designed from market research for a demographic segment, hoping to make money. "Those are different motivations."
- The carpenter analogy: a maker of a beautiful chest of drawers won't use plywood on the back, even though it faces the wall. "For you to sleep well at night, the aesthetic, the quality, has to be carried all the way through."
- Making an insanely great product "has a lot to do with the process of making the product, how you learn things and adopt new ideas and throw out old ideas." The people who made Mac were "sort of on the edge."
- Working on Mac was "the neatest experience of my life." Nobody on the team wanted to release it, as if it would stop being theirs. At the shareholders' meeting the audience gave a five-minute ovation and the Mac team, seated in the front rows, cried.
- On hype: "Good PR educates people; that's all it is. You can't con people in this business. The products speak for themselves."

## Lessons from Apple III and Lisa

- Steve calls the cost of Apple III "infinite, incalculable." Apple recalled the first 14,000 units and the machine never took off. He says the company emerged "much stronger."
- On Lisa, he lists the failures:
  - Priced around ten grand.
  - "Fortune 500-itis": chasing huge corporations when Apple's roots were selling to people.
  - Shipped about six months late, with software that "didn't come together."
  - Selling through only about 150 dealers, "absolutely foolish."
  - IBM arriving strongly at the same time.
- He says the hired "marketing and management experts" knew things that were "almost detriments" in a new kind of business. Being 23 to 25 and inexperienced, hiring pros "seemed like a good thing to do."
- When Scotty decided Steve lacked the experience to run Lisa, it "hurt a lot." The people hired from Hewlett-Packard and elsewhere brought a larger-machine, corporate-sales view and did not share the original vision.
- His response was to take a small group and "go back to the garage" to design Macintosh. He took the best people at Apple, the same people who then came up with the Mac.
- Decision-making: he tried never to have one person make all decisions. In early days he deferred to more experienced people, who were often right. In some important cases, going his way would have done better.
- Stakes: "We had to pull the rabbit out of the hat with Macintosh." Apple had nearly doubled in 1983, from $583,000,000 to about $980,000,000, almost all from the Apple II. Without Mac it would have stayed near a billion a year. If Mac failed, "my vision of the whole industry would have been totally wrong."

## Why companies decay, and how Apple tries not to

- Steve's diagnosis: companies growing into multibillion-dollar entities "lose their vision." They add layers of middle management, lose their feel and passion for products, and creative people must persuade five layers of management to do the right thing.
- Great people leave environments where individual accomplishment is discouraged, "and you end up with mediocrity."
- Apple "is an Ellis Island company," built on "refugees from other companies," bright individual contributors who were troublemakers elsewhere.
- Example: Edwin Land dropped out of Harvard, founded Polaroid, and "saw the intersection of art and science and business and built an organization to reflect that." Being pushed out of his own company was "one of the dumbest things I've ever heard of." He calls Land "a national treasure" who should be held up as a model.
- Polaroid and Xerox would have topped a list of exciting companies 10-15 years earlier and now would be on no one's list.
- The test for him and John Sculley: making Apple an incredibly great $10-20 billion company that keeps its spirit. He says there are no models for this growth, and the dollar goal itself is "meaningless."
- Apple attracts people who put in 18-hour days and "really want to get in a little over his head and make a little dent in the universe."
- On the sense of mission: "Very rarely do we get a chance to put something back into that pool" of what society has made. Everyone senses "something much bigger than any of us here."
- On staying fresh: "death is the most wonderful invention of life. It purges the system of these old models that are obsolete." The test for Apple is whether it will embrace two young people with the next thing or "explain it away."
- On age: people "get stuck" in grooves, like a record. Artists in their 30s or 40s who contribute something amazing are rare. The exceptions are the innately curious, "forever little kids in their awe of life."

## Competing with IBM and reading the market

- Steve says it is coming down to Apple and IBM. If IBM wins, "we are going to enter sort of a computer Dark Ages for about 20 years." Once IBM controls a sector, "they almost always stop innovation."
- The Frito-Lay analogy: it services more than half a million accounts a week with about 10,000 people replacing stale chips, holds over 80 percent share, and doesn't have to innovate. It studies small rivals' new products for a year or two, then copies and supports them. IBM plays the same game: virtually zero mainframe innovation in 15 years, and the PC "fundamentally brought no new technology," only a repackaging of Apple II ideas.
- Apple's counter is to go fast enough that they can't keep up, and to build "motherhood" (service and support) into the computer itself. IBM's focus is service, support and security.
- On market segments: he wants the "Fortune 5,000,000," meaning the roughly 14,000,000 small businesses, approached bottom-up by treating them as collections of people and focusing on the small work group rather than wiring whole companies.
- Hardware innovation will be hard for newcomers because of scale. Imitators of $100-200 million will be "struggling for your life." Most new innovators will work on software.
- On other players: A.T.&T. has great research technology but must learn to commercialize it and do consumer marketing, which will take years. Radio Shack "tried to squeeze the computer into their model of retailing" and missed the boat. He dismisses sub-$500 computers as "a brochure" for buying an Apple II or Mac, because they either tease or frustrate.
- On Japan: they "reinvent" rather than copy, studying a product until they understand it better than its inventor. That works for slow-moving targets like stereos and cars but is hard when the definition of a PC keeps changing. He expects them to figure out a decent computer in four to five years.
- Manufacturing response: Apple spent $20,000,000 on the industry's most automated factory and writes it off in two years rather than seven, planning to throw it away at the end of 1985 and build a better one. "That's the only way we can learn fast enough."

## Where computers are going, and education

- Steve calls the computer "the most incredible tool we've ever seen," a writing tool, communications center, calculator, planner, filer and artistic instrument by changing software. He says "people are basically tool users."
- Computers as appliances: the long-term home case is linking to a nationwide communications network, which he expects to be "as remarkable as the telephone." He compares speculation to Bell, who couldn't predict the telephone's uses.
- Software history he credits: programming languages on microprocessors, VisiCalc (first real business use), Lotus (spreadsheet plus graphics in one program), then Mac and Lisa technology. Word processing is the most universally needed application. He expects an educational "VisiCalc" but not within 24 months.
- Education: computers are "the first thing to come along since books that will sit there and interact with you endlessly, without judgment," and could restore a Socratic experience when paired with enlightened teachers.
- Apple's education push: the Apple Education Foundation gives millions in cash and equipment. Apple wanted six universities making large commitments of over 1,000 machines each and $2,000,000 each, and found 24, including the entire Ivy League. IBM countered with giveaways, but colleges saw the software investment would dwarf hardware and chose Macs.
- Next software step: computer as "guide or agent" rather than servant. It would anticipate needs and notice patterns. "Triggers" would let it monitor conditions, such as selling a stock past set limits or writing personalized letters to sales reps who beat quotas by 20 percent, then report afterward. He guessed about three years away.
- His long view: a book lets you read Aristotle directly, but you can't ask him a question. Computers could capture the underlying principles of a thinker's world view, as Pong captured gravity and angular momentum, so you could ask an Aristotle or Einstein or Land. That is "for the next generation."
- Warning: technical illiteracy in politics and underinvestment in education risk America becoming a "second-rate industrial nation."

## Money, focus, and living with success

- Steve says the money is "hardly the most insightful or valuable thing that's happened to me." He lost $250,000,000 in a year when the stock fell and said he wouldn't let it ruin his life.
- He gives growth figures with pride, as "yardsticks": about $200,000 in 1976, roughly $7,000,000 in 1977, $17,000,000 in 1978, $47,000,000 in 1979, $117,000,000 in 1980, $335,000,000 in 1981, $583,000,000 in 1982, about $985,000,000 in 1983. The milestone he prizes is seeing kids using 15 Apple computers in a classroom in 1979.
- On wealth: it is a responsibility. He says leaving huge sums to children "will just ruin their lives," and the challenge is to reinvest it in the world. "To give away a dollar effectively is harder than to make a dollar," because philanthropy lacks a measurement system, so you can't fail and learn. He wants actions, not words, to speak first.
- His favorite things don't cost money. "The most precious resource we all have is time," and he pays a price in not having much of a personal life.
- On identity: "If you want to live your life in a creative way, as an artist, you have to not look back too much. You have to be willing to take whatever you've done and whoever you were and throw them away." At the core are values, and outside images of you make it harder to keep growing.
- "I'm still a student. I'm still in boot camp." He cites the Hindu saying that for the first 30 years you make your habits and for the next 30 your habits make you. He hopes his life and Apple's will "weave in and out of each other, like a tapestry."
- On grace: the challenge is "to grow obsolete gracefully," turning the reins over to a generation that can "stand on our shoulders and go much further."

## Early influences: Hewlett-Packard, Woz, Atari, and the garage

- Steve's father, a machinist who could fix anything, gave him things to take apart, and teacher Imogene Hill ("one of the saints in my life") kindled his passion for learning in fourth grade.
- At about 12 he saw his first computer at Hewlett-Packard on a Tuesday night lecture. At 12 or 13 he called Bill Hewlett from the phone book for parts, chatted for 20 minutes, and got a summer job on the HP line.
- He met Wozniak at 13 (Woz about 18), the first person who knew more electronics than he did. They built blue boxes and pulled pranks. He credits California's culture of experimentation, Dylan, Eastern mysticism, and the intellectual flow at Reed.
- The Altair (1975, under $400 as a kit) made a computer you could own. He worked at Atari, employee number 40, and let Woz in at night, which led to Woz's terminal design.
- The Apple I: they raised $1300 by selling his VW bus and Woz's HP calculator. About 150 sold for roughly $95,000, a bare circuit board with no case, power supply or keyboard.
- The Apple II's breakthrough: a finished product, fully assembled with its own case and keyboard, so you could be a software hobbyist rather than a hardware hobbyist. He says the power supply and case were key, and that the Apple "wouldn't have happened without Woz and… without me." Woz is a brilliant designer who isn't great at turning things into products.
