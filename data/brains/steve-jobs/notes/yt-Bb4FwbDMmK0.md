## Steve's turnaround diagnosis of Apple (Macworld Boston, August 1997)

After four weeks back, Steve reports that the popular claims about Apple were mostly wrong:
- "Apple's not as relevant as it used to be everywhere, but in some incredibly important market segments it's extraordinarily relevant."
- Apple is "executing wonderfully on many of the wrong things." The organization can execute, but the plan was wrong.
- He found no anarchy. He found people who "can't wait to fall into line behind a good strategy. There just hasn't been one."
- He sees a company with talented people who are still passionate about computing and need a great plan, then execution.
- The symptom is falling sales: $11.1 billion in 1995, $9.5 billion in 1996, and about $7 billion in the current year.
- His early steps: change the board first ("start at the top"), focus on relevance, invest in core assets, form meaningful partnerships (not just press releases), and define new product paradigms. He holds back product details for later in the year.

## Focus: find where you are still relevant and double down

Steve identifies two segments where Apple still dominates and says it will focus on them.
- **Creative content** (publishing, design, pre-press): about 80 percent of computers in advertising and graphic arts are Macs. He cites 64 percent of internet websites as built on a Mac.
- He says Apple does a poor job here. Roughly 10 to 15 percent of Mac sales trace back to people using Adobe Photoshop as their power app, yet Apple and Adobe rarely co-market or work together on making a computer run Photoshop faster.
- **Education:** he asks who the largest education company in the world is and answers Apple. Of 100 Apple people he asked, only two had thought about it. Macs are 60 percent of computers in education and 64 percent of computers teachers use, a $2 to $2.5 billion annual business.
- Both markets grow over 20 percent a year. Apple's share has been slipping slowly, and he expects it to stabilize and turn around as Apple focuses.
- He calls the education figure "a bolt of lightning": an incredible base and legacy to build on.

## Core assets: the brand, the Mac OS, and the users

Steve names Apple's core assets and says they were neglected.
- **The brand:** Nike, Coca-Cola, Disney and Apple belong on any list of the five greatest brands in the world. Apple "hasn't paid much attention to it in the last several years," and that will change.
- **The Mac OS:** "the best thing in the world," with 20 to 25 million active users, thousands of developers and a $1.5 billion software industry built around it. Steve says it has "yet to really be fully exploited."
- He rejects the belief that Apple is abandoning the Mac OS. It is a joke that the OS 8 release (code name Tempo) is followed by Allegro and then "Requiem": "it's absolutely crazy." Apple will do other things "because we're creative people, but at the core Apple is about the Mac OS."
- Mac OS 8 sold 1.2 million copies in two weeks, four times the forecast, and Apple was scrambling to ship.
- **The users:** "Apple's greatest core asset is you all." Steve admits Apple has not taken care of them. He called the support lines himself, "got very acquainted with the hold signal," and apologizes for the past.

## Rebuilding the board

Steve announced the board change at the start of the turnaround.
- Two members stay: Ed Woolard (chairman and former CEO of DuPont) and Gareth Chang (president of Hughes International, bringing Asia experience).
- Four are added: Larry Ellison (Oracle, chosen partly for software industry expertise), Jerry York (former CFO of IBM and Chrysler, known for turnaround work), Bill Campbell (CEO of Intuit, formerly Apple's VP of sales and marketing and head of Claris), and Steve himself.
- The rest of the old board resigned. Steve thanks them as "very decent people" who tried to do the right thing, while saying that given Apple's situation it was time for a change and the directors agreed.
- No chairman is named until a new CEO is chosen.
- His criterion was deep industry expertise: "a lot of deep industry expertise who come together to say hey this world's a lot better place with a healthy Apple computer in it."

## Partnership with Microsoft: end the zero-sum mindset

Steve announces a package deal with Microsoft, which began as a patent dispute settled professionally.
- A full patent cross license covers all existing patents and those filed in the next five years.
- Microsoft commits to release Office on the Mac for five years, matching the number of major releases on Windows, with the first targeted near year-end (it might slip into next year).
- Apple makes Internet Explorer the default Mac browser. Other browsers still ship and users can change the default: "we believe in choice."
- The companies collaborate on Java to keep their virtual machines compatible.
- Microsoft buys $150 million of non-voting Apple stock at market price and agrees not to sell it for at least three years.
- Steve's reasoning: "We have to let go of this notion that for Apple to win Microsoft has to lose." Apple must win by doing a really good job. If it fails, "it's not somebody else's fault, it's our fault."
- He adds that if Apple wants Office on the Mac, it should treat the company that makes it "with a little bit of gratitude."
- He says the era of framing this as Apple versus Microsoft "is over as far as I'm concerned." The goal is a healthy Apple making great contributions to the industry.
- He notes Apple plus Microsoft equals 100 percent of the desktop computer market, so each is the other's only other desktop player, which should drive more cooperation.

## "Think different": who Apple makes tools for

Steve closes with the identity of the brand and its customers.
- You always had to "think different" to buy an Apple computer: the Apple II when computers filled giant rooms and had no software, the first computer arriving in a school, and the Mac as a computer that "used a totally different part of your brain" and was bought with an installed base of one.
- Today's Apple buyers are "the creative spirits in this world," people who are "not just out to get a job done, they're out to change the world." Apple makes tools for those people.
- He promises Apple will think differently too and serve the people buying its products since the beginning: "a lot of times people think they're crazy, but in that craziness we see genius, and those are the people we're making tools for."
