## Apple's three businesses and a hobby: Mac, music, phone, and Apple TV

- Steve says Apple is in "two businesses today, and a hobby," about to add a third. Mac and music are each roughly $10 billion businesses. The third is handsets (the iPhone).
- He calls Apple TV a hobby because many companies, "everybody from TiVo to Microsoft," have tried and failed to make it a business. It sells in the hundreds of thousands of units a year, not yet millions. He says it hasn't "crested" and thinks a year to 18 months of work can crack it.
- He explains the word choice: "I use the word hobby just because it's a little provocative." The iPod started the same way, smaller and feeling a lot like this.
- Apple TV's model is "a DVD player for the internet." He dropped the set-top-box replacement idea. Replacing a set-top box means cable cards, going through cable companies and OCAP billing software, with no go-to-market strategy that made sense. There are far more DVD players than set-top boxes.
- Early Apple TV pushed PC content (photos, music) to the TV. He now calls that "the peas on the side" and says the entrée may be content "you get directly from the internet." He adds, "We're learning!"
- He announces YouTube on Apple TV as a free software upgrade in mid-June. He thinks a normal web browser isn't what people want in the living room.

## Mac growth, notebooks, and commitment to the PC business

- Apple's Mac share worldwide is about 3%. In the US it is 5 to 6%, and in US retail notebooks it is about 12%.
- Mac growth since the Intel transition has been about three times the market rate worldwide, approaching five times in the US.
- Apple is about two-thirds notebooks and one-third desktops, against an industry of roughly 40% notebooks. He expects notebooks to reach 80-90% of Apple's sales.
- He calls the Intel switch "a huge thing to do" and says it was seamless for customers. Mac OS X Tiger ran on Intel and "we didn't get much credit for" it.
- On rumors that Apple is exiting PCs, he says "totally" committed. Leopard ships in October, WWDC will have record attendance, and "it's massive investments we're making in that business. We love it."
- He says "we all use Macs at Apple. We love the Mac."

## iPhone: three products in one, software lead, and no physical keyboard

- He calls the iPhone three things: the best iPod ever made, an incredibly great cell phone ("all cell phones are the same" until Apple revolutionized how you use one), and "the internet in your pocket for the first time." Any one of the three would succeed, and "they play off each other."
- It ships in the latter part of June. He says he hopes it ships in volume, and "Will it be enough? I don't know. I hope not."
- Apple sells it in its own stores and through Cingular (the new AT&T). Cingular signed an unusual deal that bent or broke many of its rules, without ever seeing the phone. He compares it to venture capitalists who "invest in people, not ideas."
- He gives Cingular two reasons for backing Apple: music on phones hasn't worked, and carriers are spending a fortune on 3G networks with little to run on them. Phone browsers give "the baby internet," and people want the real one.
- On the keyboard question, there was "none" debate about a physical keyboard. "Once you use this magical display, there's no going back." He bets Walt dinner that after a week with it he'll like it. "You have to learn how to trust it. Once you learn how to trust it, you fly."
- Software UI flexibility is a key advantage. The keyboard space can become other things, and Apple can keep changing the interface. When Cingular said it needed a button for something, he told them "we'll add it after we ship."
- It runs real OS X, real Safari and real desktop email. Steve says "the data" (desktop patterns, sound files) is what makes the OS big. Because Apple owns those apps, it could put a very different multi-touch interface on Safari. He doesn't want a mouse or pull-down menus on a phone.
- He says rivals will copy the hardware but "the software is at least five years ahead of anything we've seen out there."
- He says Apple started with an OS it had worked on for over a decade and a browser many call the best in the world.
- On third-party apps, the tradeoff is security versus openness: "we want to have our cake and eat it too." Apple will find a way later this year to allow third-party apps while preserving security. He says smartphones "that crash more than once a day" get worse with more third-party apps, and he asks for patience.
- He declines to put the iTunes Store directly on the phone yet, saying "we certainly have nothing to announce today." He argues over-the-air purchases cost more, browsing large catalogs on a phone is poor, and music would still need to sync to the PC for backup.

## Why Apple wins: software wrapped in beautiful hardware

- Steve says the iPod succeeded because Japanese consumer electronics makers "couldn't do software work as well as needed." He calls the iPod "a software product in beautiful hardware": software in the device, on the PC or Mac (iTunes), and in the cloud (the store). Apple is "software wrapped in a beautiful package."
- Handset makers have the same gap: good hardware, but they "haven't been able to make the leap to software."
- Licensed software like Microsoft's PlaysForSure runs on a small number of phones "of a certain caliber."
- He says the iPod's value comes from making the best music player. "If we have the best music player, we're going to sell iPods. If we have the best music store, we will continue to pioneer people buying music."
- He rejects the lock-in argument with numbers: fewer than 25 iTunes songs sold per iPod, while the average iPod holds hundreds of songs. Most music comes from ripped CDs and other sources, and iPods play any MP3.
- He says he wasn't sure about video on small screens but customers proved him wrong. Video has been the number one or two reason people buy the iPod, and Apple has sold nearly a hundred million TV shows. He admits he "was definitely more skeptical than customers."
- He says a hundred million iPods is a very big number for Apple. He would rather ship 100 million at a lesser share than have 80% of a 10 million unit market.
- iTunes (free, on Windows) is installed on several times more machines than iPods ship. He jokes it's "like giving a glass of ice water to somebody in hell."

## Music: iTunes Plus, DRM-free, and the AAC question

- Apple begins selling EMI music DRM-free, as iTunes Plus, at $1.29 a song with higher-quality audio, in the same store. Over half the songs on iTunes should be iTunes Plus by year end.
- Users can opt to have iTunes show only iTunes Plus versions when available. Existing purchases can be upgraded for 30 cents a song or 30% of the album price.
- His argument to labels: they already ship 90% of their music DRM-free on CDs, customers will pay a little more for DRM-free downloads, and it solves interoperability.
- On AAC vs MP3, he says AAC is superior at a given data rate and is an open standard anyone can license from an industry body (simpler than MP3 licensing). Microsoft and others license it. All iPods play MP3.
- On carrying music over the air, he argues phones are a poor place to browse catalogs, airtime costs more, and syncing back to the PC is needed anyway.

## Hiring, argument, and how Apple makes decisions

- "If you want to hire and keep really bright people, then you can't tell them what to do" very often. He says he has a silver bullet to overrule "once a year, maybe twice" but basically doesn't use it.
- At Apple "it's about ideas. And we argue about ideas constantly."
- He says Apple isn't capital intensive: "we don't build $4 billion semiconductor fab facilities that provide barriers to entry." So "all we are is our ideas, which means all we are is our people."
- He says "what keeps us going to work in the morning is a chance to hang around all these great bright people." Recruiting is "the heart and soul of what we do," and he feels this even more strongly now. He still personally recruits.

## Tools, surprise on the upside, and storytelling

- He says Apple makes tools for people to enjoy entertainment, communicate and create, whether Macs, phones or iPods. Computer-based tools "can always surprise you on the upside."
- Examples: Apple didn't design Apple TV thinking about YouTube, and didn't design iTunes thinking about education. iTunes U, with courseware from universities (about 17 now, hundreds in the pipeline, including MIT, Stanford and Berkeley), is free. Lecture recordings let students listen and take notes later, and alumni and outsiders can access them.
- On Pixar and Disney, he says he is interested in storytelling, is "one of the cheerleaders at Disney," and loves what they do.
- On advertising, he mentions the Mac vs PC campaign, which does run on the internet quite a bit.
- He says Apple doesn't plan to get into the camcorder market.
- On power: for portable devices "it's all about power. It's all about battery life." Apple applied lessons from Mac notebooks and iPods to the phone.
