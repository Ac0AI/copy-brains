## Quality as a repeatable process, not a pep rally

- Steve says the most valuable thing he took from Joseph Juran is to "look at everything as a repetitive process."
- The method: instrument the process, find out how it is running, take it apart, then put it back together in ways that dramatically improve its effectiveness.
- He stresses "no magic, no pep rallies": look at things "directly in the eye" and re-engineer them.
- As he understands it, most quality work is re-engineering repetitive processes: combining some, eliminating others, strengthening the rest.
- He credits Juran with a real down-to-earth, scientific approach. Juran did not treat quality as "the second coming." That resonated with engineers, technical people and executives at NeXT.
- Steve's context: at NeXT (where he is president in 1991) the company set out to understand "the ballyhoo about quality." Juran visited several times. He had not met Juran at Apple.

## Customers judge quality by experience, not marketing

- Steve notes that Japanese companies never use "quality" in their marketing, while American companies do. Yet ask people on the street which products have the best quality reputation and they name Japanese products.
- His explanation: "customers don't form their opinions on quality from marketing." They don't form it from who won the Deming Award or the Baldrige Award. They form it from their own experience with the product or service.
- One can spend enormous sums on quality and win every award. If the products don't live up to it, customers won't hold that opinion for long.
- His conclusion: start with the products and services, "not with our marketing department." Go back to basics and improve the products and services.

## Quality includes having the right product

- Steve says quality is not just construction or service. It is "having the right product."
- Knowing where the market is going and having the most innovative products is "just as much a part of quality" as how well the product is built.
- He observes that today's quality leaders have extended quality technology well beyond manufacturing into sales and marketing, reaching out as far as they can to touch the customer.
- Goal: super-efficient processes from the customer all the way through to delivery of the end product, so a company can have the most innovative products and understand customer needs fastest.

## Replace "that's how we do it here" with questioning

- In most companies, a newcomer who asks why something is done a certain way hears "because that's the way we do it here" or "that's the way it's always been done."
- Steve says the largest contribution of quality thinking is to approach processes scientifically: there is a theory behind why they exist, a description of what is done, and, most importantly, "an opportunity to always question what we do."
- He calls this a radically different approach to business processes and says "that single shift is everything."
- Underneath is an optimistic view of employees: they are smart, "not pawns," and if given the opportunity and a mechanism to change and improve, they will improve the processes. He calls this "optimistic humanism" and says there are countless examples that it works.

## Distribute authority to the people doing the work

- Asked what he now says yes to because of Juran, Steve says the question "capsulizes what's wrong": under this philosophy people shouldn't have to ask management permission to improve their processes.
- Authority should sit with the people doing the work, who should be taught to measure, understand and improve their own processes.
- He sees this as flattening the traditional hierarchical organization and distributing authority to those best positioned to decide.
- The permission the quality philosophy grants is "the permission to not have to ask permission."

## Why America is losing to Japan: forgetting the basics

- Steve says America is "in a tough spot." After being prosperous for so long, the country took too much for granted and forgot how much work it took to build and sustain the foundations of that prosperity, such as a great education system and great industry.
- He argues the US is being "out-manufactured," "out-planned" and "out-strategized" by Japan. It is not because the Japanese are tricking Americans or are intellectually better.
- His view: nothing here can't be fixed, but the fix is going back to basics and relearning them. He sees Juran as valuable because he stands at the heart of why this is happening.

## Traits of great people, and what drives sustained contribution

- Steve says the few great people he has met share one trait: they treat everyone the same, whether janitor, company president, president of the United States or someone in a rural slum. They answer any question directly, to the best of their ability, with the same look in their eyes.
- He says Juran was like this: any question was treated as the most important one, and even a foolish question was answered with the greatest desire to transmit what he had learned.
- On sustaining a contribution over decades rather than a peak: such people are "very internally driven." There will be times of criticism amid the ebb and tide of fads and opinion.
- When criticized, you learn to "listen to your own drummer." That isolates you from praise too, so both matter less over time and you become more internally driven.
- He notes that someone who finds their great subject early and pursues it for decades makes a deep, lasting contribution, but usually with sacrifices below the surface, in family and in other things they might have wanted to do.
- He admires that Juran, in his senior years, was still mentally alive and flew cross-country to teach, driven by his heart rather than his pocketbook, to pass on what he had learned before he could no longer do so.
