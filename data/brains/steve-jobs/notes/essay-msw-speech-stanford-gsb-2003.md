## How Pixar and Apple differ, and why decision cadence matters

- Apple ships new products every few weeks. Steve makes about ten important decisions a day there, and most wrong ones are "not terribly hard to correct a few months down the road."
- Pixar ships one new product a year at best, and a movie a year is the "holy grail." As CEO, he makes maybe three important decisions a quarter, and they are very hard to change.
- He frames the two as coming from opposite ends of the same ideal: Apple is "the most creative of the technology companies," and Pixar is "by far the most technical of the creative companies."
- Technology companies and content companies "have absolutely no understanding of each other." Silicon Valley thinks creativity is guys on a couch drinking beer and thinking up jokes. Steve says Pixar's creative process is "as disciplined as any engineering process I've ever seen," and the people work as hard and as passionately as any technical person he has seen.
- Content companies, in turn, think technology is something you write a check for and buy. They miss the wide range of capability and elegance in it.

## Why Apple built its own retail stores

- Apple might come up with a dozen innovations a year but can advertise only four or five. More messages on TV would confuse customers, even with unlimited money.
- The other six to ten innovations have to be communicated at the point of sale, and the existing distribution channel could not do that. So Apple started its own stores.
- The goal was not to sell to the 5 percent who already own Apple products. It was to reach the other 95 percent.
- Those people would not drive ten miles to look at an Apple Store if they had no interest in buying. So Steve decided to "ambush them."
- Stores went in high-traffic locations, so the customer's risk was walking ten feet, not driving ten miles, and they could escape quickly if uninterested.
- Apple paid extra for great locations, such as University Avenue in Palo Alto and A-grade malls across the country.

## Hire people better than you are

- Steve calls this "the most important lesson I ever learned."
- In normal life, the gap from average to best is usually 30 to 50 percent, and "twice as good: rarely." Examples he gives: dinner in downtown Palo Alto, a flight home, rental cars, breakfast cereals.
- In people, the range is far larger. He saw Woz, "having meetings in his head," run circles around two hundred engineers at Hewlett-Packard.
- It took him about ten years to put this into practice, because such people are hard to find, and everyone says they are prima donnas.
- He found they are not prima donnas when they work with each other. They really like it.
- The Mac team proved it: fifty people did something that hundreds or thousands at other companies could not.
- His method is to find great people who love what they do and are extremely good at it. Sometimes they have experience, and sometimes they are young "diamonds in the rough" you take a chance on.
- It is one of his mantras at Apple and Pixar: "recruiting is the most important thing that you do. Finding the right people, that's half the battle."

## Management by values

- He dismisses management techniques like management by objective as "a crock," because they are all after-the-fact: "You've failed, and I know that because we are going out of business tomorrow."
- He learned a better idea from a man Walt Disney recruited to run Disney University: management by values. You find people who want the same things you want, "and then just get the hell out of their way."
- His trip analogy: if everyone wants to go to San Diego, the group can argue freely about whether to walk, fly or take a train. If someone shows the train is slower than flying, Steve drops the train idea, because he really wants to get to San Diego. If people want different destinations, it can never be managed.
- Apple's original goals: first, make the best personal computers in the world; second, make a profit so it can keep doing the first.
- For a time those were reversed ("we want to make a bunch of money, and so... we're going to have to make some good personal computers"). He says that "never works," and things fall apart.
- Subtle changes in values can mean everything, and the higher in the organization, the more pervasive the effect.
- To preserve something, pick a destination with a long enough focal length that everyone agrees on it, and don't codify how to get there. Each generation can then argue anew about the route.

## Turning Apple around: making reluctant managers

- When he returned in 1997, he found the individual contributors phenomenal. He asked why they stayed, and they said, "Because we bleed six colors," after the six colors of the old Apple logo. The problem was management.
- He got rid of most of the management team and promoted many young people into management.
- "Nobody in their right mind wants to be a manager": it is a lot of work and you don't get to do the fun stuff.
- The only good reason to be a manager is so "some other bozo" doesn't ruin the group you care about. People who have lived through bad management will step up to prevent it.
- He talked a few hundred people into it, and over 90 percent became "extraordinary" managers. He says that is what saved Apple.

## Lessons from being fired and growing older

- Being "basically fired from Apple" was really hard, but there would have been no Pixar without it. He says "sometimes your greatest strengths are your greatest weaknesses," and you learn the most from your greatest adversities.
- He never expected to end up back at Apple: "It's a circus world, and you never know what's around the next corner."
- He agrees that people learn more from failures than successes, and says he has made more mistakes than most people he knows.
- Age and family changed his perspective on firing. When young, he didn't think twice about it. Later he realized that even someone who should have been fired a year ago has to go home and tell their family they lost their job.
- He credits this to "getting older and kicked around, and maybe a little wiser in the process," not to any accomplishment.
