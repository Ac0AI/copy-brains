## Music as emotional anchor: what moved him

- Steve drafted a 27-song "Celebrity Playlist" in April 2003 (the year the iTunes Music Store debuted), then wrote up favorites that December.
- His list leaned on 60s-70s singer-songwriters and roots music: Bob Dylan (several entries, including "Every Grain of Sand," "New Morning" and the 66 tour live material), John Fahey, Cat Stevens, Joni Mitchell, Jackson Browne, Van Morrison, Neil Young, the Beatles, Velvet Underground, Jimi Hendrix, Bonnie Raitt, B.B. King, Django Reinhardt, the Talking Heads and others.
- It also included show tunes and classic standards: Lerner & Loewe's "Camelot," Audrey Hepburn in "Funny Face," Judy Garland's live "Over the Rainbow," and Ella Fitzgerald's Cole Porter Songbook ("I Get A Kick Out of You," which he called "incomparable").
- He kept the list personal and specific: song, artist, and sometimes alternates in parentheses (for example, "Here Comes the Sun" with "In My Life" and "Yesterday" as alternates).

## Why "For a Dancer" and "Little Green" hit so hard

- **"For a Dancer" (Jackson Browne):** He first heard it on his car radio driving down Highway 280 and "started crying." He had not listened to much Jackson Browne then and "was just blown away by the lyrics." After hearing many more of his songs, this was still his favorite.
- **"Little Green" (Joni Mitchell):** An autobiographical song about giving up a daughter for adoption. Steve was adopted, and wrote: "Maybe it's because I'm adopted, but this song moves me like few others." After he realized what it was about, he cried every time he heard it.
- He also admired that Mitchell wrote it when she was young, and called it "one of the best of her many great songs."
- The pattern: he judged art by whether it made him feel something real, and he let lyrics and personal history decide, not fashion or reputation.

## Music as memory: "All I Have to Do Is Dream"

- He loved this Everly Brothers song partly because it was a hit when he was very young and he remembered listening to it as his parents did, "which brings back great memories."
- He saw it as a transition-era record, from the 50s to the 60s: "Buddy Holly was dead, the Beatles hadn't arrived yet, and groups like The Everly Brothers filled the gap nicely."
- He noticed where a work sat in cultural time and what it filled, a habit that mirrors how he thought about products arriving into gaps.

## "A Golden Age" at Pixar: how he defines it

- In May 2003, preparing remarks for a Pixar celebration (ahead of *Finding Nemo*'s release), he opened with the dictionary definition: Webster's says "Golden Age" dates from 1555 and means "a period of great happiness, prosperity, and achievement."
- He claimed Pixar was in one, citing the record: with *Finding Nemo* about to be released, Pixar had completed 5 computer animated feature films, with three more in some stage of production, all original Pixar stories.
- He called the track record of 4 for 4, "and hopefully 5 for 5," "unparalleled in the history of animation, and indeed in the history of film."

## Where the Golden Age comes from: the maturing of people

- He said the Golden Age was "fueled by the maturing of our people," not by any single hit.
- Directors: Pete, Andrew and Lee had grown into "the second generation of Pixar Directors," each with the experience of leading a feature film.
- Producers: Darla and Graham had grown into "our industry's best producers," with greater and greater mastery.
- Technology: Rob Cook and his R&D team were "ushering in a new, second golden age of amazing new Pixar science and technology."
- He saw more directors and producers coming right behind them, and described this as "the kind of sprouting that signals Springtime."
- Takeaway on teams: sustained greatness comes from developing and promoting your own talent into the next generation of leaders, across creative, production and technical roles alike.

## Pausing to recognize the moment

- Steve told the team that tonight was for celebration, but to "pause for a moment and realize that this is what it feels like to be living in-and creating-a Golden Age."
- He asked them to imagine 25 years on, when a kid (maybe their grandkid) asks what it was like to be at Pixar in its Golden Age: "you'll know-if you can only find the words to describe it."
- His view of leadership here is to name the moment while it is happening, so people feel the significance of their work as they do it, and not only in hindsight.
- He expected the world, "already amazed at what we have done so far," to be "even more amazed at what's in store for them in the coming years," pairing pride in past results with confidence about what comes next.
