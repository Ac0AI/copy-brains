## Why Steve got into computers and what early projects taught him

- Steve first used a time-sharing teletype terminal at NASA Ames at about age 10-11. Writing a BASIC or Fortran program and getting back the results he predicted was "an incredibly thrilling experience."
- At 12 he phoned Bill Hewlett to ask for parts for a frequency counter. Hewlett talked to him for about 20 minutes, gave him the parts, and gave him a summer job at HP. That shaped his picture of a company: HP brought out a cart of donuts and coffee every morning, and it was "clear that the company recognized that its true value was its employees."
- At HP's Palo Alto labs he saw the HP 9100, the first desktop computer: self-contained, with a small CRT, programmable in BASIC and APL. He loved it.
- He and Wozniak built "blue boxes" after finding an AT&T technical journal that explained the signaling tones. AT&T's flaw was putting its computer-to-computer signaling in the same band as the voice. Their box was all digital, with no adjustments, and took three weeks to build.
- His lesson from blue boxing: "we could build a little thing that could control a giant thing." He says "I don't think there would have ever been an Apple Computer had there not been blue boxing."

## How Apple started: terminal to Apple I to Apple II

- Steve and Woz built a terminal because they could not afford one and wanted free time-sharing access. The Apple I was that terminal plus a microprocessor, and it was built for themselves out of scavenged parts.
- Each hand-built Apple I took 40 to 80 hours and kept breaking. Friends wanted them too, so they made a printed circuit board to cut the build time to a few hours. Steve sold his Volkswagen bus and Woz sold his calculator to pay for the board artwork.
- Paul Terrell's computer store (a bike shop in Mountain View) offered to buy 50 fully assembled units. They bought parts for 100 units on net-30 credit, built 50, sold them to the shop at twice the cost to build, and got paid in 29 days. They paid suppliers on day 30.
- They then faced what he calls a profit-realization crisis, with the profit sitting as 50 computers in the corner. That pushed them to think about distribution and to call computer stores across the country.
- For the Apple II, Steve's ambition was "the first real packaged personal computer" for the thousand software hobbyists for every hardware hobbyist, so you would not need to assemble anything.
- Venture capitalist Don Valentine passed on them but referred Mike Markkula, who had retired from Intel at about 30. When Markkula offered money, Steve told him "no, no, we don't want your money, we want you," and made him an equal partner.
- They tooled up the Apple II and announced it at the West Coast Computer Faire, where Steve (21 at the time) says they "stole the show" with a projection TV showing the graphics.

## Business is mostly folklore: ask why

- Steve says that in business "nobody knows why they do what they do. Nobody thinks about things very deeply."
- Example: factory accounting used "standard cost" with a quarterly variance adjustment. After about six months of asking why, he found it existed because information systems were too poor to know the real cost, so people guessed and fixed the guess at quarter's end.
- For the Macintosh he built an automated factory that knew exactly what something cost "to the second."
- He calls much of business "folklore," things done because they were done yesterday. If you ask a lot of questions, think and work hard, "you can learn business pretty fast. It's not the hardest thing in the world."
- On money: he was worth over $1M at 23, over $10M at 24 and over $100M at 25, but "I never did it for the money." He sees money as useful for investing in ideas without short-term payback. What mattered was "the company, the people, the products we were making, what we were going to enable people to do." He never sold his stock.
- He thinks everyone should learn to program, because it teaches you how to think, "like going to law school." He views "computer science as a liberal art."

## Xerox PARC and why companies lose their product sense

- Steve visited Xerox PARC in 1979 and was shown three things: object-oriented programming, networked computers (over 100 Altos with email) and the graphical user interface. He was so blinded by the GUI that he missed the other two.
- The PARC version was flawed and incomplete, but within about 10 minutes it was obvious that all computers would work this way someday.
- His explanation of why Xerox squandered it applies to monopolies like IBM and Xerox: when a company has a monopoly, a better product does not increase its success, so sales and marketing people become the ones who make the company more successful and get promoted. Product people "get driven out of the decision-making forums."
- The company then forgets "what it means to make great products." The people running it have "no conception of a good product versus a bad product," no sense of the craftsmanship needed, and little feeling for helping customers.
- PARC people called Xerox's executives "tonerheads," and Steve says Xerox could have been "the IBM of the nineties" or "the Microsoft of the nineties."
- Related lesson on IBM's PC: its first product was bad, but IBM gave many other companies a vested interest in its success, and that saved it.

## Process versus content, and why ideas are not products

- At Apple, Steve found that people from HP did not grasp the GUI. They thought soft keys at the bottom of a screen were the coolest interface, and they said a mouse would take five years to engineer and cost $300. Steve went outside to David Kelley Design, and in 90 days they had a mouse that cost $15 to build and was very reliable.
- His diagnosis of larger companies: "people get confused that the process is the content." IBM "has the best process people in the world. They just forgot about the content."
- His view: "the best people are the ones that really understand the content. And they're a pain in the butt to manage, but you put up with it, because they're so great at the content. And that's what makes great products. It's not process, it's content."
- The Lisa failed because it drifted from Apple's roots. At $10,000 it was "cheap" to HP-style people but impossible for Apple's market, distribution channels and customers.
- Steve lost the fight to lead Lisa, then formed a small team to build the Macintosh. The Mac target was a roughly $1,000 product. They negotiated the 68000 chip at a fifth of Lisa's price by committing to higher volume, and it launched at $2,500.
- The Mac effort also reinvented the factory (he visited about 80 automated factories in Japan), distribution and marketing. He describes it as "the machine to build the machine."
- On John Sculley's "disease": "thinking that a really great idea is 90% of the work." Steve says there is "a tremendous amount of craftsmanship in between a great idea and a great product." Designing a product means keeping around 5,000 things in your head and fitting them together in new ways, because the idea changes as you learn the trade-offs of electrons, plastic, glass and factories. "That process is the magic."

## Building teams: A players, the rock tumbler and direct feedback

- Rock tumbler metaphor: as a boy, an old neighbor put ordinary ugly rocks in a coffee can with liquid and grit, ran a motor overnight, and the rocks came out polished. Steve says a team is the same: talented people "bumping up against each other, having arguments, having fights sometimes, making some noise," and "they polish each other" and the ideas.
- Dynamic range: in most fields the best is at most about 2x the average (a taxi driver, a car, a CD player). In software the gap between average and best is "fifty to one, maybe a hundred to one."
- He built his success on "finding these truly gifted people and not settling for B and C players, but really going for the A players."
- Once you assemble about five A players, they enjoy working with each other, don't want B and C players, and "it becomes self-policing." They hire more A players, so "pockets of A players" propagate. The Mac team was all A players.
- The Mac team members say it was the hardest they ever worked and one of the most intense, cherished experiences of their lives, though "some of those things are not sustainable for some people."
- On telling someone their work is bad: "it usually means their work is shit," or sometimes that he is wrong. With really good people you don't have to baby egos, because "what really matters is the work." The most important thing is to point out clearly why their work is not good enough, without questioning your confidence in their ability, to get them back on track. Some people hated this, but the really good ones found it beneficial.
- He says he doesn't care about being right: "I just care about success," and he changes his mind within minutes when shown evidence. "What matters to me is that we do the right thing."

## Desktop publishing, the LaserWriter and a marketing blunder

- Apple got the first Canon laser printer engine shipped in the US and had it hooked to a Lisa. Then Steve visited ex-Xerox PARC people who were better, and he talked them (Adobe) into being a software company rather than a hardware one.
- Within two to three weeks Apple cancelled its internal project (some people "wanted to kill me"), bought 19.9% of Adobe for financing and some control, designed the printer controller, and shipped the LaserWriter.
- Nobody else at the company wanted it, because everyone thought a $7,000 printer was crazy. They understood sharing it over AppleTalk intellectually but not viscerally, because the last expensive thing Apple sold was Lisa. Steve pushed it through anyway.
- When he left, Apple was the largest printer company in the world by revenue.
- His "largest marketing blunder": announcing the "Macintosh Office" in 1985 instead of focusing on desktop publishing alone. "We should have just focused on desktop publishing."

## Apple's decline, Microsoft and taste

- On leaving Apple in 1985: "I hired the wrong guy." He says that when the industry slid into a recession in late 1984, Sculley, who "didn't know what to do," left a leadership vacuum, and Steve believes Sculley chose him as the scapegoat. He argues it was a problem of execution, not competing visions: Apple needed stronger leadership, to focus spending on the Mac, and to rein in the Apple II area. He offered to start a research division with a few million dollars a year and was refused.
- In 1995 he says "Apple is dying," on a "glide slope," because it had a ten-year lead and "stood still." By his account the Mac was only about 25% different than when he left, despite roughly $5 billion spent on R&D, and Microsoft's catch-up ended Apple's differentiation.
- On Microsoft: he credits it as strong opportunists who "just keep on coming" and says they used the IBM deal as a springboard. It wrote terrible Mac apps in 1984 but kept improving them until it dominated.
- Its problem: "they just have no taste. They have absolutely no taste." They don't originate ideas or bring culture into products. Proportionally spaced fonts "come from typesetting and beautiful books," and Microsoft would not have them without the Mac. Their products have "no spirit" and are "very pedestrian." He compares Microsoft to McDonald's, and adds that what saddens him is not their success but that their products don't show more insight and creativity.
- His view on how to raise the bar: "take the best, and spread it around to everybody," so people grow up with better things and understand their subtlety.

## Taste, the liberal arts and the bicycle for the mind

- A Scientific American article on locomotion efficiency ranked the condor first and humans about a third of the way down, but a human on a bicycle beat the condor. This led to his idea that humans are tool builders and that "the personal computer is the bicycle of the mind." He believes the computer will rank at or near the top of human inventions.
- On how to know the right direction: "it comes down to taste. It comes down to trying to expose yourself to the best things that humans have done, and then try to bring those things into what you're doing."
- He quotes Picasso, "good artists copy, great artists steal," and says "we have always been shameless about stealing great ideas."
- The Mac team included "musicians and poets and artists and zoologists and historians, who also happened to be the best computer scientists in the world." They brought "a very liberal arts attitude" and "I don't think you get that if you are very narrow."
- Asked if he's a hippie or a nerd: "clearly a hippie," and so were the people he worked with. He ties the hippie spark to the sense that life has "something more going on" than a job, a family and two cars in the garage. He says that same spirit, the one that makes people want to be poets instead of bankers, can be put into products, and users can sense it.
- On why Mac users loved the product: the best people he worked with used computers because they were "the medium that is best capable of transmitting some feeling that you have that you want to share with other people."

## NeXT, objects and the web

- In 1995 Steve ran NeXT, a $50-75M company with about 300 people, the largest supplier of object-oriented technology. He says objects let you build software ten times faster and better.
- His reasoning: innovation was in software, and the Mac made software easier for users but harder to write for developers. Software was a competitive weapon: MCI's Friends & Family was a brilliant idea built on custom billing software, and AT&T took 18 months to respond because it couldn't get its billing software done.
- On the web: it fulfills the dream that the computer would become "a device for communication" rather than computation, and it is exciting "because Microsoft doesn't own it," so there is a lot of innovation.
- He predicted tens of billions of dollars of goods sold on the web. He called it "the ultimate direct to customer distribution channel," where "the smallest company in the world can look as large as the largest company in the world." He expected it to be "the defining social moment for computing."
