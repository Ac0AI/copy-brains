## Humility and gratitude: the "I did not invent" note (Sept 2, 2010)

In a late-night email to himself, Steve listed what he depends on and did not make:
- He grows little of his food and did not breed or perfect the seeds of what he does grow.
- He makes none of his own clothing, did not invent the language he speaks, and did not discover the mathematics he uses.
- He is protected by freedoms and laws he did not conceive, legislate, enforce or adjudicate.
- He is moved by music he did not create.
- When he needed medical attention, he "was helpless to help myself survive."
- He did not invent "the transistor, the microprocessor, object oriented programming, or most of the technology I work with."
- He closes: "I love and admire my species, living and dead, and am totally dependent on them for my life and well being."

The note is signed "Sent from my iPad." It frames his view of life and work: everything he builds stands on the work of others, and gratitude follows from that dependence.

## Designing for everybody: the Pixar approach to the iPad (2010)

A week after the iPad launched, Steve told employees at Apple's Town Hall that Apple approaches products the way Pixar approaches movies.
- Pixar "doesn't make kids' films." He cites Walt Disney's definition of family entertainment: it entertains every member of the family (kids, teens, adults).
- Pixar films have many layers. They are fun for kids and also "incredibly great films for adults" (his examples: Ratatouille, Up, Finding Nemo).
- Apple does not design for a demographic. He rejects "our target audience is fifteen to twenty-nine, male": "We don't think that way. We think about making a great product for just about everybody."
- The iPad had no specific audience in mind; "we're thinking about everybody."
- Software lets the products "be tailored ... to do almost anything," which is what makes one product work for everyone.

## Pride in work you can explain to your kids

Steve described what makes Apple special to him personally:
- Employees do not have to explain their job with something like "I worked on our next-generation server, you know, that'll be powering something or other."
- They can say, "I worked on our next-generation iPad. You know, the ones that you use in school."
- He called that "a really wonderful thing." Making products people understand and use in everyday life is part of the meaning of the work.

## "Only as good as our next amazing new product": Apple passes Microsoft (May 26, 2010)

In an email to all employees, Steve marked the day Apple's market cap (stock price × number of shares) surpassed Microsoft's.
- He recycled an earlier company email: "Stocks go up and down, and things may be different tomorrow, but I thought it was worth a moment of reflection today."
- He cited Walt Disney's line to his team: "We're only as good as our next picture."
- His version for Apple: "we're only as good as our next amazing new product."
- He ended with "Back to work..." He allowed a moment of reflection and then redirected the team to the next product.

## "Sic Transit Gloria": staying unmoved by praise (Nov 2007)

Fortune named Steve "CEO of the Decade." Art Levinson, Genentech CEO and Apple board chair, congratulated him, calling him the "Beethoven of business."
- Steve replied in one line: "Sic Transit Gloria. (All glory is fleeting.)"
- He deflected the accolade immediately and treated recognition as temporary, which fits his focus on the next product over past success.

## Returning after the liver transplant (Sept 9, 2009)

Opening a music and media event, Steve spoke to the press about his health:
- Five months earlier he had a liver transplant. He now has "the liver of a mid-twenties person who died in a car crash and was generous enough to donate their organs."
- He said he "wouldn't be here without such generosity" and urged everyone to "elect to become organ donors."
- He thanked the Apple community and "especially" Tim Cook and the executive team, who "rose to the occasion and ran the company very ably in that difficult period."
- On returning: "I'm vertical. I'm back at Apple, loving every day of it," working with "incredibly talented teams" on new products.
- He then moved straight into the event topic: music.

## "One simple question": the organ donor bill (March 2010)

At Lucile Packard Children's Hospital at Stanford, Steve advocated for a bill requiring the California DMV to ask license applicants whether they want to register as organ donors. The bill later passed and was signed by Governor Schwarzenegger. It also created a state registry for living kidney donation.

His numbers and argument:
- Last year in California: 671 liver transplants, over 3,400 people waiting for a liver, and over 400 of them died waiting.
- He was nearly one of them. Despite great care at Stanford, there were not enough California livers, and his doctors advised enrolling in a transplant program in Memphis, Tennessee, where the supply-demand ratio is more favorable. He got a liver in time; the talk fell the week of his one-year anniversary.
- The problem is the default. Donors must specifically request it at the DMV. "No one asks you," and there is no marketing campaign, so people who do not know about the option are never given the opportunity.
- Even with this obscure procedure, over 20 percent of Californians had signed up.
- The bill only requires the DMV to ask. "Asking this one simple question may double the number of transplant organs available in California." He called it "a very high return on investment" for the over 20,000 Californians waiting for an organ.
