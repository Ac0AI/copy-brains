## Learning from elders: the Bob Noyce relationship

- Steve was in his late twenties and Noyce in his early fifties (born 1927 vs. 1955) when they became buddies. Noyce "tried to give me the lay of the land and tried to give me a perspective that I could only partially understand."
- The draw ran both ways. Noyce was interested in what someone in their twenties thought and was fascinated by the personal computer, which at that time "had nothing to do with" Intel.
- What Steve remembers is personal, not business advice: Noyce teaching him to ski better, and being one of the first people to call when Steve got fired from Apple.
- Steve says Noyce "had a lot of soul" and was "the soul of Intel," even next to Gordon Moore and Andy Grove.
- Steve says that approaching fifty makes it "easy to see how people in their fifties know more than people in their twenties."

## Cold-calling the legends: how Steve got mentors

- While young and running Apple, Steve called Noyce, Andy Grove, Jerry Sanders (founder of AMD) and a few others. His pitch: "I'm young and I'm trying to run with this company. I'm just wondering if I could buy you lunch once a quarter and pick your brain."
- "Everybody I ever asked said yes." That is how he met Andy Grove. He also got to know a little of Hewlett and Packard.
- He credits luck plus "the chutzpah to call these guys up."
- Others do the same: the Google founders called him and he had lunch with them. He thinks it never happened a lot, and it still happens a little, because most people have their own things to worry about.
- He says Silicon Valley has a tradition with "role models, and there's legends, and there's all sort of folklore," but it is not a "well-worn groove that you drop into." Many people never spend the time to learn it, while some slowly absorb it and get curious about what came before.

## The job of a leader in your fifties

- Steve argues that older leaders should not grab "the pencil out of the twenty-five-year-old's hand to do it better than they are." Instead: "If you're smart, you're hiring twenty-five-year-olds who are smarter than you."
- He says each side knows things the other doesn't, "and it all works."
- On Noyce: "It shouldn't have been Bob that was designing the breakthrough chips, and if it was, then he ain't running the company."
- The leader's job, as Steve lists it for Noyce:
  1. Recruit.
  2. Set an overall direction.
  3. "Inspire and cajole and persuade."
- If that is what a leader is known for, "that means he's doing the job." Noyce "had his day when he was the young hotshot... But that wasn't the job."

## Optimism and passion

- Asked about Noyce's view that optimism is the essential ingredient for innovation, Steve says it is "optimism and passion, because it's really hard."
- If you don't "really, really care about what you're doing, you're gonna give up if you're a sane person—because it's just super hard."
- He assumes it was extremely hard for Noyce at times too.

## Why history and lineage matter

- Steve calls the semiconductor-into-early-computer era the "second era of the Valley." He says he "got to smell that" and held it "near and dear," with Noyce as the reason.
- He compares it to smell, the sense most tightly tied to memory: a scent in your thirties can take you back to age seven. Noyce's gift was transmitting that kind of strong connection to a wonderful earlier era of the Valley.
- "There's a human drama to most everything." History can look dry, but "if you peel the onion, there's humanity underneath."
- "Just to understand what's going on now—you can't really do that unless you understand how it got here."
- He read from Schopenhauer's On the Suffering of the World: "He who lives to see two or three generations is like a man who sits some time in the conjurer's booth at a fair and witnesses the performance twice or thrice in succession. The tricks were meant to be seen only once; and when they are no longer a novelty and cease to deceive, their effect is gone."

## Succession: letting go of the CEO job

- Steve admired that Noyce "gave up the CEO job at Intel... He wasn't trying to run it from a back room."
- Noyce "understood how important it was to have a succession, to keep the company going, not have it just be a one-man show."

## Grinnell and liberal arts colleges

- Noyce asked Steve to join the Grinnell College board. Steve attended a small liberal arts college for six months officially and about two years as a "drop-in."
- He has "a soft spot for private liberal arts colleges," which "you really only find... in America, and they're a very wonderful thing."
- The best part of the board work was traveling with Noyce, including a near-fatal flight in Noyce's Seabee amphibious plane, where Noyce pulled the wrong lever and locked the wheels on landing. Steve credits Noyce's piloting for their survival.
