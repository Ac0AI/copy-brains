## The digital hub strategy and why the PC stays at the center

- Steve describes three ages of the personal computer: the hobbyist tool (Apple II, 1977, aimed at the many software hobbyists who had no soldering iron), the productivity age (kicked off by the first spreadsheet), and the internet age.
- He says Apple believes a third great age is coming, where the PC becomes "your digital hub" for photos, movies and music. Apple articulated this 3 years earlier, and "hardly anybody's delivered on it."
- Examples: a digital camera is useless without a PC, and anyone under 25 has their whole music library on a PC rather than on CDs.
- Why the PC stays the hub: it has a very big hard drive, display and keyboard, a fat pipe to the internet, and a processor that runs big applications.
- The iPod is a "satellite device." You couldn't run a music store on it because browsing needs more screen real estate, and a pocketable form factor fights a big screen "unless there's a breakthrough in foldable displays."

## Why Apple won't build a tablet or PDA

- **Tablet:** Steve says Apple has no plans for one and thinks tablets will fail. Notebooks are already used for taking notes in meetings, so the real issue is handwriting versus keyboard.
- Apple has the best handwriting recognition technology in the world (after the Newton pain), "It doesn't matter. It's really slow to write stuff." Anyone with volume of email needs a keyboard.
- He jokes that people learned to type because "death would eventually take care of" the non-typers.
- He sees tablets as a niche: a third computer for rich people to read on.
- **PDA:** he estimates 90% of PDA users just want to get information out, and only 10% do major input. Cell phones will carry that data.
- Apple chose to put resources behind the iPod instead of a PDA. Its approach is to write great software that syncs the PC's data to the phone (the Palm cradle model), and to work with Nokia, Motorola and others.
- Why not make a phone: Apple isn't great at selling to the Fortune 500's 500 CIOs, and phones have only about five carriers to go through. "We'd rather run an ad for millions of people and let everybody make up their own mind."

## iPod focus: "It's the music, stupid"

- Steve says the iPod had sold 700,000 as of a few months earlier, and he expects the millionth sometime later in the summer.
- Video and photos on the iPod are "speculative markets." Watching movies on a tiny screen "is really not very much fun," and the best portable movie device today is a PC with a DVD in it. Photos are better shown by plugging into a TV. Apple has such things in the lab.
- Music is a real, tangible market: "Music's been around for a long time... tens of thousands of years," and portable listening is already huge. Steve says to bring value to a proven market.

## How Apple missed MP3s, then caught up

- Apple was near last to MP3s because it was focused on video. It had invented FireWire, licensed it, and everyone else shipped it in camcorders before Apple put it in the iMac.
- Chain: FireWire, then iMovie (editing "without reading a manual"), then the need to put the result somewhere, so Apple pioneered DVD-writing drives with Pioneer and wrote iDVD.
- Music "happened around the side," and Apple "got hit over the head with a brick."
- Catch-up: CD burners in almost every machine, iTunes ("some think is the best jukebox software in the world"), and the iPod in under a year.
- Apple is also the largest video editing supplier in the world, with Final Cut Pro well ahead of Avid in pro editing.
- The "Rip, Mix, Burn" ad: "rip" meant pulling the bits off a CD you own. Executives without teenagers read it as "rip off."

## Walking the middle path: iPod and the iTunes Music Store

- Steve says Apple believes 80% of people stealing music would rather be legal if offered a compelling legal way.
- The iPod was built to avoid being a "theft shuttle." It syncs with only one iTunes library, so a friend's music can't be copied back. It is a compromise: "Both sides have to give up something to walk the middle path."
- He says nothing is unhackable, so the goal of DRM is to "keep honest people honest."
- Store features: buy, don't rent (people want to own music). The store is built into the iTunes jukebox rather than a web browser because browsers are slow and not interactive, and bought songs go straight into the library.
- Free 30-second high-quality preview of every song (about 20% of a track). One-click downloads, using the same idea as Apple's online store (between a billion and two billion dollars a year), which shares one-click with Amazon.
- Pricing: 99 cents a song ("three songs for the price of a Starbucks latte"), albums mostly $9.99 (range $7.99 to $11.99). Songs can be used on three Macs, any number of iPods and any number of CDs. Over 200,000 tracks, over 3 million sold in 4 weeks, with a Windows version coming by year end. The Beatles not yet available.
- Over half of tracks sold were bought as albums. Steve guesses labels will lower album prices over time.
- Long tail: labels' online catalogs can be much larger than the roughly 20% of a catalog that record stores stock, so "catalogs are going to be worth a lot."
- He also mentions browse by genre and artist as something not seen before online.

## Bridging tech and content cultures

- Steve says he learned at Pixar that tech and content industries don't understand each other. Techies think creative work is guys on a couch drinking beer. In reality creative people are just as disciplined and work as hard. Hollywood thinks technology is something you write a check for and buy, not realizing it is created with passion.
- He calls bringing these cultures together one of Pixar's greatest achievements.
- Labels' key skill is picking which of 500 acts will be the next Sheryl Crow, which is intuitive with little data. So it wasn't surprising they missed Napster.
- Apple told them MusicNet and PressPlay would fail and was dismissed. About 9 months later labels called back.
- Argument that won them over: the real competitor was Kazaa, which offered unlimited burning, permanence after you stop paying, and portable players. Offering fewer rights is like "a big sign up that says Kazaa this way."
- Why labels trusted Apple: a smaller sandbox (about 10% of the market, so failure stays contained), control of the whole stack (OS, apps, computers, iPod), and a big company with money to sue.

## Competition, markets and hard decisions

- On whether others will copy iTunes: Steve says it's harder than it looks. Getting label rights is hard, it takes real software work, and it needs a client app plus a jukebox. Apple would be open to supporting other portable players but few had asked.
- On speech: it's been "5 years away" for 30 years, like nuclear fusion; even 1% errors drive people nuts.
- Wi-Fi: Apple was first with low-cost Wi-Fi (AirPort), first to ship 802.11g (54 Mbps), and expects G to win over A because it's compatible with B. Networks will "just disappear."
- Market share by segment: consumer share more than doubled in a year to 5-10%. Creative pros have been depressed for years and Apple made mistakes in education. Retail: almost 60 stores, over 15 million visitors in 2 years, "the best buying experience for a personal computer on the planet."
- Advice on what he'd bet on at 17: biosciences, though he didn't foresee tech at that age.
- Hardest decision after returning to Apple in 1997: laying people off. Individual contributors were "unbelievable" ("we bleed six colors"), but management had to change. He did the cuts in the first year, while competitors laid off tens of thousands, and Apple decided to "innovate our way through this downturn" by shipping more products than ever.
