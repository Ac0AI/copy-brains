## Operational apps are the next big wave in desktop computing

- Steve cites Paul Strassman's *The Business Value of Computers*: successful and unsuccessful companies all spend about 2% of revenue on information technology. The difference is where the money goes. Less successful firms spend most on management productivity; more successful ones spend most on operational productivity applications.
- He concedes PCs and Macs only ever attacked management productivity, and that he "spent the first ten years of my life" on it. You can't buy an off-the-shelf app to run a hospital or do stock trading.
- His history: the 60s and 70s meant mainframes, terminals and COBOL programmers; the 80s changed nothing. Only two or three years earlier did front offices start downsizing to servers, Sybase or Oracle databases and Sun workstations, spending about two years writing apps such as Wall Street trading apps.
- New products in financial services need an idea, a sales force and a custom app to make the product real. Without the app there is no product.
- He compares this to desktop publishing in 1985, which nobody understood at first. His bet is that operational apps are the next big revolution and are more horizontal than they look.

## You often don't know your competitive advantage until customers tell you

- With the Macintosh, Apple anticipated bitmap displays and laser printers, but never desktop publishing. That became the Mac's compelling advantage, "four or five times better than anything else". It arrived 9 to 12 months after launch, and Apple changed its whole marketing and business strategy to focus on it.
- Desktop publishing became the "Trojan horse" that got the Mac into corporate America.
- NeXTSTEP followed the same pattern. It was aimed at shrink-wrap developers such as Lotus, Adobe and WordPerfect, and was a success there.
- In early 1991 big companies told NeXT, "you don't understand what you've got". The same software that lets Lotus build apps 5 to 10 times faster does that for in-house mission-critical apps. The message took about three months to sink in.
- NeXT changed its sales and marketing strategy that summer. Revenue grew about 4x last year and he expects about 2x this year.
- On Wall Street, building a product in 90 days instead of a competitor's two years means eight new products to their one.

## Software is the sustainable advantage, hardware is not

- Hardware churns every 18 months. If you are lucky you build something 1.5 to 2 times better than a competitor's, and it lasts about six months.
- Software takes much longer to catch up with. Microsoft took eight or nine years to catch up with the Mac, "and it's arguable whether they've even caught up".
- He estimates NeXT's first true competitor is four to five years away. That gives NeXT roughly 3 to 4 years to grow a $1-2 billion company so it can compete on scale (salespeople, ad budgets) with Sun.
- His competitor read:
  - Sun's Solaris has no objects and is falling behind in software.
  - Microsoft NT is "better plumbing for Windows", with the worst development environment ever invented and no object orientation.
  - Taligent is the first true competitor, expected around 1995. If it executes, it will have by the mid-90s roughly what NeXT has today.
- IBM and Apple endorsing object-oriented programming helps NeXT, because NeXT is the only one shipping it now.
- NeXTSTEP is an operating environment more than an operating system, and the UNIX is only about 10% of it.
- Developers don't write code faster on NeXTSTEP, but a given app takes about 20% of the code. "The code that's the fastest to write, the code that's the easiest to maintain and the code that never breaks is the code you don't write."
- NeXT ships six years' worth of objects. Independent third parties selling objects (not system software or apps) had begun to appear, about ten of them. He thinks that will be big.
- He advises software startups to write objects for other developers rather than polished shrink-wrapped apps. Marketing a breakthrough spreadsheet on mainstream platforms would cost $15-50 million just to rise above the noise.

## Why NeXT is both a hardware and a software company

- NeXT was porting NeXTSTEP to the Intel 486, shipping in September or October, for $995. This answered customer requests. Intel and Novell were helping because they feared Microsoft and saw NeXTSTEP as the only thing that could challenge it in system software for years.
- Sun customers wanted it on Sun hardware. Sun said it would rather "stick needles in their eyes". Steve says NeXT, being customer-driven, will probably do what customers ask.
- He expects to sell less than 100% of NeXTSTEP hardware but more hardware in absolute terms, and would be happy to sell 20-25% of NeXTSTEP hardware.
- The deeper reason is distribution. Computer stores can fulfill demand but can't create it, and the more revolutionary a product, the less the channel helps. Phone and Federal Express are making the store channel obsolete.
- NeXT's answer was a direct sales force of about 130 professionals in the US, spending 90% of their time on NeXTSTEP software and 10% on hardware. You can't fund that with $500 software packages, but you can with $5,000 average selling prices.
- Therefore, he says, no more system software companies will succeed on software alone: "we've got to be a hardware company in order to make our software business succeed".
- He expects a fracturing as NeXTSTEP spreads, comparable to when the Mac arrived. Other environments will absorb its breakthroughs and things will balance out over time.

## Manufacturing as a competitive weapon

- At Apple he led the effort to build the Mac factory. His mistake there: the two big pieces of automation, a small-parts storage and retrieval system and a burn-in system, were just "high tech warehouses". Warehouses hide defects, delay the quality feedback loop with vendors, and tie up money. NeXT's factory has no warehouses and uses true just-in-time delivery to the point of use.
- He uses Stephen Wheelwright's five-stage scale, from manufacturing as a "necessary evil" to manufacturing as a source of competitive advantage. He applies it to software too: most computer companies see software as a necessary evil, while only NeXT, Apple and Microsoft sit in stage five.
- NeXT treated the factory as a software problem, and its first factory hires were software engineers, persuaded to move from R&D with bonuses and a promise they could go back. Robot generations:
  - First: bought turnkey.
  - Second: specced the hardware, wrote the software on NeXT machines.
  - Third: built the robot hardware itself, which turned out cheaper than buying.
- Shipping early depends on parallelism. Manufacturing is involved from day one, and engineering hasn't built a wire-wrap or other prototype in two years. Prototypes are built on the actual production line at lot size one, with CAD data flowing over a T1 to a plant about 15 minutes away. No technology change means no accumulated knowledge is thrown away.
- Manufacturing people are paid the same as R&D, have the same offices, equipment and computers, and can move in both directions. For the first few years manufacturing had more PhDs than design engineering.
- He claims NeXT machines cost roughly half of Sun's and are built in Fremont, California, on the most automated factory in the industry. Canon, NeXT's Japanese partner, rates it a high-quality supplier.
- Suppliers own getting zero-defect material to the factory on time. NeXT pushes statistical quality data back to suppliers (Motorola is a key one). "Our money doesn't break after we give it to them, so their part shouldn't break after they've given us."

## Running the team: hire only the best, decide by consensus

- NeXT's policy team is eight people. They try to separate the roughly 25 really important decisions a year from the rest, and on those they talk until everyone agrees.
- His reasoning: if you tell someone to "buy into" a decision they disagree with, you end up paying them to do what they think is right while forcing them to do what they think is wrong, and "sooner or later it outs".
- "I don't view that we pay people to do things... What's harder is to find people to tell you what should be done." He pays people a lot to tell him what to do. He can't recall once saying "dammit I'm the CEO and we're doing it this way". He has told someone "you're off the team" only once or twice, after a prolonged divergence.
- Biggest change since Apple: a longer-term view of people. When he sees something done wrong, his instinct is still to fix it himself, but he now asks how to help the person learn, because "we're building a team here and we're gonna do great stuff for the next decade, not just the next year".
- On hiring, he won't compromise. Recent hires include a VP of Marketing who spent seven or eight years at Microsoft (Mike Slade), a CFO from Intel (Marcel Ghani) and a COO he chased for about a year and a half (Peter van Cuylenburg). Great people take a year or more to pry loose. After meeting someone truly good, he says, everyone else is second best, so "I've always found it best not to compromise and just keep chipping away."

## Technology windows and why consultants learn only a fraction

- A "technology window" opens when several technologies from diverse places come together to make a quantum leap possible. In his experience it takes about five years to build a commercial product that exploits the window, and about another five years to exploit it in the market.
- Examples: the Apple II lasted 15 years, DOS has lasted over ten, and the Mac is eight years old and has four or five more. They last "because they're really hot", not out of convenience.
- NeXT's first product failed: the cube sold only 10,000 units, because they "weren't quite there yet". The Mac was a course correction off the Lisa, which cost about $100 million. He says the window has opened after six years, and NeXT now has about five years of work to exploit it.
- He calls object-oriented technology the biggest technical breakthrough since graphical user interfaces in the early 80s, possibly bigger.
- On consulting: without owning something for years, taking responsibility for recommendations and "accumulating scar tissue" from mistakes, one learns only a fraction. He compares it to a two-dimensional picture of a banana, which you can show your friends but never taste. Consultants are also a variable expense, and in hard times "you find yourself variable".
- On higher education, he says discount heavily and sell there. It gives the best feedback and trains bright people you or your customers hire later. NeXT sold on about 350 US campuses and was the top-selling workstation there. Research projects like Athena rarely become products, because pioneering ideas and commercializing them take different people.
