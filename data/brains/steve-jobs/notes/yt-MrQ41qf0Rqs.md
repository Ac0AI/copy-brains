## Starting Apple young, with nothing to lose
- Steve says to start a company young: "we have absolutely nothing to lose." At 20 he had no family, no children, no house, and a Volkswagen van.
- His reasoning: even if they crashed and burned, "the experience will have been worth 10 times the cost," so there was no risk.
- He argues the only thing you really have in life is time, and investing it in great experiences means "you can't possibly lose."
- You can go to college and become a lawyer at 25, and nothing stops you. Older founders can succeed, but it is easier before you carry responsibilities to other people.
- Advice: "don't wait. Do something when you're young."

## Building what you yourself want
- Apple was a classic Silicon Valley startup in two ways. Steve and Wozniak both had ties to Hewlett-Packard, which modeled how to build a company on values, not just profit.
- He admires the HP way. Its first value was making a profit so the company could continue, followed by how to treat individuals. He calls it "very idealistic" and says it influenced Apple.
- Apple built a product its founders wanted themselves. They knew exactly what they wanted in a computer, so they could do the marketing as well as the engineering.
- The same pattern started HP, which made test equipment for engineers.
- This changed later, once Apple sold to people unlike its founders. In the first several years, customers were "just like us."

## Blue boxes and the power of ideas
- At 12, Steve phoned Bill Hewlett, who was listed in the Palo Alto phone book. He asked for spare parts to build a frequency counter. Hewlett gave him the parts and a summer job on the HP assembly line.
- He and Wozniak built blue boxes, devices that emit the tones the phone network used to signal between its computers. They fooled the network into opening up free calls anywhere in the world.
- Theirs was the first digital blue box in the world, built for about $100 of parts. It took roughly six months of discovery to work out. The boxes were illegal, which he notes.
- The lesson: two teenagers could control hundreds of billions of dollars of infrastructure from Los Altos and Cupertino. That taught him "the power of ideas."
- He says if they hadn't made blue boxes, "there would have been no Apple." It gave them the confidence that they could build something and make it work, and a sense of magic that they could influence the world.

## Watch the fringe, not the mainstream
- His rule for predicting the next five years: "you don't look in the mainstream, you look at the fringe."
- In 1975 the fringe was the Homebrew Computer Club. Its members built their own computers because mainframes cost $50,000 to $100,000.
- The club met every two weeks at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center on Wednesday nights. He called it the most important day of his life, and he spent his spare time preparing for it.
- Despite being shy, he set up his computer with a Sears color TV and answered questions at the demo period. They went when it was 30 to 40 people; it grew to 200 to 300.

## Asking, acting, and willingness to fail
- Venture capitalists turned them down, and one called Steve "a renegade from the human race" because of his long hair. He says "thank god," because those investors would have ended up owning most of the company.
- He argues Apple showed that great ideas are not the exclusive province of people with gray hair.
- On asking: "I've never found anyone who said no or hung up the phone when I called. I just asked."
- "Most people never pick up the phone and call. Most people never ask." He says that separates people who do things from people who dream about them.
- "If you're afraid of failing, you won't get very far." You have to be willing to crash and burn, whether on a phone call or in starting a company.
- He tries to be responsive when people ask him, to pay back that debt of gratitude.

## Iterating from the Apple I to the Apple II, and finding marketing help
- The Apple I sold about 200 units. He says it was "50% right" and taught them how to make the next big jump, and they learned from the market what people wanted.
- That learning made the Apple II a giant success. It launched in April 1977 at the West Coast Computer Faire and sold maybe 10 million units over its life. He calls it the first really successful personal computer "by a mile."
- That fall he realized they needed advertising. An Intel ad in a technical journal caught his eye, so he called Intel to ask who made it.
- The answer was Regis McKenna. Regis nearly kicked them out at first but took them on as a client.

## Computers as a countercultural cause
- Steve describes the personal computer industry as a countercultural movement out of the 1960s. It stood for the individual against the big-brother world of centralized mainframes.
- Apple's colorful logo set it apart from IBM's stark black and white one. Everything before the PC was big, massive, and centrally controlled.
- Startups of that kind were staffed by youngsters with little structure, but with a process everyone understood: get something done and make a difference.
- "Give something back" was a phrase he kept using. It was a sense of being part of a cause or crusade, not just a company.

## Work is sediment, and life can be changed
- He expects all his work to be obsolete by the time he's 50. The Apple II is obsolete, the Apple I was obsolete years ago, and the Macintosh is nearing obsolescence.
- His analogy: this field doesn't produce a Principia that lasts 200 years or a painting admired for centuries. He compares it to sedimentary rock: you add a layer to the mountain, and only a rare geologist sees it.
- His closing advice: when you grow up you're told "the world is the way it is" and to avoid bashing into the walls. That is a very limited life.
- "Everything around you that you call life was made up by people that were no smarter than you. And you can change it. You can influence it."
- Once you learn you can poke life and something pops out the other side, you can mold it. "Once you learn that, you'll never be the same again."
