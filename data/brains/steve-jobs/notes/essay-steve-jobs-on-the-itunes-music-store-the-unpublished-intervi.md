## Why "Rip. Mix. Burn." was never about stealing

Steve says the "Rip. Mix. Burn." tagline "was never not legit." People who read it as an anthem for stealing music "didn't know what they were talking about" and "obviously didn't have any kids living at home." He says Apple has been against stealing music from the beginning, and it owns a lot of intellectual property itself, unlike most of its competitors. The tagline meant "rip, mix, burn, exactly what it said."
- He sees no regret in how it was read: it "certainly got people thinking about Apple and music."
- It was frustrating when people misread it, but "everyone under 30 knew what it meant, so that was probably what really counted."
- He rejects the idea that the iTunes Music Store is "an about face." He says Apple has been consistent from the beginning.

## The complete ecosystem: store, jukebox, iPod

Steve describes the iTunes Music Store as the piece that completes "the first real complete ecosystem for the digital music age."
- Buying: a legal way to buy music online that is "better than any other way to acquire music."
- Managing: the iTunes Jukebox, "the best in the world."
- Listening on the go: the iPod, "the most popular MP3 player in the world" and "one of the coolest things in the world."
- He says Apple is "the only people in the world" offering a complete solution "from one end to another."
- He frames Apple's role as leading the change of the PC into "a digital hub for a digital lifestyle": digital moviemaking, DVD burning, digital photography and digital music. "We're not followers, we've lead that charge."

## Why a better experience beats free (iTunes vs. Kazaa)

Asked whether Apple can sway the tens of millions using unauthorized services, Steve says "I don't know. We'll find out," but argues the experience is "far better."
- Downloads don't "crap out half way through."
- Tracks are perfectly encoded, "instead of having the last four seconds cut off."
- Previews of every song are "giant": one click and you hear the track. They also let you explore and browse music in a way no download service had done.
- With Kazaa, you can't browse or preview. People spend 15 minutes downloading a song, get less-than-perfect quality, and only then find out it's the wrong song.
- He says previews and browsing make you "fall in love with music again" and find both familiar hits and "gems you've never heard before."

## Why subscription services failed

Steve's verdict on subscription music services: "They've completely failed. Nobody wants to rent their music. They have hardly any subscribers."
- On Rhapsody (Listen.com), he says downloading "sucks." You can't burn a downloaded track to CD without paying more, can't put it on your MP3 player, and can't put it on multiple computers.
- Users also pay extra to download on top of subscription fees, so of course there is little download traffic.
- Apple's contrast is that buyers get broad personal use rights to the music they purchase.

## "We just make stuff": how Steve describes Apple's work

Asked about being called a "digital music impresario," Steve says he didn't know what the word meant ("Does that mean I run a carnival?").
- His description of Apple: "What we do at Apple is very simple: we invent stuff."
- Apple makes the best personal computers, some of the best software, the best portable music player, and now the best online music store. "We make stuff, put it out there, and people use it."
- On what's next, he declines to say: "we never really talk about what's next until we're ready."

## Why the store is hard to copy

Steve expects few competitors to follow, because the pieces are expensive and slow to build. He gives these examples:
- Infrastructure: pumping "oceans of bits" onto the internet, built over several years with movie trailers, cost tens of millions of dollars for server and networking farms.
- Transactions: the online store is tied into SAP and blessed by auditors, which he says is also tens of millions of dollars.
- One-click shopping: only Apple and Amazon have it.
- A popular jukebox: making iTunes and making it popular "costs a lot."
- The iPod: "nobody has done it but us. People have tried, but they haven't even come close."
- Because Apple already made these investments, it can leverage them and add the store on top. For a rival, "it's tens of millions of dollars and years." His conclusion: "I don't think this is going to be so easy to copy."

## Winning over the music industry

Steve says the talks began almost a year and a half before launch, when the climate was more hostile. Apple "had the luxury of going in at the top," talking directly to Roger Ames at Warner and Doug Morris at Universal, among other label heads.
- The labels knew the internet was in their future but were "shell-shocked with Napster."
- The main negotiation was over giving users broad personal use rights. He says Apple worked through it and the labels "trusted us to do the right thing."
- He credits Apple's standing in the industry: most people in music use Macs and own iPods, and the labels see Apple as "the most creative of the technical companies, a very artist-friendly company, very credible."
- The result was "landmark deals" that gave users broader rights to purchased music than anyone else had offered.
- Independent labels were "calling us like crazy." Apple put them off until after launch because the big five held most of the music, and planned to focus on the independents next.
- On traditional retailers, his answer is "You should go ask them. The Internet was made to deliver music."
