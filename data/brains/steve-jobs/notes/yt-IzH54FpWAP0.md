## Competition, constraints, and why the iPod stays narrow

Steve argues that products fight opposing constraints, and you must optimize for one job.
- Example: the PlayStation Portable is a great game machine but a poor music player, mainly because "it doesn't fit in your pocket."
- Games want big screens; music players want to fit in a pocket. "You have to pick one and optimize for it," and the second thing you do will be sub-optimal.
- On video: headphones give the same experience as great speakers, but "there's no such thing as headphones for video," so a small device can't match a 50-inch plasma at home.
- On wireless in an iPod: 802.11 drains battery. The deeper issue is that browsing and buying music is better on a computer with a big screen, where you can see many things at once. Steve calls this "one of the geniuses of the iPod."
- He holds that the iPod's market share rose over the prior year, and says Apple's focus is simply on "putting out the best products."

## Why Apple avoids phones and carrier-controlled markets

Steve explains that Apple's strengths do not fit industries run by gatekeepers.
- Apple's greatest successes have not been in the Fortune 500, because it is not good at "going through orifices" (corporate buyers, carriers) to reach end users.
- If Apple can't succeed with 500 corporate gatekeepers, he asks how it would succeed with the four US carriers.
- Carriers have gained the upper hand and hand handset makers "big thick books" dictating what the phone will be; "that's not the way you work."
- Carriers add 3G, video and music to raise ARPU, but it isn't rising (camera phones didn't drive photo sending). Selling songs at $2-3 on phones would be a lousy buying experience costing 2-3x a computer purchase.
- On the Motorola deal: initially just an iTunes player on some phones, with music transferred from a PC or Mac, not a store interface.
- He rejected an MVNO as an answer, saying it's unclear such ventures endure as an industry gets more competitive.
- On the claim that phones will absorb the iPod, he declined to answer directly and left it to "our actions in the future."

## Subscriptions, record labels and pricing

Steve says Apple is "not religious" about subscription music and asks customers what they want.
- Few customers ask for subscriptions. Three possibilities: not enough want them, enough want them, or people want a discovery layer on top of purchases.
- Yahoo's $5/month annual price was "substantially below their costs"; he predicted prices would rise as they gain customers. Apple staff had a betting pool on how soon (his guess: 5 months).
- Labels love subscriptions because they can keep raising prices, as cable did. Customers fear music disappearing when they stop paying and don't know future rent. A guaranteed $5/month for 10 years might be more compelling.
- Apple does ask customers: "we're out talking to iTunes customers constantly and non-iTunes customers constantly." Not driven by surveys, but uses them to course correct.
- Label economics on iTunes: labels earn more per song than on CDs because no marketing and no returns.
- Scale: over 400 million songs sold, over half a billion a year pace, over 70% share of legal downloads, which is still only about 5% of music distributed on CDs.
- Labels keep pushing to raise prices. Apple resisted. Apple also declined to fully back the industry's "intent" standard in the Grokster case because an engineer's intent could make Apple liable.

## Podcasting in iTunes 4.9

Steve previewed podcast support, due within roughly 60 days, on Mac and Windows.
- Podcasting is "iPod and broadcasting"; it began as "Wayne's World for radio." Anyone can publish a show and listeners subscribe, with new episodes landing in iTunes and syncing to the iPod.
- Stats cited: 29% of US iPod users had listened to a podcast (Pew survey), over 8,000 podcasts, 6.2 million Google entries. No FCC regulation since it uses the internet, not spectrum.
- Until now it required a clunky third-party app. Apple built subscribe and unsubscribe into one button, with a podcast directory in the music store.
- Everything is free; he expects an advertising-supported model "just like free radio."
- Content from KCRW, Clear Channel, Infinity, Sirius, NPR, BBC, Newsweek, Businessweek, Forbes, Washington Post, Disney and Adam Curry's Daily Source Code.
- Features: artwork that changes during a show, chapters, links to buy songs, and an explicit flag. Podcasts need not be hosted by Apple, any RSS feed can be added, and a submission service will be automated. Apple will highlight top podcasts with editorial, "just like we do with music."

## The Mac, the iPod halo effect, and Apple stores

Steve credits the iPod with pulling people toward Macs.
- Mac growth was three to four times the industry's in recent quarters. He credits "that iPod halo" and the retail stores.
- Stores: 105 stores (100 in the US), over a million visitors per week. The Genius Bar offers free help; the New York store has close to 100 geniuses.
- Mac US share is "starting with a four now." He has "given up predicting" a 10% share.
- Philosophy: "make the best personal computers in the world," the best OS, and awesome apps. Customers "are in love with their products."
- On Mac OS X security: no known virus spreading in the wild. He said Avie Tevanian insisted on requiring name and password authentication to load apps, which Steve's team resisted for a year before conceding. He warns never to be cavalier about security and doesn't market it, since it's a "red cape on the bull."
- Apple doesn't proactively push into corporations, but "hardly ever says no" when they want Macs.
- Xbox team at Microsoft bought roughly 4,000 Macs for development.
- On subnotebooks (no optical drive, about 15% of the notebook market), he admitted Apple had its hands full with the other 85% and that "maybe we should take another look."

## Tiger, Spotlight and Dashboard

Steve explains Tiger as Apple's best OS release by acclaim and sales.
- Spotlight: finding things on a computer had become harder than searching the whole internet. Apple built search into the OS instead of an add-on.
- Three advantages over Google/Microsoft/Yahoo desktop search: far more comprehensive (apps, preferences, PDFs); the OS tells it when files change so results are never stale; and apps (Microsoft is building it into Office) can adopt it.
- Design reasoning: users hit a vertical learning curve at the file system. Email, iTunes and iPhoto already manage files in apps. Spotlight is the last step, so he expects consumers to use the Finder less, with file management becoming "an app for pros."
- On previews, he said people mostly find items by name and type, and conceded Apple "should go look at" richer previews.
- Dashboard: hit one key and widgets (clocks, weather, stocks, flight tracker, calculators) appear instantly and vanish just as fast. Third-party widgets include Amazon, CNN, and a countdown. He called it likely "the most popular feature of Tiger."

## Pixar: story first, edit before you make it

Steve describes how animation differs from live action and why Pixar wins.
- Live action shoots 10-100x more footage than ends up on screen and builds the film in the editing room, when it's too late to fix problems. Animation is too expensive for that.
- Walt Disney's solution, which Pixar still follows: "edit our film before we make it, not after." Storyboards, filmed, with scratch voices and music, then iterated thousands of times in reels. Only when it works do they animate.
- "The story is king" is often said but abandoned under production pressure. Pixar has a story crisis on every film and stops production to fix the story. John Lasseter's saying: "no amount of technology will turn a bad story into a good story."
- Tech: about 120,000 frames per movie. Early frames took about 3 hours to render, and today's take about 3 hours even with computers roughly 10,000x faster, because "the appetite grows just as fast." The Incredibles is far richer than Toy Story, and Cars goes further. Pixar uses Macs and Linux PCs.
- Movies aren't zero-sum like computers: good movies help each other. He expects Pixar will someday have a movie that underperforms.
- On piracy: content will always reach the internet ("all there needs to be is one hacker"). The answer is business models "more enticing than free off the internet." Movies are already easy and cheap to watch, unlike CDs at $16.

## Trade secrets, leaks, and personal reflections

Steve defends Apple's lawsuit against bloggers who published confidential Apple documents.
- His line: the law says journalists have First Amendment rights, "except if they break the law," and these people took trade secrets with Apple Confidential stamps and published them. He notes Intel and others filed briefs in support, and that courts have occasionally allowed publication for the public good, but this isn't one of those cases.
- He distinguishes serious editorial outlets such as the Wall Street Journal from a world where "anybody can be a journalist" without supervision.
- Apple employees may not blog about Apple, to protect secrets. Steve himself doesn't blog because it's time-consuming.
- He pulled Wiley books from Apple stores over how Apple was treated, saying "we just decided we didn't want to do business with them."
- On Windows Media Audio: Apple chose open-standard AAC over WMA, saying Microsoft's license terms were "so onerous" Apple shelved them.
- On his health: his pancreatic cancer was a rare curable form, treated by surgery. He learned about the body's recuperative ability and was moved by the well-wishes: "it had a few blessings in disguise."
- On TV: cable and phone companies subsidize set-top boxes, which distorts the market and limits innovation, making it hard for TiVo or Xbox to supplant the box.
