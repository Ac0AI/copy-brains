## Survival came first, then stability: how Steve framed Apple's turnaround as the "Apple Hierarchy of Skepticism"

- Steve borrowed Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs to build what he called the "Apple Hierarchy of Skepticism". Each time Apple convinced people it had solved one level of doubt, the critics moved up to the next.
- He stopped seeing this as a bad thing: "what it means is we've now convinced them that we've taken care of last month's question, and they're onto the next one." His advice was to get ahead of the game by mapping the questions in advance.
- The levels, in order, were survival, a stable business, product strategy, and growth.
- Level one, survival. A year earlier the refrain was "Apple is dying." Steve points to three things that changed minds: a new management team, a new board with phenomenally experienced people, and a deal with Microsoft. The largest software company in the world helping Apple "added a lot of credibility".
- Level two, a stable business. Apple delivered $47 million of profit in the first full quarter under the new team and $55 million in the next. A third consecutive profitable quarter was due to be announced a week later. Steve also cites rising retention ("hardly any" people leaving) and hiring "incredibly good people".
- Other stability moves: realigned distribution channels, the "Think Different" brand campaign plus specific product advertising, and an online store that had become one of the largest ecommerce sites on the internet. Apple's market value rose from about $1.8 billion a year earlier to about $4 billion the day before.

## Cutting to four products so the A team can work on every one

- When Steve arrived, Apple had fifteen product platforms and "a zillion variants of each one". After about three weeks he asked, "How are we going to explain this to others when we don't even know which products to recommend to our friends?"
- He went "back to Business School 101" and asked what people want. The answer was two kinds of products, consumer and pro (design and publishing customers need pro), each in desktop and portable models. That makes four products.
- Steve's argument for four:
  - "We could put the A team on every single one of them."
  - They could turn each product every nine months instead of every eighteen.
  - They could work on the next generation or two of each product while introducing the first.
- The four: Power Mac G3 (pro desktop), PowerBook G3 (pro portable), the iMac (consumer desktop), and a consumer portable announced May 6th for the first half of the next year.

## The iMac: a consumer product Apple had lacked for years

- Within his first month Steve saw that Apple was walking away from the consumer market. It had no compelling product under $2,000 and had not had one for some time.
- He started a program immediately "to build the most kickass consumer product we knew how to do".
- The goal for the iMac was to combine "the excitement of the internet with the simplicity of a Macintosh". He says it is gorgeous and believes it will change the way computers look and should look.
- He frames consumer as the main growth lever. Apple invented the market for selling computers to consumers, then failed for years to ship compelling products at consumer prices.
- The existing strongholds were design and publishing (Apple market share between 50 and 90 percent depending on the segment) and education (over half the installed base and about 35 percent of new unit sales, bigger than anyone else). Adding consumer would strengthen both.

## Design and fashion as the competitive edge as prices fall

- Steve argues that as price points come down and the consumer market blossoms, design and fashion matter more.
- His example: the most popular watch in the world is popular because of its design, not because it tells time better. Ten years earlier the average American owned one watch; with design entering the market, the average American owned seven. "Seven watches because of design. Because of fashion."
- He calls Apple's in-house industrial design team the best he has seen in his life. The PowerBook G3 and iMac were designed inside Apple by "brilliant, brilliant, super hardworking people", and he promises "a continual stream of very innovative design out of Apple, I think the best in the industry by a mile."

## Brand as an asset that takes decades to build

- Steve says no brand in the computer industry is remotely as strong as Apple's. He puts it alongside Disney, Sony and Nike in recognition and consumer loyalty.
- He notes those other three brands all had serious problems too (Disney when Michael Eisner arrived, Nike and Sony in the previous half-dozen years), and came back stronger once the problems were fixed.
- His conclusion: "Brands take decades to build. And we think we have one of the best in the world."

## Four assets behind the growth case

- Steve names four "incredibly powerful, unique, compelling assets" for Apple's growth:
  - The brand.
  - The installed base, especially in consumer and education.
  - Design that can carry products into the consumer space of fashion.
  - Products that are dramatically simpler to set up and use than the competition's.
- He closes by returning to Maslow and saying Apple had demonstrated, step by step, that it was "coming back in a very big way."
