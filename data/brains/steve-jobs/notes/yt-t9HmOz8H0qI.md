## Computers as a new medium, and why design matters

- Steve describes a computer as "a simple machine" where "the gears, the Pistons have been replaced with electrons." Computers are adaptive, new, and "really dumb": the raw instructions are trivial (fetch a number, add two numbers, test if it's bigger than zero), but they run fast enough to look like magic.
- His analogy: if he could move 100 times faster than anyone in the room, he could run out, grab a bouquet of spring flowers and snap his fingers, and "you'd all think I was a magician."
- Over the last 20 years, people have built higher levels of abstraction on top of these instructions. "Could you go get some flowers" replaces "Left Foot, Right Foot, extend hand."
- At the time he gave the talk, the oldest person with a computer science degree was 39, and the average age of professionals at Apple was under 30.

## Fractional horsepower computing: why Apple exists

- Steve uses the electric motor as a history of computers. First came one huge motor, cost-justified only for giant applications. Then one motor shared through shafts and belts across 15 to 20 workstations. Then the fractional horsepower motor, which put power exactly where needed. He says a household has about 55 of them.
- Computers followed the same path: ENIAC in 1947 for ballistic calculations, then time sharing in the 1960s (one big computer split across many terminal users), then the personal computer.
- "The reason Apple exists is because we stumbled on to fractional horsepower Computing 5 years before anybody else."
- People looked at the 13 lb Apple and asked where the computer was. He told them it was the whole computer. The personal computer was invented in 1976. The industry would ship over 3 million in 1983 and, by 1986, more computers than automobiles in the US.

## A call to designers: make the new object great

- Steve says computers "look like garbage." The great product designers are off designing cars and buildings, and hardly any design computers.
- He argues that the 3 million units this year and 10 million in 1986 will sell "whether they look like a piece of [junk] or they look great," because demand is huge, and "it doesn't cost any more money to make it look great."
- Computers will sit in every workplace, school and home. People will spend two or three hours a day with them, longer than in a car, so industrial design, software design and interaction deserve at least the care given to automobiles, "if not a lot more."
- He says most of life's objects (cars, TVs, audio, watches, cameras, bicycles, calculators) are not designed in America: "we've blown it" on both industry and design.
- Computers and society are "out on a first date" in the 80s, so there is a chance to make them beautiful and communicate something through the design of the objects.
- Apple would spend over $100 million on media advertising in 12 months, and generate tens of millions of dollars of brochures and posters. He says this "can either be great or it can be lousy and we need help."

## Every medium shapes the communication, and new media start by imitating old ones

- A medium shapes not just the message but the process of communication. His example: the telephone requires both people present, while electronic mail lets someone retrieve a drawing at midnight, three days later, or from another city.
- New media fall back on old habits. Early TV was radio with a camera pointed at it, and it took most of the 1950s to find its own form. He cites the JFK funeral and the Apollo landing as moments that were not possible on radio.
- The video disc (55,000 images per side, or an hour of video) is mostly used for movies. His counterexample is the MIT Aspen Movie Map: every street and intersection photographed, a touch screen to walk forward, look left or right, enter shops, and switch between all four seasons.
- Computers are in the "I Love Lucy stage": business accounting in COBOL, as TV copied radio. Lisa is starting to break out. He has no drawing talent, but with LisaDraw can erase, move, shrink, grow, texture and airbrush images, then paste them into documents and email them.
- He expects the first phase of putting these tools into society to last about 15 years, and that period is a chance "to do it great or to do it so-so."

## Software captures underlying principles, not just experiences

- A computer program has no physical manifestation. It is "simply ideas expressed on paper."
- TV can recreate a set of recorded experiences, like the JFK funeral or Neil Armstrong on the moon. Programming instead "captures the underlying principles of an experience," which can generate thousands of different experiences that obey those laws.
- Examples: a Pong game follows gravity and angular momentum, so no two games are the same. In Hammurabi, seven-year-olds act as king of ancient Sumeria and manage wheat, land, feeding and planting. They play for hours with a crude macroeconomic model and learn from it. He says models must keep getting more sophisticated.
- His vision: books were great because they went from Aristotle to the reader with no intermediary, but "you can't ask Aristotle a question." Maybe a future Aristotle will carry a machine for life, and someone could later ask it what he would have said. "Maybe we won't get the right answer, but maybe we will, and that's really exciting to me and that's one of the reasons I'm doing what I'm doing."

## Apple's strategy: the computer in a book, and "liberal arts" in the machine

- Strategy: "put an incredibly great computer in a book that you can carry around with you that you can learn how to use in 20 minutes," with a radio link, within the decade. It is not yet technically possible.
- Three options: do nothing (too impatient), put a "piece of garbage computer in a book" (competitors do that), or design the computer they want to put in the book eventually. That is Lisa: breadbox-sized and $10,000 now, sold out for the next year, with an office market that pays for development.
- Next steps he projects: a shoebox-sized machine at about $2,500, then a book-sized one under $1,000, within five to seven years.
- Existing computers used ugly type (an "i" as wide as a "W"), few fonts, and no graphics mixed with text. Lisa has proportionally spaced text, 30 to 40 fonts, about 80 dots per inch on screen and up to 300 on a laser printer. He wants 600 to 800 dpi on a laser film printer. "We're solving the problems of injecting some liberal arts into these computers."
- Goal: in three or four years no college student writes a paper without one, "just like they will not think of going to a science class without a calculator today," and people think "wasn't this the way it always was."
- Computers should be easy to use: "We've got to get away from programming, because people don't want to program computers, people want to use computers." Generic programs (word processors, databases, spreadsheets) do about 90% of the work, with graphical programming next. He notes that the HP-35 and reverse Polish notation or a Casio watch with 18 alarms would have confused people 10 years earlier, so technical literacy rises over time.

## Building a company on great people

- Every professional at Apple owns stock. He had "never heard the word labor or management mentioned at Apple," and says it works like a union of everyone pursuing the same goals. Turnover has been under 5% since inception.
- A deeper motive: most of what we use (clothes, food, language, mathematics) was made by others. "You're constantly taking, and the ability to put something back into that pool of Human Experience is extremely neat."
- Hiring: "We believe in the phenomenon of great people": some people "can run circles around five pretty good people," and those are who Apple wants. They are idiosyncratic, "the artists of the world."
- Apple was approaching $1 billion in sales with under 5,000 people worldwide. Steve says they always hired people far better than the current job needed, because within six months the job grows. They hire people to tell Apple what to do: figure out what is needed, say what it costs, and do it. He wants an environment where people don't have to convince 30 others.
- The day someone decides they can't make a difference "is the day we've lost." Anyone can see him or John Sculley within a day or two. He observes that the Catholic church, the oldest and largest organization, has about four layers of management, so Apple needs no more than four and usually has about three.
- Starting with nothing, "you always can shoot for the moon, you have nothing to lose." Success tempts people into "cover your ass mode" and conservatism. The company gambled everything on Lisa for three and a half years, using the best and the brightest, and recruited people whose employers wouldn't take the risk. "Who do I have to convince to do that? Nobody, just go do it."
- He describes the culture: engineers arrive around 11, play ping pong, work hard, play volleyball around 4, work until 2 or 3 a.m. "They're a lot closer to artists" than the computer-nerd stereotype.

## Connecting computers, information, and education

- Networks: Xerox PARC linked about 100 computers on a local area network, and mailing lists multiplied: volleyball, Chinese cooking, until "there were more lists than people." He predicts networking will bring people together around special interests. He estimates five years to solve office networking and 10 to 15 for the home.
- On privacy, he is more concerned about the ability to turn information into knowledge than about giant databases. Tools for filtering are centralized, and the answer is to distribute that intelligence. His example: filtering congressional testimony on gun control, then emailing a nasty letter to his congressman.
- "Kids Can't Wait": California's Commission on Industrial Innovation found that about 44% of new California jobs in the 1980s would come directly or indirectly from high technology, and education was the biggest obstacle. Apple offered to give a computer to each of America's roughly 100,000 schools (about $50 million). Congress would pay 40% if Apple paid 10% (Apple made $40 million in 1981, so this was a significant bet). Senator Bob Dole killed the federal version, but California passed a law, and Apple would roll out 10,000 computers, one free to every California school, starting the next month. It won't fundamentally change the problem, but it avoids a computer "have-not" split.
- He says Apple doesn't do such things as a public service: "we do them because we think they ought to be done." Over half of GNP already comes from the information business, and laid-off auto workers must be retrained or "they're going to burn the cities down."
- Software distribution: there are 20,000 Apple II programs and about 2,000 IBM PC programs, and buyers don't know what to buy. Records have the radio station for free sampling, so software needs a "software radio station": send programs over phone lines, offer 30 seconds free or five screenshots, then buy with a Visa number.
- Opportunity: there were a million Apple IIs. A real-estate calculation program sold at $100 (dealer takes $50, $25 to make) to 10% of owners would yield about $2.5 million in profit, using under $10,000 of equipment (the figures in the transcript are garbled; the point is that a small developer can profit hugely). He mentions a 13-year-old who started Aristotle Software and was earning about $4,000 a week from three game programs.
- Voice recognition is "the better part of a decade away," because understanding language in context is much harder than recognizing words.
