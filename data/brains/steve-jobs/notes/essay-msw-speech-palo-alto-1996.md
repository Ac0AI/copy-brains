## Follow your intuitions, not a "career"

Steve told the graduating class that the most important things in life are not the goal-oriented, materialistic things everyone urges you to chase. He asks students to stay alert to life's "magical, mystical, and artistic sides."

- He says people rarely learn to listen to their intuitions, and that you can "make them come true" if you pay attention.
- A way to hear them: walk alone near sunset and spend time looking at the sky.
- The images he lists (sculpting in Paris, running a hospital for poor children in India, recording an album, working out a law of physics alone at 4:30 a.m.) are the kind of feelings he says are "very real" and can "blossom into something wonderful and magical."
- If you have no such dreams, he says you are "in trouble" and should recapture them before spending four or more years on a direction your heart may not want.

## Ignore the people who say your dream is impractical

He expects people to argue against following those feelings, and answers with examples:

- "You can't make a living writing songs." (Bob Dylan)
- "Helping children in India is nice, but you need to prepare for real life." (Mother Teresa)
- "You could be doing so much more with your life." He cites Einstein, working a low-level job in the Swiss patent office rather than teaching at a university, so he could stay up late working through his new ideas.

## Creativity is connecting unrelated experiences

- Definition: "Creativity equals connecting previously unrelated experiences and insights that others don't see."
- "You have to have them to connect them." Creative people often feel guilty that they are simply relaying what they "see."
- You get a more diverse set of experiences by not "traveling the same path as everyone else."
- He advises you to "feed" or "invest" in yourself by exploring uncharted paths outside your past experience, especially "those that carry a romantic scent."
- No one can know in advance which paths lead anywhere. The only thing to do is believe that "some of what you follow with your heart will indeed come back to make your life much richer." Doing so builds "an ever firmer trust in your instincts and intuition."

## The calligraphy class that shaped the Macintosh

Steve's example of connecting dots:

- At Reed College, a small liberal arts college in Portland, Oregon, then the center of a US calligraphy revival, he took a calligraphy course before leaving. At eighteen he was exposed to typography, graphic layout and font design.
- There was no hope of income from it, and some friends derided him for wasting his time on "fancy letters."
- Years later, designing the Macintosh, that experience drove him to insist on proportionally spaced type and a range of fonts, bringing a much richer typography to computers than had existed.
- It also led to the LaserWriter printer, so the letterforms could be printed at the quality they deserved, which "set the stage for 'desktop publishing.'"
- His claim: none of this would have happened at Apple if he had skipped that class for a more "substantive" one in economics or engineering.

## Don't be a career: make your avocation your vocation

- He calls the "Career" one of "the most dangerous and stifling concepts ever invented by humans," and the enemy of most dreams and intuitions. It is a concept of progressing through stages while training for and practicing your working life.
- The first problem is the notion that work is separate from the rest of life. If you are passionate about both, "they will become more or less one," which he calls a much better way to live.
- The perceived risk goes down as you meet the real world. Many people pick what they think are safe harbors (he names lawyers and accountants) and wake up ten or fifteen years later to discover the price they paid.
- His instruction: "Make your avocation your vocation. Make what you love your work."

## The journey is the reward

- People think you've made it at the end of the rainbow with the pot of gold. He says the reward is in crossing the rainbow.
- He notes he got the pot of gold "(literally)", and that anyone who reaches it knows it isn't the reward, so they go looking for another rainbow to cross.
- He asks students to think of life as a rainbow arcing across the horizon: you appear, get a chance to blaze in the sky, then disappear.
- The endpoints are birth and death, both experienced completely alone. He thinks young people are sheltered from both, out of fear that mortality will wound them.
- For him it is the opposite: "to know my arc will fall makes me want to blaze while I am in the sky. Not for others, but for myself, for the trail I know I am leaving."

## Regrets versus mistakes

- Mistakes are things you did and wish you could do over. Sometimes you were a fool, scared, or hurt someone. Some are deep, others not, but "if your intent was pure, they are almost always enriching in some way."
- Regrets are "most often things you didn't do, and wish you did." The goal is to live your arc with as few regrets as possible.
- His example: he still regrets not kissing Nancy Kinniman in high school.
