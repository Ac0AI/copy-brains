## Why software is the only place to win a step-change, and why custom business apps fund it

- Steve's rule from NeXT: when you ask people to leave the mainstream, they take a risk, so the reward has to be big. "The reward can't be one and a half times better or twice as good. That's not enough." It has to be "three or four or five times better."
- In hardware that gap no longer exists. "You can't build a computer that's twice as good as anyone else's anymore." You are lucky to be 1.33x to 1.5x better, and "it's only six months before everybody else catches up."
- Software is where a multiple-times leap is still possible. He claimed NeXT's leap with NEXTSTEP was "at least five years ahead of anybody."
- His take on Apple's PowerPC: it is equivalent to a Pentium, plus or minus 10 to 20 percent. That keeps Apple equal, but it is "not a compelling advantage."

## Revolutionary change takes technology, business, marketing and luck together

- Steve says his industry is one of the slowest, not the fastest. The graphical interface was pioneered at Xerox PARC and by Doug Engelbart at SRI in the mid-1970s and was only commonplace by the mid-1990s, a 10-to-20-year lag.
- Significant change needs a rare combination of technology, talent, business, marketing and luck, and it "hasn't happened that often."
- Business tends to fuel the changes, because it has money and pays for things that save it money or give it new capabilities. Many of the most creative people don't want to help corporate America, which slows adoption.
- His PDA example: the builders of devices like Apple's Newton and General Magic's product assumed families would buy several. At about $1,500 each with a cellular modem, the real first-five-year buyers are mobile professionals. The developers' psychology doesn't fit selling to them ("put on suits and hop on planes").
- The fix is a culture that matches the reason you built a product with the reason people will buy it.
- He values incremental improvement but has "always been attracted to the more revolutionary changes" because they are harder and more stressful. "You usually go through a period where everybody tells you that you've completely failed."

## Sell to business first, then let volume reach consumers

- Steve's history: Apple II success was driven for years by VisiCalc selling into corporate America. Apple sold to consumers on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays and to business on Tuesdays and Thursdays, to get volumes up and prices down.
- Apple gave Macintoshes to higher education while selling them at a nice profit to corporations. "So it takes both."
- For objects, he argued the consumer will not fund the revolution. Consumers don't know they have a problem with Microsoft. Corporations do, and they don't need to be educated about it.
- "There's a giant vacuum sucking us in there, and there's a lot of money in there to fuel the development of this object industry. And everyone will benefit from that."

## Custom apps are where the next PC wave is, and small teams can beat big ones

- Steve's thesis: the 1980s goal was individual productivity, solved by shrink-wrapped software. The 1990s goal is organizational and operational productivity, and the PC moves from a tool of computation to a tool of communication.
- Mainframe and minicomputer markets are as large as the PC market. Those buyers run custom applications, not off-the-shelf spreadsheets. You can't buy an app to run a hospital, trade derivatives or run a phone network.
- A new company product now has three parts: "an idea, a sales channel and a custom app to implement the product."
- Example: MCI's Friends and Family. AT&T could not respond for 18 months, costing billions, because it couldn't build a custom app for a new billing system.
- Example: McCaw Cellular runs the front end of its business on NEXTSTEP. Getting a customer onto the network used to take a day and a half; custom apps on dealers' PCs, networked to a server in Seattle, cut it to about a minute and a half with no human intervention (five minutes in his summary).
- Shrink-wrap development had become a game for big teams: 100 to 200 people for one to two years on one major word-processor or spreadsheet revision. That squeezed out creative people who work in teams of three to five.
- He called Windows "the worst development environment ever made", which suits Microsoft because it can afford 200 people on a project. With objects, "literally three people in a garage can blow away what 200 people at Microsoft can do."

## Objects explained, and how Steve judges inevitable technology

- Claim: object-oriented software lets you build "five to 10 times faster", and the result is more reliable, easier to maintain and more powerful. "In my 20 years in this industry, I have never seen a revolution as profound as this."
- His definition: objects are like people, with knowledge about how to do things and memory. You interact at a high level of abstraction. "They encapsulate complexity, and the interfaces to that complexity are high level."
- His analogy: a laundry object. You say "get my clothes laundered, please." It knows the best laundry in San Francisco, speaks English, has dollars and hails the cab. You know none of that.
- His test of inevitability: at Xerox PARC in 1979, within 10 minutes it was obvious "with every bone in my body" that every computer would work that way, even though the demo was incomplete and partly wrong. He feels the same about objects. You can argue about years and winners, but "all software will be written using this object technology someday."
- He expects the Mac's graphical interface to be followed by objects unlocking "a really big closed door" for the industry.

## Microsoft, monopoly and why the industry needs an alternative

- Steve's theory: Microsoft had two goals in 10 years, copying the Mac and copying Lotus's spreadsheet success. It achieved both and is now "completely lost."
- It could copy the Mac because the Mac was "frozen in time" and changed only about 10 percent in a decade. Apple invested hundreds of millions in R&D and produced almost no new innovation since the original Mac.
- His worry about Microsoft is not whether it won fairly. He says he is not qualified to judge that, and "I don't think it matters." The problem is that it is not an agent for the next revolution, while putting companies out of business and driving innovation out of the industry.
- Windows is Microsoft's greatest asset and greatest liability. It is so non-object-oriented that Microsoft can't go object-oriented without throwing it away, and it can't do that for years.
- He called Microsoft "the IBM of the '90s" and said its market dominance is "hurting the industry." He sees the gradual dissolution of IBM as the healthiest thing to happen in the industry in the prior 10 years.
- He proposed splitting Microsoft into three companies: systems software, applications software and consumer software. He wanted an alternative in applications (he hoped Lotus) and in systems software (NeXT).
- On Bill Gates: "a good guy"; they talk about once a month, but have "very different value systems." On gaining wealth from his ideas: "the goal is not to be the richest man in the cemetery."
- Niche is fine: the Mac was a niche product and Apple became about a $9 billion company, up from $2 billion when he left. A 10 percent systems-software share would make him very happy, and then "I'd go work like crazy to get 20."

## Faith in people, tools, and keeping a private life

- Steve calls himself "a tool builder." He builds tools he knows in his gut and heart will be valuable, feels the direction, then "stand[s] back and get[s] out of the way, and these things take on a life of their own."
- On technology: "It's not a faith in technology. It's faith in people." Technology is nothing; people are basically good and smart, and if you give them tools they'll do wonderful things. "Tools are just tools. They work, or they don't work."
- The benefit he sees: individuals can now do what only large, well-funded groups could do, with a computer under $10,000 as powerful as anyone's. The electronic web "flattened out again the difference between the lone voice and the very large organized voice" and lets people act as a "virtual organization."
- On the Internet in 1994: it is not new (10 years old). He prefers the den to the living room: he is excited about the Internet in the den, not set-top-box video rental or home shopping. He said phone and cable executives had no idea how hard and long the software would be, and "the people who are talking the loudest know the least."
- On fame: he thinks of his celebrity as "my well-known twin brother. It's not me." Not taking bad press personally teaches you not to take the great press too personally either. "People like symbols, and they write about symbols."
- On the Mac: it is like a wonderful romance that produced about 10 million children, but the whole day is ahead of you "to do something wonderful with."
- On meaning: "the goal is to seek enlightenment — however you define it," but he keeps such things private, because being in the public eye makes a private life important.
