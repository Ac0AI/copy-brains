## Steve's 1996 view of the Web: ubiquity, simplicity, and who wins

- Steve says the desktop computer industry is dead: "Innovation has virtually ceased. Microsoft dominates with very little innovation... Apple lost." He expects "the dark ages" for the rest of the decade, until some fundamental technology shift. He compares it to IBM driving innovation out of the industry before the microprocessor.
- The most exciting things, he says, are objects and the Web. The Web is exciting because it is ubiquitous ("anything that's ubiquitous gets interesting") and because he doubts Microsoft can own it.
- His rule for the Web: keep it simple. He wants the Web browser to stay like a dumb terminal and the server to do the processing, and he hopes too much client-side complexity does not arrive too quickly.
- He argues that client-side technologies like HotJava would be "dead wrong" for the next two years. They could fracture the Web into competing standards, slow its path to ubiquity, and play into Microsoft's hands.
- "There's a window now that will close. If you don't cross the finish line in the next two years, Microsoft will own the Web." He proposes freezing the client-server relationship by collective agreement and going for ubiquity.
- Complexity should be added on the server side, by providing value as Federal Express does with package tracking.
- Windows became ubiquitous through an "algorithm" of industry self-interest (Compaq and other vendors wanting software on their machines). He says the same self-interest exists in making the Web ubiquitous and not owned by anyone, "especially not Microsoft".
- He describes the phone-network effect: a hundred telephones is slightly interesting, ten thousand is when it gets really interesting. The Web crossed that critical mass, and he says "all the experts have been wrong", which he likes: "There's a phrase in Buddhism, 'Beginner's mind.'"

## Why objects are inevitable

- Recalling Xerox PARC in 1979: within 10 minutes of seeing a rudimentary graphical interface, it was obvious every computer would work that way. You could argue about the years and the winners and losers, not the inevitability.
- He says objects are the same: "It's so compelling. It's so obvious. It's so much better that it's just going to happen." All software will eventually be written with objects.
- Every company selling on the Web will need custom application software, wired into order-management and collection systems. The number of applications needed is growing exponentially: "Unless we can find a way to write them in a tenth of the time, we're toast."
- He claims objects let you build applications with about 10 to 20 percent of the development otherwise required. WebObjects, he says, lets you write Web applications 10 times faster.
- Opportunity he saw: the bottleneck is building Web applications, so NeXT would layer products above simple Web servers. Free server software (and Netscape selling "hardly any") means little money in the server itself.

## Four uses of a Web server, and the economics of information

- Steve lists four uses:
  1. Simple publishing (99 percent of people today; free server packages suffice).
  2. Complex publishing, which he expects to "absolutely explode in the next 12 to 18 months". Example: the Federal Express tracking site, which took about four months to write.
  3. Commerce, which he thinks is about two years away and "also going to be huge".
  4. Internal intranet sites, written once instead of separate Mac, PC and Unix versions, with companies running dozens or hundreds of internal servers.
- Who wins: browsers will be free; RBOCs will win the pipes (he predicts under $25 a month for ISDN plus an Internet account); servers are a modest business.
- Federal Express won over competitors through its package-tracking system; getting information out of most companies is "usually impossible".
- His car-dealer example: inventory ties up billions of dollars, is open to vandalism and becomes obsolete. He imagines one demo car and a laserdisc of colors, then ordering a car and getting it in a week. Delays come not from making things but from "how long it takes the information to flow through the system".
- He sees the Web as a direct-to-customer channel that bypasses middlemen, who "tend to slow things down, muck things up, and make things more expensive".

## The Web as democratizer, without hype

- Steve says much of what he has done has an element of democratizing. On the Web, a small company can look as large and accessible as a big one, which neutralizes the hundreds of millions big companies spend on distribution channels. He loves "things that level hierarchy".
- He rejects the "Web changes the world" framing: "The Web is not going to change the world, certainly not in the next 10 years. It's going to augment the world."
- Now 40 and a father, he says technology does not change the basic human arc: "We're born, we live for a brief instant, and we die." Technology can still matter, such as connecting parents of a child with a birth defect to support groups and experimental treatments. "Things don't have to change the world to be important."
- If the Web reached 10 percent of goods and services in the US, "it would be phenomenal", though he expects it to go much higher.
- He defends big companies as mostly ethical, hard-working and meritocratic. Large companies that ignore change get hurt, while those who see it early are rewarded.
- On the tension that WebObjects sells mainly to big corporations, he says NeXT will give it away to individuals and educational institutions for noncommercial use.

## People, television, and why things are "getting worse"

- Steve says the US is an information economy but not an information society: "People are reading less and they're certainly thinking less," mostly because of television, and people are already in information overload.
- On TV he says that when young you suspect a conspiracy, but later realize "the networks are in business to give people exactly what they want. That's a far more depressing thought."
- He says the world has been getting worse for about 15 years, for two reasons: population growth overwhelming ecological, economic and political structures, and fewer smart people in government with less attention paid to important decisions.
- He is nonetheless optimistic about individuals ("inherently good", "noble and honorable") and more pessimistic about people in groups.
- He believes Silicon Valley's engineers held that smart, hard-working creative people can solve most of humankind's problems, and that engineers are well placed to solve them, but they are not drawn to politics.

## Why technology can't fix education

- Steve says he has probably given away more computer equipment to schools than anyone, yet concludes "What's wrong with education cannot be fixed with technology. No amount of technology will make a dent."
- He calls the problems sociopolitical: unions and bureaucracy. He cites the growth of the NEA against falling SAT scores and supports a full voucher system.
- Evidence from his daughter's private school, which he called the best he had seen and judged one of America's 100 best: tuition was $5,500 a year, teachers were paid less than public school teachers, and California spent about $4,400 per student on average.
- With $4,400 vouchers, he says, schools would "start right and left", with MBAs and idealistic graduates starting schools and working for pennies because they could set the curriculum, pick books and not teach one narrow specialization. He says most of what kids study is useless while valuable things are learned too late.
- Technology only hurts if it "lulls us into thinking we're doing something to solve the problem". Lincoln had no Web site in his log cabin yet "turned out pretty interesting".

## What design really is

- "Design is a funny word. Some people think design means how it looks. But of course, if you dig deeper, it's really how it works." The Mac's design was primarily how it worked.
- To design well you must "grok" the thing: it "takes a passionate commitment to really thoroughly understand something, chew it up, not just quickly swallow it". Most people don't take the time.
- "Creativity is just connecting things." Creative people feel a little guilty because they just saw something that became obvious after connecting experiences they had thought more about than others.
- Too many people in the industry lack diverse experiences, so they have too few dots and produce linear solutions. "The broader one's understanding of the human experience, the better design we will have."
- His washer-dryer story: his family spent about two weeks of dinner conversations on it, weighing finishing in an hour versus an hour and a half against softer clothes that last longer and use about a quarter of the water. European machines wash more gently but take twice as long. They chose German Miele appliances, expensive because nobody buys them in the US. The talk was about design and "the values of our family".
- He said the Mieles gave him more thrill than any piece of high tech in years because "these guys really thought the process through".

## Desktop metaphor and storage

- Steve says the desktop metaphor existed because computers were stand-alone and users had to manage their own storage. A new metaphor needs new issues, and managing storage may go away.
- He says he stores almost nothing anymore, using e-mail and the Web, and reminds himself of tasks by e-mailing himself ("That's my storage").
- Once people live mainly in a connected, not stand-alone, world, there are new options for metaphors.
