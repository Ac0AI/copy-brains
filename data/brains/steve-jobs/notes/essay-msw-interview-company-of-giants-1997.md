## Hiring is the highest-leverage work, and quality starts with people

- Steve says "You'd better have great people, or you won't get your product to market as fast as possible." Weak teams either ship late or ship fast but "really clunky" products nobody buys.
- "There are no shortcuts around quality, and quality starts with people." He adds that maybe shortcuts exist, but he is "not smart enough to have ever found any."
- He spends 20 percent of his time recruiting, even at the time of this interview (1997). He also spends a day a week helping other people recruit.
- He calls recruiting "one of the most important things you can do."

## How to identify A players: results first, potential second

- He admits it is "a very hard question" and describes two paths.
- **Experienced candidates:** judge by results. Some people "look so good on paper and talk such a good story but have no results behind them." They cannot point to breakthroughs or shipped products where they played an integral part.
- **Reverse search:** he finds great people by looking at great results, then finding out who was responsible for them.
- **Young candidates:** they may not have had the chance to create results, so evaluate potential. The primary attributes are intelligence and the ability to learn quickly.
- Drive and passion matter too: "hard work makes up for a lot."

## Interviewing is rolling the dice, and gut feel improves with practice

- "When you recruit, you're rolling the dice." He gets at most an hour with a candidate and still has to recommend hire or no hire. Others recommend too, so he is one vote among several.
- Ultimately it "comes down to your gut feeling."
- The gut gets refined by tracking outcomes. When someone he expected to do well doesn't, he asks what he overlooked. When someone he doubted succeeds, he asks why.
- Over time, "your gut instinct gets better and more precise," and so does his digging in interviews.

## Testing candidates under pressure: provoke them on purpose

- He researches what the candidate worked on, then deliberately criticizes it: "That really turned out to be a bomb. That really turned out to be a bozo product. Why did you work on that?"
- "The worst thing that someone can do in an interview is to agree with me and knuckle under."
- He wants someone to come back with "You're dead wrong and here's why." The goal is to see whether they fold or show firm conviction, belief and pride in their work.
- He sometimes tries to really anger a candidate to see the reaction.
- Reason: a company that is a meritocracy of ideas, full of passionate people, has "a lot of arguments." People who cannot argue well under pressure may not thrive there.

## Strengths and weaknesses are two sides of the same coin

- Steve says he doesn't know his management weaknesses in the abstract: "People are package deals; you take the good with the confused."
- Strengths and weaknesses are "almost always the same thing." A strength in one situation is a weakness in another, and people often can't switch gears.
- **His strengths:** viewing technology "from a liberal arts perspective, from a human culture perspective," and being "a pretty good judge of people" who can bring them together around a common vision.
- **Liberal arts example:** Mac desktop publishing (proportionately spaced fonts, ease of use) "comes from books: the typography, that rich feel that nobody in computers knew anything about."
- **His weakness:** being too idealistic. "Sometimes best is the enemy of better." He sometimes goes for "best" when he should go for "better" and ends up "going nowhere or backwards."
- He can be blinded by "what could be" versus "what is possible," and by one fell swoop versus incremental change. Balancing the ideal and the practical is something he says he must keep paying attention to.

## Motivation: the work binds people, not charisma

- "Ultimately, it's the work that motivates people. I sometimes wish it were me, but it's not." His job is to make sure the work is as good as it should be and to get people to stretch beyond their best.
- He doesn't credit charisma for the Macintosh team's output. Part of the CEO's job is "to cajole and beg and plead and threaten at times" to get people to see things in a bigger way and do better work than they thought they could.
- When people do their best and it isn't enough, tell them straight: "This isn't good enough. I know you can do better. You need to do better. Now go do better."
- Use that card carefully: "You must be right a lot of the time because you're messing with people's lives."

## Why the Mac team stayed bound: a product worth the sacrifice

- A dozen years after shipping, most of the Macintosh team still say it was the most meaningful experience of their lives.
- That only holds because the product shipped and was good. "It wasn't just about going to camp with a bunch of fun people. It wasn't just a motivational speaker."
- The product expressed everyone's heart and soul and their "deep appreciation, somehow, for the world to see."
- "In the end, it's the work that binds." The environment, coworkers and work create the bond.
- So pick important things to do: "it's very hard to get people motivated to make a breakfast cereal. It takes something that's worth doing."
