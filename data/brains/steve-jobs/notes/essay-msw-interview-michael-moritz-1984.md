## How Steve's taste in design develops

- Steve says his aesthetic taste is not unusual: "I don't think my taste in aesthetics is that much different than a lot of other people's."
- His influences were many small things, not any one profound source: wine labels, paintings in galleries, Volkswagen Beetles and vans. While designing the Mac he went around looking at Cuisinarts ("my Cuisinart week").
- The difference is that he is stubborn about making things as good as everyone knows they can be.
- Taste improves through mistakes: "Things get more refined as you make mistakes... Your aesthetics get better as you make mistakes."
- He compares aesthetics to singing. Joan Baez's voice is beautiful because of her superb ear (she can imitate a voice after thirty seconds of listening). In the same way, good aesthetics come from the eye, from an instinct for what you see, not so much from what you do.

## Making things great costs time, not money

- "If you're going to make something, it doesn't take any more energy—and rarely does it take more money—to make it really great."
- All it takes is a little more time, not much more, and a willingness to persevere until it is really great.
- He sees this willingness to persevere as the real difference between good and great products.

## Smaller products allow more precision

- Steve wants to build products inherently smaller than anything on the market.
- Smaller things can be made more precisely. His example is a watch: it is beautiful because its precision matches the scale of the object.
- As Apple's products shrink, the company gets the chance to express their form more precisely.
- He wants everything to be smaller.

## Products you can pick up and carry

- Even products that are not truly portable should have a handle that says "Pick me up and move me when you want to change where I am."
- Users should be able to carry them from room to room, office to office, or home for the weekend.
- Lisa was too heavy for this. The question for the future is how to package the same functionality into something smaller that people can carry, with the form expressed more precisely.

## Ugly everyday products and the push for simplicity

- Steve says most designed things around us are ugly: most cars (any car before three years ago), tables, chairs, and nearly everything in the room.
- Telephones are his example: only the original phone and the Trimline were any good. The Trimline is "the only decent one." What was done to newer phones is "just garbage."
- His direction for Apple: "we're just getting simpler and simpler and simpler. Very, very simple."

## Natural light in the workplace

- He admires HP's original buildings on Page Mill Road. They have scalloped roofs with glass facing north, which gives a whole glass wall, tons of natural light for the people working inside, and could even take solar collectors.
- He criticizes Apple's own buildings as dark: after five minutes outside, you walk in and can't see anything, "living in these little tiny caverns."
- He wants "a ton of natural light."

## What the "1984" commercial was saying

- Steve endorses Fortune's response about the Big Brother and IBM reading: "If seeing Big Brother in 1984 connotes IBM to a large number of people, that says more about IBM's image problem than our intentions."
- He admits Apple saw the analogy, and that "you'd have to be an idiot not to see the parallels to IBM."
- The first message was a comment on the cultural fear of computers as powerful machines controlled by a centralized group of people to keep track of us.
