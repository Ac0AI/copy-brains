## Where Steve says the 90s breakthrough will come from

- Steve argues personal computing, mastered in the 80s, was about individual productivity and, at best, creativity. More than 50 million PCs shipped in the 80s on that promise.
- For the 90s he says improving individual productivity "isn't enough anymore." The real competitive advantage will come from group productivity and collaboration.
- He calls this "interpersonal computing": using the same desktop tool that revolutionized analysis and planning (spreadsheets) and publishing (desktop publishing) to revolutionize human-to-human communication.
- He expects it to be the third revolution of the desktop computer in the first half of the 90s. He puts NeXT "about three or four years ahead" of tools like Lotus Notes. In his view Notes is okay but starts from a PC, "so you have a lot of inherent limitations."

## The industry splits into portable and powerful

- Steve predicts the industry will bifurcate. Within two to three years, current-generation machines (IBM PCs, most Macintoshes) will all go portable and get smaller.
- Customers will also demand more power in three or four areas:
  - large color screens that can show photographs, as in high-end desktop publishing
  - motion video
  - very high-speed networking, at least Ethernet speeds
  - rich media moving around the network
- He says this second class of machine cannot be made portable. The next generation will be powerful, networked, color desktop machines.
- He describes NeXT's machines as "somewhere between a workstation and a personal computer," a description the interviewer offered and he did not dispute.

## Standards versus innovation: the evolution/revolution tension

- Steve sees a constant, healthy tension in the industry between standards and innovation.
- Standards give everybody a baseline and a low-cost economic vehicle. But standard bearers "don't have any incentive to move forward and ultimately customers lose."
- Innovators use as many standards as they can, then leap up from them. This carries risk because they are outside the standard, but some succeed wildly.
- He calls Macintosh "a real revolution versus just an evolution." Most of the PC world has been evolutionary: the 286, 386 and 486.
- The revolutionaries that reach critical mass before the evolutionary players catch up survive and prosper. The rest get absorbed into the evolutionary path.
- Example: the IBM PC world finally got a graphical user interface seven years after the 1984 Macintosh.

## Why Steve expects a stagnant five years from Apple and IBM

- He sees a lot more status quo than real revolution coming in the next five years.
- He expects little from the Apple-IBM relationship. He predicts "more of the same" from Apple (System 7, 8, 9) and from IBM (286s to 386s to 486s to 586s). Spreadsheets will "run a little faster but nothing really different."
- He says it will fall to companies like NeXT and others to provide the revolution. The standard bearers can catch up five, six or seven years later, as the PC world did with Macintosh.
- On Apple's strategy, he says the premise of Apple's existence is better, highly differentiated products that third-party developers can build exciting new applications on. If Apple gives that away by giving away its future system software, he is "not sure that the market needs Apple."

## Compound documents are a user interface problem

- Steve pushes back on the claim that Xerox Star solved this. Star opened separate applications, and all of its applications were written by Xerox.
- It is easy to give a great demo when one company writes every app, because each app can know about every other.
- The real key is an underlying structure that lets third parties who never talk to each other have these capabilities within their apps. Nobody had yet done that well.
- He says the underlying technology is "very easy to hack up and give a demo of." The hard part is the user interface:
  - A spreadsheet brings its own paraphernalia, such as a formula bar and buttons.
  - A drawing program brings a palette, sometimes glued onto the document.
  - Nobody had figured out how to handle those context switches as the user moves between objects in a document.
- He judged GO's attempt too simple and not robust enough for real-life use, and said "you'll see some things next year" from NeXT.

## Early conviction and the PC's timeline

- Steve corrects the "10th anniversary of the PC" framing:
  - The first modern PC was the Apple II in 1977, which sold five or six million units before being retired.
  - 1981 was the 10th anniversary of nothing; it was IBM's entry, which had a large effect, but the course was already plotted.
- By 1979 Apple was already shipping more computers by unit volume than IBM, which was then shipping big mainframes and a few minicomputers. He says that made them realize it would have a staggering effect.
- He started Apple at 20 and was 36 at the interview, roughly 16 years of building personal computers. He says his vocation, avocations and growing up are all the same thing and hard to separate.

## Why the industry is not mature

- Steve rejects the view that the industry is mature and headed for rapid consolidation and a predictable few survivors.
- He says "we're about one inch down a road that's many miles long." Every major technological innovation creates opportunity for new players and a reorganization of the industry.
- He sees as much opportunity for innovation in the next five years as in any five-year period he has seen, and expects customers to get much better computers than today.
- He says he works with people who work very hard so the rate of innovation doesn't slow, and he wants most of it to keep coming out of the United States.

## Owning component technology: the US versus Japan

- Steve says even though most computers are assembled in the US, a large share of the dollars for the components flows overseas.
  - The most expensive part is often the display (color CRT or flat panel). Almost all of those dollars go to Japan.
  - The second most expensive is dynamic memory (DRAM). Most of those dollars also go to Japan.
  - The third is hard disk drives. Most of those dollars go to US companies, even though the drives are mostly built in Singapore.
- He wants the most expensive components managed back to America. He says this takes real effort, especially in displays and DRAM, because factories cost half a billion to a billion dollars each and the engineering isn't built overnight.
- He warns against being "hollowed out" as an industry, where only final assembly, printing manuals and "the bow on the shipping carton" happen in the US.
- He rejects the argument that Wall Street short-termism is the cause:
  - A factory is an asset on the balance sheet, depreciated over many years, so building one doesn't hit the earnings statement.
  - Apple, IBM and others have hundreds of millions to billions in cash, so raising capital is not the problem.
- He locates the real problem in management: leaders without engineering or manufacturing backgrounds who don't appreciate their dependence on underlying component technologies. In Japan, he notes, the people running these companies are engineers or from manufacturing.
- His principle: "we have to have more accountability for our raw technologies and not just assume that we can be the final packager at the end." Component suppliers don't need the final packager and can go directly to the consumer, as Sony already has relationships to do.
- He claims NeXT has the most state-of-the-art factory in the whole industry.
