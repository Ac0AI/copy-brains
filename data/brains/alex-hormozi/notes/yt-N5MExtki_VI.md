## Diagnosing the coupleneur business: a supply-constrained offer and a weak front end

Kyle and Ariel run Couple-preneurs, a coaching business for entrepreneur couples (clients do $150K+ a year). It does $480K revenue and $206K profit (about 43% margin). Alex's read is that the business is "supply constrained", and the founders solved that by building a second product instead of fixing the first.

- **Offer 1, Rise Together:** $5,000 paid in full, or 12 x $500. It has weekly group calls, a replay portal, a Facebook group and quarterly date night challenges.
- **Offer 2, Couple-preneur Accelerator:** $25K paid in full, or 12 x $2,500. It has quarterly 2-on-2 strategy calls, weekly small-group Q&A calls, and Slack access with a 24-48 hour response promise.
- **Current load:** 42 couples, and only 4 left in 12 months (all over payment issues).
- **Slack cost:** The founders say removing Slack would give back about 40% of their time.
- **Alex's diagnosis:** The low-touch product had the same customer acquisition cost as the high-touch one, so the economics broke. He says the better question is to ask what problem they are solving, then "can we just solve this problem in another way" without a new product, new acquisition strategy and new business.
- **Fix:** Sell the same high-value thing with less demand on their time, so they can sell about 3x as many.

## Trimming and stacking the offer: cut Slack, keep few high-value items

Alex used the "trim and stack" step from his Grand Slam Offer book. First list every asset, then cut and combine to the leanest, most valuable package.

- **Principle:** Offer the few things people really want and remove everything else. "Each thing we add on its own should be worth the price of the whole thing." He quotes Jack Dorsey: "Make all the details perfect. Limit the number of details."
- **Keep:** The deep-dive onboarding, quarterly 2-on-2 sessions, and two in-person events a year. The group is for community and curation, not support.
- **Cut:** Slack, which is "a time suck". He says Slack is something people like after they buy, but it is not why they buy or renew. The weekly group calls were also the clients' least favorite item.
- **Time cost after cuts:** About 10 hours per couple for $25,000 (deep dive plus quarterly calls plus events).
- **Why Slack backfires:** Responding fast trains clients to message more, so the group stays quiet. Questions asked once in a group serve everyone.
- **Planet Fitness example:** Gyms added racquetball, pool and basketball, but 80% of members used mostly cardio and some weights. Extras widen the gap between consumption and non-consumption. People then think "I'm only using 20% of my $59" and cancel. Planet Fitness catered to the 80% and cut price. Unused bonuses hurt renewals, so include only things clients will use.
- **Reason to buy vs. reason to stay:** Free pizza night at Planet Fitness is a purchase reason, not a retention reason.

## Fixing the supply constraint: change the delivery ratio

Alex lists five ways to solve a supply constraint. He then explains why he picked the ratio option for this business.

1. Raise the price (fewer buyers, more per buyer).
2. Change the delivery ratio (1:1 to 1:4 to 1:6 to one-to-many).
3. Productize.
4. Hire people.
5. Mix of these.

- **Price:** Ruled out. They already struggled to sell the high ticket, and a pricing move would need 3-4x.
- **Productize:** Ruled out. They already tried it and could not sell it at the high price.
- **Hiring:** A hiring path makes the business "a recruiting, training, and culture business". Alex says most coaching businesses fail at scale: if an X factor is easy to teach, it is not valuable. He has not seen anyone get above about $30M a year in that space. Those who do use a more distributed model.
- **Capacity math:** At 1:1, 5 calls a week for 12 weeks maxes out at 60 clients. Alex wants 150. At $25-30K (many on payment plans), that is about $4.5M, roughly 10-11x current revenue. A group of 4 for 90 minutes allows 4 sessions on Monday (6 hours, 24 clients), and he says two days a week of this gets to about 120 clients and roughly $3M, better than the 5 days a week they work now.
- **Shot-glass analogy:** Coaches spread the X factor thin, like pouring bottles into shot glasses and refilling with water. Group delivery gives each client a smaller but undiluted dose of the founders. Because the founders keep improving, the dose gets more concentrated. Tony Robbins is the extreme: one person, 10,000 people, $5,000 each.
- **Key-man risk:** Alex says to ignore it for now and "deal with that problem" at $4M a year.
- **Group size limit:** Beyond about seven, there is not enough individual time. Going from 1:1 to a group is harder than changing cadence.

## Framing group delivery and escalation to clients

- **Don't apologize:** Don't say "we're doing 1:4 now, it's hard." Sell it as better. Clients hear other people's questions and may find their problem is already solved by someone else.
- **Shame belief:** The founders worried couples would not discuss private issues in a group. Alex says couples' problems are common. Pitch: being a couple-preneur is lonely, and the group shows struggles are shared. "Shame lives in darkness."
- **Definitions:** Shame is breaking other people's rules that you respect. Guilt is breaking your own.
- **Limiting beliefs:** Alex quotes his own line: "We question all of our beliefs except for the ones that we really believe in." He says to disprove beliefs with evidence from other businesses.
- **Fewer interactions:** Don't be available "whenever", because that outsources the client's decisions. Position the service as help with big decisions: a handful decide your life. "The fewest things that make the most impact."
- **Escape valve:** Give clients two or four "911 calls" like the Who Wants to Be a Millionaire lifelines, for emergencies only.
- **Cadence:** It is easier to change cadence (monthly 1:4 sessions) than to change the ratio.
- **Existing clients:** Keep commitments to current clients. The new terms apply only to new agreements.

## Pricing, cash flow and the new offer structure

- **Offer:** One offer replaces the $5,000 and $25K split: $25K-$30K a year (the higher figure on a payment plan). Alex says the $5,000 offer "will never be sold again" and existing buyers are sunset.
- **Deliverables:** A 1:1 deep dive, quarterly 1:4 sessions in 90 minutes, and 2 in-person events a year.
- **Events:** Reposition them as "getaways", not conferences, and as "marriage insurance". Alex would test selling three a year, and the founders should stay two extra days to vacation. "It's just the little spikes that are the moments that you remember."
- **Cash flow:** Pull cash forward with layaway. Ask for the first quarter upfront: $9K down, because the deep dive is the most intensive part. Then start $2,500-$3,000 monthly payments immediately, not after a gap, to avoid churn.
- **Downsell:** Three payments before onboarding starts. Only prepaid clients get the deep dive and event access (fast-action bonus).
- **Expected effect:** Conversion depends on the first payment. The $9K frames as $2,500-$3,000 a month. He expects about a 5x price lift with similar conversion.

## Fixing the acquisition funnel: date night event and ad creative

**Current funnel**
- Facebook lead-form ads to a free 5-day virtual challenge, 1 hour a day. About 6 challenges a year, roughly $19,440 ad spend each.
- Per challenge: 511 registrants, 26 attendees, 12 calls booked, 10 held, 6 closed.
- Show-up rate averages 5.25%, with day 1 at about 9%, dropping to 4-5% on day 2. Cost per average attendee is $815. 49% of attendees book a call. About 90-95% of sales come from the top of the funnel (ads/challenges).
- Cash recovery takes months.

**Alex's changes**
- **Lowest-risk bet:** Rerun the existing challenge but sell the new offer.
- **Spicy bet:** Replace the 5-day challenge with a single 2-4 hour "date night" and pitch at the end. The total information needed to decide can be given in 5 hours over 5 days or one session, and he prefers one session. Attendees stay in state and flow, you reach about twice as many people at the pitch, and 3 hours of real content is enough. Frame: "date night is not business or fun. It's both." A possible hook is "the world's biggest date night". He believes the day of the week doesn't matter, and says Friday is fine.
- **Reasoning:** Date night is the clients' favorite feature. It is a unique angle in a crowded 5-day-challenge market. Long single-session events work for high-ticket sales because attendees get a feel for your expertise.

**Creative**
- Post organic content daily (they post about 2 a week), then run the best performers as ads with a 5-second CTA at the end. Organic is a free testing ground for broke businesses. Also slice the podcast where hosts hired them.
- Video is volatile: your best and worst ads will be video. Images win only when videos are bad. Run about 100 images (they ran 12-16), including camera roll photos.
- Once copy works, keep it and change only the creative. For Alex's school business, he spent tens of millions on ads and never changed the copy.
- Lead-form ads beat landing pages for them on CPA and quality in a year-long split test, so he told them to leave that alone. The only metric that matters is CAC. A $1,500 CAC on a $2,500 sale is fine.
- **Ad machine:** Offer a $2,500 rebate when a client posts a video of their first date night (permission to use it, talk about the experience, not money). The business then produces its own marketing, like a "fusion reactor".

## In-person events as the renewal engine

The founders sold only one high-tier program at their last event and no renewals, because they "went too soft" and barely pitched. Alex's rule: if you pitch, pitch.

- **Run of show:** Intro, two sessions (M1, M2), lunch with a soft re-pitch, M3 and M4, wrap. A dinner for buyers gives a reason to buy there.
- **Re-pitch:** 15-30 minutes, covering objections from the prior day framed as teaching.
- **Pitch:** Give value, stories and frameworks, then "if today was valuable, do you mind if I tell you a little bit more?" Then 15 minutes of stack and close.
- **Hosting:** Greet attendees by name and bring up two facts about each business or marriage. Split attendees into rosters by lanyard color, each handled by a team member.
- **Content diversity:** Cover different avatars and objections: business strong but marriage weak, the reverse, kids.
- **Renewal incentives:** First-class tickets to the next event (about $6K net cost) or a $5K fast-action discount.
- **Sunsetting the low tier:** Existing $5K clients ascend only.
