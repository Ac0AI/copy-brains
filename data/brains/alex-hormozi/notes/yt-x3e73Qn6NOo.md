## How Alex turned a $1,000 bank balance into $26M in 12 months (Gym Launch origin)

- In December 2016 Alex had six gyms and a turnaround business. After a partner took his gym money and a payment processor froze his launch revenue, he had to wire $222,000 in sales commissions. That left him about $1,000, and he screenshotted the balance.
- His turnaround model: fly a sales team into a gym, fill it with memberships, keep the money, and let the gym deliver the service. He had no way to process the cash and about 60-80 contracts a day were piling up.
- Fix for processing: he found a high-risk processor (8% fee, 10% held back, $50,000 monthly limit) and added more processors of $50,000 each over the following weeks.
- By the end of February he had about $330,000 profit. Then about $150,000 in refunds hit because some gym owners told customers to demand refunds.
- Pivot: eight gym launches were booked for the next month. Alex called those gym owners and offered to sell them the system instead of flying out. He quoted $6,000 on the first call, then $8,000, $10,000 and $12,000 on the next calls. He sold about $60,000 of licensing in one day.
- The license was his internal sales-training and nutrition-orientation material, plus white-label landing pages and ads already proven to convert. He used videos of himself and taught gym owners how to run the ads.
- He then called the roughly 30 gyms he had already turned around and made about $300,000 that month, nearly all profit. That covered the refunds.
- Gym owners averaged $30,000 in extra cash collected in their first month. They did it cheaply because they skipped the fly-in salesperson's commission, rental car and hotel and ran the offer in their own gym.
- Result of the first full 12 months (January to January): $26 million top line and $17 million EBITDA. He says he went from broke to $3 million in the bank in about six months.
- His lesson: the knowledge, sales skill and marketing were the same all along. Rearranging the offer's variables took him from a few million in revenue with almost no profit to millions in revenue and profit.

## Making offers people "feel stupid saying no" to, and the four value variables

- At a 2013 Facebook ads workshop, a gym marketer told Alex the secret to sales: "make people an offer they'd feel stupid saying no to." It became a core concept in every business he runs.
- He defines an offer as "the terms of exchange."
- He says people who say "provide more value" rarely define it, so he broke value into four variables:
  1. **Dream outcome.** A bigger or higher-status outcome is worth more. Men would pay more to make more money than to lose weight, because money brings relative status.
  2. **Perceived likelihood of achievement.** A surgeon with 10,000 five-star reviews beats a new graduate, even if the veteran is faster. The opposite of this is risk.
  3. **Time delay.** The gap between buying and getting the result. Liposuction pays 20 times more than a personal training pack because the result is immediate. Personal training takes an hour and a half of arm-wrestling to close.
  4. **Effort and sacrifice.** Effort is what the buyer must start doing (waking early, eating foods they hate). Sacrifice is what they must give up (sleeping in, "Margarita Mondays").
- The first two are the top of a fraction and the last two are the bottom. Each has an "evil twin": risk, delay, effort and sacrifice. Raise the top, shrink the bottom.
- The ideal offer is a guaranteed dream outcome that arrives immediately and effortlessly. Entrepreneurs innovate toward that ideal without ever fully reaching it.
- Scarcity (units) and urgency (time) add value by enhancing those four variables. Example: one Gatorade bottle left on Earth, or JK Rowling announcing no more digital Harry Potter copies from tomorrow.
- If not enough people say yes, "we need to make the offer better." He calls the offer the highest-leverage thing in a business because it affects pricing, profit, marketing, sales and delivery. It is also one of the hardest things to change for the same reason. It is the topic of his first book; leads (who to sell to) is the second.

## Niching down to raise prices: the PR company example

- Alex invested in a generic PR company serving small business owners. It had high churn but a strong sales engine.
- About 85% of customers were small businesses. In three or four months, 15% bought the most expensive package and stayed "forever." Those customers were people who wanted to fundraise, not dry cleaners or plumbers.
- He proposed serving only that niche. The company rebuilt its model around that segment and cold-called and cold-emailed only that narrow window.
- Result: it raised prices 10x and got higher email response rates than before, because the messaging spoke to one avatar and delivered more value to them.
- His general method: list everything the business is good at, then ask whether it can be rearranged for a specific customer so that what it does is far more valuable to them.

## Selling to bigger customers: "same work, more zeros"

- Alex says the biggest debt people pay is ignorance, and the most expensive thing is information they don't know.
- Example: a conversion rate optimization (CRO) freelancer lifts a $1M/year store by 20%, which is $200,000 of value. At 10% of that they earn $20,000. The same 20% lift on a $100M/year business is $20M, so 10% pays $2M. That is 100x pay for the same work.
- His viral tweet: "solve rich people problems." He phrases it another way: find the people who value what you have the most.
- He tells the father/son car story. A beat-up car is worth $1,000 at a dealership, about $500 as scrap metal and $100,000 to an antique dealer. The lesson is that it isn't who you are, it's the people who value you most.
- He says selling a $10,000 package, then $100,000, then multi-million is the same skill. Bigger deals are often easier, because the customer wires the money without haggling over a $50 item.
- His first pricing in 2014 was gold, silver and bronze packages at $200, $500 and $1,000.
- He thinks a skill is worth most when applied where it is in highest demand. Another guest's equity-for-marketing story illustrates the same idea; Alex agrees with it.

## Leverage: the stairs of his income and the four types

- Alex defines leverage as the difference between what you put in and what you get out. He says people who aren't making as much as they want usually have low-leverage inputs.
- His income steps, in order:
  - Employee to self-employed: four figures to five figures a month.
  - Hiring staff (he hadn't realized he could): six figures a month.
  - Licensing, which is digital with near-zero cost of goods: seven figures a month.
  - Capital: eight figures a month.
  - He guesses nine figures a month would come from technology or more media.
- The four types are labor, media, capital and technology (he credits Naval). Labor is the lowest leverage, media is made once and licensed endlessly, capital needs no time sacrificed, and technology is built once and used by millions.
- He says stack as many types as you can, and as much of each as possible. Joe Rogan and a smaller podcaster both use media, but with very different amounts.
- Capital has degrees too: friends and family, then institutional money, then public markets.
- His summary: "the people who move faster in life don't actually move faster, they get more for every step."

## Skill stacking and finding the constraint

- Each added skill makes the others more valuable. His example is a math person who learns bookkeeping, becomes a CPA, learns tax law and insurance, then capital and debt markets, then M&A, then sales. That person becomes a CFO and then a rainmaker. He also uses Jay-Z (rhythm, rapping, promotion, label, recruiting artists).
- Skills don't have to be related. The longer you play, the more unique your mix becomes.
- He dislikes entrepreneurs who learn a new thing and dismiss the earlier skill that made it possible.
- He says he isn't the best at any single part of business. He is "decent enough" at each that none is the constraint (copywriting, hiring). So find the system's constraint and bring that skill to "good enough."
- Advice for a skilled creative (his example is a media employee): either go into business with two or three co-founders with complementary skills, or choose between artist and entrepreneur. With a wallet maker, raise prices until you become a luxury brand ("the Versace of wallets") or mechanize production.
- His definition of growth: get more customers, or make each worth more. For "get more customers" he says there are eight ways, and you pick the one you're best at.

## Learning sales and building skill through inputs and outputs

- Alex recommends two years of high-volume transactional sales: door-to-door, cold calling or a gym front desk doing 20 consults a day. You need many repetitions and quick feedback.
- He says most people who survive the first three months of sales will be fine. For new reps: shadow the best person and do twice their volume, because you're not as good, and compress the time in calendar days.
- Sales teaches handling rejection and interpersonal skills. He argues marketing is "sales one to many," and many of the best marketers started in sales.
- He defines influence (sales, leadership, management, marketing) as getting people to comply with a request. His list of what makes an entrepreneur: the power to influence, drive (toward a mission or away from a fear), impulse control, and the ability to boil success into inputs and outputs.
- He builds belief from "unreasonable that it doesn't work" statements. Example: do more sales volume than anyone, shadow the best for five years, and collect 5,000 no's, and it's unreasonable that you won't become a very good salesman.
- GMAT example: after an average first score, he read a study showing score rising in a straight line with problems practiced. He bought 16 prep books and did four hours of problems every night for three months, and scored in the 99th percentile.
- Content example: if posting once a day on one platform gets some views, post on every platform, then multiple times a day on each. He treats it as a system.
- He says to aim to be directionally correct rather than get it right the first time, and to use anger or sadness as fuel because negative motivation is underrated. He says pain motivates faster and stronger than pleasure.

## Portfolio, free content and how he works now

- Alex's stated mission at Acquisition.com is to make business accessible to everyone. Courses are free and his books cost 99 cents. The idea is to give value first, using lessons from portfolio companies, so companies come to them already having received value.
- His audience survey: 25% have a business and 75% want to start one. Content aims to be wide (first-time founders) and deep (large-company divisions).
- Timeline: Gym Launch (licensing) grew, Prestige Labs (supplements) started two years later, and a gym lead software company about a year after that. In 2021 he sold all three. The supplement and licensing businesses went to American Pacific Group for $46.2 million for two-thirds of the company. The software company went to a strategic buyer in an all-stock deal.
- Acquisition.com began investing in 2020 and he sold partly because early investments went well. He takes mostly minority stakes of 25-49% as "growth partners," writes a check and adds value. He is in talks to take a majority in one company whose founder wants to move on.
- He says he works constantly, with only exercise four times a week as a hobby, and rests when output per unit of time drops sharply. He screens hires with a values question: if your shift ends at 5 and a 30-minute question arrives at 4:59, what do you do? He says to be transparent about expectations, since honest fit helps both sides.
