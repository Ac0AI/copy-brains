## Sell to rich people first, then go down market

- Alex's rule: "Sell to rich people until you have the money to sell all the poor people. The middle is where you get killed."
- Tesla started with the $250,000 Roadster, then a $100,000 car, then the X, then the Model 3. Each step down was harder because low prices need infrastructure built for huge volume.
- Walmart and Amazon were built for volume from day one and win on efficiency (coach flights, fold-out chairs, paper cups).
- A premium niche needs no mass infrastructure. Five clients paying $5,000 a month can fund a good business and a couple of competent hires.
- Relative value is the lever. A $100 payment is 10% of a $1,000 net worth, so that customer expects a lot. A rich person barely notices $100. Rich people pay more in absolute terms for lower relative value, and that margin lets you over-deliver.
- Alex lives on both extremes:
  - Acquisition.com is the premium brand: one or two deals a year, owners already worth about $50 million who want to get to $250 million.
  - School is the mass brand at about $99 a month. It took 5 years and tens of millions in development and still loses money, to give beginners a full business toolkit.
  - He built Acquisition.com first so he could afford to fund School.
- Hack for selling to the rich: copy what someone else sells to rich people, do it in half the time, and charge twice the price. Rich people pay for time above all else.
- Using the value equation, richer buyers want more certainty (perceived guarantee through reputation), less delay, and less effort. White-glove, concierge service works because $5,000 vs $10,000 a month is a small difference to them.
- Sell the wealthy on time saved, not money saved.

## You lack priorities, not information

- Strategy is prioritization: unlimited options, limited time, money and people. How you allocate them is your strategy.
- Story: an entrepreneur at about $10M a year had 56 companies. One company was about half the revenue. Alex asked how easy it would be to 5x that one alone. He ended the other 55 partnerships and did it within a year.
- First question Alex asks anyone who is stuck: "What's the goal?" Then: what's the current state, desired state and obstacle? Without those, you're "running around like a chicken with your head cut off."
- A media company with 40 million subscribers wanted to optimize SOPs. Alex pointed out they had no product to sell and wanted to monetize. A mediocre product would make far more than moving media from 97 to 98.5 out of 100.
- A workshop attendee asked about Alex's Closer Framework but already had a 40% close rate. Sales wasn't his constraint. Leads, back-end conversion, or team capacity probably was.
- Founders solve the problems they like and know. Double down on strengths as an individual, but the business must be balanced like a physique. It is only as strong as its weakest link, and the weak link is usually what the founder dislikes (people, hiring, management).
- Businesses often grow only to the constraint of the founder's specialized skill. Either learn the next thing or hire someone who has it.

## How to hire and evaluate people when you don't know the role

- Pattern recognition saves time. Hiring the wrong sales director repeatedly can cost a year or more of growth. "You tried hiring John. And John sucked." That doesn't mean no sales director can work.
- Interview many people and act like an ignoramus to learn the role. Don't jump at the first candidate.
- School's founder Sam interviewed 600 developers before finding his co-founder. Method: ask each person for the best people they know, and keep climbing the ladder, purely to gather information.
- Alex's litmus tests:
  1. Quantity and quality of metrics the candidate uses to describe the role. A customer success hire who can only say "make sure people are happy" can't drive an outcome. A strong one names activation within 48 hours, time to value, CSAT, churn as a lagging indicator, sale-to-onboarding time, early drop-off at 24/48/72 hours, and reviews by day 30.
  2. "How does what you do make the business money?" A CFO should cover cutting costs (an extra 3 to 5% to the bottom line) and supplying data for decisions. A sales director should say they raise lead-to-sale conversion for the team, not "I'll drive sales."
  3. If they can't tie their job to revenue or profit, they won't do it.
- The vaguer your direction can be, the more skilled the person must be. "Hey John, fix churn" works only with a skilled John, so smart hires are more efficient. Still learn the details so you can judge their work.

## Raise the talent bar and pay for A players

- Advice from a mentor: "Your best talent you haven't even hired yet."
- Thought experiment: with 10 of your best teammate, you would make far more money. So why isn't your attention on getting more of them?
- When Alex met private equity buyers while selling Gym Launch, he saw the gap in average talent on their side. Those people earned $1M to $5M, or $300K to $500K in their 20s. He reset what he would pay.
- Stupid rules (be on time, no Netflix on customer calls) signal low standards. He has implemented such rules himself.
- Amazon's rule: every hire should raise the average bar of the team. Left alone, sixes hire fives and fives hire fours. Alex asks "Do they raise the bar?" in interviews.
- In the early days he hired people whose jobs he could do better, so the business was capped at him. He now prefers someone with 10 years' experience who learned on someone else's money.
- Don't give big titles (COO) to a friend or relative with no experience. The Peter Principle: a good salesperson promoted to sales manager becomes a bad manager. Many gains sit behind hard conversations.
- Framing for demoting someone: bring in an experienced person, pay for the first person to learn from them, and refocus them on what they do well.
- Acquisition.com spends the most resources on recruiting. They message people on LinkedIn, e.g. "you ran ads at a $200M DTC business; take ours from $20M to $200M, or who do you know?"
- Pay arbitrage: five B players at $100,000 each can equal one A-plus player at $170,000 in knowledge work.
- Alex's own examples:
  - First $50,000 employee managed gym one so he could open gym two, which made $250,000 profit a year (5x).
  - First $300,000 salesperson brought $5 million in sales that year (about 14x).
  - First $1 million employee (35 years' experience, 40+ sell-side deals) saved $3 million a year in her first 90 days and used her network of bankers and private equity firms to sell companies (about 50x).
  - Returns increased as he paid more.
- Attract top talent with a meaningful mission. Every Elon Musk company is framed as saving the world.

## Get better, not bigger, and fix the obvious problem

- From Chick-fil-A founder S. Truett Cathy: pursue better, not bigger, and customers will demand you grow. Chick-fil-A grew one location at a time with little debt. Boston Market grew fast on Wall Street money, diluted talent, and went bankrupt about 10 years later. Missionaries beat mercenaries.
- "The business is growing or I am."
- Elon's and Chamath's rule: enter a market only if you can be an order of magnitude better. Elon skipped chocolate because he saw no 10x version.
- Growth is a lagging indicator; the things that make you better are the leading indicators. Making growth the goal turns an outcome into an input.
- Tactic: each quarter pick one confident improvement per function, and stack them rather than swap them.
  - Sales example: contact leads in 5 minutes instead of 30.
  - Marketing example: only run ads with tested hooks, and make 5x more of them.
  - Keep building checklists (ads, follow-up, sales-to-success handoff, activation). "100 golden BBs, not one silver bullet."
- If you keep asking for cheaper leads, you may be mispriced or just not good enough to earn more per customer.
- The big obvious thing is usually the problem. Ask owners: have you tried your own food, done your own workouts, been your own customer? "The magic you're looking for is the work in the work you're avoiding." (Chris Williamson quote.)
- Test from Peloton: make it so good that the next person a customer talks to hears about it first.
- Advertise only enough to pay bills while the product improves. Add gasoline once word of mouth drives growth. Alex bought into School.com after watching it since 2018 and seeing it grow with zero marketing.
- His windfalls came in distinct windows when he "backed the truck up":
  - Gyms: 30:1 return on ads because Facebook was underpriced.
  - Gym Launch: spent $100,000 and made $10 million, helped by word of mouth in a small niche because the licensees' gyms got results.

## Protect your focus and block time for what matters

- Few things kill a business within hours. Gym story: a 5:30 a.m. call about a broken water pipe. Alex's answer: get a bucket and call a plumber. Eleanor Roosevelt: "That could have waited till morning."
- "Priorities literally mean prior." You must let some fires burn. The only real short-term killer he names is losing payment processing.
- He works the first 4 to 6 hours of the day uninterrupted on the one thing that, if true, would make other problems go away. Early on he woke at 4 and worked until 10.
- Meetings only in the afternoon, and he sometimes pushes them far out. He tells people he isn't taking meetings until his book launches, then until the company sells, so the no isn't personal.
- Executive assistants book "from the back forwards": if the day ends at 5, the first meeting is 4 to 5. The more his wife and partner Leila takes off his calendar, the more money they make. Her productivity is a stacked day: delegating, interviewing, reviewing projects.
- High-level entrepreneurship means always hiring ahead. At the top, every business is the same: marketing, sales, product, finance, legal, tech people.

## Build brand as your long-term investment

- Brand is "an association people make between something they know and something they don't." Rub something they like onto your business.
- Three channels: what you say or show (controllable), what others say (referrals, reviews), and what customers experience (strongest). Product and brand are linked. Referrers risk their own relational capital, so treat referred customers well.
- Rule of marketing: "state the facts and tell the truth." Don't promise what you can't deliver.
- Brand strength shows up as price premium. Example: 1 million units at $3 above market is $3 million. A $300,000 fighter endorsement yielding $3 million extra is 10:1. Metrics apply as in direct response, just over a longer time horizon.
- Direct response is like a paycheck (stop paying, stop earning). Brand is like a 401k that compounds.
- Delivering on promises creates a moat (Apple buyers keep buying Apple). Reviews approximate experience.
- Lead magnets, challenges and nurture sequences approximate brand by compressing trust into hours. Big brands build the same trust across millions of people. It took Alex about a decade to learn this.

## Know your inputs and outputs, and avoid hacks

- Ask "Why can't we 10x this?" to find the constraint. Example: a team spending $5,000 a day on ads and five salespeople. Why not $50,000? Bad ads mean ad process problems, call volume means hiring problems, delivery limits mean back-end systems problems.
- Break the constraint into the smallest inputs.
  - Ad process: weekly, rewatch your top 20 ads, collect ads you liked across platforms, remake the best ones, reformat them (old footage new edit, or same message new take), and make about 30 net-new remixes. Half a day prepping and half a day filming each week. Ask to see the saved hooks and remixes.
  - Hiring math: about 100 LinkedIn reach-outs yields one hire. If you lose a third of a rep a month, 3 reach-outs a day only replaces churn. To double the team in a quarter, about 500 reach-outs and about 50 interviews, so 10 to 15 interviews a week. Employee churn works like customer churn.
- Most people stop at what they're comfortable with (a 5-person sales team, $1,000 a day in ads). Alex has gone from zero to 40 salespeople in a company. A billionaire fund founder ran 200 interviews in 7 days.
- Stop chasing hacks and platform algorithm tricks. Ignore algorithm advice unless the platform's goal changes. Make something so good people share it.
- One exceptional thing beats 100 mediocre ones. Quantity teaches you how much work quality takes. A video 20% better might get 5x the views for about twice the work.
- People want value per second, not seconds of value. "You make the money in the edit... in the distillation, not in the creation."
- Coats of paint: edit in separate passes, each looking for one thing (punctuation, data, typos; for video, lighting, transitions, sound). Review at different times and moods.
- Document the process that produces quality as checkboxes (wireframe with hook, story, steps, conclusion; record; check transitions). This makes training and replacing yourself easier.
