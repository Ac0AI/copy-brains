## Choose customers who value what you sell, and pick opportunities by three variables

Alex's most repeated pricing and market lesson in this talk: price and market depend on who is buying and how much they value the outcome. A gym owner who paid $6,000 for his sales scripts and launch system said yes instantly, even though Alex named the highest number he could think of hoping to hear "no". Alex had been doing about $100,000 per gym launch for these same buyers.

- He called the next buyer at $8,000, then $10,000, and did $60,000 in sales that day. His takeaway: "seeing how elastic it was."
- Within the next month he called the 30-something gyms he had previously launched. He did roughly $400-500K in sales (about $200K collected) selling the same system.
- He argues most "saturated market" complaints are really customer-acquisition problems. A founder at $2M/year in a $60B industry has not saturated anything.

## Turning a gym launch system into a productized offer (Gym Launch)

Alex's turnaround after a near-collapse in late 2016 came from packaging what he already did.

- Before: he flew out to gyms and filled them himself (launch business, 6-week programs at roughly $500-600 each).
- Problem: gym owners told clients to refund, and he held the processing risk. He also had to work around payment-processor limits ($50K/month limits at first, later $200K).
- Pivot: he sold the documentation instead of the service. It included ads, landing pages, lead-handling and sales scripts, and the back-end training. The average gym that bought it did about $30K in additional collected cash in its first 30 days.
- Backend: he then sold a 3-year licensing program ("Gym Legacy") for everything else he used to run and scale his gyms.
- Why it worked: the cost of goods was close to zero (licensing materials, ads and pages), and it used the same front-end/back-end structure as his weight-loss programs, just "with added zeros."
- On training content: his sales training was an hour long and extremely specific to one product at one price point. It covered how the chairs are set up, how the office looks, the tour, and the joke to make. He says it worked because he had done 4,000 of these sales and his own money was on the line.

## Evaluating opportunities: market size, value-to-cost gap, competition

Alex judges any opportunity on three variables.

1. Total addressable market (TAM): how many units could be sold. He uses the example of Kangen water. "Everybody drinks water," but nobody buys a $2,000 machine, so the real TAM is far smaller than it sounds.
2. Value-to-cost discrepancy: how much profit is possible per solved problem.
3. Competitive dynamics: for example, cell service has huge demand and high gross margins, but entrenched players.

He wants all three. He also says the equation changes as you learn more. His claim: every business exists on "information arbitrage," meaning one side knows more than the other, even a cattle ranch.

## Five ways to change your market (up, down, adjacent, broader, narrower)

Alex's example is a hair salon business.

- Up market: bigger versions of the same customer (salon chains, franchisors). Fewer, bigger clients.
- Down market: smaller versions (individual stylists, people who aspire to be stylists).
- Adjacent: similar needs (lash or nail salons).
- Broader: all adjacent markets under one umbrella (beauty).
- Narrower: tighten who qualifies as a customer.

In portfolio companies he often narrows first. He runs a common-factor analysis: take the top 20% of clients (roughly 80% of revenue), find what they share, and sell only to that group. His claim is that you can 5x revenue with the same operational drag.

## What acquisition.com looks for and how it works with portfolio companies

- Typical company: $3-10M in top-line sales, business or consumer services, e-learning, and licensing models (low overhead, high cash).
- Typical problems: founder-led, personality-driven, low customer lifetime value (LTV), high churn, and dependence on one or two acquisition channels.
- The plan: a five-year plan to take a company from near-zero enterprise value to roughly $30-100M, with multiple acquisition channels and an extended LTV that pays for them.
- Hiring and partner values: "unimpeachable character, sincere candor, competitive greatness," screened mostly in that order. He frames character with a Navy SEAL-style test: would you trust this person with your wife or your money for ten years?
- Partnerships: he says all his sole-owned businesses made money and his partnerships lost money. He does not oppose partnerships. His conditions are complementary skills, aligned values and a clear role for each partner, and splits don't need to be even. He cites Y Combinator's finding that 3-4 founders works best for huge companies.
- Decision rule with his wife Layla: "if we both don't agree, we don't do it." He overrode her once, and it caused long-term problems.

## Skills, long time horizons and the path to wealth

- He argues skills are the only thing that "will always appreciate": they stack (math, then accounting, then tax, then CFO work) and survive any currency or economy.
- Learn before picking the "ideal" opportunity, because "research is done through doing."
- Pick industries with long-lived, high-margin incumbents (insurance, oil, big tech) to find where profit pools sit.
- "You could guarantee that you could become a millionaire in a decade, but you have to be willing to not be a millionaire for nine of those 10 years."
- Early jobs should check one box: "learning or earning." High earners are often people who master sales, which he says transfers everywhere.

## Brand vs direct response, and the next book

- He defines brand as reputation: "what people say about you when you're not there."
- He says branding gives a higher return on advertising over a longer time horizon but is harder to measure. It is best when you are patient and have cash, while direct response is needed when you must make money today.
- He mentions his next book, tentatively titled Hundred Million Dollar Leads, about advertising defined as "to make known." It follows the first book on offers.
- Goals he states: getting Layla on the Forbes list of self-made richest women (about $250M together, he estimates 36 months) and then a billion.
- His giving philosophy is "give, give, give, get," and he ranks himself at about an 8 out of 10 on giving after being a 10.
