## Net worth math: earning vs owning your way to $1M

- Alex defines a millionaire as net worth above $1M excluding the primary residence (assets minus liabilities). He separates liquid net worth (cash and tradeable assets) from total net worth.
- His credentials: millionaire in his early 20s, first cash million in the bank at 27, over $100M net worth by 31. He says he has helped over 100 others reach $1M+ net worths.
- **Earn path:** to net $1M after tax you need roughly $2M of gross income. At $200,000/year for a decade, saving 100%, you reach $1M liquid. Time works against you because taxes and living expenses come out every year.
- **Own path:** a business making $250,000/year in profit, truly automated with a team running it without you, could sell at about a 4x multiple, which is $1M. The income is almost the same, but the net worth arrives as soon as the profit does. He notes this is a small profit for business-sale purposes.
- He says entrepreneurs are investing too: "you're just heavily indexed on one stock which is your own."

## Don't diversify early: focus all your time and attention on one thing

- Diversification is "a hedge against ignorance" (Buffett). Alex reframes it as focus: when you're early, your scarcest resource is time and attention, not money.
- His analogy is seven cups filling at once. Spreading attention across crypto, day trading, forex, flipping, Airbnb and ATMs means no cup reaches the overflow threshold, where profit starts spilling over.
- Wealthy people do have multiple income streams, but they got there by going all in on one stream until it overflowed, then diversified to maintain wealth.
- "It's not which one will work, it's which one will you work on." Any of them could work with enough effort.
- He thinks it is arrogant to believe 10% of your attention across 10 things will beat someone putting everything into one.
- **Five stages of the entrepreneur cycle:** uninformed optimism, informed pessimism, valley of despair, informed optimism, achievement.
  - Most people quit in the valley and jump to a new opportunity, restarting at uninformed optimism. "There's poo on both sides you just didn't know about it until you got there."
  - His goal is to compress the time to informed optimism.
  - After achievement, reset the target (for example, 10x bigger) and repeat. Having been through it once, you know the dip will come and you keep going.
- Compounding also depends on not diversifying: splitting your effort interrupts the compounding.

## Build for the long game: product first, then marketing

- The tower analogy: stacking blocks end to end is fastest for 10 seconds but collapses at 10 days. Building on a stable base wins over a longer horizon.
- Typical mistake: build a product, advertise it, get sales, then do more marketing and sales. If the product is poor, customers tell their friends it stinks, and ads get more expensive as the reputation sours.
- Correct sequence: make the thing, tell people, then keep fixing the product until customers become happy and refer others. Don't crank up marketing until that happens.
- If a business is stuck (for example, a $1M business that can't reach $10M), it was often built without a foundation. Sometimes the fastest route to $10M is to start over and build it right.
- Hiring: seek talent, not "a body in the seat." He may interview 20 people for one role rather than three.

## Finding a hungry crowd: market, offer, persuasion

- **Hot dog stand story:** a genie offers a wish. Cheaper, tastier or faster hot dogs are all the wrong wish. The right one is the best corner in front of a starving crowd.
- **Order of leverage:** market first, then offer, then persuasion.
  - Market: toilet paper during Covid sold out regardless of quality. Selling to newspapers, which are shrinking about 25% per year, is a headwind.
  - Offer: a free-to-start, risk-free, pay-on-results offer outsells a "pay me and maybe you get results" offer by 10x to 100x.
  - Persuasion matters most when all the offers look the same.
- **Four criteria for a market. You need all four:**
  1. Pain: people who must have the solution, not just want it.
  2. Purchasing power: a friend's business helping unemployed people fix their resumes had huge pain but couldn't grow because they were broke.
  3. Easy to target: nurses are easy to find, while "psychedelic Aztecs in Croatia" are not.
  4. Growing market: a friend with a rev-share offer to newspapers had a great offer and strong persuasion, and beat competitors every year, yet couldn't grow because the whole market was shrinking.

## One avatar, one product, one channel (to $1M and beyond)

- Alex's simplest formula to $2M+: one avatar, one product, one channel.
- **Avatar:** one very specific person you help.
- **Product:** below $1M, adding more products adds complexity with too few customers to justify it. Ask how to sell three times as much of the same product to the same person.
- **Channel:** you can reach $1M+ on any single channel.
  - Outreach: reach out at volume, whether cold calls, cold emails or DMs.
  - Content: post more and better.
  - Paid ads: spend more, profitably. If you can't scale past $1M/year profitably with paid ads, "you have a business problem or you have a pricing problem."
- Adding a second channel, such as cold email when you already make $500,000/year on YouTube, complicates things too early.
- Channel diversification reduces risk for an acquirer, but at this size it doesn't matter yet. Get better at the one thing.

## The value equation: how to build the offer

- From his book *$100M Offers*, which answers "what should I sell." Value has four variables.
  - **Dream outcome (up):** how much the prospect values the result. A millionaire outcome is worth more to most men than being handsome.
  - **Perceived likelihood of achievement (up):** a $50 ebook and a year of one-on-one personal training target the same weight-loss outcome, but the training feels far more likely to work.
  - **Time delay (down):** micro is the daily time required, such as 3 gym sessions a week plus meal prep. Macro is how long until the result, such as 18 months to lose 50 lbs.
  - **Effort and sacrifice (down):** effort is what you must start doing. Sacrifice is what you must stop doing, such as margaritas on Mondays and sleeping in.
- The perfect hypothetical offer is a button that gives you abs instantly with no effort.
- **Hidden costs:** often the most expensive part of an offer isn't the money but what the buyer must do. Marketing agency example:
  - Version A: 60-day ramp-up, two one-hour calls a week, fly to the studio, no traction for 60 days.
  - Version B: "I'll draw stuff on my own and start posting immediately, and your phone's going to ring today."
  - Same core service, but he calls B about 10x more valuable.
- The offer affects every department: marketing, the sales script, the sales process and delivery.
- At acquisition.com (which invests in and buys businesses, and doesn't sell coaching), Alex says portfolio companies average 1.8x revenue and 3.0x profit over the first 12 months. Over 24 months they average 2.8x revenue and 4.7x profit.

## Marketing and sales: the eight advertising methods and the founder's role

- **Sequence:** make the thing ($100M Offers), get leads ($100M Leads), then convert. After that, go back and improve the product, then promote more, in a virtuous cycle.
- **Core four (you do):** warm outreach (one to one, friends), content (one to many, people who know you), paid ads (one to many, strangers), cold outreach (one to one, strangers). If nobody is buying, "you're not doing enough of these core four."
- **Other four (others do it):** customer referrals, employees, agencies, affiliates (influencers).
- Pick one method at first. Learn a core four method before using lead getters.
- **Why the founder should do both marketing and sales early:**
  - It's cheaper.
  - You need to understand the skills tactically so you can teach and hold others to a standard ("no it is, let me show you how").
- **Sales process:** early on, throw things at the wall and see what sticks. When someone buys, backtrack through the steps and replicate them. Do the same inside the sales call: ask question one, then two, then three. Write a script, because later hires won't be as convicted as the founder, and the process keeps conversion up.

## Paying yourself, setting goals, and the scientific method

- **Paying yourself:** it depends on risk tolerance. Alex regrets taking nothing out while rolling every gym's profit into the next, then losing everything when he sold the gyms and was left with nothing.
  - Taking cash out forces cash flow to appear and forces you to cut personal spending. A full bank account makes for better, less stressful decisions.
  - He suggests a bank account that goes up every month as a personal goal.
  - **Capital-intensive businesses rule of thumb:** 33% to the owner, 30% to growth (such as new locations), and the remainder to cash reserves for the business and for you.
  - **Watermark method:** work out what growth costs per month (for example, two more hires or locations). Always leave that amount in the account and distribute everything above it.
- **Goals should be inputs, not outputs:** a friend kept easing off after hitting $10k, $20k and $30k months. Alex told him to make the activities the goal, so every day he either did the activities or didn't.
- **Rule of 100:** do 100 primary actions per day for 100 days and you'll usually get the first result. He says "count in hundreds."
  - Warm outreach: 100 friends a day.
  - Cold outreach: 100 people a day.
  - Content: 100 minutes a day.
  - Paid ads: $100 a day.
  - Do the 100 even if you didn't sell today, because it sets up tomorrow's sales.
- **Scientific method for portfolio goals (four boxes):**
  1. What problem are we solving? (Tie goals to a real constraint. "Redo the logo" must answer what constraint it relieves.)
  2. Hypothesis: "if X then Y," for example if we redesign the website, conversion rises.
  3. How we measure X and Y, for example a split test every Monday over the quarter, and whether conversion beats the current 10%.
  4. Did we do it, and did it work?
- If we did it and it didn't work, the constraint or hypothesis was wrong. If we didn't do it, fix why we aren't doing it.
- He likes solving problems by deciding they aren't problems.

## Staying rich: asymmetric bets, enterprise value, people, and reputation

- **Only get rich once:** from a mentor's Goldman Sachs mentor, "you should only have to get rich once." After a win, play with house money and never bet the empire. Alex's line: "fortunes are created through taking huge risk with small amounts of money, but fortunes are preserved by taking small risks with lots of money."
- Outsized returns come from betting against conventional wisdom. Given a 10% chance at a 100x payoff, take it every time. He cites Bezos: business has a long-tail return distribution unlike baseball's cap of four runs.
- **Quad marketing calendar:** market in four directions at all times: prospects into customers, customers into repeat customers, candidates into employees, and employees into engaged and developing staff.
  - Most founders only advertise to prospects. Then they have no one to deliver, and feel like a "genius with 1,000 hands."
- **Enterprise value (EV):** the same $2M/year profit is worth far more if the business runs without the owner.
  - Genius with 1,000 hands: $5M revenue and $2M income gives about $1M a year after tax, with no enterprise value.
  - Systematized: with the same revenue and profit but the genius removed and the people and processes doing the work, the asset might be worth $10M.
  - Earning $10M of net worth through income would take about $20M of income.
  - At acquisition.com the pattern is to take a $2-5M business, systematize it, and apply a multiple. His example is $10M of profit at 7x, taking someone from a $1-3M net worth to $70M.
  - Small AI-enabled teams can reach $1M/year in profit. To reach $10M, $20M, $50M, $100M and beyond, people are still required.
- **Reputation and brand:**
  - Life experiences are flowers in a bouquet. Associating similar strengths builds a coherent reputation, and one negative experience (his example: a drunk-driving accident) distorts how the rest is seen.
  - A brand is "an association between something you know and something you don't know." His example: the acquisition.com hat people ask to buy at airports. Luxury brands work the same way via celebrity association (Louis Vuitton with Kim Kardashian), and brands drop celebrities when negative associations form.
  - **Goodwill (customer surplus):** value received above what was paid. Alex says it compounds multiplicatively, and faster than revenue. He says you can build a $100M brand in 3 years, which can outpace building $100M in revenue.
  - Brand examples he cites: The Rock's Teremana ($6B), Kardashian's beauty line, Conor McGregor's Proper 12 ($1B+), George Clooney's tequila ($1B).
  - Early on, invest in goodwill and monetize later.
  - Buffett: any number multiplied by zero is zero. Never risk your reputation for short-term money. His example is that starting an OnlyFans would make money but would be a "broken flower."

## Compounding, patience, and defining enough

- **Compounding:** $100 at 10% becomes 110, 121, 133 and so on. Buffett has made more in the last decade than in the rest of his career.
  - Equity is a great compounding vehicle because you can reallocate capital inside it. For example, 10 locations growing 10% a month: infrastructure built over time allows faster pace later.
  - Panda Express: about 2,000 locations took 45 years, then it added 600 in a single year, more than any prior year.
- "Boring is what makes you rich." Going from $100M to $110M adds $10M, while going from 0 to $10M in year one is unlikely.
- Munger: the money is made in the waiting. The hard part of a plan is sticking with it, not deciding on it (for example, 3 gym days a week for a year).
- **Patience means figuring out what to do in the meantime.** In the marshmallow test, successful kids distracted themselves (moved away, sang, put the marshmallow in a pocket). Building skills gives you more ways to wait.
- **Define enough:** he bought a Bentley, then returned it at a loss six months later, realising he didn't care.
  - His rough monthly costs of a 1% lifestyle:

| Item | Monthly cost |
|---|---|
| Private flying | $50,000 |
| $500 dinners nightly | $15,000 |
| Lamborghini lease | $5,000 |
| Nutritionist, therapy, performance coach | about $10,000 |
| Housing | $50,000 |
| Total | about $150,000 |

  - That total is just under $2M/year after tax, or close to $4M pre-tax. He says you can live very comfortably on about $50,000 a month after tax.
- Put a number on food, travel, clothing, experiences and health to see how little you need to satisfy personal needs.
- Two levels of wealth: personal needs covered, and then unlimited, where money is used to play the game.
- Wealth is not about not working. It is about "getting addicted to freedom," which means having the option to work. Without a challenge you atrophy, like lifting weights well below your max.
- **Infinite games:** the goal is to stay married, stay in business and stay healthy at 50, not to win once. "You win as long as you never quit" (Churchill).
