## What branding is: a deliberate pairing of things through an outcome

- Alex rejects the popular definitions of brand ("gut feeling", "emotional shorthand", "set of expectations and memories") as vague. None of them tell you what behavior to change.
- He defines learning as "same condition, new behavior". A definition of brand is only useful if it changes what you do.
- His definition: "Branding is a deliberate pairing of things through an outcome."
- Coca-Cola example:
  - The outcome is the "yum" of drinking it and liking it.
  - The action is drinking.
  - The product is Coca-Cola.
  - The next time someone wants the yum, they reach for a Coke.
- Branding always happens. The goal is good branding, meaning a deliberate pairing of your business with good outcomes for your ideal customers.
- What you pair with determines two things:
  - Who pays attention to your business.
  - Whether they move toward the business (like the UFC example) or away from it (like the Dylan Mulvaney example).
- Advertising and branding are different. Advertising is letting people know about your stuff. Branding is the pairing that occurs as a result.

## Good vs. bad branding: judged by money from the ideal customer

- Alex uses the Bud Light x Dylan Mulvaney collaboration as an example of good advertising and bad branding. Lots of people learned about Bud Light, but many of its customers hated the pairing, fewer people bought, and the business took a loss.
- Bud Light then paired with Shane Gillis (a "man's man" comedian) and the UFC, and sales began to recover.
- A pairing is judged by whether it nets more money, so it is not a matter of opinion. He stresses this is specific to Budweiser's customer base. Gillis and the UFC are not "magic", and Mulvaney is not "unmagic" in general.
- Every pairing has positive and negative reactions because people differ. The test is the share of your ideal customer who likes it:
  - 75% like and 25% hate is better than 25% like and 75% hate, because the first makes more money.
- The same pairing could be good branding for a different product or audience.

## Why a strong brand makes money: the pairing-to-sales mechanism

- The earliest branding was livestock branding. A burned-in symbol changed behavior:
  - An unmarked cow is left alone.
  - A cow with a neighbor's brand you recognize is treated according to how you feel about the neighbor.
  - A branded cow whose brand you don't recognize is still treated differently from a wild one.
- Step-by-step process:
  1. Start with a brand that means nothing. People know it is a brand but not what it means yet.
  2. Pair it with people, experiences or things your ideal customer likes. Nike pairs with LeBron and Tiger, who are champions and "goats".
  3. The brand starts to mean what the customer likes.
  4. The customer wants to associate with that thing but can't buy it directly. They can buy "a tiny sliver of that association", such as a shirt with the logo.
  5. You get the money. As long as the pairing cost less than the sales it produces, you profit.
- Dolce & Gabbana example: they made a line for Kim Kardashian. A customer who wants to be associated with fame, beauty, wealth and luxury buys the brand to get that transfer.
- The point of building a brand is "to change customer behavior in your favor when they see it with a product."

## The payoffs: pricing power, better ad response, loyalty

- **Pricing power.** Alex quotes Buffett: "the single most important decision in evaluating a business is pricing power". If you have to hold "a prayer session" before raising the price by 10%, you have a terrible business.
  - A strong brand turns a commoditized $5 white T-shirt into a premium product.
  - His example was a 12x price increase without losing many sales.
- **Cheaper customers and better ads.** A generic white T-shirt might get a 0.5% click rate. The identical shirt with a Nike swoosh might get about six times as many people clicking and buying, at a higher price.
- **Loyalty.** Strong brands drive repeat purchases (his example is Apple buyers) and protect you from competitors stealing the customer later.
- **Summary of what you get:**
  - You can charge up to 10 times or more than competitors.
  - You get higher returns on advertising, so you scale faster.
  - Customers keep buying for life.
- Brands also outperform commodities in every industry and give a lasting competitive advantage that is "theirs to lose".
- He cites Buffett again: "It takes 20 years to build a reputation and 5 minutes to ruin it."

## How to start and grow a brand: the bouquet and the garden

- Brand elements (products, values, experiences, people) are like loose flowers on a table. They are not a brand until they are repeatedly paired into a bouquet. The brand is the association itself, and if you unravel it, the brand disappears.
- **Narrow vs. broad.** Talk only about tacos to be narrow. Expand to quesadillas and burritos, then food, alcohol and restaurants to go broader.
  - Niche down to go deep, branch out to reach more people.
  - Distant, random pairings hurt a brand because they are hard to associate. He uses a bike, a single flower, socks and a burger as the picture of an incoherent brand.
- Most brands happen by accident, based on whatever they appear next to. Good branding happens on purpose.
- Curate it like a garden: add the good and pull out the weeds. You have to do both.
- Start by deciding which values, people and experiences you want to connect to your product, and which you want to avoid, remove or ignore.
- Early on you only have a few flowers. Each additional pairing with something your customer likes strengthens it, such as going from one flower to a bouquet of red roses.

## Recovering from bad pairings, and the product's role

- One bad pairing, like a rotten flower or a DUI, changes how everyone sees the bouquet.
- You can't delete it. You recover by overwhelming it with far more of what the majority of your audience likes, until the bad pairing shrinks into irrelevance.
- Kanye is his example. After cancel-culture controversy he kept releasing things people love (a Super Bowl ad, shoes, an album), and people re-associated the good and still bought.
- Most pairings happen before purchase, but the customer has many more experiences with the product afterward, so the product does a lot of the branding.
  - A hole in a Nike shirt's armpit transfers backward: "Nike sucks", and then LeBron too.
  - If the product is "good enough", the brand can carry it the extra distance and shape perceived value.
- Alex's tip is that if you charge a premium price, make sure the product is dialed in so it reinforces the brand instead of conflicting with it.
- Buffett quote he agrees with: "Your premium brand had better be delivering something special." Alex adds that otherwise you get the first purchase but not the ones after it.

## Measuring a brand: influence, direction and reach, and the risk of new pairings

- Three metrics:
  1. **Influence:** how likely the brand is to change someone's behavior. Do they recognize it and do something?
  2. **Direction:** do they move toward the brand or away from it?
  3. **Reach:** how many people it changes behavior for.
- A weak, neutral brand has few people recognizing it and little reaction. A strong, positive brand has many people recognizing it and moving toward it.
- Strong brands aren't necessarily polarizing:
  - Trump is the polar example, with high influence and strong movement in both directions.
  - Taylor Swift, Mother Teresa and Apple are large, strong and mostly positive. Any brand with a large audience will have some haters.
  - Alex argues you can build a strong positive brand without seeking controversy.
- The concept works at small scale too. Parents have high influence and low reach.
- To test whether growth is working, look at whether more people take the action you ask for (click, download, attend, buy), and whether they do it in the direction you want.
- Every new pairing carries risk. A local band that goes big gambles on losing the old fans ("they sold out") for a larger audience.
  - The same applies to new content, genres and songs.
  - The bet is that more of your ideal audience will like the new thing than dislike it.
  - His advice: "don't let the five mean comments stop you from gaining the 500 new people who like the new thing."

## Alex's own brand as the worked example

- He built the brand around business value: making people money and growing their businesses. The content and books are for small business owners, who then associate growth and profit with him and consume more.
- People who dislike money or business content won't like his stuff, and he's fine with that.
- Word of mouth extends it. Fans tell others to check him out, and those people get the same positive outcome and make the same pairing.
- Results he attributes to the brand:
  - A 7.8 million person audience across platforms in about 40 months.
  - Over a million copies sold of his last two books.
  - Acquisition deals worth hundreds of millions of dollars for his holding company.
