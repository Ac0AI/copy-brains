## The four business shapes and why the "shape" matters

- Alex says there are "only four main types of businesses": e-commerce, service, education/consulting/info media, and software (SaaS).
- He says understanding his businesses' shapes helped him build a portfolio at acquisition.com that generates over $250 million a year at acquisition.
- Each shape has its own growth curve, pros and cons, and one "big hairy problem" to solve.
- His main point: most owners think something is wrong with their business when the issue is a feature of the model, not a bug. Examples he gives:
  - Supply chain and cash flow problems (e-commerce).
  - Hard-to-find talent (service).
  - Constant marketing and low retention (education).
  - Cash burn and revenue retention (software).
- He says to put "disproportionate effort" on the core problem of your shape. Competitors in your space often don't know this, and he calls it "the sauce."
- He has seen the same misdiagnosis at $30M, $50M and $100M businesses.
- If you haven't started a business, pick the shape that fits your personality.
  - Software suits someone detail-oriented, creative and willing to suffer for a long time.
  - E-commerce and education suit promotional people who dislike details.
  - Service suits people who love people, develop talent and build culture.
- He has owned all four and done well in each. He notes that the first business someone starts is almost never the last one they own.

## Valuing the hairy problem

- Ask how much enterprise value you unlock by solving the shape's core problem.
- Example: if a service business can get two people up to speed per month, that might add $10M or $50M of enterprise value. Spending $100,000 or $500,000 to solve a $50M problem is worth it.
- Once you see the value, put the right resources on it. Don't question the business.
- Solve it thoroughly: "not halfass, not duct tape."
- He says the market will pay "gobs of money" to whoever figures out their shape's core problem. "This is what we get paid for."

## E-commerce: fast growth, capital and supply constraints, brand as the moat

- **Shape:** growth is fast but hits breaking points that flatten it. The unlocks are usually a supply chain unlock or a distribution unlock.
  - Constraints: cash to buy inventory, ad performance, maxed distribution (e.g. 7-Eleven, then CVS), or a manufacturer that can't keep up.
  - Distribution can be physical retail, ads, affiliates or influencers ("synthetic distribution").
- **Alex's experience:** Prestige Labs (retail distribution plus an online store with ads) and his record-breaking book launch. For the launch he needed multiple 3PLs across states that could ship millions of books in 72 hours.
- **Pros:**
  - Fastest to grow revenue, because people spend more on tangible goods.
  - Less key-man risk from the founder, so it is very sellable.
- **Cons:**
  - Capital-intensive. Growth is dictated by free cash flow because profit has to buy the next round of inventory.
  - Example: 10 units, $10 profit each, and 300 units of demand means you can only buy 10 more next month.
  - Founders can reach hundreds of millions in revenue and live mostly on salary.
  - Heavy reliance on partners (raw materials, manufacturing, logistics, lenders). Build redundancy in each.
- **Big problems, in order:** cash flow forecasting (e.g. predicting sales over a 12-week lead time), distribution, supply chain, then brand. Logistics is "relatively well" solved.
- **Brand is the moat.** He calls pure media-arbitrage a "smash and grab" business (buy eyeballs at $10, make $11 gross profit). Anyone can copy a widget. Brand gives:
  - Higher click-through rates.
  - Higher repurchase rates.
  - Premium pricing.
  - Together that means lower CAC, higher LTV and higher gross profit.
- Decide deliberately from day one which affiliates, influencers and discounts fit the brand.
- **The "perfect" e-commerce company:**
  - Owns its manufacturing, with trade secrets or unique ingredient partners. At larger scale it builds decentralized manufacturing per market.
  - Has strong logistics.
  - Runs a lean performance marketing team alongside a brand-first team.
  - Spends about 70/30: 70% on top-of-funnel brand awareness and 30% on direct-to-purchase. Most starting companies spend about 100% on direct purchase.
  - Uses retail partnerships. Modern brands (e.g. Gymshark) start online and open flagship stores later. At saturation, stores cut shipping costs and raise order values, and CAC becomes rent divided by monthly sales.
  - Has "savage" email marketing, including flows, campaigns and attribution.
- The product has to be exceptional. CAC rises as you scale, so the compounding asset is word of mouth: repeat purchase and customers bringing customers.
- He says 60-70% of a new cohort buying again after the first purchase likely means a strong product.

## Service businesses: talent is the bottleneck

- **Credibility:** six brick-and-mortar gyms, 30-something gym turnarounds over almost two years, a gym consulting firm (B2C and B2B) that reached the mid-$30M range per year, and the acquisition.com advisory practice, which he describes as a nine-figure-plus business.
- Alex says 78% of US businesses are service businesses.
- **Shape:** slow and steady, near a straight line. The front end (attraction) can scale. Conversion and delivery involve people, and good people are harder to find than product.
- **Margins:** run higher gross margins than is typical. The padding absorbs volatility, e.g. a new salesperson converts worse for about 30 days, or a new account rep isn't as good as a 5-year veteran.
- **Hiring is the constraint, not sales.**
  - Done-for-you services usually have no trouble getting customers if they're any good.
  - Fitness coaching is the exception: it is hard to sell and retain because people don't like working out.
  - Talent is hard at every skill level, from maids to trades to white-collar roles with multi-year learning paths.
  - Founders are often the best technician, so every complex problem lands on them.
- **Pros:**
  - Least risky model. You can cut headcount and stay profitable if priced right.
  - High cash flow with little reinvestment (e.g. sell $100K of work at $50K cost, repeat monthly).
  - Sticky customers who don't want to learn the work.
  - The founder can live well along the way, unlike models where money comes mostly at exit.
- **Con:** slowest to scale of the four.
- **Big problem:** recruiting, onboarding and training must become a core competency.
  - A business is the delta between input value and output value. Take under-skilled people, add skills, and sell those skills at a premium.
  - "If you're in a service business, you're actually in a teaching business."
  - Don't expect to train someone in a weekend to do what took you 10 years. Training is front-loaded, but it makes people ramp faster, stay longer and support higher prices.
  - Reduce dependence on the founder's technical expertise by productizing the knowledge.
- **How to win:**
  - Around $1-3M, choose the real customer and avoid "any kind of person with a pulse."
  - Limit deliverables to one to three consistent ones so delivery can be systematized and productized instead of bespoke. That raises operational efficiency and gross margin.
  - Build training for each step, then onboarding, hiring and selection on top.
  - Replace yourself in operations.
  - Define clear career paths so talent stays. The acquisition.com advisory practice runs analyst, junior associate, senior associate and director tracks.
  - Aim for demand to be the constraint rather than supply, which is rare but doable.
- **Reinvest cash in:** talent quality (the "raw materials") and brand. Brand supports premium pricing and attracts premium talent.
- **North-star metric:** what you charge.
  - If you're good, you're supply-constrained, so you raise prices and gross margin. Reputation then brings more demand, and you go after better ICPs who write bigger checks.
- **Low-cost model:** viable only if decided on day one (offshore talent, heavy automation, cheapest as the USP). He prefers premium.
  - "I'd rather be making a million dollars per person" because it justifies the headcount headache.

## Education / consulting / info media: fast cash, no stickiness

- **Credibility:** he co-owns School, a software platform for info businesses with millions of data points. His 20 million users figure is quoted for School.
- **Shape:** almost a straight line up, then a hard stop.
  - Graduates leave, so you must refill constantly.
  - Taught students can become competitors.
  - Cheap, easy startup brings many competitors.
- **Value logic:** a skill that moves someone from $40K to $100K a year is worth a lot, and the skill duplicates at near-zero cost. He cites education as an industry of about $10 trillion globally.
- **Pros:** fast, easy and cheap to start; the fastest way out of poverty.
- **Cons:**
  - Hard to scale past $1-3M a year (rule of thumb, anecdotal). The cause is usually weak promotional skill.
  - Little retention, constant marketing.
  - Reputation risk if the founder is weak at ops, which is a different skill.
  - Many founders become "offer hoppers," launching a new thing each year.
- **Scaling delivery:** he says to stay one-to-many (which he recommends for many people) or to dilute access without diluting quality.
  - Milk analogy: 10 customers, one glass of milk. Giving each a pure shot beats giving each a glass of watered-down milk.
  - Training coaches in a weekend to deliver a high-priced skill means the product isn't that valuable or you're misrepresenting their ability.
  - The best in the world can give a tiny shot and still beat a mediocre person giving 100%.
  - Technology democratizes access and consolidates production.
- **Big problem:** create stickiness in a fundamentally unsticky model. Tactics:
  - **Consumable component.** Examples: ad creative for an ads course; a weekly list of trending 3D-printing items; community.
  - **Split pricing, "big head, long tail."** Charge a high price for the one-time education and a lower price for recurring elements.
  - **Don't hide the split in a payment plan.** Example: $25,000 education paid as $2,000 a month. After the skill is learned, a math course is worth "approximately zero," so the user feels they're paying $2,000 a month for community alone.
  - **Discount buying.** Group buying power gives members discounts on materials (e.g. paint brushes) that exceed the subscription price, which makes the ROI clear.
  - **Continued education.** News, new techniques and tools. His comparison: about $250,000 to become a doctor versus about $3,000 a year to stay one.
- **How to win:**
  - Price one-time and recurring elements correctly.
  - Add consumables to the recurring layer.
  - Use the cash engine from one-time purchases to fund acquisition.
  - Build brand, the biggest factor.
- **Brand logic:**
  - Harvard versus CCBC calculus, $200,000 versus $2,000.
  - Le Cordon Bleu graduates get hired more, so the school can charge more.
- **Overkill bias:** people want the best (e.g. Tony Hawk teaching an ollie). Real-world proof is the best branding strategy.
  - His content got more attention after his exit.
  - Warren Buffett's teachings would sell less without Berkshire Hathaway's results.
- **Winning path:** become one of the best at the thing first. It is slower, but it pays down the biggest risks. The competition won't do it, since "be patient and do the work" is the differentiator.

## Software and SaaS: slowest start, biggest upside, retention is everything

- **Shape:** the slowest start, then the steepest curve.
  - Engineering is expensive and has to be fronted for a year or two before income, which is why the venture industry exists.
  - Few businesses sit here, because barriers to entry are higher.
  - Once you have product-market fit, scale is the most of all four models. He cites Y Combinator: before PMF it's pushing a boulder uphill, after it you're chased by the boulder.
- **Vibe coding:** he has yet to see a truly vibe-coded business at $100M a year. He says it is getting better, but it still struggles with robustness and scale. He recounts a conversation with Replit's CEO, Amjad, who cited many $1-2M businesses built on vibe-coded software.
- **Pros:**
  - Infinite scale and very high gross margins.
  - Sticky revenue if done well, because it integrates into workflows.
  - Huge enterprise value. Valuation drivers are gross margin, growth rate and revenue retention.
  - Low risk at point of sale, since another owner gives customers the same experience.
- **Cons:**
  - Slow start, high burn, and founders often quit early.
  - Low willingness to pay, because there's no incremental cost. Enterprise software at a few hundred thousand a year is small versus enterprise spend. Consumer pricing like $9 a month is hard to earn.
  - Needs huge volume, or dependence on VCs and dilution (he mentions ending up owning 4%).
- **Information business versus software:** a software business looks identical to an information business until you have revenue retention.
- **Big problem:** survive the early years before PMF and make sure users keep using it.
  - Marketers struggle here, because you must make something people want and keep wanting.
  - He cites the Dropbox founder paying people from Craigslist to try the product: the first time, five of five users did not upload a file. Keep iterating user flows until they do, then fix the return loop.
  - He cites Facebook's head of growth: growth is users who activate and then never churn, so the base keeps stacking.
- **Retention benchmarks:**
  - Consumer: over 60% logo retention by year end, ideally over 100% revenue retention.
  - Revenue retention example: 10 customers at $10 a month is $100 a month. If a year later those same customers pay $100 or more, that's 100%+ revenue retention, achieved by building ways for them to spend more.
  - Enterprise is easier to retain because switching costs are higher.
- **How to win:**
  - **Product quality and customer feedback.** Be "absolutely militant." Don't give customers everything they ask for, because feature creep builds a "Frankenstein." Complexity is the "antithesis of elegance," so it's like "sanding off the edges."
  - **Friction removal.** Find an outcome everyone wants and remove all the friction between where someone is and that outcome. The iPhone is his example: Steve Jobs asked how to make a phone easy enough for a child to use.
  - **Viral loop.** Aim for each customer to bring 1.1 more. At low price points, paid ads alone get hard. Facebook is his example, and ChatGPT reached a million users in a week through word of mouth.
  - **Talent.** There are 10x and 100x engineers, and they cost 10x or 100x as much. Pay for quality. If you aren't technical, educate yourself or get a strong technical co-founder as a filter. Build with scalable code and good documentation from day one.
  - **Loops.** Build return, referral and upgrade loops into the experience.

## How acquisition.com maps to the shapes

- **Advisory practice:** pure white-collar service. Analysts, associates and directors learn through an apprenticeship model like accounting, consulting or legal. Many team members are Ivy League graduates or ex-entrepreneurs.
- **Books and media:** a blend of education and media, with a scalable deliverable. The book launch is also literal e-commerce.
- **ACQ AI:** software.
- **Apparel drop:** e-commerce, but it contributes minimal profit.
- **Combined launch:** the books and ACQ AI bundle did a significant amount of revenue in a weekend. He says "zero to 100 million in 60 hours" if you promote well, have a good offer and product, and have audience history. He doesn't cite exact revenue figures here.
- **Why he keeps the products strong:** "I don't care about the first sale, I want to care about the 10th sale." Each product has to be exceptional so the next launch's audience shows up.
- **Investment firm:** he calls it a "vehicle that contains businesses" rather than a business. Capital leverage lets few people make a few big decisions.
- **Portfolio examples:**
  - An insurance company (service-like, high headcount).
  - A teeth-whitening chain (brick-and-mortar service).
  - A B2B operational consulting business, a blend that skews toward service because of strong revenue retention.
- **Brand versus culture:** brand is how you shape customer behavior, and culture is the "internal brand" that does it for employees.
