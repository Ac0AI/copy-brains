## SPCL framework: four components of influence

- Alex defines influence as the "high likelihood of compliance with requests". Requests range from subscribing, to buying a book, to attending a webinar or workshop. Bigger asks need more influence.
- His framework is SPCL: Status, Power, Credibility, Likeness.
- Any one component gives some influence. Stacking all four gives the most, like "min-maxing" a stat in a role-playing game.
- Each component is a continuum, not a binary. A person with $10,000 has more status than one with $1, and a billionaire has far more.
- Influence is domain-specific. Alex says he would have low influence giving fashion tips, because he has no scarce resources, results or third-party proof there.
- He says the goal is to build influence, not to get as many views as possible.
- He credits 35,000 pieces of content this year and 32.7 million YouTube views in the month. He also cites over $100 million in sales in 72 hours from the $100 Million Money Models book launch.

## Status and power: definitions and examples

- **Status**: Alex defines it operationally as controlling reinforcers (the "good stuff" people want) in a given environment.
  - Example: a bartender in a busy bar controls a scarce resource, so he has status there. Outside the bar he has none.
  - Money alone gives status, even if inherited.
- **Power**: comes from "say-do correspondence".
  - You say something, the person does it, and a good thing happens. They are then more likely to comply with your next request.
  - Example: someone gives 10 crypto picks, you follow them, they all go up. That person has status and power, so more influence than a trust-fund kid who only has money.
- If forced to pick one component, Alex would pick power.
- One event can check several boxes. The book launch's $100M+ in sales gave him money (status) and third-party credibility. Telling people how to launch similar things, and having it work for them, builds power.
- Parents are his example of maxed-out influence. They control shelter, food and money (status). They have many reinforcement cycles from childhood directions (power). They look and think like you (likeness). Credibility is the one they often lack.

## Credibility and likeness

- **Credibility** is third-party validation you can observe.
  - For the book launch he paid Guinness judges to be on site, so records were verified by a corporation whose business rests on trust.
  - A $10 million building behind him in ads works because it is "hard to fake".
- **Likeness** is relatability. It covers shared values, a vibe or behaviors that match people who were positive in your life, or looking like you.
  - Example: his audience is about 89% male. Leila's, on nearly the same money and business topics, is about 54% female.
- His likeness advice is to just be you. He says "there's zero ROI" in acting differently from who you are, and that most people are "NPCs" playing archetypes.
- Lean into the quirks that make you unique, and be able to defend why you do what you do.

## Putting SPCL into every video

Alex builds each video so it demonstrates all four elements:

- **Status**: open with results you control. His intro cited 32.7 million views and the book launch's sales in 72 hours.
- **Power**: tell viewers what the video will teach. If they follow it and it works, they are more likely to comply with the next request. That covers watching the next video and any call to action, whether subscribe, like, share, a small purchase or a booked call.
- **Credibility**: use third-party proof, such as Guinness verifying the launch.
- **Likeness**: be himself. This is why he is moving toward live streams.

## Why live and long-form beat shorts

- Alex's stated media strategy is two words: "live interactive". He describes this as the Acquisition.com 3.0 / Mosey Media 3.0 direction. He says he "honestly hate[s]" scripted camera-staring videos but will do what's required.
- Applause ranking, as Mr. Beast described it to him at a UK vs US soccer game:
  - Traditional A-list celebrities got almost no applause.
  - Short-form creators got a bit more.
  - Long-form creators (podcasters, YouTubers) got a rowdy response.
  - Live streamers made the whole arena erupt.
- Shorts are a way to get people to risk time on a long. Alex still makes plenty of them.
- Exposure math: 2 hours of long-form equals about 480 shorts at 15 seconds each (4 per minute × 120 minutes). So long-form gives far more reinforcement cycles per prospect.
- He thinks the Trump–Rogan three-hour podcast and the Musk–Tucker Carlson interview nudged the election, because undecided people got hours with a candidate. He frames this as persuasion, not politics.
- His meta-theme: "the internet will always move towards truth". Raw, unscripted formats win over polished, curated ones, provided SPCL is still included.
- His formula is "do SPCL and do it as many times as I possibly can".

## Volume

- Rogan, PBD and Dave Ramsey each put out hours of content every day.
- Alex contrasts one post a day (365 a year), common for $1-2 million businesses, with his 35,000 pieces. He calls that about 100 times the volume, and says it brings about 100 times the prospects.
- He says the relationship is "far more linear than you would expect".
- Rule of thumb: about 1 in 10 shorts is a 2-3x outlier. The same pattern holds for longs.
- His approach is to put as much as possible through the input-output machine while checking the SPCL boxes.

## Interest media: make content for your avatar, not for views

- Alex says social media is now "interest media".
- Judging content by views is "dumb". A grandma slapping him would get views from people who like humor, but not from customers.
- If you are an educator, make content for your avatar: "the content is the targeting". The algorithm reads your topic, background and clothing, and shows it to people who watch similar valuable content.
- Example: a piano repair channel may get only 1,000 views per video. That can still be a room of 1,000 people who all want to fix pianos, which is a strong opportunity.
- Do not compare your views to Mr. Beast's. You are playing a different game.
- His preferred feedback is IRL responses. He counts texts from business owners he respects ("that was fire").
- When business owners fly to Vegas and he asks their favorite content, he says it is "dollars to donuts" the Cash Cows format. In it, a business owner presents their business and Alex shows how to improve it. He makes more of it even though the audience is smaller.

## Sizing your market and expectations (US data)

Alex cites Census Bureau data from memory and notes it may be imperfect:

- About 9% of people own a business, roughly 32-33 million business owners.
- About 95% of those make under $1 million in revenue. About 5% (roughly 1.5 million) make over $1 million.
- Roughly 1 in 250 businesses is over $10 million.
- Roughly 1 in 3,000 reaches $100 million a year, depending on the data source.
- Implication: 100,000 views on a video aimed at $1M+ owners is "crushing it". If 1.5 million views were all business owners, that would be "insane".
- He says to set expectations from the size of your actual market.
- He has looked at two businesses doing over $1 million a year with fewer than 5,000 followers. Small audiences can pay well if the content is directly valuable to them.
