## Give away what others charge for to generate leads

- Alex's favorite lead-generation strategy: "look at what other people are charging for", work out the hard cost for you to deliver it, then give it away free.
- Five steps:
  1. See what others charge for.
  2. Calculate your hard cost to deliver it.
  3. Give it away for free.
  4. Count the leads.
  5. Count how many convert.
- The aim is to give away something significantly better than what competitors give away, ideally something others charge for. He says to make it even better than theirs if you want to be a "Savage" about it.
- Example: for Black Friday he spent 200+ hours building a free scaling roadmap. It takes a company from $0 to $100 million in 14 hours of content, across 10 stages of growth and 8 functions: marketing, sales, customer success, product, IT, HR/recruiting and finance. Users fill in questions and get a personalized solution. It is better than what others charge for, and it keeps pulling businesses into his world.
- The free asset is built once and generates unlimited leads, in the spirit of "build once, sell a thousand times", like a book.
- Free stuff also moves people from needing more information to needing less before they buy, which is what branding does at scale.
- Claim: the Alex-affiliated portfolio (acquisition.com) companies get a new lead roughly every 6 seconds.

## Fewer, better marketing people, with the founder close to marketing

- Alex says every founder must know the core business, how to get customers and how to deliver. Most other things can be outsourced, but not getting customers.
- When he drifts away from marketing, the output of his skill gets diluted. He ends up with a department using their best skills, not his, and the business loses its biggest asset (him).
- Examples: Mr Beast is still involved in creating his videos. Steve Jobs reportedly approved every final Apple ad.
- His motto is "fewer better". Back the talent with money.
- Marketing resembles a developer role with a technical career ladder. Top talent produces outsized returns, like the Google Maps coder who rebuilt the product over a weekend.
- The gap between a zero and a ten marketer is far larger than in manual work. A lawn cutter might be 3x faster, but one skilled person with an iPhone can get a video with 100 million views.
- Marketing is usually understaffed (just him plus a couple of people) or overstaffed (he steps away and someone brings in many bodies).
- Gym Launch had 17 people in marketing. After he removed the leader and moved in, he cut the team from 17 to 5 and output tripled.
- Example math: three people at $75k creating 3x output.
- Pay for performance: keep the best marketers in the highest-leverage positions with incentivized performance, to access their discretionary effort.
- "The best advertisers don't need many people."

## Ask "what would it take to be number one?"

- He asks his team: "what else would have to be true for us to be number one in our market?"
- It skips incremental thinking (10% or 20% better). You assume the goal outcome and reverse-engineer it into the present, even if you are 1/100th the size of the leader.
- Process:
  1. Name the one or two big things that would have to happen.
  2. Ask what they would cost and whether you have the resources.
  3. Often you do. Then ask why you aren't putting every resource toward them now.
- His own chain of reasoning for becoming a top investor:
  - He can't speed up years of experience, so he needs the most deal flow.
  - Broker networks mean competing on shared deals, so he wanted proprietary inbound deal flow.
  - That requires a massive business brand, which requires better content than everyone else plus a business book.
  - So he put his resources into content and books, even though it meant nothing at the time.
- He credits Stephen Schwarzman (the book "What It Takes"): building a big business is about as hard as building a small one, so "you might as well go after something really big". A restaurant may cap at $1-2 million a year, while a top software company can reach billions.
- It's about resourcefulness, not resources.
- Selling in a vacuum means you don't compete on price or terms. There are two ways:
  - Sell where no one else sells (a pond with no other fishermen).
  - Sell something no one else sells (Elon Musk's approach).
- Inbound demand at scale puts prospects in a "work with you or not at all" position, not choosing between you and six others.

## Pre-production beats post-production, and minimize need-to-believes

- "An ounce of pre is worth a pound of post." Most people skip planning, hit record or post, then try to fix it in post, which has low leverage and high cost.
- A strong video or ad concept has more leverage than anything else. Even poorly executed, it can get an order of magnitude more views.
- For his School ads, he spends time on pre-production:
  - Reviewing past winners and hooks that keep working.
  - Preparing props and visual aids.
  - Keeping the recording short, which leaves little post-production.
- Post-production in education should only improve comprehension. Razzle-dazzle doesn't make a video more educational.
- What changed his mind: an Indian channel (he calls it "calculus Walla", about physics and calculus) with ~20 million subscribers. Its videos get 10-20 million views and it's just a person at a whiteboard.
- He now writes notes, orders points so nothing needs reordering, and prepares examples and clear language, which makes the team's work easier and faster.
- Need-to-believes (a term he first heard from Perry Belcher): minimize what a customer must believe in order to buy. Ideally zero or one.
  - Bad example, Prestige Labs: he had to convince gym owners that selling supplements is good, and that Prestige Labs was the right supplier. Two hurdles made it a hard sale. Fix: run a multi-layered campaign, first the big belief, later a warmer middle-of-funnel campaign.
  - Good examples:
    - Real estate investing: little to believe, since people already see it as an asset class.
    - Amazon: upload your product and you can make money, with no marketing, sales or support needed.
    - Uber: download an app, upload your license, have a car and a clean record, and believe your phone will ping.
- Going "cradle to grave" in one move sells to a smaller share of the market unless you make the sale a "6-inch putt" for everyone.

## Split test in order of leverage

- Testing is high leverage. A 10-30% lift in three or four funnel stages can double or triple results. Profit then rises disproportionately to revenue, because extra revenue doesn't carry the same fixed costs.
- His ranking of split tests:
  1. Offers.
  2. Packaging: headlines and subheadlines.
  3. Images tied to the headline and packaging.
  4. Third-party integrations.
- His biggest winners came from #1 and #4. #4 ranks fourth only because it is hard to do.
- Third-party integrations: swap the software you use (CRM, forms, scheduling and so on) and test it. Third-party tools rarely report their own conversion rate or UX data. Alex says swapping a tool caused a company "explosion" in sales and profit four times in his career, even with nearly the same look.
- It is messy to split customers across systems, but valuable.
- Winning tests are mostly permanent lifts: a one-time effort for ongoing gains.
- Thumbnails: he tests YouTube thumbnails because one can 3x views.

## Spend more by raising LTV

- Small businesses often say lack of customers or leads is their top problem. Alex says the real issue is usually that the business can't afford to acquire them, because it doesn't make enough per customer.
- When a company said "leads cost too much", he says that is rarely the issue. The issue is usually too little money per customer.
- Across companies he sees (he mentions wanting to buy a med spa chain with around $5 million EBITDA), customer acquisition cost is usually only about 1x-2x apart. The big differences are in LTV.
- The rich get richer through LTV. Example: Starbucks LTV is $14,000. Starbucks is likely inefficient at acquisition, but has 40-50 years of optimizing what brings customers back (products, seasons, flavors, options).
- Tip: Google "average cost per customer [industry]" to see if your CAC is in range. If it is, your problem is LTV.
- Small owners often undercut ("those guys overcharge"). Alex says the bigger company charges more because it makes more per customer.
- "The ability to spend more is LTV and LTV is the arms race of advertising."
- Advertising costs only rise over time (impressions, eyeballs), so LTV must rise to keep pace as you reach colder markets on pricier channels.
- Raising prices is among the strongest LTV levers, yet owners fear it. They chase cheaper leads, like addicts chasing the first high.
- The first campaign in a new market or with a new offer always performs best. Lead costs will get worse, so build the business to absorb it.

## Affiliate deals so good it hurts

- Go to someone who has already built the audience and ask to be promoted. Alex built affiliate businesses by recruiting many smaller businesses ("nodes of new revenue"), not super affiliates.
- Standard 20% referral deals fail because nobody wants to hand over a trusted relationship for 20% of a ticket. The deal must be "so good that it hurts" and large enough to change behavior.
- His method: peel off a piece of your product and give the affiliate 100% of that piece's revenue to sell.
- Massage example (a $100 massage, $30 hard cost):
  - Offer a partner business three massages to sell for at least $300 (the floor protects lead quality). The partner can charge $500 and keep it all.
  - Your cost: 3 x $30 = $90 for a pre-paid, qualified customer. There are no ads, landing pages or lead calls, only a calendar handoff.
  - The partner may use it as an upsell or bundle it into a $1,000 package.
  - If one in three converts to your $2,000 offer, cost to acquire a customer is 3 x $90 = $270 for $2,000.
  - Do this with 100 businesses.
- Discount codes: 10% off doesn't change behavior. Discounts need to be 50%+ to really move demand. He gives 100% of a peeled-off piece, not a cut of the main product.

## Use truth and damaging admissions in marketing

- "Nothing sells like the truth." Authentic advertising means saying what's true, including facts that seem like weaknesses.
- Small businesses can say so:
  - Limited capacity creates scarcity and implies personalization.
  - Many buyers prefer "Buy Local, Buy small business".
- Admit a flaw, then amplify the good. Formula: bad, bad, bad, "but" good. The "but" amplifies the good thing, and the good should be relevant to the admission.
- Examples:
  - Gym with bad parking: "we don't have the biggest parking lot" or "more equipment than the guys down the street", but members will have so much fun they won't care.
  - A gym owner who was not in great shape: "I'm not the most in-shape guy... even though I like donuts a lot still", but the gym is fun and delivers results.
- Telling a less exciting truth first makes your second claim more believable.
- If people will find out anyway, control the narrative. He compares it to Eminem in 8 Mile saying all the negatives first, so no one else can use them.
- "The hardest thing in all of marketing is getting someone to believe you." Trust building is one of the best ad investments, and truth is the "only long-term strategy".
