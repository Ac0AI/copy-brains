## Why Alex says every business should be emailing

- Alex says the average ROI for email is roughly 35 to 45x, depending on the source: about $45 back per $1 spent on software or writing time.
- He gives two reasons it pays so well.
  - The contact is already acquired, so email is just a reminder that you exist. The main costs are the software and whoever writes the emails.
  - One writer can reach 10 million people, which is a lot of leverage.
- He cites these usage figures:
  - 61% of people check email several times a day.
  - 20% check it several times an hour.
  - Usage is similar across age groups.
- Email has the same purpose as content: raise the odds that someone buys now or later.
  - Unlike a social post, where the algorithm decides who sees it, an email goes straight to your list.
  - Your past performance decides whether it lands in the inbox.
- Alex calls waiting so long to email "one of the biggest mistakes" of his career.
  - He did not email in most of his businesses. Gym Launch started emailing 2 to 3 years in.
  - He says that was "just saying no to money."
- Scale from his portfolio: Acquisition.com sent just under 10 million emails over 90 days. Across the portfolio, email generates tens of millions of dollars.
- The first email he sent to his own list used the subject "is this on." It opened with his quote: "It only takes 20 hours of focused effort to get proficient at any skill. The problem is most people waste years before they start the first hour."
  - His point is that you can start whenever you want. The day you start is the day you begin getting the return.

## Unsubscribes, list health and deliverability

- Alex does not treat unsubscribes as a negative.
  - Thousands of people unsubscribe from his Instagram and YouTube every day. He just gains more than he loses.
  - Many people who show up to workshops had unsubscribed. They know who he is, and the inbox they used may just not be the channel they want.
- Make unsubscribing easy.
  - Keeping people captive means they go dormant instead.
  - Mailing an uninterested segment lowers domain deliverability for the people who do want your emails.
  - He hates brands that make unsubscribing hard.
- The first send after a long gap brings a higher unsubscribe rate.
  - He calls this "shaking the tree," or pulling churn forward.
  - Recurring-revenue businesses see the same thing when they finally contact customers.
  - His highest churn was on his first email, just over 1%. Now it is about 0.42% per email, which is fine as long as the list grows faster than that.
- Fear of unsubscribes taken to its logical extreme means never emailing. He compares that to not posting content because people might unfollow.
- Deliverability:
  - For B2B, 16.99% of marketing emails never reach the inbox, let alone the primary tab, because of domain reputation.
  - There is no silver bullet. It comes from many small "golden BBs": consistently sending emails people open, click and read.
  - He says "don't try and figure out hacks." Align with what the email provider wants, which is the best inbox experience for its users.
  - A bad send can hurt more than silence. If he has no good email, he would rather not send one.
- Tactics for staying out of the promo tab:
  - Make the email look like a normal email: mostly plain text, a few sentences, no images.
  - Use one or at most two links.
  - Limit money language, especially in the text and title. He has seen more money language push emails into promo.
  - Get replies, because replies reinforce your reputation with the server.
  - Use Google-native links, such as YouTube, or Google Drive for a sales letter or VSL. They may deliver better, and "hokey" duct-taped assets sometimes convert better too.
  - If people do not open your emails, you end up in the promo tab, which is "kind of email death."

## Structure of a Mosley Money Minute email

Alex built his newsletter, the "Mosley Money Minute," around one promise: value in under 60 seconds that a business owner can use immediately. He says these are choices, not rules.

- **Subject line:** He keeps a consistent branded name, like a podcast episode title, instead of split-testing hard. He is willing to give up a little short-term open rate so the audience learns to always open it. He notes that proof-over-promise subjects are also a valid choice.
- **Opening reward:** He starts with a quote or tweet, something that can be absorbed in one glance. This rewards the open immediately.
  - He uses tweets that have already been shared the most.
- **Body:**
  - Roughly 200 words or less, about one minute.
  - One concept only. He keeps cutting until one clear idea is left.
  - The structure is a problem, a solution, then an easy tactic.
  - Example: strangers do not believe you because you are biased. The solution is overwhelming social proof. The tactic is to print every five-star review, frame the prints with dollar-store frames and cover the lobby from floor to ceiling.
  - Other emails run longer. Lila's emails go to the highest-revenue businesses and teach through story, and they perform well too.
- **CTA:** A sign-off, then a call to action matched to the reader. Scaling businesses go to the scaling workshops, and beginners go to School.
- **PS statement:** He includes one in every email because it is, after the headline, the most-read part. "Not having a PS statement is PS stupid."
  - He uses it for a related CTA or a meme, and says there is no science to it.
- **Consistent template:** He uses the same format each time, like McDonald's: "I want Big Mac, fries and Coke." Today's Big Mac differs from yesterday's, but it should be just as good.
- **Reward every step:** Behavior is driven by past rewards, not by the trigger before it.
  - Reward the open with the quote, the read with the tactic, and the click with something worth the click.
  - If you send a blog, put an interesting nugget in the email and link to the blog. This trains readers to click.
  - One terrible email among four good ones can make people disengage.

## Segmentation, cadence and send timing

- **Segmentation:**
  - HubSpot research found segmented lists had a 791% increase in ROI from email.
  - Alex says results depend less on email quality and more on who receives it. A beginner email sent to advanced readers does not apply to them, and the reverse is also true.
  - Acquisition.com uses qualifiers in the opt-in, such as whether they have a business and their revenue. It then sends to segments like 0 to 100K, 500K+ or 5M+.
  - Be honest in the opt-in. Claiming a higher bracket just gets you content that does not apply.
- **Cadence:**
  - The best email marketers he asked recommended about 3 emails a week. Most found that more email converts more.
  - He does not treat that as a rule. His own approach is a quality threshold: if he has two good emails, two go out. If he has none, none go out.
  - If you have not started, begin with once a month.
  - To break his own barrier, he committed to twice a month and wrote 24 emails in about half a day. After that, everything extra is bonus.
  - He now sends significantly more than that. The newsletter goes out twice a week.
  - He avoids separate "promotional" emails. Every email delivers value, with a link available for those who want the next step, like a link in a bio. That way he does not have to balance a give-to-ask ratio.
- **Days:**
  - B2C: Mondays and Tuesdays, with an average open-rate lift of about 18%.
  - B2B: Wednesday is the bigger lift day, comparable to the B2C lift.
  - He got this from Neil Patel and says he may be adding a narrative to explain it.
- **Time of day:** Two sweet spots, about 10 to noon and 1 to 3. First thing in the morning is competitive because people are clearing real business email.
  - This matters mainly for local businesses. Across time zones, he would not lose sleep over it.

## Other tactical email settings

- **Mobile design:** About 58 to 60% of global internet traffic is mobile. Check emails on both desktop and mobile.
  - Broken layouts, especially with images, look unprofessional and hurt clickthrough. This is especially common in e-commerce.
- **Preview text:**
  - He learned this recently.
  - Without it, the first 150 characters of the email show, which may just be "hey John."
  - Reported data shows a 24% increase in opens, meaning about one in four people check the preview. He thinks the real number may be higher.
  - It takes 10 seconds. Pull your most valuable or curiosity-inducing nugget forward.
- **CTAs:**
  - Give a reason to act now and say exactly what they get.
  - One or two CTAs is fine if there is a clear reason to click one versus the other. Use one per audience or objective. Five gets aggressive.
  - A continued-interest CTA plus a buy CTA is an acceptable pair.
  - Make CTAs contextual to the content, not copy-pasted. He criticizes YouTubers who splice in the same mini-ad. The CTA is the short-term conversion point, so spending 30 extra minutes on it is worth it.
  - Example: this video's CTA was about email. A beginner topic points to School. Enterprise value points to scaling workshops or the portfolio.
- **A/B test subject lines:** Test on a small percentage, then send the winner to the rest. He compares it to how YouTube tests thumbnails and titles.
- **Metrics:**
  - Many email marketers now track click-through rate (CTR) over open rate, partly because Apple's privacy changes made opens hard to track.
  - Alex's last 3 months: average open rate 35.7%, which he is unsure is accurate, and CTR 8.5%.
  - CTR can be read two ways.
    - Percent of everyone sent to.
    - Percent of openers who clicked. For example, 100,000 sent, 30,000 opens and 10,000 clicks is a 33% click rate among openers but 10% of the list.
  - His CTR of 8.5% means 8,500 clicks per 100,000 sent.
  - He optimizes CTR over time, not per email. Some emails need less of a click ask, so he "deposits before withdrawing."

## The opt-in email: sell them on staying subscribed

- Alex peels off valuable book chapters that do not fit the book's theme and finishes them to book quality. He gives these to people who opt in, with other chapters saved as launch bonuses.
  - He wants platform-specific bonuses that give people a reason to be subscribed.
- The welcome email after opt-in does four things:
  - Builds anticipation. A big announcement is coming, and subscribers hear first.
  - Delivers on the promise, which reinforces the opt-in.
  - Asks for a reply, "reply yes," before sending the chapter. Replies help the sender's reputation and keep the email out of the promo tab.
  - Re-sells the reader on regular communication.
- He puts the "by the way, here are the other things you get" part near the end, after the immediate promised reward. The reader has huge motivation in that moment and the goal is to capitalize on it.
- The pitch for the secret chapter lists specifics, such as "how to make your advertising 9.5 times as profitable," a list of 5 ways to add hoops that increase lead quality, and 4 ways to display discounts. The chapter is positioned as one he starts almost every new market with.
- He names two failure modes.
  - A good lead magnet with no selling on staying subscribed, so people take it and leave.
  - A lead magnet that makes the opt-in work feel not worth it. That second one "keeps me up at night," because he wants every interaction to return more value than the time spent.
- If someone opts in only for the lead magnet, they do not want regular communication. Sell the ongoing relationship.

## Profit and conversion tactics from the sample emails

- **Annual renewal fee** (for membership or recurring businesses):
  - A one-time fee charged after the customer has stayed a year, usually the price of 1 to 3 months.
  - It adds 8 to 24% to revenue at no added cost. It does not hurt front-end sales because it happens a year later and is not part of the advertised monthly price.
  - Someone who stays a year is unlikely to leave over it.
  - Example: a $39/month plan with a $99 renewal fee raises annual revenue per customer from $468 to $567, a 21% increase. That makes the effective monthly rate about $47. If $47 were advertised instead, he would likely convert significantly fewer customers.
  - On a 20% margin business, adding 20% revenue with no added cost doubles the profit.
- **Speed to contact:**
  - Call leads within 60 seconds of opt-in.
  - Alex cites a Harvard Business Review study showing an average increase in sales of 391%, or roughly four times.
  - He says that if nothing else on your docket could quadruple sales, this should be the first priority. You can afford the sales staff with the lift it creates.
  - He says he almost started funding the buying of businesses just to implement this tactic.
- **Email follow-up on School:** Before, they did none because of technical issues with the platform. After starting, conversion lifted 40%, which he calls free.
- **Testimonials into ads:**
  - Turning testimonials into ads is profitable but hard, because most testimonials are weak.
  - He reviewed 2,500 Gym Launch testimonials. The top four all shared a pain-based hook.
    - "We were two months away from shutting our doors."
    - "We were barely making enough to survive."
    - "I paid payroll and rent and finished the month with $0 of profit."
    - "I pay all my expenses every month, work all day and end up with $0 of profit."
  - Two of the top four said exactly "zero dollars of profit."
  - Tactic: do not ask how life was before. That produces a long story with no clear hook. Ask for their worst moment.
  - Moments make hooks, and details make them powerful. "Barely making enough to survive" beats "stuff was hard."
- **Social proof:** The email's premise is that businesses already have five-star reviews they are not displaying. Brick-and-mortar shops should print and frame them. Online businesses should use the online version.
- Alex also says that tweets that perform well also tend to perform well as emails, and that people screenshotting the email to their stories brings in new subscribers.
