## Focus means saying no, from the top down

- Alex defines focus as "the quality and quantity of things that we say no to." The most focused person does one thing.
- Focus applies from the top of the company down. If a company has 5 yearly objectives, he says he hasn't done his job, because one of them is the most important. If he can't name it, the team can't judge priorities without his context.
- He asks: "What one thing, if it happened in 12 months, would make the rest of your goals either accomplished or irrelevant?" Four years ago his one thing was building a big business brand. That would give him proprietary deal flow, so he wouldn't need to be the best investment picker or negotiator.
- Strategy is "limited resources against unlimited opportunities." Choosing where to put those resources is the strategy.
- He tells team members that if they did only the one thing that makes their job most valuable, they should drop everything else. They report back that it is much easier. He sees a boss's job at every level as pruning what is on each report's plate.

## Do things in sequence, not concurrently

- At a day with executives from his largest portfolio company (a software business that grew from $2M to roughly $110M this year), the team arrived with 5 objectives of 5 sub-tasks each, 25 things in all.
- Alex wrote 1, 2, 3 on the whiteboard and said "we don't do number 2 until number 1 is done." Doing #1 multiplies the value of #2.
- He says task switching makes people about 75% less effective. All resources go to one problem, and the other fires then create urgency to solve the first one. He argues you get 5 things done faster in order than all at once.
- The mistake he made at Gym Launch (the company he sold): when it was crushing, he started a second company instead of doubling down. Revenue rose about 30% that year when it could probably have doubled.
- He felt the same urge at acquisition.com and refused to repeat it.
- At the portfolio level he pruned from 24 companies to 10 in 12 months, at a loss on some. He did 1 deal this year versus 12 the year before. The portfolio still outperformed the previous year by focusing on the best performers. He notes he needed the 12 deals first to learn.

## Farm system for deal flow

- His top-performing portfolio companies (#1, #2 and #4) were ones he had met and helped before doing a deal. He decided to recreate that on purpose.
- He ran about 14 small workshops this year (roughly one a month) at the acquisition.com headquarters, limited by office size. He invites entrepreneurs and his team meets them.
- Many aren't ready for the portfolio yet. If the team helps one go from about $5M to $20M a year, he hopes that when they want a growth partner or exit, they call first.
- It's an incubator weekend, not a full incubator, because he doesn't want to take his eye off the ball.

## Talent arbitrage and recruiting

- Steve Jobs' taxi driver analogy: the best taxi driver is maybe 2-3x faster than the worst, but in knowledge businesses the best can return 100x more than someone merely good.
- Alex says it's worth paying someone millions a year if they make you tens or hundreds of millions. Example: a good engineer at $250K versus an amazing one at $1M with 100x return is still the better deal.
- A mentor told him "the best talent is always in the future." Keep raising the standard, because people outgrow their level of competency (a season, not a fault).
- He sees himself as lead recruiter. The best people only want to talk to the CEO, already have careers and headhunters, so recruiting is more you selling them. Think "I need John, who works somewhere else," not "I need to fill a role." It can take months. He cites Sheryl Sandberg's weekly dinners with Zuckerberg for a year.
- On risk: a 1-in-5 shot at a 100x payout is worth playing every time even though you lose 4 of 5. Entrepreneurs are paid for taking risk that others won't.
- For his media team he gave large raises and let go of people who weren't up to standard or invested in business. He frames it as kindness: release them the day you know it won't work, because they'll do better elsewhere.

## Branding: delayed payoff and the 70/30 ratio

- Ben Francis (Gymshark CEO) told him a story: New Balance's new CMO flipped ad budget from 70% direct response (discounts, buy-now) and 30% brand (influencers, sponsorships, "cool factor") to 70% brand and 30% direct response. They lost money for 18 months, then in month 19 sales shot up.
- Lessons Alex took:
  - Branding pays at a delay. "Dig the well before you're thirsty." He's living on work from 2 years ago.
  - Founder-led companies invest more in brand than quarterly-measured corporate managers. Shifting to direct response pulls forward revenue, like picking fruit early, then there is nothing left because no trees were planted.
  - The ratio matches media: roughly 3.5 good experiences per ad before people stop watching. He expects his own to be closer to 25/75 ask versus give.
- His plan: go above 75% give, closer to 95%. He can't sign athletes like Gymshark, so he does big aspirational things with portfolio companies and leads with free value: content and books. The books are free unless you want a physical copy, and calls to action shift toward the book. He puts out 65 pieces of content a day.
- He ties it together: 10x the brand is the one focus, which needs the absolute best people, backed by his money and time.

## Money rules and investing in skills

- Rule 1: spend less than you make. Rule 2: invest what's left to make more money. Starting out, make money while awake (active income), not asleep. Rich people choose passive only because they've maxed active.
- Active investments multiply time: a roughly $1,000 snowblower can take someone from 2-3 driveways a day to about 20, a 7x earning boost. No $1,000 stock does that.
- He viewed expenses in hours of work. At $6.75/hour at Smoothie King, a $12 lunch was about 2 hours. A $30 shirt wasn't worth a shift.
- He checked his bank account daily for years to keep a "pulse of the money."
- His line: "Don't invest in the S&P 500, invest in the S&Me 500." $1,000 in stocks at 20-30% gains makes $1,300. $1,000 on a skill that doubles earning capacity could mean $30,000 saved versus $1,300.
- Best-return example: he couldn't afford Facebook ad agencies. One asked $5,000 a month. He negotiated $750/hour for 8 one-hour sessions (about $6,000) on terms that his hands were on the keyboard and the teacher explained each decision. He recorded and rewatched sessions and took notes, and by session 6-8 didn't need him. He says that $6,000 made him millions.
- Skills are "the ultimate hedge against inflation" and currency changes. He spent $350,000 for one hour with a $7B-net-worth person, whose advice was "protect your reputation with your life."
- Get rich quick, "you get poor even faster": without the skill to wield money you can't keep or multiply it.

## Learning skills, getting started and gaining reputation

- Start with a service business since you can use time instead of money: "first rule of entrepreneurship is you use what you have." It took him 9 businesses before Gym Launch, learning from each.
- Skill barter: he had sales skill. At networking events he offered business owners a free sales-process rework, treated like a $10,000 consulting gig (research, talking to the team, rescripting, training). They'd say "what can I do for you?" and he'd ask for their PPC (or similar) know-how. They gave less than he gave, but more than he had. Some won't reciprocate, so do it enough times: "volatility only appears volatile with low volume."
- Before opening his first gym he saved $50,000 at 23, moved to California, and joined a gym owners' mastermind without owning a gym, to learn from others' mistakes (locations, square footage, equipment, buy vs lease). "If I do what the best people did, I will get what the best people got."
- Two questions he asks himself 10-20 times a day: "Does this action, person, or decision increase or decrease the likelihood I achieve my goals?" and "What would it take?" The latter assumes success and reveals the price.
- Do more than the best: if they spend $100/day on ads, spend $500; if they make 1 piece of content, make 10.
- Speed example: taking over a media division, he cut a weekly cadence to same-day turnarounds and did 3 months of work in 3 days. He says it is elimination of waste, not activity speed.
- He treats traits like charisma or confidence as bundles of concrete behaviors. Confidence is just repetitions, which give you a statistical prediction. Operationalize vague words into actions.
- On quitting his job: he imagined the worst case in detail (business school, couch-surfing, or getting a comparable job) and reasoned it'd be harder later with kids and a wife.
- Reputation is a weighing machine long-term, as in Buffett's quote on markets. Decide what you want people to say about you, then do those behaviors. Changing perception takes sustained exposure, so reputation lags like branding.

## Promotion first, then product

- To earn more, learn to advertise. If no one knows you exist, no one can buy. He defines advertising as letting people know about your stuff: 1-on-1 outreach (DM, email, calls, knocking on doors), content, paid ads, and affiliates with the audience you want.
- Early on, advertise to get people in even if the product isn't great, then keep improving until people stick or come back (membership retention, repeat purchase).
- Once retention works, go back and double down hard on advertising.
- Great marketing can reach a few million to $10M a year, but you burn through a market. Negative word of mouth is stronger and more viral than positive, so a weak product silently suppresses sales and raises acquisition costs. Long term, the product is what lets you scale big.

## Scaling roadmap

- He released a free scaling course and assessment (acquisition.com/training) that maps functions (sales, marketing, customer service, product, IT, HR, recruiting, finance) from zero to about 500 employees.
- Stages are defined by headcount rather than revenue because organizational structure tracks headcount more closely (a software firm can do $100M with 20 people).
- It lays out what must happen to reach each next level and gives a personalized plan. He vetted it with 3 other billionaires.
