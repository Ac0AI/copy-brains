## Start with free or low prices, then raise 20% per batch

- Alex's rule: start low or free, because "it's very difficult to come down." Early on, the goal is to get flow through the system.
- His examples:
  - **Online personal training (13+ years ago):** clients donated $500 or $1,000 to a charity of their choice in exchange for 12 weeks of training. He got his first ~13 customers through posts and DMs. Afterward he asked if they would pay him directly, and many did and referred others.
  - **Gym Launch turnarounds:** for 2+ years he flew to gyms and set up pricing and marketing for free. He paid for flights, hotel, food and the ad spend, and only made money if the gym made sales. That tested the system in 30+ markets and led to licensing it to 5,000+ locations. He sold the company to APG for $46 million.
  - **Software company (Allen):** he began with free customers to get case studies and testimonials. Alex says this applies to big owners launching a new product or division, not only beginners.
- Four returns from free customers:
  1. They convert to paying after the period if you do a good job.
  2. They refer others.
  3. They give testimonials and reviews.
  4. They give feedback. Ask "tell me how I can make this better."
- Make the testimonial and referral contingent on doing a good job.
- The real test is whether they stay and pay at full price afterward.
- Give a reason for the discount, such as being new at this or trying something new, so you get grace.
- Tactic: after the first batch of 10-20 case studies, raise the price 20% for every 5 customers. For example, $1,000, then $1,200, then $1,500. Keep going until conversion rate times price falls. That is the sweet spot.
- His saying: "create flow, monetize flow, then add friction." Run water through the machine to see where it breaks, "rather than just trying to get rich on Excel."

## More, better, new: the scaling order

- **More comes first.** A content mentor showed Alex he was posting once a week while the mentor posted 3x-10x more on each platform. The advice was just "do more."
  - A billionaire friend posted once a day. Alex's team was posting 50+ times a day across about 5 million subscribers.
  - A portfolio CEO made about 15 new ads a month. Alex made 35 a week when going from that size to 5x.
  - Alex's saying: "volume negates luck." People ahead of you are often doing 100x the volume, not 2x.
- **Competition and saturation.** A $2M/yr chiropractor ad agency spending $40K a month thought it had saturated Facebook. Alex pointed out that all chiropractor agencies together probably spend about $4M a month. That spend is the opportunity.
  - Paid ads are "an auction for attention." If your LTV is higher, you can outbid everyone and still profit.
- **Switch to better when the return on effort of better exceeds one more unit of more.** Going from 1 to 2 salespeople is a big jump. Going from 10 to 11 is only +10%, so a 20-25% throughput gain from tweaking scripts, headline tests or CTAs wins.
  - After better, go back to a 10x more.
- **Find the constraint.** Ask "What would stop me from 10x-ing the business right now?" If nothing, do it. If something would break, fix that first, then scale. Businesses grow until their constraint.
- **New comes last.** The cost of change is certain and the return is not. Entrepreneurs want to spend 80% of their time on new, but should spend about 90% on more and better of what works.
  - Do new things only when nothing works, or when you have hit a real ceiling. For example, a local gym spending about $10K a month that has saturated its radius should move to YouTube, Google search, display, direct mail or radio.
- **Give a new channel time.** Payback can take 3, 6 or 12 months. Don't judge on one month of spend. Track progress along the path: clicks, then opt-ins, then the right people, then calls or events, then sales.
- A second channel doubles lead flow and also makes the business more stable and more valuable.

## Optimize ads front to back: hooks, headlines and call outs

- Alex cites David Ogilvy: after the headline, you've spent 80 cents of your ad dollar. In 13 years, the importance of the first 5 seconds, the headline and the packaging has only increased.
- Evidence:
  - Thumbnail changes alone can raise average view duration by 2-3 minutes.
  - Dean Graziosi spent $250K on a Larry King infomercial with no results. Larry King was flown back to re-record the first 30 seconds of the hour-long interview. The re-run did $100M in sales.
  - A huge creator stopped a copycat video 5 seconds in because the thumbnail and first 5 seconds weren't confirmed. The copy had 30M views against the original's 10M.
  - At an 8-figure portfolio company, changing only the landing page headline lifted booking rate 62%. The opt-in rate stayed the same.
- Effort split: if he has an hour, he spends about 55 minutes on the first 5 seconds. Per session he writes about 50 hooks, 3-5 "meat" sections and 1-3 CTAs.
- Why the front: a back-end fix like show rate going from 50% to 75% is a 50% jump. Hook work can take CTR from 1% to 4%, a 4x.
- Writing rules:
  - Assume the viewer doesn't know you, is in a rush, and has a third-grade education. "Clear beats clever, deletion beats explanation."
  - Shorten it, then run it through a third-grade reading-level calculator and keep editing. This doesn't attract dumber people. It makes the copy accessible to everyone. 30% of people can barely read, and some of them are rich.
- **Call outs.** A call out is whatever gets your audience's attention. Specific ones ("moms in Nevada," "homeowners in Clark County") make the reader say "this is for me." Very specific call outs often bring in more qualified leads than broad ones.
  - Find call outs by looking at competitors' long-running ads, which signal what works. Write down the words your customers use. Start with a problem or a question they'll say yes to.

## Alex's weekly ad creation process and the kaleidoscope method

- **Collect data constantly.** He keeps no premium (ad-free) accounts so he sees and hears ads. He screenshots or records interesting ones into one swipe folder. If you are your avatar, "the ads count double."
- **Record the same day.** He does one session a week, first thing in the morning. He "crams for the test" by reviewing the swipe file and his best ads from the last 1-3 years, watching the first 10-30 seconds of each.
- **80/20 hooks.** Of 50 hooks, 40 are proven and recycled and 10 are new experiments. Most people flip this. The experiments become the next control if the old hooks fade.
- Meat angles: education, a belief to break, lists, steps, stories, or proof. Then a quick CTA.
- Ad results follow a power law, where the top 5% makes 95% of the income.
- **Kaleidoscope process.** When an ad wins, squeeze it. His example: a Gym Launch ad where customer Kale stood in his full gym and said he had gone from a full-time job and a 2-month-old to a gym with a $250,000 offer. Ways to remix it:
  1. Change the background or backdrop color.
  2. Add props or visuals.
  3. Re-enact it with the same script.
  4. Reorder the sections.
  5. Add visual filters (black and white, sepia, contrast).
  6. Add VFX.
  7. Change fonts and captions.
  8. Change the pacing or speed (1.2x or 1.5x).
  9. Add music.
  10. Keep the winning hook and re-record a different back half.
- His takeaway: "squeeze the living hell out of the winners."

## LTV to CAC and back-to-front business optimization

- LTV:CAC is the one metric Alex would pick if he could know only one about a business.
- Definitions:
  - LTV is lifetime **gross profit**, not revenue. He uses LTGP.
  - CAC is the all-in cost to acquire a customer: sales, marketing, software, everything.
- Benchmarks are irrelevant. Everything comes back to gross profit.
  - Example: a bow with $500 gross profit, a $20 lead and 1 in 5 leads buying gives a $100 CAC and 5:1. At a $10 lead it would be 10:1.
  - Meal example: $10 meals that cost $9 to deliver leave $1 gross profit. Five weeks of 10 meals is $50 LTGP, so CAC must be about $15 or less.
- Rules of thumb:
  - Aim for at least 3:1 LTV:CAC.
  - Spend at most a third of LTV to acquire a customer.
  - Alex wants to recover CAC in the first 30 days. Sometimes that takes a one-time upfront charge.
- Estimating LTGP: take last year's customers and total gross profit, then divide gross profit by customer count. This underestimates recurring businesses, and he prefers that to overestimating.
- Starbucks makes about $14,000 in LTV per customer. Even a $500 CAC would be 28:1.
- His biggest wins came from ratios of 30:1 up to 100:1 or 200:1. Those windows are short, so dump money into the machine. Wealth is made in short bursts and sustained by 5:1 to 10:1 periods.
  - Ratios fall as you scale. For example, a 100:1 at $1,000 a day may become 5:1 at $100,000 a day. A big starting ratio shows how much room there is.
  - He cares about absolute return, not relative return.
- Back to front: advertise front to back, but optimize the business back to front. LTV sets what you can spend on the front. Either lower CAC or raise LTV. Doing both creates "huge arbitrage."
- Alex estimates it would cost about $50M to become an A-list celebrity, based on a no-name actor in a big Marvel hit. His point is that a $1B-gross-profit sale could fund reaching that many people.

## The core four, the 4-hour rule and the scaling ladder

- There are only four ways to let people know about your stuff:
  1. Warm outreach (one-to-one, people who know you).
  2. Cold outreach (one-to-one, strangers).
  3. Content (one-to-many, people who know you).
  4. Paid ads (one-to-many, strangers).
- "If you're not spending your day doing one of those core four things, you are not advertising."
- At Gym Con, Alex asked owners doing about $500K-$3M a year who spends the first 4 hours of the day promoting. Nobody raised a hand. "This is why you're small."
- If you're under about $1M-$3M a year, spend the first 4 hours promoting, then run the business. Most businesses would make more money with more leads.
- "Nail it, then scale it": get flow in, fix the back end, then spend more.
- Scaling ladder:
  - $0 to six figures: one product, one avatar, one channel.
  - Six to seven figures: the same, but consistently.
  - Seven to eight figures: one avatar, one channel, two products (an upsell).
  - Multiple eight figures: two channels, one avatar, two products.
  - AG1 is an exception: one product, one avatar, many channels.
- **Awareness and runway.** Alex uses Eugene Schwartz's five levels: unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware, most aware.
  - Beginners profit from most-aware audiences (their list and customers). If ads stall at a ceiling ($2K, $10K, $100K a day), the ads usually aren't good enough to reach the next awareness level.
  - His ~$100M/yr portfolio company spends millions a month and targets the most unaware, taking them through all the stages in one ad.
  - "The size of the plane is directly correlated with the length of the runway." More expensive and colder means more selling. Scaling trouble often comes from a mismatch, such as a warm message to a cold audience.
  - Cold audiences are like watermelon seeds: fewer buyers per thousand eyeballs. LTV:CAC may fall from 20:1 to 5:1 to 3:1, but at 100x the spend the absolute return is higher.

## Proof, truth and "say what only you can say"

- **State the facts, tell the truth.** The top creator told Alex that his titles are clickbait but true, so the truth backs them up.
- Most businesses don't track results. "If you don't track, you don't care." Measurement itself improves outcomes.
- Gym Launch stats: the average gym after 12 months makes $100K+ in added profit. The bottom 20% averages $40,000 in additional revenue, roughly break-even. The top 20% is at $500K+.
- School.com: 30.44% of people who finish their first month make their first dollar online. After a new rollout, ~35% more people are succeeding, which he puts at a little over 40%.
- Steps: collect data at day 1, 30, 60 and 90 and at program end. Improve the product until the data is compelling. Then present four variables: the percentage of people, who achieve X outcome, in Y time or attempts, under Z conditions. Fewer conditions is better, even at a lower result.
- **Say what only you can say, show what only you can show.** Do epic stuff, then talk about it. This is "how I" instead of "how to."
  - Value per second matters more than seconds of value. Curated summaries work. He suggests selling to rich people the "same result in half the time for twice the price."
- Demonstrate rather than tell:
  - Agency: play a one-minute live lead call and ask "could you take 50 of these a month?"
  - Software: run a live demo on the prospect's own video.
  - Door-to-door sale: a salesman scrubbed gum out of Alex's gym turf and sold him $100 of cleaning product in about 13 minutes.
- Prospects discount testimonials by how likely the result is for them, so showing raises that likelihood.
- His portfolio case studies (adding 40 salespeople, cutting refunds by two-thirds) come from doing things, then reporting them.

## Narrow the avatar, expand deliberately, and provide value

- **Better beats bigger.** A coach-of-coaches making $2M a year asked about going broader. Alex told him to get better, since the best takes the market.
  - Gym Launch served only micro gyms doing weight-loss before-and-afters. It excluded personal trainers, big-box, yoga, spin and Pilates for the first 4-5 years. It still makes $30M+ a year.
- Five ways to expand: up market (bigger, fewer customers), down market (more volume, lower price), adjacent (for example chiropractors or medspas), narrower, or broader. Most businesses need to narrow first.
- Vista Private Equity's lesson: study the customers in the data room, find the top 20% generating 80% of revenue, stop the rest, and expand that 20%. That can give the same size business with 5x the revenue.
- To find the top 20%, compare their psychographics, geographics, demographics and actions. Narrow to 2-3 variables, put those in marketing, and have sales sell only to them.
- Examples: a generalist agency found IT firms were 8% of customers but the most profitable. A second agency found divorce attorneys. Alex says a single avatar can support $100M-$200M a year.
- More than half of the portfolio companies they bought were made narrower. "Better creates bigger. Bigger creates bloat." "Growth is not a goal. Growth is an outcome of inputs."
- "You get far more leverage by doing the same thing a hundred times than doing a hundred things one time."
- **Value equation:** dream outcome, perceived likelihood of achievement, time delay (speed), and effort and sacrifice (ease). The ideal is something people want, with no risk, fast and easy. Find problems from content comments (the "endless content wheel") and from friction points in your own implementation.
- **Give away the secrets, sell the implementation.** Make free stuff better than competitors' paid stuff.
  - A dietitian-billing creator makes just under $1M a year in profit from 5,800 Instagram followers. She sells 5-10 cheat sheets a day at $99 plus 2-3 high-ticket sales a week.
  - Alex's 6-month broad-content test produced views but fewer business owners. Returning to hardcore business content raised opt-ins, book sales, workshop and portfolio applications even as views per video fell.
  - A multi-nine-figure marketing firm owner posts deep SEO content that attracts Fortune 500 executives who want to hire him.
- Steps: give away your best stuff (including low-ticket products). Add CTAs and team follow-up. Frame the CTA as personalization ("help apply this to your business"), not "more."
- 99% of free-content consumers never buy, so free content builds reputation.
- He has moved away from down-sells: 10% of buyers at 1/10th the price changes revenue by only 1%. Give that product away and focus on the best customers.
- **All advertising works. It's a matter of efficiency.** Reframe "Facebook doesn't work for us" as "I don't know how to make Facebook work for us."
- **Reminders over teaching.** "The news never changes, just the names." Repeat concepts in new formats, stories and avatars. Henry Ford's marketing director said "We haven't even started running it yet" after Ford had seen the ad for 3 months. Alex gets a viewer for one of his 50+ daily pieces if lucky.
