## How he prices and uses a cheap book for distribution

- Alex priced the book "100 Million Dollar Offers" at 99 cents on Amazon. The goal was not book income but putting it in as many hands as possible.
- His reasoning: Amazon pushes products that make money, and a 99-cent book is easy to sell and taps Amazon's traffic. At 99 cents Amazon takes two-thirds of revenue; at 2.99 he would get two-thirds.
- He says a paid book beats a free one because there is "a difference between a customer and a voyeur".
- The book still earns about 60,000 a month, which he calls irrelevant next to his income (his top employee makes a million plus a year).
- The long-term play is inbound deal flow to acquisition.com in about five years: people who consumed his content, grew to 3, 5, 10 or 20 million, and now want to work with him.
- He opens content with "I have nothing to sell you" because early commenters accused him of selling a course. He says this was an accident, not planned positioning.

## Zero to six figures: how to start a business

- Six figures is "literally just sell something to someone": one channel, one product, one avatar.
- Seven figures is the same thing plus "consistently". Run whatever got you customers (outreach, content, paid ads, affiliates, referrals) consistently. A million a year is only about 20K a week.
- One to three million is mostly raising the main channel's output slightly, baseline efficiencies, and building a core team. The owner usually gets out of delivery, with one or two competent people and a handful of frontline staff (roughly 5-15 employees, only about 2 good ones).
- Three to ten million is where his company takes people on. The minimum bar is three boxes: product-market fit, at least one reliable acquisition channel, and a core team in place, even if dysfunctional.
- Employees see top line as income. A 10M business may pay its owner only around 1M. Early on you are still an employee of your own business wearing all the hats.
- Sequence: pick one of the five ways to advertise, do it consistently, decide what to sell (what the offers book covers), sell it, then break delivery into chunks so five people each do 20% of what you do, rather than finding someone to "do what I do".

## Finding what to sell and why service businesses come first

- Everybody knows something. He uses three buckets: your parents' jobs (knowledge absorbed by osmosis), your past jobs, and your current interests. Pick the bucket where you can solve a problem that makes someone the most money or that they value most.
- He cites Y Combinator weighting past industry experience because of the "ignorance debt" of knowing nothing about an industry.
- Favors people ask you for are a signal of what you can later charge for.
- Most entrepreneurs have "a graveyard of failures"; only Bezos had a first business become the most valuable. Don't start believing it's the one thing for life, which he calls an employee fallacy.
- "Once you start taking steps, the next step becomes illuminated." Planning 100 steps ahead is irrelevant because "chaos is going to break your plan anyways".
- He favors service businesses to start: lowest risk, no capital, just your time (e.g. cleaning houses, lawn care).
- No novel idea needed: look at what everyone else does and do it better. Look at what people hate (slow dry cleaning, messy lawn trimmings).
- You trade time for money "to learn, not to earn". Most of the value of early income is education, paying down ignorance debt.

## His own path: free pilot, online coaching, then the gym

- Last year of consulting, age 23: he ran the "free training project". Clients donated 500-1,000 to a charity of their choice instead of paying him, and let him use their before/after pictures and testimonials. He ended up with about 12 strong before/afters.
- He then asked those clients to pay the same amount to "the charity of Alex". All he needed was an LLC, a bank account and a PayPal account.
- Months later (2013) he posted publicly with a website and testimonials, signed about 20 guys at 200 a month (about 4,000 a month). Fulfillment was 4 hours every Saturday updating programs, macros and meal plans, delivered by text.
- He hated doing the same delivery daily, so instead of doing more of it he decided to own a gym.
- He chose between three ideas: a test prep company, a frozen yogurt business (250K to start, which he lacked) and fitness. The test prep plan collapsed after a miscommunication with a professor partner who used the materials without him.
- He emailed 40 gym owners offering to apprentice free. One replied, and Alex paid 10K to join his mastermind before owning a gym. He worked there 4 AM to 4 PM for about 12 weeks, learning sales, email lists and the full customer life cycle.
- He argues small businesses teach more than corporate roles because you see the whole life cycle end to end.
- He spent roughly 50K of savings fitting out a facility and took a 5,000-a-month lease. He calls it the riskiest thing he has done.
- His fallback plans: a business school story, or stripping at night and driving Uber (he estimated 250K a year). After learning to sell, his baseline rose to car sales at around 400K a year.

## Launch offer, Facebook ads and structuring the front-end program

- Two weeks before opening he spent 3,000 on a weekend Facebook ads workshop (2013). It was the only reason the gym worked; his mentor's Groupon strategy failed for him in a competitive Orange County market.
- First ads: about 1,000 spent, 27 sign-ups in two weeks at 300 each (about 8,700), which paid the first month's rent. He had an empty gym with turf and a table.
- The offer was a six-week challenge: lose 20 pounds in six weeks or get your money back. About 80% succeeded.
- The core was food: meal plan, grocery list, prep instructions, eating-out guides, plus gym sessions (five days a week, later three).
- The close for people who hit it: "Do you think working out six weeks and then going back to not working out is a good long-term solution?" He credited the 300 toward a year, often selling the year halfway through the challenge.
- His principle: define a front-end program with a specific outcome and timeline. It works as onboarding, and you can charge roughly three to four times more than on a recurring basis, which pays back acquisition cost faster. He has used this in almost every business.
- He did about 4,000 sales consults before consuming any sales training.
- Growth: 5K a month at start, 35K a month by month nine, gym outsourced so he only managed it. By month 15 he took profit to open gym two (pre-launched at full capacity), then locations three to five.

## Buying education, skill stacking and the earn-vs-invest argument

- On paid courses: you can learn it on YouTube; you pay for speed, troubleshooting and someone who has already made the mistakes.
- He paid a coach 750 an hour for 8 sessions (about 5,600) to learn retargeting, and used them to learn how the expert thinks while he clicked the buttons himself.
- Skills stack and multiply: ads for gyms alone could make 10-20K a month with no employees; adding sales made it a 100K-a-month skill. Running ads, working the leads and selling them himself, he made 100K a month with no employees working 14-hour days.
- He argues investing in your ability to earn beats the S&P 500 for most people. His example: a minimum-wage worker spends 500 on a two-day phlebotomy certification and goes from 7 to 25 an hour, versus saving 500 to earn a few dollars a year in an index fund.
- "They don't have an investing problem, they have an income problem."
- He notes 4% inflation compounding means 4 million at retirement is worth about 1 million today.
- On college: 2,000 a month on a Mastermind or skill course for 6 months each (ads, selling, courses) returns more than a four-year degree. He gives an example of a friend who used student loans to buy rentals.
- He says 88% of his followers (from his site survey) want to start a business.

## Scaling model: turnkey, licensing and the content engine

- Stagnating with the gyms, he joined an internet mastermind and was told to teach others how to fill gyms. He moved to a turnkey model: fly out, run marketing and sales, and keep 100% of the cash collected, with no employees (about 100K a month, 30 employees later at four times the gym revenue).
- After losing money twice and selling the gyms, he moved to a licensing model in 2017. The first year did 7 million and the next 26 million, with headcount going from 8 to about 30.
- Gyms are a great learning vehicle (many sales reps, a service business with heavy headcount) but a limited vehicle for money. Someone who runs a multi-seven-figure gym can run a 10 million business elsewhere.
- Content: his YouTube costs about a dollar per subscriber per year versus over a dollar per subscriber in AdSense. YouTube earns about 41-42K a month; his wife Layla's about 6-7K. Content spend is around 100K a month.
- Team: four in-house plus vendors for podcast, LinkedIn, YouTube and shorts/TikTok/Reels. He still does Twitter himself.
- His outsourcing playbook: find the best person already doing it, pay whatever to get them, say upfront the intent is to take it in-house, hire an interface person with the skill, then split volume half vendor and half in-house, and "when the in-house person beats the vendor you fire the vendor".
- His team sends video ideas with talking points drawn from his other videos; he picks three and uses them as a loose outline.
- He wants content that is both wide and deep, e.g. reframing sales as "persuasion" to widen the audience.
- Targets: 10 billion, with the mission to make real business education available to everyone. He plans bigger media investment, majority acquisitions of companies in his portfolio (e.g. an accounting firm held in-house), and says he spends about 5% of what he earns while keeping large cash reserves.
