## Core Four growth: spend the first 4 hours a day on being known

- Alex says that under $1 million in revenue, "basically everyone on Earth doesn't know you exist."
- He says the first 4 hours of every day should go to moving from obscurity to awareness.
- The method is the core four: outreach (cold or warm), content, or ads. Pick one, go all in, and spend those 4 hours on it daily.
- He calls advertising a "boom", his internal term for a business order of magnitude change. Optimizations are capped at 100%. For example, raising close rate or opt-in conversion by 10% has a ceiling. Advertising can 100x the number of people who find you.
- He warns against the "optimization mouse trap": nudging a ratio from 10.1 to 10.2 when the answer is "just get 100 times the leads."
- On results: Used Allen reached $1.2M/month by the end of year one, Prestige Labs $1.5M/month, and Gym Launch over $2M/month.

## Shrink competitors by growing, not by attacking

- Most owners see the market as one pie that each new competitor shrinks. Alex says the real market is far bigger, and you are usually advertising on one slice of one medium.
- Gym example: a gym needs about 200 members to be very profitable. With 1 million people in the city, it needs a tiny fraction of the audience, even if 100 other gyms advertise on the same platform.
- To "shrink your competition," you can grow so big that no one hears them, or you can respond with kindness. His Eminem analogy: "Who?" for the first, and claiming your own flaws for the second.
- On the building analogy: you can knock others down or build the tallest building so they look small. He says to build the biggest building.
- His hate-response rule: start with "you are right." He then adds a compliment, such as congratulating the critic on their audience. This avoids back-and-forth.
- He also states his flaws first, for example "I'm here to make money," so critics have nothing to expose.
- Repetition is not bad. At his 100 Million Dollar Leads launch, with roughly 7 million followers across platforms, 19 out of 20 people he stopped on walks in Las Vegas did not know about the launch. His line: "people need to be more reminded than they need to be taught."

## Clear, not clever

- Alex analyzed his highest-converting ads and checked the grade level of the language. He ties this to research that the president who spoke at the lowest grade level tended to win.
- His rule is "clear, not clever." For landing pages, ad copy and content, ask: does a third grader understand this?
- He says simple language helps experts understand faster and helps beginners get it at all. He does not see it as talking down.
- Email test: run emails through a reading-grade app and keep editing until they are below fifth grade (third grade is better). He did this on email follow-ups and got a 50% increase in conversion without changing the message.
- Match analogies to the audience. Mechanics get car analogies, realtors get house analogies, baseball people get baseball. A broad audience gets universal ones like food, sleep or driving.
- His reasoning: complexity hurts the value elements of speed and ease. He quotes Feynman: if you can explain it to a third grader, you understand it.

## Proof over promise, and the hook

- Alex says proof beats promise. Example: a business with a long promise and one review versus one with 11,382 reviews at 4.7 stars. Buyers choose the second.
- Before entering a new space, he gets beta users first. He works for free in exchange for testimonials, reviews, feedback and referrals. He rejects "never work for free", saying it comes from people who don't make much money.
- Show, don't sell. He also says to "do epic stuff first, then talk about what you did." His channel worked mainly because he had sold a company for just under $50 million.
- Keeping early negative feedback quiet is another reason to start free.
- "The hook is greater than everything," because no one sees the proof unless the hook works. Four ways to advertise: warm outreach, cold outreach, paid ads, content.
- A better hook can raise click-through or open rates from 1% to 5% or 2% to 10%, which can move a whole business 2x to 5x.
- His hook formula, from reviewing his top YouTube videos, is proof, promise, plan. He uses both a visual hook and a verbal hook.
- He cites Ogilvy: once you've written the headline, you've spent 80 cents of your advertising dollar.
- Speaker intros: changing the 1-2 minute intro for a 60-minute talk changed the speaker's net promoter score a lot, even though the talk itself was identical.
- Case: his team cut the first 3 seconds of a video before the real hook. It went from about 40,000 views to 780,000, a 19x improvement.

## More, steal from yourself, and low volume looks like volatility

- Of more, better and new, he calls "more" the highest risk-adjusted return, and says shiny object syndrome is "a cancer." The goal is one to N, not zero to one.
- When you change something that works, you "incur the cost of change" but are not guaranteed the benefit.
- Sales example: a rep closing 30% might reach 40% with effort, a 25% lift overall. Hiring three more reps is a 4x or 300% improvement, and changing the script risks dropping from 30 to 20.
- How to do more: go from 100 to 200 outreaches a day, $100 to $200 a day in ads, or one post to two posts a day. He notes some businesses spend $2 million a day on ads.
- Story: his first gym's flyers. He put out 300 flyers and got one call, from a man who said the flyer damaged his Mercedes. His mentor tested with 5,000 and then ran 5,000 a day for 30 days, about 150,000 flyers. His lesson: "what presents as volatility is typically a symptom of low volume."
- Volume math: if you close 12 customers a year, getting 12 a day takes 365 times the activity.
- Steal from yourself, with the 70/20/10 split. Put 70% of effort on carbon copies of what works, 20% on adjacent variations, and 10% on brand new ideas. He says most owners have this flipped.
- Example of adjacent: rewording "starting school communities is the fastest way to start an online business" into "one of the fastest ways to start an online business is to start a school community." Another is the same hook with a different shirt or setting.
- Portfolio story: a new advertising director's new ideas all failed. Reusing the hook that had worked for 3 years worked again. His Nike "Just do it" point: you get bored of a message long before customers do.
- Building analogy: there is one arrangement of bricks that makes a building and unlimited ways to destroy it, so deviating from what works usually breaks it.

## Negative word of mouth

- Alex says negative word of mouth is much stronger than positive. He cites a Disney study that 37 tragic moments are needed to make up for one magic moment, and the idea that happy customers tell 5 people.
- Diagnosis: if CPM (cost per thousand impressions) is flat but cost per lead is up 2x to 4x, fewer people are responding. That means word of mouth is working against you.
- The audience that is neutral or has not heard of you shrinks over time.
- His tactic: keep early negative feedback concentrated by working for free and asking customers how to make it right. Introduce strangers only after consistent positive feedback.

## Brand: high-information buyers and the 70/30 give-to-ask ratio

- Alex rejects the "emotional vs logical buyer" split. He sees a continuum of how much information a buyer needs and how much they have received.
- Low-information buyers are a small pool that everyone fights over. High-information buyers are orders of magnitude more numerous. Direct response alone caps growth because it only reaches the small pool.
- He uses Eugene Schwartz's five awareness levels: unaware (broad curiosity hooks), problem aware, solution aware, product aware, and most aware (where you just make offers).
- New Balance example, via CMO Chris Davis: the budget was 30% broad emotional awareness and 70% "buy these shoes." He flipped it to 70% pairings, endorsements and athletes, and 30% direct asks. Returns took 18 months.
- Alex applies the same ratio to his own impressions: 70% or more should be giving, not asking. He cites the roughly 3.5 to 1 give-to-ask ratio seen in TV (1 minute of ads per 3.5 minutes of content) and in Facebook feeds (1 ad per 3 posts). He also credits TikTok's early ad-free growth.
- When founders leave, executives often flip the ratio and "suck all the water out of the well" without digging the next one.
- B2B version: aspirational outcomes you achieved, outcomes for people like your avatar, and outcomes for the prospect. The prospect's own results come from free content and free products, like his free books and courses. This works because of degrees of separation (two, one, zero), and zero is strongest but slowest.

## Marketing laws: state the facts, say what only you can say, and target the right list

- Law one is "state the facts and tell the truth." Make the truth more compelling by changing reality (do epic things), and show only what you can show.
- Martial arts example: the owner was one of six "double secret black belts" in the nation, with none in his area. Alex told him to say exactly that.
- The Mad Men "It's toasted" example: other brands may also toast their tobacco, but only one says so. Narrow down to something unique. Be the best in a puddle: "It's more important that you are the best than what you are the best of."
- Rockefeller overpaid for the biggest Cincinnati refinery to claim the top spot. In the next 30 days he did over 20 M&A deals, which paid for the overpayment.
- The list: wrong audience is why many marketers think marketing doesn't work. A winter coat shown in South Florida fails regardless of offer.
- Targeting by channel: reach people likely to have the problem (outreach), use platform targeting (ads), and note that content is shaped by the algorithm.
- Make the person in the ad look like the avatar. On Gym Launch's thank-you page, swapping his photo for Leila's boosted response and show rates.
- Test of a good list: if the right 1,000 people are in the room, even a mediocre offer gets some response and starts the feedback loop.
- Masters have more ways to win. A recruiting expert asked about time to fill, two-sided fit and cost to acquire talent, metrics Alex had never tracked.
- When he started outbound, his team did an "intervention" after 4 months and one sale. Progress was hidden in milestones: finding a good list provider, enriching data, local numbers, fixing the hook and script, fixing the offer, and fixing no-shows. "A heart surgery looks like murder to somebody from the outside."
- Track leading indicators, not only lagging ones like revenue, churn or sales.
