## Pick the highest-return motion when you are small: go all in on what already makes money

- Alex's frame for a tiny business (here a roofing company 2 months old, about $41K revenue, 59% profit margin): there is one owner and little time and money, so "focus all efforts on the highest return stuff."
- He values reliability of cash. The owner could knock 20 doors and get a deal, which Alex calls the "cats have nine lives" advantage: entrepreneurs need as many lives as they can get, and knowing how to go get money is one of them.
- His advice on scaling: "go all in door to door." "I don't think we need to be cute about this... get really violent with it."
- Reliability early gives the business a heartbeat. Early on everything is "feast or famine"; cash flow lets "water start flowing through the pipes."
- Result after 90 days: revenue went from a little under $32K to about $247K, with 62 five-star reviews (from under 20) and 85 services sold, built and collected on (from 32). The owner reported a "monomaniacal focus" and said he got ROI on every move.
- Alex's point on why advice helps: it is like replaying a video game level. Someone ahead of you saves the months you would stay stuck. After that "the only hold back is just pure execution."

## Find the cast-aside, high-volume offer: repairs instead of replacements

- Alex likes work other people have cast aside, because competitors "think they're too good for it." He then builds the business so it earns good gross profit on that work.
- The numbers he ran:
  - Replacements: about $7K gross profit each, average ticket $18-25K.
  - Repairs: 64% margin on a $1,500 ticket, about $1,100 gross profit.
  - His test: could the owner sell 3x as many repairs as replacements? The owner said yes.
- His target was to raise the repair ticket to $2.5-5K (a $2K-$4K range was floated as the goal) through bundling and upselling. At that ticket repairs become "an incredibly interesting business" because of volume and reliability.
- Why he likes high-volume businesses:
  - More stability.
  - Reps get many more reps, since sales cycles are shorter.
  - Less admin headache, with no insurance claims or delayed payments.
  - Almost no cash flow constraints.
  - "You just add more of these and that literally becomes the machine."
- Execution result: services per month rose from 15-20 to 60-80, more than 4x. A repair tech did 3-5 jobs a day, and a second tech was expected to take that to 6-8.
- Getting the first sale is the hard part; "getting the approximate sale is significantly easier." Repairs get a foot in the door, and the follow-on work comes from there.

## The menu upsell script for transactional sales

Alex calls the menu upsell his favorite chapter of his book *Money Models* (in the upsell section; the book is free online). He says it works for any sale that can close in one conversation. His four steps:

1. **Unsell what they don't need.** "Looks like this is fine, you probably have a couple years on this." This builds trust because the customer expects an upsell and does not get one.
2. **Prescribe what they do need.** "You don't need this, this, this. What I am saying is you're probably going to need this and this."
3. **Offer a fake choice (A or B).** Examples: male or female installer, Monday or Tuesday start, neon nails or white nails, coffee or water. "It's a fake choice, but it just makes the close a lot easier."
4. **Card on file.** Put it on the payment method already stored. "Awesome."

- Example flow: compliment the home, rule out what is not needed, then say "would you rather this or this? You want to take care of that on the card on file? We already have the hood popped up."
- Practical tactics:
  - Have the crew arrive with dumpsters out and tarps open so the customer thinks "you might as well have us fix everything while we're here."
  - The sales rep or a separate closer (Amanda, in this case) runs the onboarding call after the first repair is booked.
- The owner's result: prescribing and taking options off the table built trust, and "people are just taking every recommendation." He called it one of his most effective strategies.

## Use two VSLs to pre-frame sales conversations

- Alex's setup: one video the customer watches before the on-site visit (about the company), and a second before the call with the closer (about the other work they probably need). Each frames the conversation so the rep arrives to a pre-sold prospect.
- The VSL structure he outlined (about 7 minutes):
  1. Intro and promise: what the video will explain and the outcome they want, such as an amazing roof and avoiding past pain.
  2. Proof early: named customers with houses as big as or bigger than theirs, "so you can feel like I work with people of your caliber."
  3. Old way vs. new way: how the industry does it, then the company's named process (the owner's "special K process").
  4. Objections 1 through N: for each, state the belief, why it is wrong, what is true, and proof (a similar customer story). "That's just a belief breaking mechanism over and over again."
- How it helps the rep: it "completely edifies" the company and adds social proof and stats before the rep arrives.
- A VSL is more specific and targeted than a general YouTube channel, and gets more targeted as the sales team grows.
- Resale framing for the second video: homes with a roofing plan resell at higher value, and the worst move is fixing everything the day you sell, so you "fix your house for the next guy."
- The owner said he planned to shoot the VSL the week after the 90-day check-in.

## Add margin with speed incentives (pay to move to the front of the line)

- Alex's rule: if you are supply constrained, meaning more people want your work than you can deliver, "add speed incentives for people to just move to the front of the line. You do the same amount of work and you just make more money."
- Logic: jobs A, B, C take the same resources. If C will pay $1,000 more and A is still inside an acceptable window, do C first. "Speed's all margin."
- Offer wording for repairs: "We always try to be really fast, but for a lot of our clients time is money. We have a 20% priority boost." The guarantee is that it is done by the end of the week or the boost is free.
- Pitch it at the end of the repair offer, tacked on to the initial sale.
- Result: the owner offered a commercial client next-day work for an extra $300 of "straight margin." The client took it, was satisfied and bought the next job right after.
- With the menu upsell and speed, a crew can do 2-4 repairs a day, "like doing a full roof replacement a day."

## Turn one-time customers into recurring revenue with a maintenance membership

- Alex's offer, pitched at job completion when you also ask for a review: a VIP or "diamond roofing" annual plan.
  - Price: $15-20 a month, paid annually, so $180-240, depending on market. It can go higher.
  - Credit: $2 of future repair credit for every $1 paid.
  - Annual roof check: it brings the company back to look at the roof once a year.
- A review incentive: price it at $500 a year, but leaving a review drops it to $200. "It's a little carrot."
- The real purpose is not the fee: "We don't even care about the money. We care about the fact that we're going to have an appointment" to see the customer and "sell more stuff."
- Why: it moves the business from one-time to recurring revenue, which is "significantly more valuable" and "dramatically increases LTV."
- Alex's caveat from the follow-up: the owner had not cracked residential adoption yet. Alex suspected timing and the exact script wording, saying "sometimes just tweaking a couple things" makes it click.
- Also covered: stay in contact with past clients every 6-12 months with detailed notes to keep LTV high.

## Commissions, door-to-door scale, and building the team

- Make commission math simple for door knockers. On a repair averaging about $4K revenue with 10% commission, a rep earns $400. Divided by about 50 doors, it is roughly $10 a door: "Want to make another 10 bucks? Knock that door." The rule: "How much money do you want to make? Just knock that many doors."
- Alex's plan was to build a "door-to-door army" as "the clear straight shot." He called door knocking the main growth engine.
- Treat salespeople as a new customer avatar. Marketing should now recruit them, because with 4-5 reps the owner can manage the team alone.
- At 1 to 4 and 5 to 9 employees, the work is specializing the product, creating free content for marketing, and tracking sales more closely as the team grows.
- Volume brings reliability, and reliability lets you forecast, which lets you cut costs. For example, knowing a volume might let you cut your cost basis by half or two-thirds.
- The owner also added an ERP system, a custom CRM built for his job flow, to run jobs.
- On advice at different sizes: at zero, the steps are identical (for example, form an LLC to accept money). At very large sizes, the advice is unique to the business. "The bigger the business, the more tailored the advice has to be."
