## Starting a business: sequence, skills first, investing last

- Alex says the common mistake is conflating sequence. Many people believe making money comes from investing, but "investing is the last thing you do, not the first thing you do."
- He argues active income funds the bets. The people with the most money typically have very high incomes, and the excess cash flow lets them take riskier swings.
- Example: his neighbor owns Panda Express, with about $3.7 billion in revenue and about a 27% net margin. Alex says that is about $935 million in personal income, built over 45 years. The neighbor can take big bets because of that regular cash.
- He rejects "active vs passive" as a false binary. He thinks in "money per unit of time," and says nothing is truly passive (a passive deal means looking at about 100 deals first).
- He dislikes speculative investing as the "greater fool theory." If you don't know the variables, "you are 100% gambling." Retail investors usually hear about things at the peak.
- Rule: "the more it feels like luck is usually where you should have your first red flag." He believes that if you control the variables, you can predict the outcome.
- He says to build income skills first. A business is track (promote), convert (sell), deliver. He says you can build something very big in about 5 to 7 years, but most people spend those years "reliving the same 30 days."
- His five stages: uninformed optimism, informed pessimism, valley of despair, informed optimism, achievement. At stage three, people either jump to a new thing (back to stage one) or stay in the pain and learn. "Staying in the pain is what gives you the catalyst to learn how to get out of pain."

## Picking what to sell: the three Ps, services first, four steps to a business

- He says most businesses come from one of three Ps: Passion (inherent interest), Profession (what you are already paid for), or Pain (a problem you lived through, e.g. a mom making non-allergenic food for her kids).
- Profession is the fastest route. You already have something valuable, so you only add promotion and conversion.
- He prefers that most people start with services. He cites that 78% of US businesses are service-based. They are the easiest to start (you trade time for money) and the lowest risk.
- Four steps to a business:
  1. Form an LLC.
  2. Open a bank account.
  3. Connect a payment processor.
  4. Ask a stranger to do something in exchange for money.
- "First rule of entrepreneurship is use what you got." Starting is the perfect condition. He calls waiting for the perfect pick the "fallacy of the perfect pick": every path has trade-offs, and you must decide what you are willing to give up.
- Myth 3 he names: overbelief in the idea and underbelief in execution. The idea doesn't have to be the next Facebook.
- Keep your job if you have a family or a mortgage. Use the "5 to 9" (5-9am and 5-9pm). Quit when side income surpasses the job income, and he suggests sustaining that for 6 months, since there will be volatility.

## Getting the first customers: warm outreach, 10 by 10, and the price ladder

- Start with warm outreach. Look through your phone contacts (e.g. 400) and Facebook friends (e.g. 300). Send a personal video or text saying what you're doing and what you solve, and ask "do you know anybody?" He says you are not asking friends to buy. About 90% of the time the responder says "you could do that for me."
- "Consistency is the currency of credibility." Referrers risk their social capital, so show you're still doing it.
- Add risk reversal: "I'm doing the first 10 for free" to get testimonials, reviews and learning.
- "10 by 10": give 10 people 10 hours of free one-on-one work (or 5 people for 5 hours). It works because even minimum-wage labor has value, and you iterate fast with no tech setup. Build the website or write the copy in front of them.
- Call script:
  1. In the first call, ask what they want to solve and ask permission to tell them what you do at the end.
  2. At the last hour, recap the progress and ask if they're happy.
  3. Use the hours to gather other problems you could solve.
  4. Pitch a longer engagement (e.g. "problems 1, 2 and 3, maybe 6 months").
- Reciprocity builds up. If they say no on the first call, don't continue.
- Qualify with BANT, which he says IBM figured out about 70 years ago: Budget, Authority, Need, Timing. He notes timeshares use roughly six-hour blocks because trust takes time.
- On not wanting to work for free: "I actually think it's entitlement." "You're not working for free. You're learning and then you're earning." Set terms: feedback during the work and a review in three formats, plus a video and a text version. He says a glowing review is worth more than $1,000 long term.
- Pricing: once demand exceeds your time, say "if you want, you can pay." Then "bump the price by 20% every five people until eventually people stop saying yes."
- He names supply and demand and leverage as the two forces he's most moved by. Free work creates artificial demand.

## Core Four, CLOSER, and the Rule of 100

- Core Four, the only four ways one person can promote:
  1. Warm outreach (one-on-one, people you know).
  2. Cold outreach (one-on-one, strangers).
  3. Posting content (one-to-many).
  4. Paid ads (one-to-many).
- Higher-leverage ways come after: customers who bring customers, affiliates/partners, employees, and agencies. But "everyone must start with the core four."
- CLOSER sales framework:
  - **C**larify: why did they respond?
  - **L**abel: name the problem ("so what I'm hearing is...").
  - **O**verview past experiences: what have you tried, what worked, what didn't. This is the pain cycle, which increases short-term motivation.
  - **S**ell the vacation, not the plane flight. Keep it under about 320 words. Use three points, each with a short anecdote.
  - **E**xplain away concerns after asking for the sale.
  - **R**einforce the decision. He added this later: people judge a business by the first 24 hours after purchase. Restate next steps and tie them to their goals.
- The five concerns, each a fallacy in decision-making:
  - Timing is really priorities.
  - Money is really value.
  - Decision-maker means they need support, not permission (use past agreements, e.g. "your husband knows you're overweight, does he want you to stay that way?").
  - Stall: help them decide.
  - Preferences: "if you change the variables, you change the outcome."
- After each explanation, ask again if it solves that concern, then ask if they're ready to move forward.
- Rule of 100: do 100 per day of either 100 minutes of posting content, $100 a day on ads, or 100 outreaches. Do all three if you want.
- Volatility is "a symptom of insufficient volume." If 700 efforts yield one sale a week, then 700 a day yields about a sale a day. Alex says that compression is what separates small businesses from large ones.
- Story: an influencer told him he wasn't doing anything wrong, "you're just doing too little." He had 4 posts a day on Instagram, 10 on LinkedIn, and 70 to 80 a day total, while Alex posted about every other day.
- Improvement loop: do about 100 reps, find the top 10% (best response, views, clicks), work out what differed, and repeat it in the next 100.

## Persuasion, imposter syndrome, and fear of selling

- Selling is "arranging the variables to increase the likelihood a sale occurs." The difference between helping and manipulation is intention.
- His persuasion rule: "state the facts and tell the truth." Imposter feelings come from misrepresenting. Once you've done about 20 reps, your conviction rises because you can say "I've done 20 of these."
- Use damaging admissions. In the gym space he told owners with bad parking and old equipment to say so in the ad: "we have the best workouts on this side of the Mississippi."
- Every position has an advantage. When he ran Gym Launch and a software company (Alan, sold in 2021), he told smaller agencies to pitch "you get the CEO, not account rep number 77." Compare the person you'll actually work with, not company size.
- Don't claim to be a one-stop shop. If the facts aren't compelling enough, "do the free work so the facts are compelling."
- Some of the best deals are the ones you never do. Setting expectations up front makes cleaner relationships.
- Fear of rejection: the worst they can say is no. Sales is like dating, a numbers game. The Rule of 100 habituates you, like exposure therapy for phobias.
- Fear of judgment: most people are fine with sucking but not with being judged for it. Name whose voice is in your head.
  - Example: when selling his company for $46.2 million, he realized only "Tom" would judge it as not enough. "If you don't know why you believe what you believe, it's not your belief, it's someone else's."
  - Criticism is a fixed cost: you get criticized if you do nothing and if you do something.
  - It takes about 20 hours to become proficient at most things.
  - Listen to the people closest to your goals, not closest to you.
  - "Fear is a mile wide and an inch deep" (he credits Leila Hormozi).
  - Play out the specific worst case to defuse catastrophizing.
  - Hate translates to "he lives his life in a way that I would not prefer."
- Also: if someone earns more than you, they know something you don't. Use comparison for study, not envy. Separate criticism (gap between expected and actual) from insults.

## Money Models book, value equation, and the economic unit

- *$100M Money Models* is the third book in the $100M series, after Offers (what to sell) and Leads (how to promote). Alex says he began it in 2019 and has not publicly discussed these structures.
- Definition: a money model is "the core economic unit of the business," a deliberate sequence of offers. Its financial goal is to collect enough cash from one customer in the first 30 days to acquire and serve two more customers.
- Four objectives:
  1. Get more people interested (attraction offers, e.g. buy X get Y free, giveaways).
  2. Get them to spend more.
  3. Get them to spend faster (pull cash flow forward).
  4. Get them to buy again and again (recurring, like a membership, or reoccurring, like Coca-Cola).
- You only need one to start, and he'd start with an attraction offer. The book details five structures that he says he has used.
- Why it matters: "when you have a superior money model, it gives you padding to be bad at everything else." Advertising, sales and offer quality can be weaker, and the cash flow lets you pay down your ignorance debt.
- Alex says the businesses that were exceptionally successful, compared with moderately successful ones, had a better money model.
- Value equation (from Offers):
  - Bigger dream outcome raises value.
  - Higher perceived likelihood of achievement raises value.
  - Shorter time delay raises value.
  - Less effort and sacrifice raises value.

## Feedback, volume, and focus: how he improves and works

- Behaviorist view: people do things because they were rewarded for them in the past. Explanations beat descriptions ("Timmy is dishonest" is circular).
- Example: an exec who threatened to quit over a title change had never left despite many title changes, so the threat was low risk.
- Education means "same condition, new behavior." Intelligence means rate of learning. If your day looks the same every day, you've learned nothing.
- Good feedback is fast and specific. Story: a high-performing director described as "a dick" didn't change after four talks. Alex stripped out the judgment and asked what behaviors caused it (interrupting, telling people how to do their jobs). He gave a script ("I heard this thing, mind if I share it?") and the change was visible in a week.
- Don't dig for the past narrative. Ask what to do instead under these conditions, like fixing a pickleball serve. He defines trauma as a permanent behavior change from an aversive stimulus, which can be good or bad.
- Push vs pivot: pivot if a need-to-be-true assumption proves false. Push if conditions hold and it's a skill gap. Commitment is "the elimination of alternatives." He cites Naval: "it's not 10,000 hours, it's 10,000 iterations."
- Learn fast with full shutdown days: he finished a web page by noon on a weekend he'd put off for weeks. "Fear is a mile wide and an inch deep. So is ignorance."
- Productivity: "do nothing except for the task you set out to achieve." He gets better results through elimination. His setup is a good night's sleep, knowing what he'll start the day before, caffeine, double earplugs, and a windowless room.
- Fear, inverted thinking: list all the ways to kill your business, then flip them (he credits Charlie Munger). Find a concrete fallback (e.g. a friend's couch for 6 months).
- Seasons: "deleting work life balance for a season nets you more work life balance over a lifetime." He cites Paul Graham's idea that you can endure a million dollars of pain over 40 years or crunch it into four.
- Action beats listening: "if someone just listens to the episode, tomorrow will be the same for you." His example action is recording a selfie video and texting it to 100 people.
- Final five:
  - Best advice: "Do so much work it would be unreasonable for you not to be successful."
  - Worst advice: "Follow your passion."
  - Wish he'd known sooner: find people better than you and help them first.
  - Lesson that took too long: "Focus. One thing all in."
