## Selling with logic: the philosophy behind closing

- Alex says buyers are a mix of emotion and logic. Emotion lowers the action threshold, but logic makes the decision stick. He cites Charlie Munger: "we have a high moral responsibility to be rational."
- Emotional buyers often get a call two days later asking "I don't know why I bought." Giving the buyer a rational foundation keeps the relationship going after the emotion fades.
- Prospects want to believe you and want to buy. Your job is to help their logical brain justify a decision they already want to make.
- Selling happens before you ask for the sale. Closing begins once you ask.
- Expect "no" and plan for it. If they could decide alone they would already have sent the money.
- "If you didn't get a gasp from a price tag you didn't go high enough." A high anchor lets you walk down. He says $1,000 feels like a rounding error after $100,000, but sounds huge after $10.
- Selling is a transference of belief over a bridge of trust. Hot and cold streaks come from conviction, not skill.
- Trust needs real intent, because people sniff out whether you only care about closing. "The person who cares the most about the prospect wins the sale."
- Closers ask hard questions because they care. Stay curious: "ha, that's so weird, tell me more."
- Win the argument and you lose the sale. Closing is "a dance not a fight."
- Record every sale. Study the hot streaks to see where you paused, what you joked about, and how you asked.
- Red zone analogy: the top five NFL red-zone offenses made the playoffs 90% of the time. A strong closing skill covers many other weaknesses.
- Of 100 prospects, about 10% will never buy and about 10% always buy. Closing skill is for the middle 80%.
- Success metric: did I help them decide? If you are happy when they choose to own their power and not buy, the pressure disappears.

## Obstacles vs. objections, and the three distortions

- **Obstacles** are disagreements raised before you ask for the sale (for example "I just want more information"). They are easy to break then.
- **Objections** come after the ask. Alex calls handling obstacles early "killing zombies" or "defusing the bomb." Confront things like spouse or time before you state the price.
- For the sale to work, the prospect must want the goal and believe three things: the product gets them the goal (the way they want to get there), others will support them, and it will work for them specifically.
  - Gym Launch lesson: people declined a $100 service but bought $400 of supplements, because they wanted to lose weight their own way.
  - For "it won't work for me," use proof: "How many people would you need to see for it to be more unreasonable not to believe than to believe?"
- Alex uses Albert Ellis's (CBT) three core distortions: circumstances, other people, and self.
- People peel the onion in order: circumstances, then others, then self. Time, money and fit sit on the outside. Authority comes next, then avoidance ("I need to think about it"). "I need to think about it" means you are finally talking to the decision maker.
- Say you understand, then ask a question and walk them one layer deeper. Do not argue.

## Overcoming circumstances: time, money, fit

**Time**
- Seasonal ("I'm busy"): Do you want this to last? Will you be busy again? Then start now, while busy, so it sticks.
- Micro ("no time in my day"): "My wife pulled out my phone and said 'I guess I just found your time.'" A good program cuts the 90% of what you're doing that isn't working.
- The "when-then" fallacy: "when I have X, I'll do Y" flips the sequence. Examples: "I'll save when I'm rich" or "I'll buy the program that helps me make money once I have money."

**Money (four frames)**
1. A lot is good: "Is this a lot of money to you?" If yes, that's why you'll go all in, and the best stories come from people in your exact situation.
2. A lot in absolute terms but little relative to the result. If it adds $10k/month or gets you in a bikini, is it worth it? If yes, the issue is belief in the product, not price. Compare it to a degree costing $100-200k and four years.
3. "What is money good for anyway?" You will pay either way, in money or in time: "do you want it to take 12 weeks or 12 years?"
4. Resourcefulness over resources. He uses the Shoe Dog story, where Phil Knight borrowed and stalled vendors to survive. Everyone finds money for an unexpected tax bill or a Ferrari at $5,000.

**Fit**
- New identity, new priorities: the Sephora story, where girls were told to budget for makeup as they became women. Show the prospect's spending (nails, outfits) against their goal.
- Change the change: "The way you spend your first four hours of the day makes your bank account look the way it does." Is staying the same more painful than changing?
- Hypothetical: "If this were perfect, would you do it?" or "1 to 10, what makes it a 10?" If no, the issue is trust. If they name a gap ("no broccoli"), fix it ("green beans?") and close.

## Authority and avoidance (spouse, stalls)

**Spouse or decision maker**
- First sidestep it: "What do you think they wouldn't like?" Then overcome that point directly.
- Use past agreements: "Do they approve of your current struggle? Then why would they oppose fixing it?" Flip the roles: "If your spouse needed this, would you support them?"
- "You're asking for permission instead of support." If you hand off the decision, you will resent them in two years.
- Delayed close: take the card now with a 3-day no-sweat guarantee. Alex jokes about the husband wanting you to sit with Cheetos.

**Avoidance ("I need to think about it") by time frame**
- **Past:** "It's a fast decision" gets sunk cost and "How long have you wanted this?" "Don't let it burn you twice": a bad past program should not block a good one. A 2018 prospect texted him years later with regret.
- **Present:** The rocking chair story. Time is not what they lack, information is, and you are the only source. Three yeses decide it: it can get you the goal, you trust me, it will work for you. Then ask whether they have access to the money.
- **Informed decision:** use a guarantee, "you can only make an informed decision from the inside."
- "Decide" comes from the Latin word meaning to cut off: which future are you killing?
- **Future:** magnify the pain ("five more years of this"), then lay out the options: win, don't try, or try with a guarantee.
  - Only walking out guarantees no result.
  - Other nudges: "Are you tired of another year of almost?" and "Does this move you closer or further from the goal?"

**Bonus close:** the reason you give for not doing it is the reason you need to do it. Fortunes come from risking a little money; invest in skills, because "the time tax of ignorance" costs more than any tax.

## What top salespeople do: opportunities and prep

- Alex's three jobs for a rep: maximize opportunities, convert the highest percentage, and do both consistently for years.
- **Hours and availability**
  - The top seller works the most hours.
  - His software company Acquisition.com scheduled over 4,000 appointments a day. Availability was the strongest predictor of sales.
  - Nail salon example: the shops that said "no same-day" lost the customer.
- **Pull appointments forward**
  - Drag Thursday's call into an open slot today for a better show rate.
  - If a lead hasn't confirmed, push that slot and fill it with a responsive lead.
  - The solar rep who called every new booking immediately sold about four times more units than the second-best rep. He either closed right away or filled a later slot.
- **Two SOPs:** one for on-call (the script) and one for off-call (follow-ups, dragging appointments, cancelling). Most companies have none for the second.
- **Book a meeting from a meeting (BAMFAM, from Shiron Bamfam):** never end a call without the next meeting set. Address time obstacles on the spot.
- **Don't take rejection personally.** Work leads in volume. Keep a visible "kill list" of whales.
- **Referrals:** ask "who do you know?" so they must name someone. "Who do you know who is as awesome as you?" One new rep was 50% above the next rep just by asking everyone this.
  - Referrals close at roughly 80-90%.
  - If one in four refers, 1 sale in 4 becomes 2 in 5.
- **Prework:** personalized texts before the call ("which shirt size/gift card?") raise show rates.
- **Research and notes**
  - Spend about 10% of call length on prep. Five minutes of research makes the rapport and trust instant.
  - Alex built profiles of gym owners from their socials, noticing the gym layout before calling.
  - Read previous notes at the start of every call so the prospect doesn't repeat their story.

## Running the call: listening, scripts, and looping

- The best reps listen about twice as much as they talk. The one answering questions is the one being interrogated.
- "They believe nothing you say, they believe everything they say." Answer questions with questions. Example: "What have you done before? What did you like and not like?" Then highlight what they liked.
- Do not ask for the sale until you know the answer, like a lawyer on the stand. If you sense hesitation, ask more questions. Name it: "I'm feeling some hesitation around price, talk to me about that."
- Use commands as well as questions to break patterns, such as "tell me about your day." Use childlike curiosity, with a tilted head and rising tone.
- Tone and emphasis change meaning. The same words delivered badly (say, "what are your main concerns?") land very differently.
- "Breathe the script." Know it cold so you can be fully present.
  - Cut words. Reps add coughs and extra questions that got rewarded by luck, and scripts bloat.
  - Shorter calls let you take more calls.
- Role-play every morning and work on one narrow part at a time (intro, close, "I need to think about it"). Run three good reps in a row. A coach who lists nine fixes gets none.
- Use ghetto tone and humor to confront hard topics in a light way, a trick he learned from Sam Bakhtiar.
- Confront hard things before the close: "What are you most afraid of happening? Let's play it out." Worst case vs. best case. He has probably closed more on this than anything else. One buyer with a Dave Ramsey envelope was handled this way.
- Ask again. "If you do not ask for the sale you will not get it." Handle the concern, then ask again, "looping." He once kept asking because the 3 pm slot was the last of the day.
  - Resolve the concern each time. Do not just repeat the ask.
- Meta skills
  - Three types of salespeople (from Mike Ary): dogs (need feeding), horses (need whipping) and tigers (kill for sport).
  - Treat low-quality leads as free practice. Jacob (his protégé) took the worst leads and improved fastest.
  - No blaming leads or circumstances. Give feedback one-on-one, not on group calls.
- **Metrics:** close rate alone is just an outcome. Track lead-to-booking, show rate, offer rate, close rate, cash collected percentage, and calls per close.
  - Raising the schedule rate 20% equals raising the close rate 20%.
  - Give the best leads to the best closers and the worst leads to new reps. He cites a timeshare rep who 5x'd his income this way and the company rolled it out nationwide.
  - Score leads to do this.

## The CLOSER framework and selling the vacation

Alex built this from hundreds of scripts. It works for B2C and B2B, at $500 or $100,000. Each step is a milestone.

- **C - Clarify why they are there.**
  - "What made you come in today? What's your goal? Why is that important?"
  - "I just want information" is an obstacle to confront: nobody collects information all day, so what is the problem?
- **L - Label them with a problem.** "What I'm hearing is X, sound right?" They must admit it: "can't cure cancer until you admit you have it."
- **O - Overview past pain.**
  - What have you tried, how long, how did it work? Repeat the "pain cycle" until they run out.
  - Their sense of being understood is what makes them buy. Say it isn't their fault: they were missing one piece.
- **S - Sell the vacation.**
  - The pitch is under 3 minutes, three pillars (fitness, nutrition, accountability; or exclusive, timely, qualified leads), each told as a short anecdote.
  - Examples: brushing teeth for accountability; "do you need motivation to watch Game of Thrones?" for nutrition.
  - "Sell the vacation, not the plane flight." Skip modules, macros and URLs. Everyone gets to Maui (swim, boat, flight, private jet), and the price varies by service level.
- **E - Explain away concerns.**
  - Price: "If we gave away Ferraris at $5,000, they'd find the money."
  - Decision maker: "What if they said no?" Then past agreements, then forgiveness over permission.
  - Stall: teach a decision with three questions (does it meet your needs, do you want to work with us, do you have access to funds).
- **R - Reinforce the decision.**
  - Send a personalized CEO video and a handwritten card.
  - Customers decide in the first 48 hours after a sale whether they believe in you.
  - Memorize two things: your short stories and your obstacle overcomes.
- Train reps on the prospect, not the product. Don't let reps talk about the company's $100 million.
- Conviction fixes tone: re-read testimonials aloud daily, fix everything you can about the product, and never blame the customer. In the mortgage-leads story, the rep John had weak conviction until Alex showed him how he'd talk if he believed.
- Daily training: 60 minutes, five days a week. About 25 minutes reading the script aloud (restart on errors), 5 minutes of obstacles, then listening to a recorded call.

## Scaling a team and the diagnostic sale (case studies)

**The six C's for scaling:** CLOSER sequence, consistent daily training with conviction, call recordings (Gong), communication cycles with feedback, cut the fat fast, and competition plus career path. Detail on four of them:
- Cut: if a rep can't close in the first one to two weeks, they likely won't. Fire a dud within 14 days.
- Competition: six-week cycles with mini-teams of mixed ability.
- Career path: milestone pay bumps (50, 100, 150 deals).
- No prima donnas or special snowflakes, because one top seller who ignores the process can lower the whole team's output.

**Case 1: replace outsourced "mercenaries" ($21.6M/yr profit gain)**
- The outsourced team took 20% commission, and less than 30% ascended.
- Five steps: hire a director (one who hires many), rebuild the comp, rebuild the process, scale to 40 reps in 12 weeks, and drop the outsourced team.
- Director criteria: been there and done that, metric driven, and humble, servant demeanor.
- Hiring outreach: recruiters, content and outbound. 100 to 1,000 messages is normal.
- Comp: pay Setters and closers, with ratchets (for example 10% to 15% to 25%) so winners win. Overall cost fell from 20% to 9%.
- Show rate rose from 74% to 91% (less aggressive copy, same or next-day bookings, personalized 3-way intros).
- Set-to-close scheduling rose from 53% to 94% (Setters paid on the outcome, not deqing early).
- Close-call show rate went from 63% to 87% (daily role-play, game tape reviews, one-on-ones, end-of-day reports).
- A video sales letter was added between calls, which handled common objections early.
- Close rate fell from 80% to 42%, but throughput rose: the ascension rate went from 20% to 32%.
- Cash collected went from $7M ($1.4M commission) in Q1 to $12M ($1.08M commission) the next quarter, a $5.4M change in one quarter, or $21.6M a year.

**Case 2: lifting a sales team in about 60 days (show rate, offer, close, cash)**
- Starting numbers: show rate 49%, offer rate 83%, close 27%, cash collected 47%, 56 units a month.
- Show rate fixes: ad targeting and the buyer's optimization were wrong (lead cost instead of cost per sale), no double-dialing, slow speed to contact, and no morning-of reminder.
- Org fixes: the CEO was a bad sales manager, so they hired an experienced director first. They then cut the weakest closers, raised Setter targets from two to four a day, made one Setter a lead-nurture specialist, and fixed the discovery script and looping.
- Results: show rate 70%, close 41%, cash collected 82%, units 56 to 93 a month.

**The diagnostic sale (10x recurring revenue)**
- At a 14-location chain (grown to 32), Alex rewrote the sales process in four hours. Average revenue per customer went from $200 to $800, and monthly revenue on that service line went from about $20,000 to over $250,000.
- The seven steps:
  1. Pre-sale questionnaire (agitates the problem).
  2. Collect info and a credit card as "profile setup."
  3. Current state.
  4. Desired state.
  5. The obstacle between them.
  6. Present the price tied to the goal and your vehicle (weight loss: 60 lb ÷ 1.5 lb a week = 45 weeks at $99/week = $4,500, with a guarantee).
  7. Incentivize prepayment.
- Principle: sell the goal, not the membership. Let the customer set the goal (the yogurt-scale analogy).
- Pricing steps (present the highest price first, then step down):
  - Anchor at the full price of $4,500.
  - Prepay discount of 20% ($3,600).
  - Half down for 10% off ($4,050).
  - Third-party financing.
  - Payment plan.
  - Final fallback: "let's book your next session."
  - Presenting full price first, then discounts, beats starting low and adding interest.
- Say "prepayment discount" and not "paid in full," because "paid in full" benefits the seller.
- Upsells should cost about 5x the base price. If 20% buy a $5,000 upsell on a $1,000 product, average ticket doubles.
- Recurring tricks: a friend replaced $300/month with a $10,000 program on a 36-month plan, and customers stayed 8 months instead of 3. Another friend ended a payment plan with an automatic recurring charge at the same price.
- Secret shop your own business. Alex found the chain skipped the pre-sale questionnaire and credit card, and never asked where customers wanted to be.
- Results: recurring revenue share from 9% to 60% of that service line, memberships 0 to 30%, visits from 1 to 4-6.
