## Why Alex left a working gym business: his Gym Launch origin story

- Alex graduated from Vanderbilt in three years, then spent two years in defense-contracting management consulting. He decided no job paid what he wanted except investment banking and consulting, and those take about 20 years to get there.
- At 23 he skipped business school (he couldn't answer "how will this MBA help my goals?") and put about $60K of savings into a business instead.
- He considered a test prep business, a frozen yogurt store, and a gym. He picked the gym because yogurt cost too much and the test prep partnership fell through.
- To learn the business he emailed roughly 40 gym owners offering to work free. One replied. He sold his condo, drove 36 hours from Baltimore to California, and apprenticed under that owner for 3 months.
- The mentor started work at 4:00 a.m. Alex kept the habit of doing high-quality work before most people wake up.
- He explains his career jumps this way: "each time it was by giving up something that everyone else told me was really good to have something that was better."

## The gym offer: sell the result, charge upfront, guarantee it

- Most gyms "sell your soul" on the front end, giving away as much as possible to attract less committed people. Alex flipped it: charge when people are most excited.
- He sold a $500 defined program instead of a membership, because "no one really wants a membership. They just want like a result."
- The guarantee was "lose 20 pounds and you get all the money back." About 78% of people who started hit the goal.
- Three components were pitched: fitness (3 sessions a week with a trainer), nutrition (a personalized plan with grocery lists, prep and eating-out guides), and accountability (peers, alumni, and coaches).
- Supplements were sold 48 hours later and were non-refundable, so they covered the cost even if a client refunded.
- At about 3 weeks, he converted people to a year, with a paid-in-full discount, crediting the $500 toward staying. He frames it as "this is not about 6 weeks, it's about 6 years."
- The cash flow math: $2 leads, 1 in 5 close, so $10 acquisition cost. Then $500, plus $250 of supplements, plus about $1,000 for the year paid in full. He recouped acquisition cost and made money before any backend, so opening more gyms was the logical growth move.
- Front-end and backend cash flow ended up roughly 50/50.
- Facebook ads came from a $3,000 marketing event in 2013. He learned enough there to run ads for his partner's gym and "we killed it."

## Scaling gyms and the first-month survival story

- His partner couldn't fund half the lease the night before signing, so Alex took the whole gym. It cost $50K of his $60K.
- He slept in the gym because he couldn't afford two rents. Month one revenue was exactly $4,973, the rent amount. Then roughly $10K, $15K, $20K, $20K, up to about $35K a month.
- By about month nine it was fully outsourced to a manager and trainers.
- He opened a new location every 6 months and reached six gyms, planning 10 under a "United Fitness" brand.
- He took no dividends and put all cash flow into new gyms. He sold all six for roughly $300K total. He now says he should have waited about a year to clean up the books, but he prioritizes opportunity cost. Within 30 days he was making $100K a month in the next business.

## The pivot: "level 10 skill set and a level two opportunity"

- At a ClickFunnels mastermind (about $25K), Alex was the only brick-and-mortar owner. He presented his model: customer acquisition, LTV, cost to open a gym, and gyms openable per month.
- Russell Brunson said: "I think you have a level 10 skill set and a level two opportunity," and told him to teach others the model.
- Alex sold all his gyms within 90 days.
- He considered franchise, license, or private ownership. A friend said franchising is slow and litigious, so he tried a license-like version first: Gym Launch.
- First version: he flew to other people's gyms, ran the ads, kept all the cash, and gave them about 200 free customers to convert onto membership.
- It failed because the gyms were weak operators. A gym with 70 members went to 270 in 30 days without systems or trainers. Alex carried the processing risk, and he had about $150,000 in refunds in a week.
- The turn: his now-wife Leila's small online training business gave him the idea to sell fitness directly. They hit $1,000 a day within 14 days.
- One gym owner begged him to still launch. Alex instead offered to sell him the system for $6,000 (the highest number he could imagine then). The owner said "Done." The next paid $8,000, and six more calls produced about $60K that day.
- He then called back 30-40 turnaround gyms and made about $300K more. Lesson: "the cost of making the thing once versus selling it 100 times was the same." That's leverage with no operational drag.
- He says acquisition has never been the bottleneck in any of his businesses; that's why the holding company is named Acquisition.com.

## Acquisition.com: what it does and how Alex runs it

- He calls it "value acceleration capital," the intersection of private equity, venture capital, and management consulting.
- He takes minority stakes in companies with $1M-$10M of EBITDA. Most do $5M-$25M revenue (minimum around $3M). The smallest at the time was $5M.
- Three steps:
  1. Fix data infrastructure (finances and reporting are usually a mess).
  2. Build a strategic plan, pick the two highest-leverage things, and "ruthlessly focus" on those only.
  3. Recruit talent, since they are very good at it.
- Recruiting framework: a business has two mirrored pipelines. Customers flow in (attention, leads, prospects, customers, ascension). Talent also flows in (marketing to get applications, interviewing as the sale, onboarding, ascension).
- Entrepreneurs under about $3M-$5M usually need to learn more. Past that, a company that is a subset of the founder's knowledge is capped. It's better to have 10 good brains, so bring in a finance director, controller, recruiter, and so on, moving from "the genius with a thousand hands" to a company.
- The mission is to document and share best practices of building world-class companies. He plans minority deals for at least 5 years to prove track record, then possibly majority buyouts. He doesn't plan to raise outside money and doesn't intend to sell.
- He sold Gym Launch, Prestige Labs, and Allen (software) majority stakes in one year, plus a house and cars, to focus on one thing.

## Money, wealth, and capital allocation

- Alex states he had about $60M in liquid-ish assets (not counting equity in his operating companies), and was probably over $100M including them. His path to a billion is equity appreciation, not index funds.
- Roughly 25% is in cash. He is considering a dividend ETF and borrowing against it to fund deals.
- Rest: index funds and large real estate buildings, in a few multi-million allocations. His $5M minimum chunk was set after $1.5M across 8 deals felt not worth the paperwork.
- On real estate, a mentor who sold a company for $3.6B told him to take GP participation proportional to his capital. Alex funds whole deals for a few operators he trusts, so there are no LPs and he also gets the GP share.
- Debt view: "debt increases risk." His marginal utility of more money is low and the cost of going to zero is high, so he doesn't risk what he needs for what he doesn't.
- On expensive purchases and coaching, he judges price relative to income and upside. He paid Grant Cardone $120K-$130K (his wife's gift, about $30K per call) and says he got it back "in five seconds." He thinks mentors should be about 2 steps ahead, not 10; the leverage must be usable at your size.
- He values education and belief-breaking above objects. He avoids status purchases and minimizes the mental headspace his assets take.

## Advice for a media creator: monetize the audience you have

Alex advised Graham Stephan and the hosts, who had a large audience and small ad revenue.

- Theory of constraints: find what's actually stopping the next level and work on only that. Their constraint wasn't eyeballs; it was monetization. "You're working on the wrong side of the bridge."
- Ad revenue and courses are weak vehicles. Hosts reported about $10K of ad revenue in the last 28 days from the podcast. Alex said with that audience he would have a billion-dollar thing.
- Options he gave:
  - Look at what sponsors sell on your channel and do the best one yourself. Sponsors get 10x their money, which is why they keep buying.
  - Take equity instead of cash for promotion. He suggests simply drawing the line: no promotion without equity. (The hosts said the good companies refuse and the willing ones were overpriced.)
  - Syndication: raise the money from your audience, partner with a full-time operator, and have an experienced investor. Typical fund roles are raising the money, doing the investing, and operating. He saw this as the quickest route to $100M, citing Grant Cardone.
  - Add a sales team and phone system to the Teachable offer. He thinks that could take it to $1M-$3M a month.
- Product criteria: he wants things that can sell for 5-10x the cost of goods. His shorthand for a business: "unique, expensive, sticky air": something only you can sell, premium priced, near-zero cost, and repeat purchase. He pairs it with a manager with a hardworking, impeccable, sharp, owner-minded profile. His example for Mr. Beast was an energy drink ("Beast Mode").
- On "half measures": don't mix two priorities (e.g., dream house plus good investment). Decide whether a project is for YouTube or for yourself and optimize for that.

## Work, relationships, and operating rhythm

- Schedule: asleep around 10, up at 4:00-5:00 a.m. with no alarm. He does deep work roughly 4/5 a.m. to 11 a.m., then meetings from about 11 to 3/4.
- He works because he enjoys it and says he works to have the option to work. In 2021 he did almost nothing after owning all the companies, and it was miserable because he couldn't spend the money. Returning to building Acquisition.com restored his joy.
- He says "work-life balance" criticism is a belief, not a fact: optimize for what stimulates you and adjust if you trade off something you don't want to.
- On marriage: he has seen two durable dynamics: partners in the game together, or cheerleader and quarterback. His own is the first, and couples who work together can make about five times more money. His fix for becoming too familiar is separate sides of the house and separate meetings so they have things to tell each other at dinner.
- His branding tip, from Dan Kennedy, is that recognizable facial features are remembered, which is how the mustache stuck. It was listed as intellectual property in the business sale.
