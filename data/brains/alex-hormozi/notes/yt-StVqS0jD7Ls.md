## Sales multipliers: sell seven days a week and book weekends

- Alex has personally closed 4,000 sales over 13 years. He says the biggest gains come from what happens around the sale, not on the call itself. "These are all the things that happen outside of the sale that affect your sales more than anything that happens inside the sale."
- Selling 7 days a week adds 2 days x 52 weeks = 104 selling days a year, a 29% increase over a 5-day week.
- Many prospects are only free outside 9 to 5. In fitness, Alex closed all his sales between 4:00 and 8:00 p.m.
- Skipping weekends stacks Monday with appointments that no-show or reschedule. People click the ad and book on Saturday and Sunday, so give them same-day or next-day slots then.
- In his portfolio companies, many people schedule on Saturdays and Sundays for Monday. Weekend bookings for the weekend also show up at higher rates.
- He cites two data sources: a Harvard Business Review case study across large industries, and Acquisition.com's software company Allen (4,000+ appointments per day), where click-to-close data was visible.
- At Allen, opening up calendar availability was the strongest correlate of overall throughput, more than a 10 to 20% lift in closing percentage. Closing rate can't realistically rise 400%, but these multipliers can.
- Policy: same day or next day only, because show rates are higher. If that leaves appointments unbooked, "you need to hire more sales people".

## Speed to lead and "call now"

- Contacting a lead within 60 seconds raises the likelihood of closing by 391%. Alex says that alone could 4X a business.
- Beyond 5 minutes, close likelihood drops by 80%, for example from 4 in 5 to 20%. He argues "lead quality is going down" is usually really about how well leads are worked.
- 50% of prospects go with the business that responds first, not the best one. His example: Lila called salons asking "are you available right now?" and booked the one that said yes.
- People have "huge motivation for a tiny period of time". Speed of response also signals how buttoned up the business is. A call within 5 seconds of opting in makes a strong first impression.
- Add a "call now" option on the scheduler. If qualification matters, show it only after the lead is qualified.
- If you lack the sales capacity, hire one more rep when the revenue lift (double or triple) is far bigger than the rep's cost.

## Scheduling design, off-the-call SOP and show rates

- Alex's team replaced 1-hour slots with 15-minute start increments. A call blocks the next three blocks, then another rep's booking can start. Allen saw higher schedule and show rates because people pick the time that is precisely convenient for them.
- Don't fake busyness by hiding slots. "It doesn't do anything... it just makes it less convenient."
- Awkward gaps between calls are handled by hiring more reps and by an off-the-call SOP (most teams only have an on-the-call one).
- Off-the-call SOP: call booked leads, qualify them, then say "I had an opening later today, do you want the slot?" This turns a three-day-out appointment into a same-day one with a much higher show rate. If they can't move it, you have still contacted them within minutes. It also raises rep utilization and frees a future slot.
- Show rate vs close rate: a 20% lift in show rate equals a 20% lift in close rate, and it is easier if reps already close at 35%. Alex shouts out the closers with the highest show rate every week, optionally with a flat $500 weekly bonus. He says this can lift sales more than sales training.
- Reminder sequence (from Allen): 24 hours before, the morning of, and 1 hour before.
  - Automate the early reminders but don't pretend automation is a person.
  - Add manual ones layered on top. At 24 hours, show prep, for example "I looked over your site, we found three things we can help with", or "I set aside a t-shirt, what size?" for a weight loss customer. Five minutes of prep can lift show rate by 30%.
  - Prefer blue iMessages over green texts, and use voice memos and video.

## Feed the killers: lead allocation

- Alex's rule: the best leads go to the best closers, and the worst leads go to the worst closers. This minimizes net waste.
- Story: a time-share rep in his building was the top rep out of 3,000, winning five of six years, and earned about $3 million a year. His prize for winning was an hour with the CEO. He used it to argue against round-robin lead distribution. The CEO tested it at his location and his income went from $250,000 to $3 million a year. Rolled out nationwide, it 5x'd the company.
- Fringe benefit: reps will take below-market pay for the shot at being top dog.
- New reps learn on the hardest leads and earn better leads by surviving.
- Lead scoring (greens, yellows, reds) supports this. New reps should "be a trashman" and take as many hard leads as the company allows, treating "yellow equals gold" as free training. Closing the hardest prospects makes easy ones easier. He adds: if someone truly can't benefit, don't sell it.

## Sales training: drills, game tape and culture

- Most orgs run churn factories. If you can train, you can pay below market, because you turn unskilled hires into good closers. That is the business owner's arbitrage.
- Daily huddles are role plays. Set the frame first: "no one's perfect, that's why we practice". Leaders must drop their ego.
- Give feedback one piece at a time, immediately, and in real time. Say "great, keep going" during the role play, and warn reps you will stop them 30 to 40 times.
- Once they get a line right, have them repeat it 2 to 6 times in quick succession to drill it in. Focus on a single skill for a week or two.
- 1-on-1s: the manager listens to calls, finds all 28 mistakes, then picks the one that most lifts close rate. This is why training is a full-time job. Examples: asking for the card, setting the agenda, losing the frame, zooming out.
- The best closer is usually not the best sales manager (hunter vs patient leader). Coaches like Phil Jackson need not out-play Michael Jordan. Tell the team "I hope you're better than me at sales".
- Competition: make it "us versus them", with a big enemy or rival, rather than rep vs rep. Internal one-upmanship costs closers who thrive in teams.
- Record every call across the customer lifecycle. Run a weekly game tape review with sales and customer success together. They review sales calls and onboarding calls so sales sees what happens after close.
  - This reduced CS resentment at over-promising. CS sometimes offered extra features sales hadn't known about.
  - It cut cold-feet drop-offs of 5% to 10% and improved sales conviction.
  - Alex copied this from software companies when he started Allen.
- When a closer is "hot", record and study those calls: tone, opener, transition to the close, how objections are handled early. "Good closers try and get hot, great closers never get cold."
- In a slump, read testimonials to the team and credit the closer who closed that customer. Conviction fixes tone better than drills. At his gyms, close rates were absurdly high on weigh-out day because prospects had just watched 5 to 20 people show results.
- Sales is "passing conviction from one person to another... over a bridge of trust". If the prospect knew what you know and wouldn't buy, but you got them to, you deceived them.

## Before the sale: pre-framing, qualification and scripts

- The sale starts at the click. The ad, the setting script and the questions asked all influence the buy, so build a seamless click-to-close experience.
- Setter script (local business): "Hey John, question..." then pause. "It's Alex, you opted in on Facebook for our promotion... I wanted to make sure you weren't a crazy person on the internet." Give three mental cues about what they opted into, since people forget. Offer two time options ("2:00 and 4:00 today, which works better?").
- Edify the closer. A setter says "Sean has helped 400 people just like you... he has an opening this afternoon, let me confirm". It beats "someone will reach out". Edify the business too, for example with a "best of" list that, per Alex, usually costs about five grand to be on.
- Alex admits that early on he pretended his gym had multiple employees. He calls that a white lie and says it was deceiving, so don't do it.
- Qualify with BANT (from an IBM study): Budget, Authority, Need, Timing. Confirm it on the application, the call and again before asking for money.
- Outbound hook: "Hey, I'm calling about [competitor]. Give me a call back." It works by email and voicemail, and curiosity drives response.
- Prep for five minutes before each call. Notes must be in the CRM so the prospect never repeats themselves. Repeating back what they told you helps the sale, and making them repeat it hurts.
- Energy: Alex had a mini trampoline in his sales room. He did 20 to 25 in-person consults a day, up to 48 in a 12-hour day, and jumped between them. "You can't be in a bad mood and jumping on a trampoline."

## The call itself: agenda, script adherence and the CLOSER framework

- Open with proof, promise, plan, in 15 to 20 seconds, the way he opens content. Example: what you're here to fix, "we've done this with lots of people like you", the process, and "at the end we'll see if it makes sense; if not, no sweat". A banker's "can we skip the foreplay" is the model. His rationale: controlling the frame shows professionalism.
- BF Skinner analogy: control every variable and you could close every prospect, like salting a horse's mouth.
- Follow the script. Script adherence is the first drill. "If everyone follows the script and doesn't close you have a script problem."
  - The script runs up to the ask, and the first two-thirds should be near word for word. After the ask, objection loops branch.
  - Script the tone: emphasis (where you pause), tone (up or down, a question vs a statement), and pacing (slow for key points).
  - Use Google Docs formatting as cues: underline = slow down, caps = emphasize, italics = create space.
  - Keep it to about 12 questions.
- CLOSER framework: Clarify why they're here, Label the problem (the gap between present and desired), Overview past pain, Sell the vacation, Explain away concerns, Reinforce the decision.
- Clarify by reminding them of the action they took (for example "what made you set a call on my calendar?"). People don't want information, they want a problem solved.
- Pain cycle: "the pain is the pitch." Deprivation creates motivation. Quantify time, money and effort spent so far, and the daily cost of inaction. Anything they tried before is an advantage because it failed. Example: "I already have a personal trainer." "Yeah, he's obviously not doing a good job, but... you're here."
- Three-pillar pitch: chunk the offer into three parts, because people think in threes. Weight loss: fitness, nutrition, accountability. Lead gen for mortgage brokers and Realtors: timely, qualified, exclusive. Keep it under 2 minutes and spend about 90% of effort understanding the prospect's pain, not the product.
- Sell the vacation (Maui), not the plane ride (TSA, shoes, security). State the pillar, then give a metaphor. Examples:
  - Three-legged stool.
  - Toothbrush: as a kid you needed a parent's external accountability, and later it became internal habit.
  - The Amazing Race couple who hired a local in the back seat.
  - SEO vs ads as an investment account vs a paycheck.
- Ghosted prospects: send a meme (the Kevin Hart "where you at" meme). He says it has the highest response rate of any message they've sent; sent to 200 cold leads it re-engages roughly 5 to 10.

## Closing and objection handling

- Train sales back to front. Teach payment collection first, then asking, then the top of the script. "Closing begins once someone says no." No is to be expected.
- When they say yes, shut up and take payment ("don't sell yourself out of a sale").
- If an objection surprises you, you're underprepared. Use AAA: Acknowledge (repeat their last phrase, which buys time and builds rapport), Associate (a positive, or someone like them who got results, or an authority such as a senior engineer), then Ask the next question.
  - Associations act as foils so you can say harsh truths without attacking the prospect: "Someone earlier had the same issue; can I share what I told them?"
  - Get permission first if you hold authority ("can I be a coach instead of a friend?").
- Core laws: "He who asks the most times gets the most sales." The person asking questions is in control. Stay curious ("huh, that's interesting"), don't disagree with prospects, and be "like smoke". You can agree with the reason behind a statement. "Be right, not rich."
- Rapid-fire stall questions, in order:
  - "What's your main concern?"
  - Later in the sale, "What are you afraid of having happen?"
  - "What variables are you using to decide?"
  - "What makes this a yes?"
  - "What makes this a no?"
- All-purpose closes:
  - "Do you think [their objection] is the reason you haven't succeeded so far?"
  - The 1-to-10 close: "What would make it a 10?" If they say 6, ask "why not a 1?", then "are we feeling tennish?"
  - Best case / worst case (strong with a guarantee): both options are risk-free.
  - When lost in the details, say "big picture" and zoom out.
- Details are death traps. Never give a blind answer.
  - Ask a question about their question: "Which certifications are you looking for?"
  - Chunk up to the reason behind the detail, then offer your answer.
  - Use the mechanic/secretary close ("I can't ethically answer before looking under the hood").
  - A detail objection is a preference obstacle: they want your results their way. "You change the variables, you change the outcome."
- "Kill zombies early." Handle BANT items (decision makers, timing, budget) before asking for money, while you are still a guide on the same side of the table. When in doubt, repeat it back.
- Stack closes from different angles on the same obstacle. For "no time": cut 90% of what isn't working, it's a prioritization question, and "will you ever not be busy again?" Don't memorize closes; understand the theory behind each type:
  - Time: it's priorities.
  - Decision maker: support, not permission.
  - Details: preferences.
  - Money: value and resourcefulness, since a Ferrari for five grand would get funded.
  - Stalls: decision avoidance (fear, or not knowing how to decide).
  - Keep only 2 to 3 closes per obstacle.

## Specific overcomes and handling price

- Cheap competitor: "If ours was the same price as theirs, which would you choose?" Let them list why you're better.
- ID close (in person): ask for ID first. The wallet comes out, the "forgot my card" excuse dies, and you can verify the ZIP code. On Zoom, ask them to hold ID up to the camera.
- Decision maker, in four steps:
  - Ask "what would happen if they said no?" About 1 in 3 say "I'd do it anyway".
  - Ask what the partner's main concern would be, and isolate it.
  - Use past agreements. If they already know about your pain, why wouldn't they approve fixing it?
  - Last resort: support, not permission. Involve spouses or partners earlier for expensive purchases.
- "Give me some time" is decision avoidance. For a "let me circle back in a couple of quarters", ask "what will you do instead?", then "will it make you more than this?", then put math on it. Example: a 30% conversion lift with 90% gross margin and 30% net margin could double profit. If something else really is better for them, he says to let them go.
- No guarantee: the anti-guarantee. "If you're the type who needs a guarantee, this isn't for you", since you can't control what they do.
- "Let me use your free stuff first": agree and say "do that; either you succeed and hit a new problem I can solve, or you fail and I can help."
- Enterprise and government sales, with 6 to 12 month cycles and committees, don't fit this transactional approach. There, the number of contacts between both teams raises the chance to close.
- "Wow, that's expensive" is an observation, not a no. Don't waste bullets on zombies that aren't there.
- Never change price to close. Test prices ahead of time, premeditated. Change terms (payment plan) or components (a feature downsell) instead.
- Reply to "can you do it for less?" with "we could do it for more." Alex says it has never failed him. It counter-anchors, creates fear of missing out, and signals buying intent.
- Reinforce the decision. After payment, do a handshake handoff to customer success, not a hand-off. Introduce the CS person with edification, then restate the proof, promise and plan personalized to the customer's pains.
- BAMFAM (book a meeting from a meeting): every customer knows when and with whom the next meeting is, and that person knows the whole history. This cuts back-outs, cold feet and 30-day refunds.

## Sales team, hiring, comp and mindset

- Hiring:
  - Resumes don't matter for transactional sales. Twice his teams couldn't scale because they screened resumes heavily. Alex prefers group interviews with 1 to 2 minute turns: stars speak up, communicate clearly and encourage nervous peers.
  - Hire for the lowest skill deficiency, not "attitude over aptitude". Soft traits bundle many skills (kindness may be about 20), and training the script takes only 2 to 3 weeks. This flips for rare technical expertise, and matters less for lower-level roles.
- Compensation recruits but doesn't retain or change behavior. The one exception is paying on cash collected, which gets more cash collected. Fast, low-intensity feedback loops change behavior more. Tip: a Zap from the CRM to Slack for submitted, processed sales, so Slack kudos reflect real closes.
- Sales and marketing are one continuum. He puts them under one revenue leader (a CRO) and says conflict disappeared. Sales is personalized marketing: if marketing covers pieces 1, 2, 3, sales covers 4, 5, 6.
- Trust and belief are continuums, not binary. A bigger purchase needs a longer runway, like a bigger plane. If you need 3 to 5 calls to close, pre-market and educate more so the first call starts near the 5-yard line.
- Mindset as behaviors:
  - The person who cares most about the prospect wins the sale. Turn away people who aren't a fit. Be "kind, not nice", and ask the hard questions. Avoid "commission breath".
  - Volume negates luck. Do double the best closer's effort (the Kobe Bryant model). Skill rises slowly while income spikes at mastery, so don't quit in the early dip. Alex did not close his first sale.
  - Aim for prospects to talk about two-thirds of the call. Let silence sit.
  - "Don't be cute": run the fundamentals every time (from a Long Island serial private-equity CEO's backyard-football analogy: "two fat guys in the middle run to the right"). Great closers always prep, hand off warmly, check notes, and set the agenda.
  - "No for now" is not "no forever". Pulse-check every 3 to 6 months, with memes or short emails.
  - People want to buy. Give them the excuse to justify it.
