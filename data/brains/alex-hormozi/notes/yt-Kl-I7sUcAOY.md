## Fear, shame and the decision to start: "play it out" and guaranteed bad vs. a chance at good

- Alex says people from zero to one think they need a tactic, but what they need is "the courage to be willing to be wrong" and to take shame by failing in front of people whose opinions they care about.
- His own path: white-collar consultant at 22 with a GMAT above Harvard's mid score and a clear path (top business school, consulting or banking, private equity). He left the state and drove halfway across the country before telling his father, then took a minimum-wage personal trainer job cleaning gym floors to learn the gym business.
- He says the decision to leave was the hardest of his career, and that in about 90% of universes he is "just a consultant somewhere."
- Fear is a label problem. He argues you are only a coward if you let fear change your behavior in a way that moves you away from what you want.
- Quit math: 3-6 months of personal savings, plus a side business that has matched your job income for 3-6 months. He notes this is almost never the real reason people don't quit.
- His decision frame: "guaranteed bad, chance at good." He asks people to look at "the don't," the alternate path, which is a guaranteed outcome they don't want.
- Fear "exists in the vague, not in the specific." Play the worst case out step by step:
  - You get a story you can tell, and possibly a better job later.
  - The real loss is some savings.
  - Worst case you stay on a couch or at your parents' place.
  - The leftover cost is self-inflicted shame, which becomes funny later.
- Delay discounting works both ways: people say they'll quit tomorrow for 20 years, and they "massively discount what a whole lifetime of misery would be."

## Picking an idea: the three Ps, first principles and leverage

- Alex says business ideas come from one of three Ps, and you need only one:
  - Pain: a problem you've lived.
  - Profession: a skill from a past job, which is proven because the economy already pays for it.
  - Passion: something you'd spend time on anyway, such as model cars or Dungeons & Dragons.
- Pain gives deep prospect knowledge. His example is nasal products: he has tried every product since 8th grade, so he knows what's wrong with each. That story is more compelling to investors and customers than a feature list.
- Missionaries make more money than mercenaries, because they feel the problem. "What everyone wants to see is obsession."
- Anyone can break a business into fractional jobs (sales, marketing, customer success, support, design, web) and learn one to trade for money.
- The foundation is time. Everyone trades time for dollars, so the goal is the highest return on that time.
- He built Acquisition.com on two theses, shown in the logo as a fulcrum for leverage:
  - Supply and demand are the two foundations of business.
  - Leverage gets the most output from each input.
- Three levels of leverage:
  - Attract attention: one piece of content a million people see beats one-on-one outreach.
  - Convert attention: a checkout page or video sales letter beats a phone salesperson.
  - Deliver: software and media can be made once and sold a thousand times.
- With nothing, your assets are your brain, hands and time, so skill acquisition is the first move.
- On goal size: the bigger the goal, the narrower the path. $10M or $100M is possible in almost any business over a long enough timeline. Trillion-dollar goals need tech and AI. He says about $25M covers essentially any consumption.

## Standing out in 2025: courage to be yourself

- Using a new personal trainer as the example, Alex says not to try to out-science the PhD who is already jacked.
- Your fingerprint, life and experiences are unique: "you're not going to beat me at being me but you'll beat me at being you."
- His own brand mix is business, marketing and sales, plus fitness, philosophy, his wife Leila, and dry or dark humor. The proportions differ for each person.
- Most products and services are commoditized. Personality creates a brand premium, like Prime against equivalent drinks: "if I have two things that are basically equal and I just like this guy better," people vote with their dollars.
- Sequence for a trainer: long term is building content, short term is outreach to people you know with a compelling offer.
- Branding is a mosaic. Each piece of content is a tile, and the proportions of colors become the brand. Don't plan "one post about family, one about finances"; be yourself and the proportions shake out.
- Leila's advice: "never dilute yourself." There are more people who need the message than who hate it, and impact brings equal and opposing force.
- Being disliked is a fixed cost, so be disliked for being you. He cites the Nike ad he posted on LinkedIn: about 80% neutral or negative, 20% "that is me."
- He is fine changing his views as he learns. His first podcast, from July 2017, started 90 days after he lost everything, and episode 8 is called "stop branding."

## Hiring, people and the swamp

- Alex believes an organization's potential is tied to the combined intellectual horsepower inside it. The richest founders own small stakes: Jensen Huang about 4% of NVIDIA, Bezos 7-9% of Amazon, Musk about 20% of Tesla.
- He cites Keith Rabois's barrels and ammunition model: most people are ammunition, and the rate limiter is the number of barrels (people who can fire independently). He also cites the idea that the square root of headcount generates 50% of the work.
- Building a business is like a video game. Each new company you start, you move faster through the early levels, but each function has bosses (first sales hire, sales manager, marketing director). The goal is pattern recognition across every function.
- Standards rise with scale. A director of sales at $1-10M differs from one at $10-100M.
- If you don't know the job, do it yourself, then train with the 3 Ds: "document, demonstrate, duplicate." You document the steps, demonstrate them in front of the hire, then they do it in front of you. If you can't follow your own checklist, fix the checklist.
- A players hire A players, and a bad leader's rot can cost "three-quarters" of what's beneath them. Remove them fast; the pain of the fix "feels wrong but is right."
- Mental test: picture 5 versions of your best employee. If that would 10x the company, nothing else you're doing is likely worth more.
- Hiring mix (the "barbell"): young hungry people in learning mode, and late-career people who are there to share what they know. The middle, who want a tit-for-tat, are the biggest pain.
- Hire for the smallest skill deficiency. For low-skill roles, hire on attitude and train aptitude. For high-skill roles, hire the skill and teach the behavior.
- To lure someone exceptional, ask "what would it take?" He lists four elements of equity: control, risk, profits and sale value. Most hires want profits and upside, not risk or control.
- The swamp is roughly $1-3M in revenue. At $1M with 20% margin, an A player costs about 100% of profit. The choice is to sacrifice profit or work six more hours a day. "We have to be willing to make impossible choices."
- Example: a restoration company paid his aunt $60,000 a year only to call leads at once. It converted 55% of leads and brought in millions in revenue. He cites research that calling within 60 seconds can 4-5x lead conversion.
- To free up your time, log what you do in 15-minute increments for a week. Then hire for the roles taking 40% or 15% of your time. If you can't find a unicorn, hire three people for the pieces.

## Interviewing, feedback and culture

- To test candidates, present the actual problems of the role and ask how they would fix them. A good answer covers:
  - The quality and quantity of metrics they track.
  - The behaviors that move those metrics.
  - How those metrics and behaviors drive revenue.
- Beginners think in binary ("ads don't work"). Experts break a failure into parts (hook, click-through rate, traffic quality, offer clarity, page congruence). He keeps asking "what does that mean?" until the candidate reaches concrete actions.
- If they can't explain it simply, they don't understand it, or they are obscuring it. Ask support reps how their job makes the company money.
- Culture is "the rules that govern reinforcement": what you reward and punish. Not correcting a late arrival sets the rule that lateness is fine. He cites Drucker: culture beats strategy.
- Reinforce immediately, as with training a dog. In script training he interrupts about 30 times in the first 30 minutes, covering a third of the script, with a repeat-until-right cycle.
- "Kind, not nice": not giving feedback is unkind.
- Criticism is a gap between actual and desired. An insult is the judgment attached to it. Use stop / start / keep, with a condition and a behavior.
- Example: a high-performing director people called a "dick" changed in a week with three or four specific behaviors (don't interrupt, don't give unsolicited advice).
- Replace "lazy" with data: slow Slack replies, incomplete projects, a messy background. Then set rules such as a 10-minute reply window during work hours.
- The ability to train people from B to A+ is large "Alpha," because you keep the gap between their pay and their output.

## Staying focused: the entrepreneur life cycle and the 10-year slug

- The six stages (a loop he calls "the entrepreneur life cycle"):
  - Uninformed optimism.
  - Informed pessimism.
  - Crisis of meaning, or valley of despair.
  - Then either restart at stage one, living "the same six months for 20 straight years," or continue to informed optimism.
  - Success.
- The "woman in the red dress" is the Matrix metaphor for a shiny new business that is actually an agent trying to pull you off the path.
- His former CFO (four companies from $0 to $100M, over 40 M&A deals, one for $5B) told him "the grass is always greener... you just don't know that it's fertilized with [expletive]."
- 2024 was the first year he had no FOMO. He measures focus by the quantity and quality of things you say no to, and defines commitment as "the elimination of alternatives."
- Doing three things is "an exercise in arrogance and ego." Splitting attention caused his biggest mistakes. Bolting an e-commerce business onto his licensing company slowed revenue growth. At one point he ran Gym Launch, six gyms, and chiropractor and dental agencies. He distinguishes being an owner from being a CEO.
- Compare year 3 of your current thing against year 0 of a new one, not year 0 against year 0.
- Timeline: about 5 years to find which way is north, another 5 to build something that creates generational wealth. Most multibillion companies reach big numbers between years 6 and 10.
- After quitting a job you will work more and earn less for a long time. The first-dollar rush reinforces quitting, which he calls the lesson to "immediately unlearn."
- Entrepreneurs mostly fizzle rather than quit. He notes the "currency" of hardship changes at each level. Quoting Elon Musk, running a business is "staring out to the abyss and chewing glass."
- Your hypothesis is likely wrong from the start. Experts diagnose the constraint. His example is Facebook's fix of "10 friends in 14 days."
- Entrepreneurship is a game to be successful, not to be right. That can mean stepping aside as CEO.

## Growth mechanics: volume, testing, allocation and learning

- Do more first. His mentor's gym flyer test size was 5,000, and then 5,000 a day. Alex put out 300 and called it failed. Low volume reads as volatility.
- Alex's team publishes 450 pieces of content a week. Most people post one or two and wonder why they lag.
- The Core Four to advertise: post content, run ads, cold outreach, and warm outreach.
- The 70/20/10 allocation (Google): 70% to core, 20% to adjacent, 10% to moonshots. Don't pull the stars off the core to build the new thing.
- Three strategic buckets:
  - Get more customers.
  - Increase lifetime gross profit per customer.
  - Decrease risk.
- Strategy is prioritization of unlimited opportunities against limited resources.
- Two question types: ones a spreadsheet can answer (LTV, CAC), and ones testing can answer.
- He tested book titles the same way ("$100M Leads" kept winning) and tested the cover and subheadline too. People want "the hole in the wall, not the drill."
- Market-expansion hack: spend $1,000 running your best promotion in five candidate markets, compare lead cost, and open in the cheapest one.
- The Four Rs of customer success: retain, review, refer, resell.
- Fast skill learning:
  - Do 100 reps.
  - Study the top 10%.
  - Add what they share to the next 100.
  - Repeat, as in Chris Rock's comedy sets.
- Consulting-style rapid learning: interview five experts, ask each for five more, categorize the notes, distill the truths, and infer next steps. Expert interviews cut through information overload.
- "Models, not mentors": you don't need one, but learn from people ahead of you. He drove across the country to seven-figure Sam's gym. A meetup of gym owners saved him years on equipment, square footage and rent. His rent limit was no more than $1.50 a foot.
- The value of a skill is the delta: $1M a year minus $50,000 means not knowing it costs $950,000 a year.
- Procedural knowledge (doing) beats declarative knowledge (knowing about). Be a practitioner, not a parrot. If you can't explain why you believe something, "it's not your belief, it's someone else's." Aphorisms can't be re-derived into the full reasoning.
- YouTube opening formula: proof, promise, plan, then picture and pain. He says this nuance separates a 10,000-view video from a million-view video.
- On copycats: if a thumbnail or format is all that makes you special, "we're [expletive] anyway." The 99% below the surface is culture and the next step competitors can't see. Copying means you are second.
