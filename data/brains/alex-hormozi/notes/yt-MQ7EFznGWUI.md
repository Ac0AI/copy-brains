## Closing is a decision, not a sale: Alex's sales philosophy

- Alex learned sales by doing, not studying. He took roughly 4,000 sales before he found out sales training existed. He ran Facebook ads from 2013, got cheap leads, and for four to five years took about 20 in-person consults a day at his gyms.
- His team would stack a week of sales appointments for him. He would do about 25 sales in one location in a day, then move to the next.
- In gym turnarounds (33 over two years) he sat at a fold-out table at the front door with pens and contracts, booked an appointment every 30 minutes, and ran the same pitch repeatedly. He averaged about $100K in cash collected in 21 days. Ads returned about 30 to 1, and he spent $1,000 to $10,000 on them.
- He defines the end point of a sale as "a decision not a sale". If he does not think the product is right for the prospect, he tells his reps to push the prospect away. That way a rep wins every conversation.
- His belief: "the person who cares more about the prospect wins". He says the best frame is to keep the human first.
- He says he has no secret closing line. The close is easy when everything before it was done right. Like a lawyer, he does not ask a question he doesn't already know the answer to.

## The CLOSER framework and why Alex deletes from scripts

- **C**larify why they are there. **L**abel the problem so you can solve it. **O**verview their past experiences. **S**ell the vacation, usually as three main points. **E**xplain away concerns. **R**einforce the decision.
- "Sell the vacation, not the plane flight." Prospects don't care about TSA or the flight. They want to hear about Maui.
- Reinforce the decision with clear next steps right after the yes. This prevents cold feet, chargebacks and refunds.
- His training for gym reps was only about 60 minutes. The goal was closing, not selling sales training.
- Alex's team compresses scripts and deletes as much as possible. He cites the Basecamp founder's shoe-store story: customers only want to know whether the shoe is comfortable, looks good and is affordable. Salespeople usually talk themselves out of sales.
- His rule: "every time you make a statement you give the other person something to disagree with". So he asks questions instead.
- Example: asked "are your trainers certified?", the rep replies "what certification are you looking for?" and then "what would that accomplish for you?"
- He recently bought a company and built a 40-person sales team from zero in 90 days. The team calls buyers from an automated e-commerce front end and sells them an ascension offer.
- His team sayings: "Simple scales, fancy fails." "There is no such thing as advanced. Advanced salespeople never don't do the basics."

## Handling objections: curiosity, not combat

- Because he has had the same conversation more times than anyone, he says no objection should be new. Typical ones: "I have to think about it", "talk to my spouse", "too expensive", "will it work for my body type".
- He calls overcoming objections "a dance, not a fight". Ask genuine questions, be curious, and show no aggression.
- Use a mental cue. He says "huh" to himself whenever he feels adrenaline or the urge to push. This avoids "commission breath", where the rep's urgency makes the prospect defensive.
- Do the opposite of what the prospect expects. A prospect who says "I don't have the money" expects a pressure response. Agree and smile instead. Then ask:
  - "What's your main concern?"
  - "What would make you feel 100%?"
  - Make them name the criteria, then resolve it on the spot.
- Own the negatives first, in the way Eminem does in rap battles: "Yes, it's expensive, it's going to be hard, you should still do it."
- On money objections: "You're going to vote with your dollars. What are you voting for?" He also asks "how long do you want this to be a problem in your life?"
- Use a story of someone just like them who walked out the door and decided to do it anyway (his example: Susan, a single mom with four kids who drove Uber to afford it and lost 60 pounds).
- On "I have to talk to my spouse": ask what happens if the spouse says no, and what happens if nothing changes in five years.
- Sell from the back foot, never leaning in. "If you want it, I'm here." Set the frame that you are happy to exit.
- His belief: "If you don't close a sale, it means you didn't understand the prospect enough."
- Frame-setting on follow-ups: after a no-show, use a light, humorous line and go straight back into the meeting. Alex learned this from his gym mentor Sam, who said "I know you ain't about to leave my gym without putting your weights back." Alex would say "I know you didn't just ghost me, I'm heartbroken... I'm sure something came up. Same time tomorrow?"
- For internet leads, he opened by establishing who he was ("a real person, across from the 7-Eleven"), then asked how they heard about the gym, naming a couple of awards to signal credibility. He then asked why they signed up and offered a specific appointment time.

## Raising show rate and schedule rate

- Alex owned Aleen (a scheduling software) and saw about 4,000 appointments a day, with two data scientists testing throughput variables. He says fixing show-up rate can make a company more money than improving close rate.
- Number one lever: availability. Offer more time slots per day, seven days a week. Owners who only take appointments Tuesday and Thursday 7 to 9am lose leads. His example: his wife Leila booked the spa that could see her right now.
- Number two: shorter increments. Use 15-minute slots instead of 60-minute ones. An odd start time such as 3:05 signals detail and gets higher on-time rates.
- Number three: drag appointments forward. Tell the lead "I just had a 4:00 cancel, I can pull you up". Same-day show rates are far higher than appointments 2 to 4 days out. His team aims for same-day or next-day wherever possible.
- Reason: people get busy, and the outside world doesn't sell your product.

## Training, adherence and managing a sales team

- Belief: any skill can be trained by breaking it into sub-skills. The more skilled the person, the vaguer the instruction can be. How far to train is a question of how many resources the organization will invest.
- Alex defines learning as "same condition, new behavior". The phone rings or an objection arrives, and the rep does something different.
- Hire an exceptional sales director first. Alex describes them as a sales operator who runs culture and training, genuinely cares about people, repeats themselves, plays therapist when someone is in a cold streak, and role plays "until their eyes bleed".
- His first objective is adherence. "You follow the script, period." If a rep follows the script and the cadence and does not close, that rep still wins in his eyes. If the whole team fails, the script is wrong and that is on him. Most companies have 10 reps selling 10 different ways, so nothing can be improved.
- Training steps:
  - New reps shadow 60 to 100 hours of recorded calls from good reps. Alex shows positives only, so they learn what to do.
  - They take half or third schedules at first. Their first calls are reviewed with the manager.
  - The manager pauses the call and asks the rep what they could have done better, rather than telling them.
  - In weekly team game-tape sessions, the team comments on what went well first, and then the rep comments on what to improve.
  - The manager drills the weak part (rapport, softness on the close, the "I need more information" question) in a one-on-one, repeating the exact words until the rep says them naturally.
- Role play matters because it removes adrenaline. A rep who has heard an objection 100 times can be present with the person instead of searching for words.
- Rule: "Replicate before you iterate". A rep who wants to change the script must first hit the team standard. Then the change is tested on that one rep. If their close rate rises, it goes team-wide. Most tests show no change, in which case the rep goes back to the script.
- Hire the first 3 to 5 salespeople carefully, because they set the culture. Alex defines culture as "the series of rules, spoken or unspoken". He wants killers and a culture where the team polices itself. One example is an 18-year-old who emailed every company employee a personalized cover letter and is in the office 6am to 6pm, seven days a week.
- Alex favors team incentives. If everyone hits a number, everyone gets a payout. Then reps help each other (drill Johnny until he is up) and the enemy is competitors, not teammates. He says his older approach was cutthroat and his thinking changed as teams grew.
- He says about 95% of organizations don't know how to train. They give new hires a script PDF, and a hire who closes was good beforehand, so the system gets no credit.
- Alex adds that hiring in batches of three makes it clearer whether the problem is the person or the script.

## Community product lessons from School

- Alex co-owns School (a community platform) with Sam. He wrote a large check after seeing the growth, referral rates and zero marketing spend.
- His principle: "100 golden BBs, no silver bullets". The product is hundreds of small details, such as how the green online indicator works across devices.
- The team came from Riot Games and gamified engagement. Points and leaderboards reward quality posts, which lifts the overall community. Discovery sends traffic to the best communities.
- A community is "fractionalized access", a small slice of the owner. Alex says the top reason people cancel is overwhelm, so what matters is "value per second, not seconds of value".
- Most people buy for one or two reasons, so offer fewer, better things. He once ran three calls a week and people said they were getting behind. He cut to one call a week plus one eight-hour training per month.
- Crowdsource value: a great member post can be tagged into the curriculum so it stays permanently.
- Email notifications to members are limited to once every three days so each one is worth opening.
- One super user said moving off Facebook let her cut about $400,000 a year in support costs, since every post gets 100% reach.
- In the School Games, the top-community-owner training (the eight-hour monthly session) unlocks at three community members. The Games are free for 14 days.
