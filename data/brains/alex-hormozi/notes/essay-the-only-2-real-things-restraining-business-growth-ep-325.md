## Constraints, not new businesses, are what stall growth

- Alex's rule: when a business plateaus, the cause is almost never the market. The only reasonable, "not your fault" reason is that you have saturated your total addressable market (TAM).
- His example of real saturation: a poodle-food seller who has reached 70% of poodle owners. Even then, the fix is to hop verticals (another dog type), not to start an unrelated business.
- His second example: a friend's company turns newspaper ads into digital products. Newspapers shrank 25% year over year for about 10 years, so the market is roughly 5% of its size a decade ago. The friend grew "at an inverse rate to the shrinkage of the industry."
- For almost everyone else, he says the reason is simpler: "you just don't know how."
- His default recommendation is to fix the existing business before adding another.

## Case: $550K/month clinic owner who wanted to start a new business

- A business owner doing about $550,000/month (clinics moved to telehealth during COVID, profits "exploded") reached out to join Acquisition.com. He had grown it to that level in two years.
- He planned to start a business teaching physicians to improve their practices, licensing his model the way Alex did with gym owners.
- Alex declined, for these reasons:
  - It is an established, saturated marketplace.
  - The owner's value proposition rested on a handful of examples, not deep personal experience.
  - It was not unique; plenty of others do the same thing.
  - It meant a new space, a new acquisition system and an undefined offer.
- He asked why the owner didn't take the $550K/month business to $5M/month instead. The honest answer: "I kind of got stuck and I didn't know how to grow it from here."
- Alex then asked if he would still want the physician business if the existing one tripled to $1.5M/month. The owner said no.
- Alex's summary of the owner's logic: "you got stuck in this existing business, you thought the solution would be, I will start another business." He calls this poor deductive reasoning, even from a smart operator, and says he does it too.
- The owner's constraint was acquisition. The product could not be marketed on Facebook, so the real question was: "How do I acquire more customers than I already am from a different channel that I'm allowed to?"

## Case: $4M/year weight-loss info-product seller planning product-line extensions

- A woman with a large following sold weight-loss programs and ebooks at about $4M/year. She planned an apparel line, a supplement line and a cream line.
- Alex told her it was a terrible idea and asked why she wouldn't take the $4M business to $10M.
- Her answer matched the clinic owner's: she didn't know how.
- His response: solve that problem instead of creating a new business, and put all effort into it.

## Case: $150K/month owner who needed a sales team

- A young owner doing about $150,000/month had many possible improvements: lifting the lead-to-sale number, changing the pricing model, fixing some messaging.
- Alex saw the one thing that mattered was that he had not scaled a sales team.
- When the owner came back two weeks later asking how to get the most from a call, Alex asked whether he had scaled the sales team. He hadn't, so Alex said "we don't really have anything to talk about," because "there's nothing else that matters right now besides this."
- It took two or three repetitions before the owner accepted that nothing else would be discussed.
- Alex's point: pricing and other improvements were real but not the constraint. The constraint was the lack of a skilled sales team and the skill to build one.
- He framed the deficiency as a question: "How do I learn how to recruit, manage, and train a sales team?"

## Theory of constraints and how Alex applies it

- "A system will grow until it is constrained." Remove the constraint and it keeps growing until it hits the next one.
- At any moment there is typically only one or two things limiting growth, and by definition only one true constraint. Other issues may be emerging but are not yet the constraint.
- Alex credits his speed of growth to prioritizing which problems to solve.
- Example sequence of constraints:
  1. Not enough salespeople: increase the sales team.
  2. Salespeople perform unevenly: better onboarding and training.
  3. Need consistency: standardize the process.
  4. Not enough leads: focus everything on lead generation.
- Each time, put all attention on the current constraint and let removing the bottleneck carry the business to the next level.
- Result: growth looks fast from the outside, but feels "manageable and clear" from the inside, because everyone is focused on one problem.

## Questions to ask to find the constraint

- Alex opens conversations with entrepreneurs and portfolio-company CEOs with a run-rate question. If the business is at $1M/month: "What's stopping us from being at $5 million a month?"
- Until that question is answered clearly, "there's nothing else we need to do."
- Wording matters: "Why am I not growing?" is weaker than "What is stopping us from doing an extra million dollars a month?"
- Reframe the gap as a "How do I..." question (how do I scale acquisition, how do I learn to build a sales team). Phrased this way it becomes a solvable problem, not "I don't know what to do, therefore I will just start another business."

## Why starting another business doesn't fix a plateau

- Alex says starting a new venture is the common entrepreneurial reaction because "it feels good," and people assume the new thing will grow.
- His observation: "the businesses will grow to the level of incompetence of the entrepreneur." A $4M entrepreneur's next business will also be a $4M business, because the cap comes from the owner's beliefs, traits and skills. Unless one of those changes, the new business hits the same ceiling.
- Running two businesses adds complexity: "Business alone is hard enough." He argues you can't be CEO of two things, noting that almost no CEOs of publicly traded companies run two companies, with Elon Musk as the exception (as founder, and "literally like the smartest human being").
- His closing advice to owners who think a new business or product line will fix a stall: it won't. Find and fix the constraint in the current business.
- His broader maxim: "We need to be reminded more than we need to be taught." Do the boring work and repeat successful actions.
