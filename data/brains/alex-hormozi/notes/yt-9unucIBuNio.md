## Why branding matters: the CAC and LTV math

- Alex says a strong brand can get people to pay 10x more than they pay a competitor. It can also get customers to convert on ads at 10x the normal rate, so acquisition costs a tenth as much. He calls that a "100x differential" and says it is how massive brands "print money over time."
- He treats the cost of acquiring a customer (CAC) against lifetime value (LTV, the gross profit made over the customer's life) as the basic economic unit of every business. Example: spend $1 to get a customer who produces $10 of gross profit, and the LTV to CAC ratio is 10 to 1.
- A brand improves that ratio in four ways:
  - A higher percentage of the audience converts.
  - Customers buy more times.
  - Customers buy from no one else.
  - Customers pay above the commoditized market price.
- Alex says this is arguably the most important outcome in business, and a strong brand with positive associations in the ideal audience is the most powerful way to get it.
- The catch is that building associations takes time. Once the teaching is done, it pays back in lower CAC, higher prices and repeat purchases, and it starves out competitors.
- He says that in 40 months he went from zero to 8 million subscribers across all channels. His portfolio went from starting out to over $250 million a year.

## What a brand is: pairing and pruning associations

- Alex defines branding as "the association between two things." The entrepreneur's active verb is pairing: you pair the thing people don't know (your business or personal brand) with something they already know and like.
- Repeat the pairing enough and you can remove the other thing. The positive feeling stays attached to you.
- His illustration is a stage test. Standing next to all the Republican candidates, all the Democrats, or a group of porn stars would each change how people see him. If he appeared with porn stars 100 times in a row, people would assume he works in that industry even after the porn stars were removed.
- Most businesses make these pairings haphazardly. He compares a brand to a front garden: weeds grow naturally, so you have to prune and weed. A garden of all red roses has one strong association. A mix of roses, daffodils and sunflowers means "nothing to nobody."
- Even a harmless element can dilute a brand. He ran a 3 to 6 month stretch of wider topics, including his relationship with Leila, and he calls it a small mistake. His audience is "business first," so he lost some business owners who weren't married.
- His rule is to keep one central pillar that the whole audience shares, which for him is business. Personal uniqueness can be sprinkled around that pillar, but never put ahead of it.
- Every new association trades away part of the audience for a bigger share of the people you want. Good branding means the people you gain are your "who" and the people you lose are not.
- He turns down podcasts in controversial niches, such as black pill or red pill shows and porn-world shows, even when they offer big view counts. He has no moral objection. He says the trade isn't worth it because a segment of his audience cares.

## Step 1: choose the "who" with four qualifications

Alex says branding starts with who you want to attract. The six steps are the same for a personal brand and a business brand, and only the target avatar changes. He points to the "$100M Offers" book for the full treatment. His four filters:

1. **Growing.** Pick a growing market over a shrinking or flat one. Even if you stay the same size, you grow with it.
2. **Can afford it.** If you want to charge a lot, they need money. Given poor, medium and rich options, he'd pick rich.
3. **Easy to target.** If you can't find them, you can't sell to them. Choose the easy-to-find group.
4. **In pain.** They have to actually want what you sell. A market where nobody wants the service is hard to sell to, even if the people are reachable.

He also says to avoid being a generalist and serve a specific market. That lets you charge a premium, and it is operationally easier because you repeat the same work instead of building custom solutions for each customer.

Worked example: an accounting firm (CPA).
- He picks med spas and anti-aging clinics serving Boomers, riding health trends and aging.
- That market is growing, has money, is easy to find, and its customers are in pain from aging.
- The same firm could instead target residential homeowners or W2 employees. The content and associations would be entirely different even though the business is still accounting.
- He notes you can choose to appeal to one political group, since political party is not a protected class. He warns this would also ward off some people who don't want politics in their accounting.

## Steps 2 and 3: find what they like, then associate through advertising

- After choosing the who, list what they like and what they struggle with. For med spa owners Alex lists:
  - More customers
  - More profit
  - A sellable business
  - Saving money
  - Making life easier
  - Help recruiting talent, so they work less in the business
  - Retaining talent, because nurses and nurse practitioners are expensive and in high demand
- Then "associate," which he says is code for advertise. You pair yourself with the solutions to their problems, because everyone wants less pain.
- For the accounting firm, that means giving away videos, blogs, written content, cheat sheets and PDFs. Examples:
  - How to pack the refrigerator to fit more stock and free up room for services.
  - How to set up the lobby so more patients are seen in less time.
- Each piece of content is a micro promise kept. That raises the odds they believe the macro promise, that the firm can help them make better decisions and drive profit. It also sets up the firm to help with financials when they sell the business.
- Advertising versus branding:
  - Advertising is the process of making something known.
  - Branding is what you make known, meaning the associations created while advertising.
  - You can have excellent advertising and terrible branding at once.
- His example is Budweiser's Dylan Mulvaney campaign. It reached the whole world, but for Bud Light's core audience it was a bad association.
  - It might have been good branding for a company whose who is very progressive.
  - Alex says it strengthened Bud Light with a sub-segment, but more people hated it, so the brand lost overall.
- He names four ways to advertise: make content, reach out to people you don't know, reach out to people you know, or run ads.

## The three levels of belief: tell, others tell, they experience

- Alex ranks three levels of influence, using a movie as the model.
  1. **What you tell them.** This is the trailer. It is the least influential level, but a brand-new person judges you 100% on this first exchange, so quality matters.
  2. **What others tell them.** This is word of mouth, and it matters far more than what you say. His example is Top Gun: Maverick. He saw the trailer and thought it looked cool, but he went only after about 10 people told him it was the best movie they'd seen in two years. A bad word of mouth on a Godzilla trailer kills the sale.
  3. **What they experience themselves.** This is seeing the movie and deciding for themselves.
- A movie is a single product and is not really a business. Business owners want repeat purchase, so the promise must be delivered at the product level.
- When customers have a great experience, they believe your next claim and tell others. Alex calls this the virtuous cycle of branding:
  1. Make a promise.
  2. Keep the promise.
  3. People tell others you kept it.
  4. New people try you and you keep the promise again.
- Getting other people to talk on your behalf takes time. Anyone can get a customer to buy once, but only an exceptional business gets repeat purchases.

## Step 4: keep your promises, which he calls the most important step

- Keeping promises comes down to product. The product does what you said, the way you said, by the time you said.
- Alex says this is very easy to say and very hard to do. Don't over-promise, and over-deliver.
- Apple is his example. Its brand rests on exceptional products tied to the association of elegant, simple, easy-to-use design that makes technology accessible. He says it started as a niche and bet on that becoming the majority. He adds that it gives hope to anyone who isn't premium yet.
- Without the product step, the cycle breaks. With it, branding reinforces itself.

## Proof that a strong brand outperforms direct response tactics

- Alex's first experience of a strong reputation was launching "LNS," the first beta of what became Acquisition.com's software company, Leila's. He ran a webinar for his existing audience, and the product was automated lead nurture build-outs for gyms, delivered in the order orders were received.
- Busy gym owners who missed the webinar called in saying they didn't know what he was selling, but to "just bill me." Some wanted to pay to get in line sooner.
- He says they behaved that way because every earlier promise to them had been delivered on for more than they had paid.
- His conclusion: direct response "hacks and tricks" become irrelevant once you have a strong positive association. That association makes people more likely to buy frequently, exclusively from you, and at higher prices.
- He admits the path is hard because it takes time and has to be deliberate.
