## Building AI into a business: where Alex says it pays and where it doesn't

- Alex's test for AI adoption is one question: "are you making more money?" He says many founders are "token maxing" without it.
- Example he gave: a business paid about $11,000 a month for 11 virtual assistants doing data-cleaning work. It spent $350,000 on an AI system to replace them, which is over 3 years of the VAs' cost. The task wasn't the growth constraint, because the business still lacked demand.
- He says people use AI in the wrong places:
  - They start AI businesses instead of using AI inside their business.
  - They advertise "AI" when they should advertise the customer's outcome.
  - Many customers are afraid of AI, so the label isn't necessarily a plus.
- He thinks startups built on things the models will swallow are a poor bet.
- He does recommend automating small businesses with AI as a real opportunity ("there's lots of money to be made there").
- He says outsourcing hard thinking to AI is a bad decision for now. He asks Claude, OpenAI and others the same obvious question and "they're just all over the place". He also argues that delegating decisions makes you "dumber", so he keeps his hardest thinking for himself.
- He feeds survey data into AI to run a Van Westendorp pricing analysis, which takes about 6 minutes instead of manual work.

## Where value and moats remain when intelligence is cheap

- **Stakes:** AI can recommend, but someone must own the decision, take the liability and keep the upside. AI "isn't a citizen of any country" and pays no taxes. Someone owns the LLC and pays for the tokens.
- **Real stakes in media:** MrBeast with fake money would have no stakes. Chess is more popular than ever even though robots beat humans, and F1 would lose viewers without Lewis Hamilton in the car.
- **Reality is the moat in content.** Elon Musk, Bezos and Buffett are top business and investing influencers because they own the companies. A teacher quoting Buffett word for word still won't get his views, because he has no Berkshire Hathaway behind him.
- **Higher risk means more weight on credibility.** Beauty tips carry low risk. Personal finance carries more, and business advice can cost someone their business.
- **His own content response:** do only what he alone can do, such as live, in-person events. Hundreds of $500K to $10M-a-year business owners fly in monthly, and he launched a show called Scale or Fail with real entrepreneurs and stakes.
- **Content to avoid:** commoditized tips with no proof. His test is "why should I listen to you?", which has to be answered in the first few seconds. Authority also makes content lower effort to consume.
- **Starting out with no track record:** document proof of effort. Examples are recording a full day of sales calls and clipping the best moment ("I took 22 calls today"), or Jimmy's first viral video of counting to a million.
- **Hard and scarce:** Steven's phrase, which Alex endorses as the moat for content.
- **If super-intelligent AI were a few years away,** he would build as much real-world proof, track record and brand as possible. He expects reputation to matter and distribution to still cost money. He would also keep building trust and distribution.

## Build for the long time horizon: the blocks and foundation idea

- The foundation of a tower depends on how tall you plan to build it. A mentor told him: "the fastest way to build a $10 million business is not the fastest way to build a $100 million business." Likewise a $1M business isn't the fastest path to $10M.
- He says a one-person agency can reach $1M a year quickly.
- Founders who want the speed of a 1-story build and later want 10 stories skyrocket, then plateau. Sometimes the fix is to "take 2 steps back, rebuild the foundation, and then it goes up again."
- "Focus and patience are the 2 enduring competitive advantages because they're so anti-human." People see the choices you made, not the options you had (a line he credits to his partner Sharran).
- Examples of long-horizon bets:
  - Bezos began as an online bookstore and chose to own trucks and warehouses.
  - Musk built the battery and charging network from scratch.
- Bezos's principle, as Alex cites it: bet on what won't change. People will want lower prices, more selection and faster delivery in 10 years. Alex calls this one of his pillars for planning.
- Slow-then-fast curves: most founders quit during the slow part. Steven's example was 1,000 Instagram posts to reach 1,000 followers, then 300,000 followers in 3 posts. His podcast got almost no listeners for 3 years before it took off. Alex says "there are more mistakes of abandoning the right path" than of picking the wrong one.
- People abstract a future reward to zero if it is far enough away, such as an index fund held for 30 to 40 years against today's latte. He says consistency can only be witnessed by being consistent yourself.

## Why $1M businesses stall at $10M: retention and the two-company example

- What he says the $1M owner is missing is staying longer in the product-market fit phase. Customers must keep buying, either periodically (a soda you like) or on subscription (Netflix).
- If customers never left, the business could only stay flat or grow.
- Worked example: two companies each sell 100 new units a year at $1M of revenue per 100 customers.
  - Company B loses all its customers each year, so it must sell 100, then 200, then 300 new customers to show $1M, $2M, $3M.
  - Company A keeps its customers and adds 100 new ones a year, reaching 100, 200, then 300 total customers and $3M in year 3.
  - Both look like $3M businesses, but he would invest in A. B must sell about 600 new customers the next year, and acquiring 300 new customers costs much more than adding 100 to an existing base. He says this compresses the bottom line.
- He calls this the problem with "growth at all costs". Once the revenue stays, adding a big distribution channel (10,000 new customers) can turn the business into a billion-dollar-a-year company.
- Better marketing and sales skill speeds revenue growth, but it can hide a leaky product. A business with a "hole in the back of your bus" craters the moment sales stop.
- Fast, shaky businesses are hard to exit. He describes holding your breath through the process ("please just go through").

## Pricing, offers and the value equation

- **Value equation:** value rises with the size of the outcome and the perceived likelihood of achievement (the inverse of risk). It falls with time delay and with effort and sacrifice.
  - Effort is what you must start doing.
  - Sacrifice is the good things you must stop doing.
  - Example: a $19 fitness PDF versus a $3,000 personal trainer differ mainly in perceived likelihood.
- He considers time delay the most slept-on element. His advice for disrupting any business is to look at what others do and "do it half the time", since someone will always pay a premium for speed.
- **Don't sell out of your own wallet.** Things that are easy for you feel easy for everyone, so you undercharge. That squeezes margin, which means you do all the work, can't afford hires and have no time to train anyone.
- **Overwhelm diagnostic:** he asks first, "What's your margin?" Thin margins mean you can't afford help. The cause is usually a wrong offer (too much for too little) or a sales motion that doesn't show the value. At 70% margin, he says, hire.
- **Start unscalable and premium.** Take your target price and add 1 or 2 zeros, then list what you'd have to do to make it worth that. His fitness example: go to their house, grocery shop with them and drive them to the gym, at $100,000 for 5 clients. Benefits he lists:
  - You get better customers.
  - You learn from people richer than you.
  - You practice the unscalable thing and find which parts scale.
- **Tesla model:** he suggests starting with the Roadster-level premium offer, with high cash flow and margin and low scalability. Then work down to cheaper versions for more people, so you are already anchored high.
- **Price as a ladder:** in service businesses, price tracks how advanced the business is. When demand exceeds supply, raise price. A higher price gets you better customers, better margins, less headache and a quality signal. He says a $2,000-a-month social media offer wouldn't be taken seriously by him, and his minimum for a vendor is around $15,000 a month.
- **Van Westendorp pricing analysis** (from the 1970s), which he runs on most new offerings. Ask four questions:
  1. At what price is it so expensive you wouldn't consider it?
  2. At what price is it so cheap it can't be valuable?
  3. At what price is it expensive but you'd still consider buying?
  4. At what price is it a bargain?
  - Plot the answers, find the range with the most sales, and factor in gross margin. Also collect customer stats (rich versus poor, home services and so on) to get a pricing curve per segment. He notes that double-peaked curves happen when there are different customer types.

## Market selection, niching and moving upmarket

- At the start, take money from anyone. "You just need to get anybody to give you money." Between roughly $1M and $3M, he says to pick who you serve and what they pay best, which simplifies delivery and advertising.
- Example: instead of "podcast agency for everyone", say "we help doctors generate patients using podcasts". This lets him templatize delivery and "cut once, sell twice", and it tells the right customer the offer is for them.
- Gary Halbert's idea, which he endorses: "We don't want to create demand. We want to channel it." Attention already exists, so the job is making your option the nicest and most convenient one.
- He sees wealth as a market map: if all US wealth is $100, the bottom 50% hold about $2, the next 40% about $27, the next 9% about $36, and the top 1% about 31 (his numbers, "somewhere in the neighborhood"). He says beginners see only the poorer slice, assume no one has money, and stay broke. He says to move upmarket.
- Steven's point, which Alex builds on: Coca-Cola-sized clients were easier to manage than a founder with a $10,000 budget, but you need credibility and a track record to get that meeting.
- A millionaire-level business can be built by doing slightly better than underserving competitors. His example is a pool cleaner who shows up on time and cleans better, and who knocks on doors asking people to switch.
- **Opportunities he named:**
  - Reddit reputation management and clipping agencies, which he sees as newer than old-school Meta ad agencies.
  - Longevity, med spa and peptide businesses ("a huge supply demand issue").
  - Wealth advisors, insurance, and wealth-related services generally.
  - Unglamorous businesses nobody wants to discuss at parties, such as waste management for old people.
  - AI automation for small businesses.
- He told a $10M-a-year service-based gym owner that his skill set could earn more applied to a "superior vehicle", since service gyms (personal or semi-private training) are "easy to start, hard to scale".

## Every problem is a marketing problem: demand, supply and hiring

- About 70% of the $1M to $50M businesses he sees are demand constrained, and about 30% are supply constrained (more customers than staff). He says his content therefore focuses on acquisition.
- **Customer pipeline:** lead gen, nurture, sales, onboarding, retention and ascension.
- **Hiring pipeline (his supply-side version):** application generation, application nurture (get applicants scheduled and booked quickly), interview as the sale, onboarding, retention and ascension (career path).
- Run it like marketing:
  - Check who writes your job posts and outbound messages. He says a bigger owner would be "horrified".
  - Use an interview script and role-play it.
  - Build a 30/60/90 onboarding plan.
  - Ask a person you like: "What would it take for you to work here forever?"
  - Ask a customer: "What would it take for us to be your sole provider forever?" and offer exclusivity or a 3-year contract with extras.
- **Referral incentive story (secondhand):** a roughly $10M business paid $500 per agent referral on agents worth about $250,000 a year in gross profit each. A mentor told him to raise it to $25,000. Alex says the company went from $10M to $400M.
- **Hiring standards:** his own change is a higher bar for talent and less tolerance for mediocrity staying. The highest person in a department should hold the highest standard, and if someone holds a higher standard than he does in a department, they should run it.
- **Don't hunt unicorns.** Founders want one person who does everything they do. He says to find the horse, the rhino and the fireflies, or three people, rather than one unicorn. He calls "no one can do what I do" ego, and says the business's job is to serve customers, not your emotional needs. You can also teach it in a tenth of the time by teaching it the right way.
- Solopreneurship is fine if that's the goal ("what do you want?").

## Getting started, handling fear and making decisions

- **Minimum to start:** get an LLC, open a bank account, be able to process money, then ask a stranger to pay you for something. He says that beats about 95% of people and turns a wantrepreneur into an entrepreneur. First-time buyers also change identity, which makes ascensions and renewals easier.
- When people ask him "I'm thinking about starting X", he asks "what have you done so far?" Nine times out of ten the answer is nothing.
- **Fear exists in the vague.** Define failure specifically:
  - Asking someone to buy and hearing no isn't failure.
  - Ask 100 people, then change the offer.
  - Plan B in detail: he split a bedroom in a 6-person house for $400 a month and kept living costs low.
  - He says people won't sacrifice short-term lifestyle for long-term goals.
- **His own start:**
  - He pondered for 6 months, reading roughly a book every two days.
  - He did the business school math: about $240,000 of opportunity cost over 2 years against a $60,000-a-year cost and a $120,000 starting salary.
  - He used his own savings rather than a loan, because he wanted to know it was his.
  - He drove halfway across the country before calling his father to say he had quit.
- **Gym Launch exit:** he almost didn't sell for $46 million because of one person in his circle whom he thought wouldn't consider that enough. He frames it as "letting him control me" and says to put a name on the voice you fear. It's usually two people, not "people".
- Only take the steps you can see (the fog on the mountain). Early on, advice is the most specific and the same for everyone. As the business grows, the doors multiply.
- **Push versus pivot:** pivot if a fundamental assumption of the business has been proven untrue by your activity. If none of your theses are wrong and it's just slower or harder than hoped, push.
- **Local versus global failure:** quit the doggy skateboard idea, not business. His mentor ran 5,000 flyers per test batch and 3,000 a day after that, about 150,000 a month. Alex put out 300 and quit, so he says successful people try far more attack vectors and far higher volume than beginners.
- **Decisions:** the main question is "what do I want to have happen?" Then be specific about what it changes in daily life. Unmade decisions can last forever, whereas a feedback loop pushes you forward. Commitment means giving up the other doors. He says decision-making is the highest-leverage skill.
- **Behaviorist approach:** he uses operant conditioning, meaning you arrange conditions, not persuade. His grandpa example: crush the pill into ice-cold lemonade with salty peanuts and a backgammon board, so the pill is the easiest and nicest option. For a business that isn't selling, he asks whether you've made buying from you the most convenient, nicest option.
- **Plus/minus persuasion (copy):** do the thing and get more good and less bad. Don't do it and get less good and more bad. He says to ping-pong between these.
- **Bad days:** he says a year has about 36 bottom-10% days and about 36 top-10% days, which is about 3 each a month, purely from large numbers. "My emotional discomfort is not an adequate reason to change what I'm doing." Founders often wreck a fledgling business after a bad day.
- **Mental toughness (his framework):** fortitude (how much before you change), tolerance (how low you fall), resilience (how fast you recover) and adaptability (whether you come back better or worse).
