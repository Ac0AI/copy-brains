## Writing a book: surface area of thought and problem definition

- Alex writes a book roughly every 18 months to two years. He says each is "the last book" until a six-month refractory period passes.
- He keeps notes on his phone for about 10 books at different stages and starts writing once a topic has enough material.
- He believes in "surface area of thought". Spreading the thinking over a long time puts him in different states and conversations, so the book has "density of thought and ideas per word" that a two-week sprint can't match.
- He forms a working theory, battle-tests it in the real world, and when he finds himself using the same framework repeatedly and solving problems with it, "this is it, and that's what the book becomes."
- Problem definition is the hardest part of any book:
  - The leads book (his second) was the hardest he has written. He tried to put all advertising in one book and it took 19 versions.
  - The Money Models book was the fastest, because it was narrow (wrappers for promotions) and easy to consume. The offers book was also super narrow.
  - All three started as one giant tome. Someone told him he couldn't do that, and he split it so people would actually read it.
- He only writes what he has a unique take on: "I want to write something that only exists because I made it." He applies the same test to businesses.

## Media strategy: volume, live, interactive and legacy distribution

- Early YouTube:
  - He hired an agency that did three videos a week and webcammed them from a travel suitcase setup, dumping out the thoughts he had.
  - A cold-reaching editor offered to clip him for Shorts when Shorts launched, so he was early.
  - That editor proposed filming him directly. Alex agreed to one day a quarter and did about 100 shorts in a sitting. He still prefers "marathon days" over spreading filming out.
- "Volume negates luck." He says value per second is the game, so get as many seconds out as possible.
- The formats he is most excited about for 2026-27 are live and interactive, separately and together. Examples are the book launch live and the shows Cash Cows and "Scale or Fail".
- His AI-era logic: people want stakes and things that can't be faked, like a real prize or a real chess match.
- Legacy media is access to distribution he doesn't have:
  - Dave Ramsey has run for 30-40 years on about 600 syndicated radio stations while "everyone says radio's dead".
  - Reality TV stars keep strong followings even with weak social execution.
  - He calls himself an "equal opportunist when it comes to attention".
- He sees polished, produced media as top of funnel (authority, new distribution). Volume content sits closer to middle and bottom of funnel and nearer to purchases.

## Offer ladders and pricing for high-end customers

- Customers are "super fractal". 1% of customers can generate as much revenue as the other 99%, and each price tier typically adds another doubling of revenue.
  - At $100 a month, expect another double at $500 a month, another at $2,500 a month, and so on at 5-10x steps.
- Founders struggle because they feel weird and "sell out of their own wallet". His fix is to make the offers available, state the facts and tell the whole truth.
- Sample framing: "This is absurdly expensive and only for people for whom that's not absurdly expensive. If you're offended, we have less offensive prices that are lower. If you're a special snowflake, we have special snowflake prices."
- Brand evolution: his move from "I have nothing to sell you" to selling books was handled with the whole truth ("I didn't have anything to sell, and now I do"). Capitalism is voluntary exchange and lots of free content remains.
- You can't please everyone. Decide whether you care more about the people you help or the people angry at you for trying to help.
- International is a struggle. He doesn't want non-English speakers clogging funnels and the team has no Italian versions, though he is moving toward services for that audience.

## Using AI in a small or mid-market business

- Common mistakes:
  - Starting a new AI company instead of applying AI to their existing business. This competes with the biggest companies on something they know less well.
  - Doing the wrong things faster. Example: an owner who wasn't making money still isn't, but now has token costs.
  - Meeting-note tools. He says he has never needed notes to achieve his goals, just "what's the next most important thing".
  - Rebuilding software you already pay for (e.g. recoding Calendly) with hundreds of hours and breakage risk.
- The right approaches:
  1. Move each function from org-chart thinking to workflow thinking. If an editor was in six workflows, have AI do three of them. This can 3-5x output, so revenue per headcount rises and margin goes up. Prices won't adjust for a while, so margins get "absolutely stupid".
  2. For service businesses, he advocates not telling people you use AI. Charge human prices and keep tech costs.
  3. Take it department by department.
- Narrow point solutions can make sense, such as an internal tool that flips live streams with captions and ad end cards.
- Build a data layer. "If you want anything that's unique, it starts with data."
  - Acquisition.com has a repository of sales copy, testimonials, videos and collateral.
  - It feeds several hundred ads in real time, matched to several hundred dynamic landing pages, programmatically at acquisition.com.
- Organic: a teenager on the Mormo Mosy highlights channel posts about 5 "mids" (3-10 minute clips) and about 15 shorts a day. He runs Alex's business-owner conversations through AI for highlights and inserts CTAs automatically.
- Distribution philosophy for clippers: "I want three agencies working for me and an in-house team." He says all the impressions are out there and he wants to catch them all.
- E-commerce is the most cutting edge on the paid side. The winners run decentralized UGC with an AI backend for screening and control, then put money behind winners.
- Stay focused. If you want to be a trillionaire you need to go all in on bleeding-edge AI and drop the dry cleaner. Otherwise, just win at dry cleaning, because competitors are distracted by "orange glasses".

## Choosing a business, a path, and a risk level

- Output equals volume of activity multiplied by leverage of the activity, so pick the right boat for your goals.
  - Working hard on the wrong thing is the worst outcome.
  - It's hard whether you build a restaurant or a unicorn, so you might as well play big.
  - He cites Stephen Schwarzman: level 10 talent only attracts level 10 opportunities.
- Compare year one of a better opportunity against year five of your current one, not year one to year one, because time can't be recovered. Compounding reps can make even an inferior vehicle win.
- Every category has an amazing business. Some categories have a "bolus" of bad businesses, which signals structural difficulty:
  - Apparel is structurally hard and competitive.
  - Social media marketing agencies are hard because the good marketers can make money elsewhere, clients are volatile SMBs, and big clients bring things in-house.
  - Airbnb looked silly but was not a bad category.
- Most businesses aren't tech. You can be a billionaire in a boring business like HVAC, pools or building materials (he cites Brad Jacobs). Less sophisticated players and less capital make it easier to compete.
- Decide what you want. Example: $7M topline, $2.5M profit with a crew and little work is a fine outcome. Then pick the highest-likelihood path. "If you stick with that path for 20 years, the likelihood that you fail is basically zero" as long as you have a feedback loop for improvement.
- Don't let strangers' opinions dissuade you.
- Risk is personal. His approach is to build a nest egg early so bets are "free swings" and the downside is just his life going back to zero. His test for a big decision is whether what you eat, wear, where you live and who you're married to would change. He cites Keith Cunningham's framing: know your upside and downside, and don't take the bet if you can't live with the downside.
- On staging a career (build a cash-flow agency first, fund a brand later), he says there is no single right answer. It depends on goal and risk tolerance.
- Mindset trends (sauna, cold plunge, meditation): you must have a strong argument that it increases output per unit time. His morning routine is wake, caffeinate, shut off distractions, start work, and compress the time from waking to working. Rich people's habits reflect the plateau, not the rise.

## Personal brand, legitimacy and business-owner dissatisfaction

- If the money you make is the goal, media that doesn't translate into money is fake work. If media outgrows the original business, perhaps media is the business.
- Don't build a personal brand first. Make something great that benefits millions and the brand comes by default. Bezos has no content output and everyone knows him.
- Legitimacy needed scales with the risk the consumer takes in acting on the content:
  - Beauty content has low risk.
  - B2B and finance have higher risk, so you need evidence of results.
  - His podcast went from about 2,000 downloads a month to millions after he sold his company for $46.2M, because "I had evidence".
- Founders compare themselves to their peer group. Tech people want tech approval, an HVAC owner wants HVAC approval.
- Humans are inherently dissatisfied and want the benefits of a trade-off without the negatives. Tech founders who exit for nine figures often buy cash-flowing businesses like gas stations. Cash-flow owners complain about low multiples and unreliable labor.
- His former CFO Suzanne Shifflet, who has been on 20+ deals, said "It's all shit. It's just a different type of suck." He says every business has its own flavor of difficulty.

## Acquisition.com strategy and spotting opportunities (med spas)

- Acquisition.com started as a cash-flow family office run with his wife Leila. He did 24 deals in 24 months, real purchases rather than VC-style bets, and was "bored to tears".
- Portfolio theory played out: about 3 of the 24 were really good companies. Their brand also blew up, so they recalculated.
- The advisory practice started in January 2024 and did $36M in its first year. He says the book launch last year did about $105M (as he stated it). The advisory practice grew the next year.
- They stopped doing passive deals. The model is now "brand + capital + work" on fewer bets for outsized returns, like School (a 2024 investment, now 30M users and $1B+ GMV). His reasoning: "Zuckerberg didn't have Airbnbs as a side hustle."
- His bar is that the product must be "insane". Then they bring distribution and cash, which he calls "the holy trinity".
- Acquisition.com is about 30 days from closing a "gigantic deal" (unspecified).
- Target audience is $1M-$50M owner-operators. He says plenty of people in that range don't know him, and helping 1-to-10 founders also draws in 0-to-1 founders. He is a "big fan of capitalism" and wants as many people as possible to start businesses.
- Med spas, which he is still looking for the "magical" one in:
  - Demand: an aging, wealthy population that wants to look young. He calls the zeitgeist "I want to live forever", with Bryan Johnson as the face.
  - Supply: the business owners he meets weekly are understaffed and doing better than they should. One owner did about $1.2M free cash flow within a year off roughly $400K invested. Another did about $6M topline at 40% margins with essentially no marketing ("you just open up and put the sign up").
  - Customer acquisition cost is near zero and service gross margins are very high.
  - The business is highly fragmented (10,000-15,000 med spas) with no one rolling them up.
  - A de novo launch is not that expensive. The hard part is technician talent: they can open their own door and take customers, since clients are loyal to the injector or laser tech.
  - The talent strategy is to pay as directly as possible on what they generate, like a partner firm or law firm. He borrows from his godfather, a wealth manager with several billion under management: "let the other person have a little more", for example giving a 51% share so they never want to leave.
- Life and death: he doesn't think about living forever. "I expect to die." He quotes Marcus Aurelius: the old and young lose the same thing, the present.
- Mistakes and falls: he has "lost everything two times", rejects the pre-fall/post-fall binary, and commits to not stopping and controlling the controllable.
- Work environment: he works in a windowless, soundproof room with earplugs and artificial light because he is very distractable. He wants zero stimulation for deep work.
