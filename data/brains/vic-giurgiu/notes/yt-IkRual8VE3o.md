## Path from game dev freelancing to an agency

- Vic got into game development as a kid because he loved console gaming. His uncle worked on Watch Dogs and Assassin's Creed at Ubisoft and helped him with questions.
- He built his first simple game around age 12 or 13, then joined game-dev Discord servers and forums, helping people for free.
- His first paid gig came from a "paid work" section of a Discord server. A client paid $5 per hour and he earned roughly $75-85 in a month and a half, in seventh or eighth grade. He notes the average Romanian salary is about $800 per month, so this was meaningful money.
- He met his agency co-founders through local programming clubs and contests. They were slightly older, around 16-17, and he told them he had paid projects they could work on together.
- He started the agency "without really knowing this agency concept": he had more projects than he could code himself and took a commission by bringing in talented friends.
- He spent about two to three years on game development without real results. He says passion carried him through that period, and that being able to do the work himself meant no payroll pressure early on.

## Scaling the agency: channels, pricing and tech stack

- Discord projects paid about $50-150, which was not enough to live on.
- To raise prices he advertised on Fiverr and did outreach to 10-15 local companies every week. Average project price rose to about $400-700 per app or website.
- He moved from games to mobile and desktop apps because companies would pay for them.
- Stack: Capacitor JS for iOS and Android, Electron JS for desktop, all JavaScript. A common job was a Windows-only app that the client wanted on Mac and Linux, so they recoded it natively.
- Client sources were SEO, Fiverr, Upwork, Guru, and outbound.
- Notable clients: a listed Swiss mechanical-parts company (conference slideshow software, hired through its manager on a tight deadline), a European jersey store, and gaming YouTuber Super Shiftery (150-200k subscribers).
- Revenue was seasonal. Summer was the peak because companies build products and features then, ahead of the late-fall marketing season.
- Total agency revenue reached "five to six figures."

## Why he left the agency for SaaS

He gave three reasons:
- Revenue was declining month over month, and no-code tools for desktop, mobile and web apps were rising.
- Software development agency work has no recurring revenue: clients pay for one project, and only small updates or maintenance follow.
- His co-founders had exams, college and internships, and he had stopped enjoying the calls and project management.

He treated the agency as a stepping stone toward a subscription company. He finished existing projects, slowed client work, and brainstormed and tested SaaS ideas on the market until one became profitable. The transition was smooth.

## Validating a SaaS idea before building

- Put up a quick landing page (WordPress, Webflow or Bubble) with a mockup image and a hook that states the problem or solution, plus an email collection field.
- Do outreach to about 100 people on LinkedIn or Twitter and talk to people in communities like Indie Hackers. Perhaps 10 reply, but expert opinions count ten times more than friends and parents saying it's a great idea.
- Then pick the tech stack that gets an MVP out fastest, because fast shipping lets you iterate.
- Example: Fistash started targeting affiliate marketers and had to flip to companies. Because it was built fast, the pivot was cheap.
- Without coding skills he suggests no-code tools: Webflow, Bubble, Zapier, with Gumroad, Paddle or Stripe for payments.

## Fistash: how he found the problem and how it works

- Fistash helps companies and startups find and recruit affiliates and partners using AI. It started in January 2023.
- The pivot: he first built it for affiliate marketers to find products. Advisors in the industry told him businesses have more revenue and capital than marketers, so he flipped it to target companies.
- The problem came from his own agency days. He wanted people who could resell his services for commission and found no efficient, affordable way to do it.
- Companies pay affiliate or partnership managers to do repetitive work: collect affiliates in a spreadsheet and reach out manually. He cites that 84% of US companies have an affiliate program.
- Two sides:
  - An AI-powered marketplace where affiliates sign up and apply to programs. They get two daily software offers with content to start promoting.
  - A custom AI search engine his team built from agency experience. It scrapes social platforms and the web, and returns contact details and traffic numbers.
- The base plan gives 150 affiliate leads from a form the company fills in at signup.
- Why software for affiliates: recurring revenue, the same thing he missed in the agency.
- Stack: JavaScript with Next.js for the app and landing page, and a Python API. He built it in two weeks with a friend from the agency, with whom he had good chemistry.

## Growth numbers, the acquisition offer and exit plans

- MRR: $110 in month one, about doubled in month two, and crossed $500 in month three.
- At about two and a half months, with MRR around $380-480, a company known in the affiliate space offered about $35,000.
- He rejected it because MRR was growing 50-100% every month, so he thought it was undervalued. He projected over $1,000 MRR in six months. He stays in touch with that company, and they have sent him referrals and customers.
- He hired a commission-based CMO because he describes himself as "really bad at sales, marketing", so he can focus on product and AI matching accuracy.
- Goals: $5-6k MRR in about a year and a possible exit above $400,000 later. If it doesn't work, he would rethink strategy or pivot.

## Twitter, AI and handling setbacks

- He returned to Twitter at the start of 2023 with a goal: sell the product and connect with people. He observed that big SaaS founders get promotion for their companies through personal brand.
- On AI, he argues there are many niche problems that automation or AI can solve right now. His example is the story-sync app Story Heap, built by a Romanian entrepreneur at 17, which reposted Snapchat stories to Instagram and Facebook and reached $1M in sales in six months. He describes the lesson as finding a repetitive manual task, automating it, and making an offer companies can't refuse.
- On churn: think about what happens if you 100x the company, since then you'll have hundreds of churned customers every month. So get used to it, and talk to churned customers to understand why instead of making excuses.
- He ends his days by writing three things he's grateful for.
