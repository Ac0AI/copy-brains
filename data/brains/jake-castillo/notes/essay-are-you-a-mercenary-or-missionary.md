## Missionaries need to care about the thing itself; mercenaries need only the payoff

- Jake frames people (and operators) as either mercenaries or missionaries, and says this is a motivation split, not a discipline split.
- **Mercenary:** wants to make as much money as possible, as fast as possible, and is honest about it. Can work on something "genuinely boring for years and be completely fine," because "the dollar signs trigger the dopamine hits to keep them stimulated and on task."
- Jake calls this "a real, legitimate, and extremely effective way to operate." He says some of the best operators he knows are mercenaries and "will out-earn almost everyone."
- **Missionary:** needs to care about the thing itself. Not in a precious way; they just can't sustain effort on something they think is pointless.
- Failure mode: put a missionary on a boring product with great margins and they quit around month four, can't explain why, and feel like a flake.

## Six years of "I need more discipline" was really a motivation mismatch

- After USC, Jake worked in management consulting with a two-hour train commute each way into LA, every day. He was paid what he thought was good money.
- He couldn't focus on work that bored him. Performance conversations repeated: "You lack attention to detail," "You seem unmotivated," and a proposed performance improvement plan.
- His story was that he needed better systems and routines. Meanwhile, colleagues three desks away did the same work (with "god awful slide decks"), liked it, and were moving up.
- His realization: those people weren't more disciplined. They were mercenaries in a mercenary's job and thriving. He was a missionary in a mercenary's job, "blaming myself for it, and calling the mismatch a character flaw."
- Takeaway: "I spent six years thinking I needed more discipline, when really I just needed different goals that mattered."

## Proof: the attention-to-detail problem vanished once the work was his

- Two days after Jake quit consulting, Zach Yadegari called and asked him to work on Cal AI.
- For the first time in his adult working life, he was working on something "actually mine and meaningful."
- Passion did not make it easy. Building and scaling Cal AI was "the hardest two years of my life": twenty-hour days, no weekends, no income to speak of, an empty bank account, and a fiancée watching him disappear into a laptop.
- Yet "the attention to detail problem never came up again." What six years of performance reviews and discipline couldn't fix "evaporated the moment the work was mine and mattered to me."

## Ask the identity question before the market question

- Most people pick what to build by looking at markets: where the money is, where growth potential is, which niches are underserved. Jake says those are good questions and you should ask them.
- But ask the identity question first, "because it determines whether you'll still be putting in the necessary hours at fourteen months in."
- **If you're a mercenary:** find the boring, high-margin thing and grind it out. "Don't let anyone tell you that you need to be passionate about accounting software."
- **If you're a missionary:** take the identity question seriously, because around month eight, when numbers are flat and the work stops being fun, you'll need something to hold onto.
- The goal isn't to become one or the other. It's to stop picking as one and operating as the other, "the version where you burn two years and call yourself lazy at the end of it."

## Seven questions to ask before committing to a project

1. The last time you worked hard on something with no money attached, what was it? That's the missionary signal and "the most reliable data you have."
2. If this product made $30,000 a month forever and never grew past that, would you still want to run it in three years?
3. When you imagine the win, do you picture your bank account or the thing existing in the world? No wrong answer, but there is a true one.
4. What's the boring middle of this business (support tickets, ad reviews, contracts, reconciliation)? Picture doing that specific task three hours a day for a year.
5. Have you ever quit something that was working? What was actually going on? That pattern will repeat, so plan for it rather than be surprised.
6. Who are you trying to prove something to, and will they notice? Know the honest answer before spending two years on it.
7. What would a mercenary version of you say about this idea, and a missionary version? Which one is doing the talking right now?
