## Core thesis: AI moved the moat, it did not remove it

- Jake says consumer apps have "never been easier to build AND harder to succeed."
- A founder can go from idea to working app faster and cheaper than ever. Features that took a team six months can now be copied in a few days.
- The moat now sits in everything around the product:
  - Distribution
  - Brand
  - Creator relationships
  - Proprietary data
  - Customer trust
  - Experiment velocity
  - Retention
- He keeps saying distribution matters more than product. He does not mean a terrible product can be marketed into success. He means a good product with no repeatable way to reach customers is "really just a side project waiting to be copied."
- Winners in 2026 build the fastest system for creating demand, proving value, and turning what they learn into the next thousand retained customers.

## Market shape: growing, but the middle is disappearing

Figures Jake cites (RevenueCat, unless noted):
- About 14,700 new consumer apps launch every month, roughly 7X the 2022 rate.
- Only 17.3% of apps reach $1,000 in monthly revenue within two years, and just 4.6% reach $10,000.
- Consumer in-app spending hit $167 billion last year, and non-gaming apps passed gaming apps in revenue for the first time.
- Apps launched before 2020 still generate 69% of all app store subscription revenue.
- The top 25% of apps grew revenue by roughly 80% last year while the bottom 25% shrank by 33%.

His takeaway: more demand does not mean everybody wins. Operators with working systems take more of the market. Four questions to ask:
- Can you get someone's attention?
- Can you turn that attention into an install?
- Can you prove the app's value before they leave?
- Can you make the economics work well enough to do it again tomorrow?

## AI apps sell better than they retain

- AI apps convert downloads to paid users roughly 20% better than non-AI apps and generate about 41% more revenue per payer.
- Annual AI subscriptions retain at 21.1% after one year versus 30.7% for non-AI apps.
- Jake's read: AI gets people interested but gives them no automatic reason to stay.
- Positioning rule: sell the outcome, not the technology. "Nobody wants an AI language app because it has a chatbot." They want to feel comfortable speaking Spanish before their trip to Spain.
- "AI is the mechanism, and the outcome is the real product."
- Stress test: if your app stops being interesting the second Apple, Google, or OpenAI ships the same feature, "you do not have an AI business."
- Diagnostic question: which part of your product gets stronger as AI models improve, rather than easier to replace?

## Organic and paid distribution do different jobs

- AppsFlyer data he cites: paid installs drive only about 30% of non-gaming subscription revenue, and organic users generate 69% to 80% of installs depending on region.
- His framework: organic is the testing ground; paid is where you put spend behind winning concepts and formats for sustainable, predictable growth.
- Attribution trap: each scaling channel wants credit for the same customer. Judging channels in a vacuum instead of as one funnel ecosystem means you may cut something that helps the whole system convert.
- Winning apps do not look for one perfect channel. They build a multi-path system where every channel makes the next one convert better.
- Evaluate acquisition sources by which ones produce first renewals, not just cheap installs.

## The first session is the battleground

- Retention keeps getting harder as new apps launch daily and attention spans shrink, so the first session cannot be a seven-screen product tour.
- It must answer three questions fast:
  1. Am I in the right place?
  2. Can this app solve the problem I'm experiencing?
  3. Why should I pay for it now?
- Best onboarding does not explain every feature. It gets the user to the magic moment, then presents the offer in the context of the result they just experienced.
- Paywall type (hard paywall, free trial, or freemium) depends on the product. "There is no universal best paywall setup. There is only the best access model for how quickly your product can prove itself."
- Metric to check: how long it takes a retained customer to reach the "aha" moment.

## Web checkout is another funnel, not free money

- Big operators are adding web-to-app funnels, web subscriptions, and external checkout, to save platform fees and own more of the customer relationship.
- Counter-example: in a RevenueCat-reported Dipsea test, native in-app purchase produced 42% more initial conversions than an equivalent web checkout. The web flow saved fees but added friction that cost more revenue than the fees saved.
- His rule: "margin saved is not the same as revenue earned."
- Use web2app when customers need more education, personalization, pricing flexibility, or payment options before installing.
- Use native checkout when speed and trust matter more than the fee.
- Test by audience rather than forcing every customer down one path.

## iOS and Android are different businesses

- By Day 35, iOS apps convert about 2.6% of downloads to paid versus 0.9% on Android. Once a trial starts, conversion rates are almost identical, so the gap is before the trial begins.
- His advice: the fix is not automatically a lower price. The problem may be the offer, payment method, onboarding, device performance, or trust required to start a trial.
- Android paid acquisition is growing fastest in markets like India and Latin America, while iOS still produces the majority of subscription revenue.
- Android needs its own customer behavior analysis, unit economics, and growth strategy. "Stop treating Android like an iOS version you remembered to launch later."

## Experiment velocity is the operating advantage

- The best operators do not predict the market. They build a faster path to finding out when they are wrong.
- Adapty found bigger median lifts from testing localization, trial structure, plan duration, and the number of plans on the paywall than from price changes alone.
- He does not advocate running 100 random A/B tests. Test the biggest structural question first:
  - Who is the customer?
  - What promise gets their attention?
  - How quickly can they taste the dream outcome?
  - Which access model fits that experience?
  - Which channel brings customers who renew?
- The app that learns the answers faster reinvests faster, and that speed compounds.

## Jake's five-question funnel audit

1. How long does it take a retained customer to hit an "aha" moment?
2. Which acquisition sources produce first renewals, not just cheap installs?
3. Where is the biggest drop between ad view, install, activation, payment, and renewal?
4. Which part of your product gets stronger as AI models improve instead of easier to replace?
5. What is the biggest structural test you can ship in the next two weeks?

- Do not rebuild the whole funnel at once. Find the weakest handoff, fix it, then move to the next.
- Closing line: "The consumer app market is not too crowded. It is just no longer forgiving."
