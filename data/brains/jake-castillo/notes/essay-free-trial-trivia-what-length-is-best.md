## When Jake runs a free trial, and what it requires first

- Jake says free trials fit any consumer app growth strategy, but only after "significant proof that people actually want your product and will pay for it."
- Trials can't compensate for a boring product that doesn't solve a real problem, bad marketing, or poor messaging.
- His starting recommendation, once an app has PMF, a product people pay for, and is ready to test trials, is a LONGER trial rather than a shorter one.
- A short trial only makes sense if everything around it holds up. Otherwise "all you did was shorten your trial and take a lower conversion rate for it." The conditions:
  - The app hits its magic moment in the first session.
  - Onboarding is optimized.
  - The paywall is optimized.
  - Traffic spend is high enough that attribution actually matters.

## Why longer trials convert better

- Someone downloads the app, sees 14 days, figures they'll cancel later, and forgets.
- The trial turns into a subscription for at least a month. If you run ads, that helps liquidate some of the ad spend.
- Longer trials convert better because "you're banking on subscribers forgetting to cancel."
- With a 1 or 3 day trial the reaction is the opposite: users panic, go straight to settings, and cancel the same afternoon.
- He cites RevenueCat: 84% of 3-day trial cancellations happen on day 0 or day 1.

## Short trials as a price for attribution (Cal AI example)

- At Cal AI, Jake ran 300+ creators at one time. Big promo days produced a massive spike in installs.
- With a 3-day trial, he could compare Monday's install spike to Thursday's revenue spike and attribute installs to a specific creator.
- With a 7-day trial, Monday's installs line up with the following Monday's revenue. By then four other creators have posted and one video may have gone viral, so credit can't be assigned.
- Every extra trial day adds variables between the install and the credit card charge.
- Attribution matters more than conversion rate, because it decides which creators to keep or cut.
- Two creators can drive identical installs and very different paying subscriptions. A non-U.S. audience converts far less than an American one because of lower disposable income.
- Without clean attribution he'd risk re-signing the wrong creator and pouring budget into an audience that would never pay for Cal AI.
- The real question, in his words, is "whether a few points of conversion are worth losing the ability to read your own channel." For Cal AI, "it wasn't close."

## Decision rule for trial length

- Not paying for traffic: run the longer trial. There are no creators to attribute.
- Buying installs: shorten it. "You're not optimizing conversion at that point, you're buying signal, and trial length is what you pay for it."

## Freemium vs. paid: ask what people came in the door for

- Jake treats the freemium question as the same logic as trial length, not an unrelated one.
- Old calorie-tracking apps drew users with their food databases. They could give the database away free and upsell barcode scanning, macros, and other premium add-ons. Users came for one thing and paid for four others they didn't know they wanted.
- People downloaded Cal AI for AI food scanning. That feature IS the product.
- Making it free would leave no second feature anyone would pay for: "a pile of users and no revenue."
- Rule: freemium works when there is a great baseline feature plus upsells on the backend. When the draw and the paid feature are the same thing, "freemium is just churn with extra steps."
