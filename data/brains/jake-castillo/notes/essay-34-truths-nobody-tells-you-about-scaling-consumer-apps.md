## Diagnosing the funnel before scaling

- Jake's frame from scaling Cal AI to $50M ARR in roughly 18 months (he ran marketing and day-to-day operations): "You CANNOT scale a broken machine." Founders ask "How do I get more users?" when the real question is where the machine is broken.
- His diagnostic map:
  - Not enough attention: distribution problem.
  - People see content but never visit the App Store: creative or positioning problem.
  - They start onboarding but never reach the paywall: onboarding problem.
  - They reach the paywall but don't buy: offer problem.
- More traffic into a broken funnel "just helps you lose faster."
- The paywall is a "lie detector". It shows whether the promise was compelling, onboarding built desire, value was understood and the offer made sense. A weak paywall rate does not automatically mean "redesign the paywall"; the lie may have started in the ad, the App Store page or onboarding.
- Downloads, paying and staying are three different signals. A free app with a million downloads can have zero product-market fit. Downloading proves curiosity, paying proves the promise was valuable enough to act on, staying proves the product delivered. Only paying and staying can support a business.
- Higher conversion can hide causality: a longer trial raises conversion but gives other campaigns, organic content and outside events more time to influence the user, so you lose the ability to tell what caused the conversion. "The best metric in isolation can still create a worse decision system."
- His closing principle: "Nobody is coming to identify the bottleneck for you." The operator's job is to see the business clearly, find the current constraint, pull the right lever, and repeat when the constraint moves.
- He says the founder's calendar should expose the current constraint. If distribution is broken, a product meeting won't help; if people click but don't pay, more impressions won't help; if the core feature fails, a new logo won't help.

## Consumer apps vs B2B SaaS, features and retention

- Consumer apps are not tiny SaaS companies. They can reach massive audiences with relatively low switching costs, so acquisition is priority #1. B2B has fewer qualified buyers, painful implementation and higher trust costs, so reliability and retention come first. Both need both; the order of operations differs. "Stop importing B2B SaaS playbooks into consumer."
- Every new feature creates another place to break: marketing promise, App Store page, onboarding, paywall, support burden, retention loop and the number of things to diagnose. Earn the right to expand by making the core job undeniable first.
- Power users are often seasonal. A fitness user obsesses while training for an event then disappears; a budgeting user lives in the app while paying off debt. That doesn't mean the product failed. Before building another retention feature, ask whether the user's goal just became less urgent.
- The fastest retention gain is usually not a new feature. Find the action retained users already repeat and make it faster, clearer, more reliable and its result more valuable. "Users stay because the thing they came for keeps getting better."
- Loudest users aren't always the most important. Power users give useful, detailed feedback but asking for 40 settings and an advanced dashboard is not what most want. Listen to them, but build primarily for the quiet basic-user majority who want one outcome with minimal effort.
- Streaks, badges and challenges amplify value but don't create it. Build in this order: value, then repetition, then progress mechanic. If the core action is useless, "you're just gamifying disappointment."
- Community doesn't need to be permanent. A utility app doesn't need a feed, comments or followers. Community can exist around a high-intent window (a shared challenge, a small accountability group, a team goal with a clear start and finish) while the core product stays simple and individual.

## Freemium, email and winback

- Freemium test: if you give away the feature that makes people download, is there another outcome they would confidently pay for? If yes, freemium may work. If you must invent six weak upgrades to justify the subscription, it probably won't. "Free should create demand for paid value. It should not eliminate the only reason to buy."
- Stop using email like a receipt printer (install confirmation, trial reminder, cancellation notice). Use it to teach users how to get value, introduce another use case, reconnect them to a goal and bring back someone whose timing changed. You paid to acquire the relationship; it doesn't disappear when the user closes the app.
- Winback should reconnect the user to the original dream outcome, not open with "WE MISS YOU" and a random discount. Ask: is the goal relevant again, did their context change, did the product improve where they got stuck? "Winback works when the reason to return is stronger than the reason they left."

## Influencer and creator marketing economics

- A creator's fee is not their actual cost. Add time chasing replies, negotiating, revising content, rescheduling posts, fixing links, collecting analytics and managing usage rights. A lower-fee creator can cost more than a higher-fee one. Ask whether they can become a repeatable partner or you restart each month. "Creator CAC includes management bandwidth, so price it that way."
- Creator rates are set by brands outside your category. Your competitor for a creator may be a high-margin physical-product company that can pay far more for the same audience. A niche can have perfect fit and trusted creators and still be a terrible channel. Ask who else is bidding for their attention and what they can afford.
- Integration style depends on the product. If the product is instantly understood and the magic moment looks good on camera, let the creator weave it into normal content. If value needs context, subtle integration confuses the audience; use a direct problem-first explanation or demo. "Native is good. Confusing is not."
- Flat creator fees buy upside and variance. One breakout post gives asymmetric upside, but you pay full price when a post dies. Follower count isn't enough: you need realistic expected views, a read on performance volatility, and a price that works across many attempts, not the one outcome you daydream about.
- Creator outreach: the inbox preview is the first landing page. The recipient should immediately understand that (1) it's paid, (2) you chose them for a reason, (3) the ask is clear, (4) next steps are easy. If someone needs a call to understand the opportunity, you created friction before the relationship started.
- A creator spreadsheet is valuable because it exposes where the system fails:
  - Not enough replies: fix outreach or the list.
  - Creators agree but never post: fix onboarding, scheduling or incentives.
  - Posts go live but don't perform: fix selection, integration or angle.
  - Winners never renew: fix follow-up and deal structure.

## Execution, moats and process

- The visible tactic is almost never the moat. People constantly copied Cal AI's influencer strategy and Jake didn't worry. The hard part was finding creators daily, sending outreach, negotiating, reviewing content, tracking delivery, renewing winners, cutting losers and repeating faster than everyone else. "Information is easy to copy, but sustained execution volume is not."
- Do manual work before automation. Run a process manually until you know which steps repeat, which exceptions matter, what good performance looks like, where people get stuck and which decisions need judgment. Then automate the predictable parts. Automating too early "just scales your own messy setup."
- Move fast on reversible decisions (test the angle, ship the landing page, try the creator, change onboarding). Legal, safety, financial and high-cost decisions deserve a higher threshold. Speed is a weapon when you know which mistakes you can survive.
- Be wary of anyone who turns your problem into a 15-step proprietary framework before looking at the basic numbers. Complexity can be real, or part of the pitch. "Real expertise usually compresses the problem into the few variables that actually matter."

## Team, hiring and founder dynamics

- Hiring minimums differ by role. For an early marketing hire he often bets on a hungry person who learns fast and won't let the opportunity slip, since marketing has lots of surface area where reps, taste and effort compound. Technical work has a baseline motivation can't replace. Don't use one hiring rule for every function.
- Over-systemizing can lose ambitious people. Two early Cal AI marketing team members left after the work was systemized enough that it stopped challenging them. Pay keeps people comfortable but can't always keep an ambitious person engaged when learning stops. If your best people outgrow the work, create a new problem worth owning.
- Prefer complementary founders over identical "killers." The team needs different forms of leverage: someone who sees opportunities others miss, someone who reduces messy decisions to first principles, someone who can build almost anything, and someone who systemizes the work and gets it in front of people. "The team wins when the strengths multiply."
- Being a little successful can be more dangerous than being a beginner. After a small win, people stop taking feedback, assume the first win proved their worldview and protect the identity of being successful. The best operators eventually look like beginners again: curious, hardworking, open to being wrong.
- Understand luck. Separate what you controlled, earned, what others contributed, what happened at the perfect time, and what could have gone the other way. Crediting everything to your own brilliance means you'll try to repeat the wrong part.
- Money can't manufacture long-term interest. He spent years in management consulting earning more than ever and hated almost every day; at Cal AI he worked longer hours for almost no money and had more energy. If every week needs a new productivity system, the problem may be the thing you chose to work on.
- Reinvest after evidence, not ego. Early traction means the company may deserve more resources, not that you should act rich. Strengthen the product, upgrade the technical foundation, bring in great designers, pay for legal and financial help, fix what breaks at scale. "The lifestyle rewards can wait. The market window may not."

## AI as leverage for skilled operators

- Give the same AI tool to a beginner and an elite operator and the elite operator usually gets more leverage: they know what to build, recognize weak output, know which detail matters, and connect results to distribution.
- "AI lowers the cost of execution. It does not remove the value of taste, context or reps." In many markets he expects AI to widen the gap between skilled and unskilled operators.
