## How Cal AI found its influencer marketing playbook and the niche it ran on

- Jake credits co-founder Blake Anderson with the original playbook. Blake had scaled his app UMAX with influencer marketing, and Cal AI ran the same principles: "let's test this out with this influencer marketing strategy."
- Jake says it "just took off like we couldn't have imagined." The team's job was then to take the ball and scale it as high as possible.
- Jake picked the health and fitness niche because it is oversaturated with creators and short on brands. There are only so many protein powder and athleisure companies, so many creators made content but had no way to monetize.
- Cal AI offered those creators a brand deal. The content integration was seamless because they already post "what I eat in a day" and weight loss or muscle building videos that show their food. The ask was to scan the food with Cal AI before eating it on camera.
- Creators said yes because it was easy, did not interrupt their content schedule, and was the first time they could monetize their work.
- After signing creators, the next problem was managing 300+ influencers at once.

## Influencer vs UGC creators: how to work with each

- **Influencer:** an established creator with an existing audience and trust. They will not make every video about you. Maybe one or two videos a month. You are paying for the audience and trust they built.
- **UGC creator:** a regular person who makes a brand new account dedicated to your product (for example "Jake eats" with every video about Cali). They have around 10 followers, and videos only spread if the algorithm picks them up.
- **Influencers:** do not micromanage. "They're the artists. Let them paint." Give general integration guidelines only (how the app is shown, how many seconds in it appears, a caption tag), then "let them cook."
  - Cal AI gave full creative control. Influencers called it the easiest brand deal they ever had.
  - The common mistake is forcing an influencer into an infomercial-style script. The video then gets 50,000 views instead of the 1 million the CPM was priced on, and the brand is thousands of dollars in the red.
  - Have creators make their normal content and slide the app into it. That lets you estimate average views with confidence.
- **UGC creators:** micromanage them, since they have no audience. Steal viral formats that have already been proven.
- Jake sources UGC creators by searching "UGC creator" on the explore page and picking ones who feel like real people, not "NPC bots reading a script."
  - Sideshift has millions of creators and lets you post a job, but he thinks 99% of applicants are not good creators.
  - A brand new UGC account tends to be shown in the country the creator posts from. Influencers matter by where their audience is, not where they live, so ask for audience demographics.

## Testing mindset and risk tolerance

- Jake's motto is "ABT: always be testing." He says if you are not testing new things "you're leaving money on the table."
- Example: Cal AI tested moving the app integration earlier and earlier in the video to find the earliest point that did not hurt views. For Cal AI that landed in roughly the 20-30 second range.
- He warns against copying numbers bar for bar. A friend's app has its sweet spot at the 60-second mark, and copying Cal AI's timing would have cut his views. "What works for my app may not work for your app." Take the principle and test the specifics.
- Cal AI's co-founders were 17, 18 and 23, and Jake was the oldest with a mortgage and a girlfriend (now wife). He says the team's risk tolerance was far higher than his, and he held on to his seat during the crazy tests.
- His lesson: have an incredibly high risk tolerance, because big bets give the asymmetric payout, and "otherwise you're just never going to learn."
- Cal AI kept exploring new creator niches. Mukbang creators and UFC fighters did not work. New moms did.
- A winning format "exists on a spectrum." The first video showed signs of life and Cal AI kept optimizing. "Cali is not a finished product. Our marketing system is not done and it never will be."

## Pricing creators: RPM vs CPM, view bonuses and caps

- **Core rule:** the CPM only needs to be lower than your RPM (revenue per thousand views). Do not chase a fixed CPM. Measure how much revenue each thousand views produces, and keep your CPM under it.
- Competing for the same creators gets harder when other brands have far higher RPMs. A clothing brand with a $200 average order and $1,000 lifetime value can afford much higher CPMs than an app with a $30 order and $50 lifetime value.
- **CPM ceiling by channel:**
  - Paid ads can afford the highest CPM because the ad links straight to the app (least friction, highest conversion).
  - Influencers need a lower CPM because the viewer must leave the app, search the App Store and download.
  - UGC needs the lowest CPM because it has the same friction plus no brand trust from a brand new account.
- **Influencer deals:** a base rate plus view bonuses. Example from the talk: "$400 for four posts," asking for cross-posting to both platforms casually ("it's all good, you're already making the video"). Jake says creators usually agree if you do not make it a big deal.
- Estimate views conservatively: look at the creator's last 20 videos and average the 5 worst. He prices on that worst-case average, since all deal videos are unlikely to be the worst ones and anything above is profit.
- **View bonuses:** cap them, usually around 1 million views.
  - Most UGC videos get 500 to 1,000 views and lose money. The profit comes from the one or two videos a month that go viral, and paying for every view eats that upside.
  - People do not feel the difference between 1 million and 2 million, though it is double.
  - Very viral videos reach a broad, untargeted audience that converts worse. His example is Pizza Rat: "the first 100,000 viewers might" subscribe, but the later hundred million views were not worth much.
- **UGC pay structure:** about $25-30 per video as a base (roughly $600 a month), plus a view bonus. Example from the talk: a creator with one 1M-view video would be around $2 CPM, or roughly $1,000 total for about 1.5M views (roughly $1.50 CPM).

## Choosing influencers: views over followers

- Jake's contrarian take: "We don't care about followers." What matters is average views per video. A creator with 1M followers who gets 1,000 views speaks to only those 1,000 people.
- The best targets already get strong views but have few followers (for example averaging a million views with 500 followers). They are not used to big brand-deal rates, so a reasonable offer feels generous, while brands that pay by following give inflated rates.
- **View range:** his minimum is about 25,000 views per video, because an influencer with 10,000 views takes the same management effort as one with 1M views. He sees the sweet spot as roughly 25,000 to 1M views per video.
  - Creators getting millions of views have a more general, less loyal audience and are less likely to be trusted on recommendations.
  - Cult-like audiences convert best.
- **Comment sections signal conversion:** a comment section of "fire emoji, fire emoji" converts poorly. One with a real community converts far better (for example people thanking the creator and sharing their own struggles, with replies between commenters).
- **Detecting fake views and comments:** click the commenters' profiles. Bots have zero posts, follow about 10 people and have zero followers, and the same fake-looking accounts comment on every video. Jake says he has been burned this way himself.
- He treats TikTok and Instagram as roughly the same bucket. Some apps do about 1.2x better on one or the other, so "you're majoring in the minors" if you obsess over it.

## Download, paywall and conversion benchmarks

- Benchmark for organic content: **3 to 7 downloads per 1,000 views**.
- Funnel targets he cites: at least 75% of downloaders reach the paywall, and at least 10% of those subscribe.
- Content style does not matter to him as long as it converts: "I don't care what the content's like. As long as it converts, it's cool with me." That includes faceless content.
- **Attribution:** it gets harder with scale.
  - Cal AI now has millions of videos, and old videos still produce views, which pollutes the data.
  - Early on, every influencer video produced a visible download spike. With a baseline of around 100 downloads an hour, a post would push it to about 200, and they could tie the spike to that creator.
  - Early on, learn what comment sections and content formats look like for converting creators, then extrapolate from that.
  - At scale, "you have to almost become a data analyst," using methods like incrementality tests.

## Scaling operations: outreach and creator management

- Cal AI sent so many DMs that TikTok and Instagram blocked further messages for the day. The fix was creating about 20 additional Cal AI accounts, one per outreach specialist, each messaging hundreds of creators a day.
- Outreach tactics:
  - Put a name to the message so they know they are not talking to a faceless brand.
  - Send a voice memo instead of a wall of text. Record it once and reuse it across creators, so it feels personal but scales.
- Jake built an internal tool to manage creators at volume. One click sends a contract. After signing, the creator gets a text with the content brief and asks which day works. That day feeds a content calendar, and every morning the team gets a batch of texts to send to creators scheduled to post. He calls it his "magnum opus."

## When to add paid ads and how to find an app idea

- Paid ads: add them as soon as cash flow allows. Influencers and UGC are volatile because they depend on views, while Meta spending $500 a day "will consistently get your ROAS."
- At the start Cal AI used influencer and UGC content as its ad creative because it had no budget for anything else. Now paid ads are a full department with dedicated paid-ad creators. Paid creatives are written differently from organic ones because you pay for every view with no algorithmic push. The product appears earlier and is a harder sell ("hey guys, let me tell you about Cali").
- If he started from scratch today, with a different financial situation, Jake would begin with paid ads. Eighteen months ago he would have started with UGC.
- He says he and the team were also running web-to-app campaigns, and sees an arbitrage between Eastern Europe (heavy on web-to-app) and the US (heavy on influencer/UGC).
- **Finding an app idea:**
  - Pick a niche you are genuinely interested in. He says he woke up excited to build Cal AI, which he doubts would be true for a period tracker.
  - Think about apps you use daily and how to make them better, then take an existing app and make it 10% better or 10% less friction.
- On copycats: copying a successful app leads to limited outcomes. Cal AI was not the first calorie tracker; it took the concept and made it more interesting and better. "You're never going to make the money that you're hoping for" by blatantly copying.
