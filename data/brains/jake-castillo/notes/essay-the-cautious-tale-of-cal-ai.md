## Luck, not a template: Jake's view on how Cal AI's founding team came together

- Jake's most common founder question is how to find a group like the four Cal AI founders (cracked co-founders, CEO, COO, CTO, designers, devs).
- The team of four got along through roughly two and a half years of intense stress. Two were still in high school when they started, and Henry's mom had to co-sign for the company bank account.
- Cal AI went from nothing to #1 in Health & Fitness and $50M ARR in eighteen months, then the company was sold.
- His answer is blunt: "We got extremely lucky." He calls that kind of team "a freak thing" that does not happen repeatedly, even compared with something like the PayPal mafia.
- He says he has "watched enough founding teams up close now" to know how unusual it was.

## Hard work and smarts increase surface area for luck

- Jake is explicitly not saying outcomes are random.
- He used to believe the hardest working and smartest people win. He no longer does, because "often the luckiest people win."
- Working hard and being smart "just increases your surface area for luck." He calls that real and most of what a founder can control.
- Luck can still happen without a big surface area, and "people do all the time."
- He says pretending otherwise is "a lie that makes everyone reading it feel worse about themselves."

## Why Cal AI's origin story is not a template

- Jake argues people read the origin story as something engineered, which it never was.
- The chain of events that led him to Zach Yadegari and Cal AI:
  - A YouTube video his cousin sent him about Tabs Chocolate.
  - That led him to hear Oliver Brocato talk about internet money and his businesses.
  - He followed Oliver on X and happened to be scrolling when Oliver tweeted that he needed a COO.
  - He cold DMed Oliver, never expecting a response.
  - Oliver saw it and asked to jump on a call.
  - Jake started moonlighting for Oliver at Studybuddy.
  - Zach happened to be interning at Studybuddy too.
- His takeaway: if you are waiting to assemble a once-in-a-lifetime, "Avengers style" co-founder group who are brilliant, get along, and are available, "you might die waiting."

## Great people are expensive, hard to find, and already have options

- Jake's core hiring principle: "You have to pay to play."
- He compares cheap great talent to "a gold watch on the beach," and says once you find it, it only gets more expensive over time.
- Great candidates usually do not need you. They already have a solid job, and you are asking them to risk joining something that might not exist in a year.
- So even after you get a few solid candidates, you have to sell them hard on why they should work with you.

## Use a headhunter for vetted talent

- For finding vetted top talent fast, Jake recommends a headhunter.
- Fees usually run around 20% of first-year salary, which he says is "100% worth it."
- His reasoning: sourcing great talent is mind-numbing work, and most founders have not hired enough people to be good at it.
- When someone says they will not pay that fee, he tells them they will not get great talent.

## Be generous with equity

- If you are running a startup, Jake says to be prepared to be generous with equity.
- This applies especially when someone has the intangibles you are looking for.

## Hire for availability and ability to learn, not experience alone

- Jake does not hire for experience alone. He hires for availability and for whether someone can and wants to learn.
- The two questions he asks: "Is this person actually here, and can they figure out something they do not currently know?"
- He says those two things predict more than any resume he has glanced over.
- Experience can work against you. An experienced hire arrives certain about how they like to do things, and that way is often not compatible with, or best for, what the company needs.
