## Jake builds for the silent majority, not the loud minority

- Jake argues founders are power users of their own product, and in consumer apps this is a costly bias. From the inside it just feels like caring about product quality.
- He says the second version of the mistake is the handful of users who are furious about a missing feature and ask for it repeatedly. In his view this is usually a misleading demand signal.
- His default is to ignore or delay the off-the-wall requests from users and put power-user projects on the backburner.
- He says to "always be building apps for the majority first, not the minority."
- Power users are die-hard fans and ambassadors who want the most out of the app, but they are a small share of those who open it.
- His rule for evaluating a feature: "this feature is the best thing we built" may be true, yet it can serve only about 5% of the people who will open the app tomorrow.

## The gym analogy: mass-market products are designed for the average user

- Jake points to mass-marketed gyms (Planet Fitness, Crunch Fitness, Anytime Fitness), which follow a similar blueprint.
- The floor is about 80% cardio equipment, a section of muscle-isolation machines, and a few benches and squat racks.
- Most members are walking on a treadmill, because that is exercise to them and what they pay for.
- Only about 10% keep detailed workout logs and track every set, rep, weight and everything they eat.
- Building a commercial gym for that 10% would mean building a gym most members do not want to be in.
- His point: the biggest chains in the country make this call deliberately, so app founders should make it too.
- Most users of an app are basic users. They do not need or want all the features built for the few.

## Cal AI's Android mistake: a real number attached to the wrong population

- Jake says Cal AI listened to the loud minority while scaling, and it went badly.
- The team built iOS first. Users then kept asking when Android was launching ("When's the Android app launching?", "Why isn't this on Android?", "We NEED this on Android.").
- On paper the logic looked airtight: Android is roughly 70% of global smartphone users. The team built it expecting the market to at least double.
- After launch, the app "MAYBE grew 10%."
- His explanation:
  - iPhone users are predominantly in the U.S.
  - Android users make up most of the rest of the world, where disposable income is lower and paying for an app is much less culturally normal.
  - People outside the US who do have money to spend on apps are, by and large, already carrying an iPhone.
- His conclusion: "the 70% was never addressable. It was a real number attached to the wrong population."
- Lesson: check whether a big addressable-market statistic maps to people who actually pay before building for it.

## Why founders hear the wrong people: you only sample from the group that talks

- Power users talk to you through behavior. They are in the app constantly, hit every corner, and their usage shows up loudest in analytics. You build for what you see, and what you see is them.
- Complainers talk through feedback channels: endless comments, a full support inbox, reviews. You hear them, so you build for them too.
- The roughly 95% silent majority is silent in both directions. They do not leave feature requests and do not show up as power sessions. They open the app, get what they came for or do not, and stay or leave quietly.
- Jake says the silent majority is also "almost all of your revenue."
- His warning: your loudest signals and your biggest revenue segment are, 99% of the time, two different groups of people.

## Two things to do this week: simplify the first screen and score requests before building

1. Audit the first screen:
   - Open the app and count how many elements on the first screen serve fewer than 10% of users.
   - Move every one of them a tap or layer deeper, and simplify everything.
2. Before building the next thing everybody is asking for, write down three numbers:
   - How many people actually asked.
   - What share of them would pay for it.
   - What share of the silent majority would ever touch it.
