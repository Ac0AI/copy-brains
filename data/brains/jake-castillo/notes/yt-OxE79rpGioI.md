## Cal AI go-to-market: how Jake got to 15M users and $40M ARR

- Jake joined Cal AI as CMO and COO two days after quitting his consulting job.
- Cal AI launched in May 2024 and was acquired in December 2025, 18 months later.
- By then it had 15 million users, 300+ influencer and creator partnerships, and $40M ARR.
- Jake's first role was scaling the influencer program. He spent his days on calls with influencers, reviewing posts, and building systems to manage, track and attribute revenue across 300 of them.
- The pitch was a calorie tracker where you "just take a picture" of your food and the app calculates calories and macros.
- Jake's view of the old way: tracking by typing everything in "turns tracking your food into homework."
- On the 18-month run, he said the team "outworked everyone in the space."

## Saturation, differentiation, and niching down

- Jake disagrees that saturation is a problem. A crowded market means people are actively engaged in that niche. If your product is better, the opportunity is there.
- Cal AI's edge was removing friction. The car-vs-horse-and-buggy analogy was his: people switch once they see the easier mode.
- Don't build a clone. Unless you improve on incumbents, you can grab some share with competent marketing but never reach something great.
- Look for industries that feel stagnant and haven't changed in about 5 years.
- Any feature can be made sexy with a unique spin. Calorie tracking wasn't seen as fun before Cal AI; they made it fun.
- His niche example is a workout tracker for new moms:
  - Pick a very specific avatar, since there are too many generalists and not enough "super targeted painpoint problem solvers."
  - Niche first, then marketing angle, then UI.
  - The niche tells you your ICP, what influencers and UGC creators should look like, and what paid ads should look like.
  - Make the UI look like the apps that audience already uses, so the app isn't intimidating.
- He would avoid social apps because of the cold start problem: you need a network effect to get a network effect.

## UGC strategy for apps

- UGC is where Jake would start if launching an app today. Influencer prices have risen, while UGC creator prices have not really increased.
- The game is a swing at the algorithm. About 90% of UGC videos break even or lose money; the 10% that go viral make the money.
- Deal structure:
  - Start with low-risk, trial-based deals and no big upfront commitments.
  - Pay flat fees, not pay-per-view. Pay-per-view means you pay for all the views but give up the upside of a viral video.
  - His line: "Don't pay for viral hits."
  - Going viral is "not an if, it's a when" if you take enough shots on goal.
- Pick one of three formats (talking head, wall-of-text, skit) and only sign creators with a proven viral record in that exact format.
- Jake prefers going "deep and narrow" over "far and wide." Competitors get bored and chase shiny objects, so staying focused is where the success comes from.
- Without discipline it can be a money pit. He warns it's possible to spend $10K on UGC and end up at a $100 CPM.

## Influencer marketing: when it works and how to pick creators

- Influencers vs UGC creators:
  - With an influencer you pay for the trust they have with their existing audience.
  - With a UGC creator you pay for a swing at the algorithm on a new account dedicated to your brand.
- Check three things before choosing influencers:
  1. There are a lot of creators in your niche.
  2. You aren't competing with brands whose LTV and average order value let them outbid you. Example: a fashion brand with ~$1,000 LTV vs an app with $10/month and ~$30 LTV.
  3. The creators have high-value audiences.
- Cal AI's situation fit this:
  - Health and fitness had millions of creators.
  - Most weren't monetizing through brand deals, because few brands were available (protein powders, a few fitness fashion brands) and many paid only commission.
  - Creators were desperate for deals, so Cal AI signed hundreds at favorable CPMs.
  - Their audiences were cult-like ("I want to look just like you").
- Jake's creator-quality test is the comment section, a lesson from co-founder Blake Anderson:
  - Look for whether the creator connects with their audience and has real conversations.
  - Back-end revenue numbers shared by agencies or creators can't be trusted.
  - "You can't fake the comment section."

## Paid ads and choosing a channel by stage

- Early on, Cal AI ran influencer videos as paid ads in Meta, with decent results.
- Jake says they misunderstood what you pay for in paid ads:
  - UGC: a swing at the algorithm.
  - Influencers: audience trust.
  - Paid ads: clicks, one to one, with no algorithmic lift from virality.
- So paid ad creative should be hard-selling. UGC and influencer content has to hide the ad so viewers don't swipe; paid ads don't.
- Cal AI found success with paid ads once they made dedicated paid ad creative instead of reusing seamless organic influencer videos.
- Paid ads should have the highest view-to-subscription conversion, since the click goes straight to the app store. If they don't, "we got to look at your paid ads."
- Highest converting is not the same as most profitable. A $100 UGC video with 10M views can beat $100 in Meta on cash-on-cash return even with a poor conversion rate.
- Jake's stage framework:
  - No money: UGC. Everyone has a phone and can create the content themselves.
  - Some budget: influencers.
  - Cash flow isn't a concern and you're scaling: paid ads. Done right it's a "money printing machine" (a dollar in, two out). It isn't the most profitable but it is the most scalable.
- He notes the pattern: beginners say paid ads, the middle plays influencers and UGC, and the people making hundreds of millions per year say paid ads are the only place to play.
- On AI UGC and AI influencers, he says it doesn't matter: "good marketing material is good marketing material," and a cheap 50-cent CPM on bad content still converts at zero. AI UGC wasn't good enough while Cal AI was scaling, so they didn't use it.

## Pricing and paywall testing

- Cal AI priced below every competitor on purpose. The calorie tracking TAM was huge, and the aim was to gain mindshare by being the cheaper alternative.
- The target audience was young and chronically online, with less disposable income. Cheaper pricing also made it easy to convert users of other apps.
- Pricing depends on TAM:
  - A broad, general-population app that "talks to everyone" should price lower so price is never the deciding factor.
  - A smaller, targeted app (like the new-moms workout planner) can charge more, because the TAM is small and the avatar thinks "this is for me."
- Tactical rule of thumb:
  - Benchmark competitors, then start 20% cheaper.
  - Go 20% higher to be the prestige option.
  - Match their prices for bar-for-bar.
  - Then keep testing.
- Testing cadence at Cal AI:
  - About four paywall tests a week, roughly 300 tests over 18 months.
  - Having fewer than one or two live tests at a time meant they were "dropping the ball."
  - Individual tests were estimated to add $50,000, $20,000 or $10,000 in monthly revenue.
  - Each test took about 20 to 30 minutes to launch.
- Start testing once you get about 100 users a day.
- Why LTV matters most: "the way apps win is who can spend the most to acquire a customer," and LTV determines that. "The only way you will be able to durably win is if you can acquire customers at a higher cost than your competitors."

## AI in apps, hiring, and co-founders

- Most "AI" apps add little value. Jake sees real value now in agentic use cases, where AI does the work.
  - Example: co-founder Henry Langmack's app Soore books flights end to end, including seat preference, passport info, check-in, and rebooking.
  - His filter: if your mom knows about the tool, it's popular enough that you have to provide real value, with no misinformation arbitrage left.
- Co-founders:
  - Align on the mission first (pump and dump, sell, or change the world). Misalignment is "a recipe for disaster."
  - Pick complementary skills (technical plus creative), not similar ones.
  - He joined Cal AI to be near Zach Yadegari, "the smartest person I've ever met."
- Hiring at Cal AI:
  - For hard-to-teach skills (e.g. AI systems), hire experienced people.
  - For easy-to-teach skills (e.g. marketing, which is mostly basic principles), hire for "hunger and drive and work ethic."
  - Every marketer Cal AI hired had no prior experience. Jake wanted a blank canvas with no bad habits to unlearn.
  - He preferred people without other opportunities: "who's the least picky eater in the world? Someone that's been starving."
- His closing advice: it's "not that hard" if you take the bet on yourself, execute every day, and outwork the competition.
- When he left his job, his goal was $10K a month; at points Cal AI did that in an hour.
