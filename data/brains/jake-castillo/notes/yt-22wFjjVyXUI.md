## Choosing creators and niches: the "magic moment" and creator-app fit

- Jake's thesis for Cal AI: a calorie tracker is used most by people into health and fitness. That niche has more creators than any other, but few brands to work with (protein powder, athleisure), so many creators had never had a brand deal.
- Creators already made "what I eat in a day" videos, about one a week, so Cal AI slotted in by scanning the food on camera.
- Dawson proved the format converted. The team then copied and pasted it across the many fitness creators making the same content.
- He says the playbook only works if those conditions hold for your app. Example: an astrology app has no millions of creators, and the few brands in that space have likely signed exclusivity deals.
- Cal AI had a "magic moment": a one-two-three punch of scan, analyze, results. Viewers did a double take ("Whoa, what was it?").
- Without a magic moment, an integration gets blinked past and won't convert. In that case he says paid ads with a hard sell fit better.
- The magic moment must be digestible in seconds and need no long explanation. Otherwise it feels like an ad, won't go viral and won't be profitable.

## Small product details that made creator content work

- The team built a creator-specific build of the app. The scan screen showed the Cal AI name and logo so the marketing message was clear.
- Cal AI has a streaks feature. The streak popup appeared after a scan and interrupted the three-step sequence, so creators were told not to show their first scan of the day.
- Jake notes people digest things in threes, and the streak added an unwanted fourth beat.
- With subtle integrations, they added an on-screen caption "tracked with Cal AI". With less subtle ones, they dropped the caption because it hurt views.
- His rule: "no marketing program should ever feel like a finished product." Keep testing and adapting.

## Building creator relationships and why copycats failed

- Dawson was one of the first creators contacted. Jake says he did anything asked, iterated on formats and angles, and never nickel-and-dimed. The team gave him bonuses and he became a close friend.
- Jake's advice: early on you have few creators, so there is no excuse not to build personal relationships. A creator who loves working with you refers friends and shouts about you; a miserable brand gets no referrals.
- If you treat it as strictly transactional, it stays transactional.
- The team built publicly on Twitter and shared wins and failures, which drew many clones. Jake wasn't worried: the clones weren't obsessed the way he was. He says he dreamed about signing influencers.
- Small incremental improvements compound into a mountain competitors can't climb.
- Clone coverage sent traffic to Cal AI: Twitter threads about clones' revenue, and searches for "AI calorie tracker" that surfaced Cal AI first in Google and the App Store.
- Hard work and dedication increase your "surface area for luck."

## Reading comments to qualify niches and creators

- Quote: "views can lie, followers can lie, but comments cannot. The truth is always in the comments."
- Niche test: read the comments on big creators in that space. If people are looking for a solution your app provides, go full steam ahead.
- Creator test: for roughly every 10 creators, nine won't convert and one will. Comments tell you which.
- Bad sign: a comment section of fire emojis and sweating-face emojis (for example, under a female creator's leg-day video).
- Good sign: comments like "thank you for posting this, I didn't know how to work my hamstrings/quads/glutes."
- You want a cult-like following, not just a following. It is a lagging indicator. Great creators will get one, so look for diamonds in the rough with good content and low views, and sign them cheaper.
- Example: a creator who was about the 12th to 15th Jake signed was getting 10-12k views. In month three one video hit 7 million, and he never fell below 500k after. Jake connected him with other brands.

## Niches that failed and what he learned

- UFC creators: the thesis was that fighters track calories to cut weight. Several big creators were signed, with thousands of dollars spent each, and posted to crickets, with no download spike.
- Why it failed: the typical UFC viewer is on a couch with beer and pizza. They want to watch someone else do a restrictive diet, not track their own.
- Mukbang creators: eating 10-20k calories on camera looked like a big wow factor. It also failed because viewers see it as a reward or cheat day and don't want calorie information.
- Lesson: understand not just what content a creator makes but why viewers consume it and their psychological motivations.
- Big bold bets were a core value. For every deal that loses a couple thousand dollars, one wins hundreds of thousands. After a failure, dig into why it failed instead of skipping it.

## Pricing creators: RPM cap, flat rates and bonuses

- The real money in influencer and UGC marketing is in viral hits. Set your break-even on a creator's average views, and the occasional mega-viral video is profit.
- Flat-rate deals capture that asymmetric upside, so use them whenever possible. Jake also used bonuses and scaled deals.
- Step 1: know your revenue per thousand views (RPM) in the niche. You cannot pay a CPM above that RPM or you are guaranteed to lose money ("it's just math"). Some creators will say no, and that is fine.
- Step 2: estimate the creator's expected views and set a profitable CPM-based rate on that.
- When a creator asks for something like $1,000 a month, meeting their cash demand often lets you raise the deliverables.
- Be human, not a "sleazy agent." If it doesn't work, decline kindly and refer them to a friend who fits.
- Early on, RPM is easy to see: no posts means no downloads, so a post shows as a spike (for example, creator X got 1,000 views and $Y revenue). Jake wasn't sure of the exact figure and says it's either a $10 or $1 RPM, so treat that number as unconfirmed.
- At scale, attribution is murky. When 50 to 70 influencers post per day, you analyze secondary metrics (comments, likes, saves) and incrementality tests, such as looking at the demographics of a creator's following and where the spike appears.
- Jake created a "renewal score" at Cal AI that combined many metrics to decide whether to renew a creator.
- He gave bonuses even when not required ("dude you crushed it this month"). He says nobody does that and it pays off over time.

## Sourcing and managing creators at scale

- The secret is working really hard for a really long time. The team sent thousands of messages for 18 months, most went unanswered, many replies demanded rates they couldn't afford, and every negotiation was "pulling teeth."
- It never stopped being a grind: "we weren't stopping until we were the number one calorie tracker in the world."
- In 2026 creators are savvier, charge higher rates and have negotiation courses. Demand rose while supply stayed flat. Jake says it is harder but also easier, with tools like Codex and Claude Code. If it's hard to sign creators, your competitors won't do it.
- Management consulting taught him systems. Early on he ran everything in Google Sheets (his "magnum office"), later built formal software.
- That software, Rally, is built for influencer and UGC marketing. It stores deals, automates contract sending, auto-tracks posts and views, computes view bonuses and payment amounts, and gives a renewal score. He planned to launch around the end of October 2026.
- He also does consulting; people can reach him via Twitter and LinkedIn.

## Paid ads, funnel congruency and pricing

- Jake says paid ads are easier than influencers: creative can be bought, and tools like Tribe let you get creative for free and pay a percentage of ad spend (aligned incentives). Influencers have friction in outreach, negotiation, management and murky attribution.
- Cal AI began spending on paid roughly a couple of months in, and paid grew as a share of the marketing mix. He says to diversify risk.
- At first they recycled influencer content. Later they invested in dedicated paid creative, which performed far better.
- Why: organic content swings for a viral home run with a subtle integration. In paid you pay for every view, so make every view understand the product with a hard sell. The hook must relate to the product (for example, a baseball hook for a pitching app, not an accounting app).
- He learned a lot from the Speechify team on paid creative.
- Keep the ad, onboarding and app experience congruent, like a hotel lobby that matches its rooms. In onboarding, hit the pain points from the marketing, drive home the impact of the pain, then present the solution.
- Pricing: Cal AI is about $30 a year. Co-founder Blake Anderson insisted they optimize for mindshare, not profit. The audience is young with little disposable income and hasn't suffered the problem as long, so it hurts less and they pay less.
- Jake's warning: copying what Cal AI did (or something from a podcast) is the easiest way to burn money, because your app isn't ours. Think about your market, pain point and motivation.
- Affiliates: anyone could sign up in the app and earn $10 per sale (not per download), a one-time payment, about 33% on gross.
