## Getting from $9.50 in the bank to a $50M ARR exit: the founding path

- Jake was a management consultant who hated the job, riding a two-hour train each way and watching YouTube.
- A video of Oliver Brcado explaining how Tabs chocolate went from zero to millions a month online showed him a path he hadn't known existed. He followed Oliver on Twitter for weeks, unsure it was real.
- When Oliver tweeted that he needed a head of operations for Study Buddy, Jake DM'd him offering to fix operations for free. His pitch: "All we do is go into businesses to streamline their operations." Oliver replied within an hour.
- He moonlighted on Study Buddy after work. There he met Zach Yadegari, who was 16 and shadowing Oliver. Jake spent time making sure Zach actually learned things (paid ads, org structure).
- Jake quit his corporate job and left Study Buddy. Two days later Zach called about an app he'd been building and asked Jake to run operations and scaling. That became Cal AI.
- Cal AI was fully bootstrapped and reinvested everything for over a year before the founders took out any money.
- Jake had a mortgage, student loans and a fiancée. He burned his savings, pulled from his 401k, borrowed from his girlfriend, and fought daily to avoid overdrafts.
- Cal AI went from early stage to $50M a year in recurring revenue and an exit to MyFitnessPal in about 18 months, with no outside funding.
- Jake's framing: as revenue rose he felt like the poorest he'd ever been, because nothing came out of the business.

## The core growth engine: influencer "what I eat in a day" integrations

- The first test was a fitness influencer posting a "what I eat in a day" video and scanning each meal with Cal AI. Downloads spiked instantly, bigger than anything they had seen.
- His consulting-derived rule: "when you see something that works, don't try and reinvent the wheel. Just try and replicate and do it over and over until it stops working."
- They signed as many fitness influencers as possible and asked for the same format. Every video crushed it.
- One team kept signing new fitness creators. Another explored new niches (teenage girls, new moms) to see whether the same format would replicate.
- Once a formula works in a niche, find as many influencers as possible in it and have them copy and paste it.
- Jake's view: the hard work is sustained volume of this loop, not a secret formula.

## Fitting the product into each creator's existing content

- When expanding to new niches, Jake does not make creators change their format. He asks how the app can fit into their normal content.
- The reason is economics. Sponsored posts that deviate from a creator's usual style tend to be their worst performers.
- Profitability is calculated off CPM based on the creator's average views. The goal is to make the floor of a sponsored video their average views, with the ceiling being an outlier hit.
- Example: if a TikTok dancer has never done "what I eat in a day," forcing that format risks a video that badly underperforms.
- Seamless vs hard sell depends on two questions about the product:
  - Is it easy to understand?
  - Does it have a magic moment?
- If yes to both, do a seamless integration. Viewers think "wait, what was that?" and understand it immediately.
- Cal AI fit this: everyone knows calorie tracking, and snapping a photo to get calories was jaw-dropping in early 2024.
- If the product is harder to understand or has no wow moment, use a more explicit hard sell.

## Picking niches: supply, demand and price

- Fitness had many creators relative to advertiser demand, which gave favorable pricing versus mass UGC or paid ads.
- Niches where supply is thin and demand is high are expensive or locked up by exclusivity deals.
- Fashion is a bad fit for apps: brands there have an order value in the hundreds of dollars (up to about $200) and can pay high CPMs, while an app's order value is about $30 to $50. Skincare and other consumer packaged goods have the same problem.
- Jake's under-used opportunity: college kids.
  - There are many of them and few brands know how to work with them.
  - They need some money but not much, and have time to spare, so they accept lower CPMs than white-collar professionals.
  - He says he doesn't see many apps using them successfully.

## Vetting influencers in 10 to 20 seconds

Jake reviewed over 10,000 influencer profiles and worked with roughly 300 for Cal AI. His checklist:

- **Views:** at least about 25,000 plus views per video. Managing a 20,000-view creator takes the same effort as a 1M-view creator (messaging, scheduling, reviewing posts, comments, renewals, payment), so spend bandwidth on bigger fish.
- **Comment quality:** he wants real comments like "thank you for posting this workout, I had no idea what to do in the gym, you're a lifesaver," not strings of fire or flexing emojis.
- **Replies within comment threads:** people replying to each other, even disagreeing, show a real community. "True communities convert better than anything else."
- **Parasocial connection:** does the creator talk to the audience (talking head, voiceover, personality) or just show their body. Talking creators build the relationship that leads to conversions.
- Final gut test he cites: does this person look like someone you'd want to be friends with.
- Three videos of scrolling is usually enough.

## Writing outreach DMs creators actually open

- Start from first principles: put yourself in the creator's shoes. They get 100+ DMs a day, so most outreach is ignored.
- The message preview is what gets opened. Lead with "paid promo" and that you want to pay them.
- Skip the life story, the bootstrapped-founder pitch or telling them you're a fan. "Everyone is selfishly motivated."
- Keep it short: paid promo, want to make a video for me, I'll pay X. The goal is an easy yes or no, or the start of a negotiation.
- Whether to state a dollar figure depends on the creator:
  - A college kid new to paid promos: mention a figure, since even $100 impresses.
  - A wealthy, experienced creator such as a stay-at-home mom with many brand deals: don't open with a number. Say "I want to do a paid promo with you, what would you be looking for?" and let her start the money conversation.
- Pay upfront and say so. Creators have been burned by sketchy brands, so this removes their risk and shows you have the money, which matters since Jake looks young.
- Same principle applies in reverse for pitching him: he turns down "take you out for coffee" invites because an hour for a $5 coffee isn't worth his bandwidth, and he responds to concrete value (for example, proof of prior reach).

## Operating cadence and the volume behind the results

- Jake rejects the idea that copycats are a threat. He says nobody else will do the volume and the hours, and that whoever works hardest wins.
- His routine during Cal AI's growth:
  - Up around 6 a.m. Pacific to overlap with co-founders on Eastern time.
  - Reviewed prior-day posts and overnight submissions from international influencers, gave feedback.
  - Calls with influencers all day, fixing tracking sheets and sending contracts between calls.
  - Also handled accounting, legal and meetings with his co-founders.
  - From about 6 p.m. to midnight Pacific, when co-founders were asleep, was his deep-work block.
  - Often fell asleep with the laptop on his lap, and sometimes did not leave the house from Monday to Thursday.
  - Roughly two years of 20-hour days.
- Speed mattered: if an influencer replied and had five minutes to talk, he jumped on a call immediately, including middle-of-the-night calls with creators in Eastern Europe.
- On hunger: "Anyone can work for 20 hours a day for one day." What separates great from average is doing it with no end in sight.
- His test for yourself: if you are asking whether you are working hard enough, you are not.

## Advice for people thinking of leaving a job to build apps

- Corporate jobs aren't safe, since someone else signs your paycheck. "The safest bet is the bet you take on yourself."
- There are people earning 10x more who are 10x less capable, so go do it.
- The path is simple but not easy: find an idea you like, build it so it works, and get it in front of people for less than it costs them to buy.
- Specifically: acquire customers at a cost lower than what they pay. If you do that, "you're going to be fine."
- There is no secret hack. "The secret hack is working your ass off for an unbelievably long time."
- He advises surrounding yourself with exceptional people. His three co-founders are his biggest inspirations: Blake Anderson for creative ideation, Zach Yadegari for logical first-principles thinking, and Henry for technical ability.
