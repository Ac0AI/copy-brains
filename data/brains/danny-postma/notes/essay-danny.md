## Landing pages: Danny's repeatable conversion template

- Danny has used the same hero for 5 to 10 years: picture on the right, a three-point checklist, title, subtitle, a really good button, and only one call to action. He calls it "CRO 101".
- The page works through the checklist from Robert Cialdini's *Influence*: social proof (people already using it), authority (logos) and scarcity. He says someone who doesn't know marketing can learn it from that book.
- Scarcity: every time he removed the scarcity banner on profile picture AI, conversion dropped. He never lies about it, so he keeps finding new honest offers. A "buy one, get one free" banner became "40 bonus photos", and he says he won't bring either back.
- He shows real examples on the page and lists only the photos and headers that converted best.
- Copy is the most common mistake he sees. Founders know how to explain their product but not how to market it.
  - His advice: use an AI copywriter, describe what the product does, and put the output on the page.
  - "You don't even need a design": a blank page with a photo, title, subtitle and call to action is enough.
  - "People buy the template, make it fancy, but the copywriting is wrong." If visitors don't understand it in 500 milliseconds, they're gone. "You can't polish a turd."
- There are three ways to pitch: tell, have others tell (testimonials), or show. He says the best page does all three.
- Keep it simple: show a problem, present the solution, and say why you are the right person to deliver it. No "sketchy" fancy titles.
- A hyper-focused product has a clear "why" that can be explained in a second. That "why" keeps motivation high, which broader products like Intercom lose.
- He learned landing page design 10 to 13 years ago from the site One Page Love.

## Pricing: lifetime deals first, subscriptions later

- For long-term revenue he says MRR wins, because money keeps coming in without constant marketing if churn is low.
- To validate an idea he prefers a one-time payment. He has been switching many products to one-time pricing "because it's so much easier to get sales".
- He launched StockAI with a subscription and got about ten subscribers. A one-time payment let people try it faster.
- Headlime launched as a lifetime deal, and he added subscriptions later. The sequence is:
  - Get people into the product and talking about it.
  - Add the features they actually want.
  - Then layer a subscription on top. "An expensive subscription is not going to work" at the start.
- Early lifetime buyers are "your core fans, your actual investors". They took the risk, and he thinks they deserve to be rewarded for life.
- Headlime's lifetime plan cost $49 for roughly five years of a GPT-3 copywriter, about $1 a month. When he sold the company he negotiated that Headlime stays online and lifetime holders keep their plan. It was still online two years later.
- Some people called him a sellout anyway. He says the deal was good and he added many features.
- He found a black market for lifetime deals. People resold $49 plans for thousands of dollars on Facebook Marketplace, and some bought 20 to 30 as investments.
- He learned about it when a Twitter follower with about 300 followers posted Headlime in a lifetime-deal Facebook group before launch. Danny had just read *Influence* and was applying lifetime deals and scarcity, and he didn't yet know what AppSumo was.
- Resellers sell old Zapier-style lifetime deals to big companies for around $20,000.

## Competing on AI products, clones and avatar hype

- Profile picture AI made $50,000 in its first two weeks. Margins collapsed when Lensa priced at $2.99, below cost, as a loss leader. Danny calls them "the Amazon of AI". He still makes a few dozen sales a day.
- His view is that avatars will be commoditized and the trend will fade after Christmas, so "you've got to milk it now". Then he builds a product around what people actually want.
- His long-term edge is SEO. He got lucky choosing the "profile picture" name because people search for it. After the hype he plans to focus on SEO and be first in that market.
- Mistake he names: he didn't build a mobile app in week one. He should have hired someone, because search interest moved to the App Store and he missed those downloads. He tried learning React Native in about ten days, then dropped it once the hype faded.
- When everyone uses the same models (Jasper, Copy.ai and others use the same tech), the only differentiators are marketing and a crisp UI. "If your UI sucks, why would anyone use your tool?"
- Lensa won on distribution: an existing app with millions of users, push notifications, and Apple handling payments.
- Lensa also grew by hiring 30 to 40 influencers in Indonesia who all posted on Instagram.
- Cloning is a positive: Headlime clones forced him to build Headlime 2.0. Without them he says he might have "chilled" and not built the next product.
- Ship in public. Pieter Levels and he got cloned 100 times, but without public shipping nobody would know the products. "No one is going to copy a bad product." If you're too scared of clones you never take off, and clones come once you succeed anyway.
- He now keeps quiet about the "secret sauce": AI tricks and SEO tactics. He still shares revenue because it drives engagement.
- You can only know what to build by listening to customers. Headlime started as a headline generator. After launch customers asked for emails and Facebook ads, and someone suggested GPT-3. "You cannot build it in secret for a year."

## Build stack: use infrastructure to move fast

- Stack for recent products: Next.js, MongoDB, hosted on Heroku. He says the stack "doesn't matter at all".
- He tried self-hosting and called it "hell". It worked, but scaling was a problem because it ran single-threaded.
- AI providers he named: Replicate (you can request a model from GitHub and they upload it), Hugging Face, and a hosted Dreambooth service for the DreamBooth products.
  - Risk: they set the prices and can change them. "If they're out then you're out."
  - Still, without them he wouldn't have earned the profile picture money.
- Rule: validate first, then build your own stuff. "Use infrastructure. It creates speed, and speed is your validation and your actual low investment."
- "You pay five times more for the API, but you save 2 to 3 weeks of your time while someone else could have launched. That time is already worth all the money."
- Web3 experience: he built a smart contract project and quit after 4 to 5 months. Contracts can't be updated, so one bug could lose users' money. A single contract took three months with security work. That pace didn't suit someone who ships in a day and builds an app in 30 days.

## Domains: .com, leasing and brand names

- Stock AI domain: he leases stock.ai (spoken as "stock AI") at $300 a month on a 60-month installment plan, about $18,000 total against a $20,000 asking price. He owns it after 60 payments.
  - If the idea fails he cancels anytime and loses only what he paid. He paid $600 for the first two months.
  - He calls it genius for founders: a good brand name for a few hundred dollars if validation fails.
- He bought headlime.com for $3,000 after launching on headlime.io. At the time that was about 10% of what he'd earned. His top Google query was "headlime com", because people didn't know the domain and typed .com.
- He thinks the .com helps many companies, since someone could take it and build a competitor. He doesn't know if it affected the Jasper acquisition.
- Advice: you can start with .io and upgrade to .com later. He cites a founder who paid $30,000 for the .com of a testimonials company and recouped it through an affiliate link on mistyped traffic.
- He says he has a "brilliant" .com idea for about $3,000 as a follow-up and will buy it once he has a company to put on it. He declined to name it.
- Naming: people heard "Headlime" on podcasts and it stuck. "Branding is everything."

## Exit, money and personal finance

- He sold Headlime to Jasper for a seven-figure sum. The sale took three weeks, he worked there one week, and it closed after four weeks. He was burned out, so he didn't push hard. He says he "could maybe have gotten double", but isn't complaining. There is a non-compete until 2024.
- Lessons:
  - Hire a lawyer to check the contract and add solid clauses (he did).
  - Consult a tax advisor before selling, not after.
  - Talk to people who've sold before negotiating.
- He calls himself financially independent: "you don't have to do things you don't like". Before the sale he made short-term decisions out of stress. Now he focuses on long-term things and new learning.
- He is conservative: he bought a laptop and a house and invested the rest. He treats it as retirement money paying dividends, and new startups are for a nicer life now (for example, flying home more often).
- He found startup motivation from building things he uses himself: "you made a product for yourself", and thousands of others liked what he liked.

## Distribution, markets and team

- His wife ran the Indonesian push for profile picture AI. She contacted influencers, who posted on Instagram (2 and 3 million followers), and it drove lots of traffic from Indonesia. The catch: many people there don't have credit cards and Stripe doesn't cover local payments, so conversion was limited to people with PayPal.
- Local payment methods are super apps and QR codes. He thinks an Indonesian-language version with local payments is a golden opportunity (Indonesia has about 500 million people, most not English speakers), though payments are hard to set up.
- He dislikes partnerships ("90% big mouth, big words"). He prefers working with translators or localizers.
- Working with his wife: she is a realist and he is a dreamer. After she woke him at 6 a.m. with customer questions, he set ground rules (no live chat questions after 5 p.m., no work before 7:30). Three weeks in it was working well.
- Idea he said he couldn't build earlier for lack of tech: a spoken morning digest of top Hacker News and Product Hunt stories. He also sees an opportunity in AI dubbing of creators' videos (like MrBeast's manual multi-language voice actors) into other languages.

## AI outlook and ethics

- He says AI is underrated and ChatGPT overrated. He doesn't think ChatGPT will severely damage Google.
- 2033 prediction: everyone has a personal AGI running on their phone as GPUs get cheap, and content is personalized, including individualized videos and podcasts. He is skeptical of VR (it makes him sick) and prefers overlay screens through glasses.
- Ethics: he thinks copying a specific artist's style with a prompt and claiming the output is wrong. He argues his own profile pictures don't sell any artist's style.
- On training data he says AI is like designers who are inspired by others, citing how he learned design from One Page Love. He supports opt-out systems, like Stability's opt-out plan, because opt-in only would stop progress on AI. He compares it to organ donor programs: opt-out means only people who care leave.
