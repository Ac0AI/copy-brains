## Danny's AI product-studio model: ship fast, hand off, stay small

- Danny builds many small AI products instead of one big company. His stated goal is "100 AI products doing the same amount of revenue" in about 10 years, each ideally 100% automated.
- Target shape for a product: SEO traffic comes in, it earns "five six figures a month", and the only human work is live chat (his wife handles it).
- He starts products and gets bored after about three months. He says he can hyper-focus on something that interests him for a month, ship it, then lose interest. He builds around that weakness instead of fighting it.
- Division of labor: he hired an operations person (content creation, live chat) and a developer friend two weeks before this interview. Danny builds and launches new ideas. The developer builds out the "more boring features" once Danny gets bored.
- He prefers a small trusted team over managing people: "I don't want to manage the whole [...] team." Both hires are people who know what to do without being managed.
- He frames this as a deliberate choice. He could have scaled to millions of customers, but that means a big support load and a big team.
- Following curiosity is the strategy. He only works well on things that really interest him.

## Selling Headlime and choosing not to be a CEO

- Danny built Headlime, an AI copywriting tool (blogs, emails, Facebook and Twitter ads), one of the first products built on GPT-3. He has a screenshot from OpenAI saying he was the first developer to go live with such a product, in December 2020.
- It grew fast: revenue doubled every two to three weeks, and it made a lot of revenue within three months.
- Competition arrived (he compares it to Jasper). He had talks with Andreessen Horowitz and Sequoia and an acquisition offer that would have been "life-changing" money.
- He weighed three paths for one to two weeks, talking to friends, parents and his wife:
  - take funding and hire about 50 developers, becoming a CEO
  - bootstrap it
  - sell
- He decided it wouldn't be his life to "work 80 hours, manage people" and that it wouldn't make him happy, so he sold.
- His view: CEO roles are romanticized. If you're a maker, being "the boss" isn't fun. He says to work out why you're an entrepreneur and what you want before choosing.
- The first thing he did was build a tool for himself: he built Headlime because he was bad at writing headlines. His advice is to build for your own need, since "if you're lucky" thousands of others share it, and if not you still made your own life easier.

## ProfilePicture.ai and why competitors help a small bootstrapper

- ProfilePicture.ai: a user uploads about 20 photos, a personal AI model is trained on them, and they can then prompt any scene (e.g. Game of Thrones warrior, anime style) that looks like them. He is expanding it toward LinkedIn headshots and a "photo studio".
- He expected a hype cycle but says it has persisted past three months. He says there is a lot of search volume around it and he has thousands of customers.
- Lensa launched one month after him and reportedly made about $30 million on the app store. Danny's reaction: he got roughly 10 times more sales after Lensa launched.
- Why: Lensa's marketing created awareness of "AI avatars". Some searchers used "AI profile picture" instead and landed on his domain.
- His stance changed from Headlime days, when he panicked at funded competitors. Now: "it's great if some big shots marketing money spend their build something like me because they do the marketing for me, I'll eat the scraps."
- He doesn't need $30M in revenue because he has no employees to pay.
- Strategy: niche down. Take a specific part of a big tool that people love, don't bloat it with features to chase growth, and "then you have a reason to exist". Big players use broad terms, so a niche keyword set is where a small one can win.
- He expects Instagram or TikTok to eventually ship a built-in editor where you type what you want to see in your own face, and chose not to compete there.

## Mini products as a marketing channel (This Model Does Not Exist)

- Danny says he is bad at ads and dislikes conventional marketing. The one thing he's good at is SEO. So he launches mini products as marketing for his main product.
- This Model Does Not Exist ("A-list"): an Instagram account of a consistent AI-generated virtual influencer. A new photo is generated every 15 minutes, visitors upvote, and top-voted photos go to Instagram. Banners every 15 photos point to his main products.
- Build time: roughly a morning or an afternoon, because he already had the upload tooling and model-training knowledge from ProfilePicture.ai.
- How he made the consistent face: grabbed many fake faces from an AI face generator (he mentions Generated.photo), picked the ones that look alike, uploaded them into his training tool, retrained, and got one person who always looks the same.
- Upvoting backfired in a small way: people upvoted broken images (two hats, bad hands), so he switched to just posting the most upvoted. He says only about 5-10% of outputs are bad.
- It brings "quite some revenue" via banners to his products and is on the back burner. Possible future: turn the tech into a tool for others to create their own influencers or characters (people have DMed him asking).
- His self-assessment: he doesn't know Instagram well and would need a partner for that side.

## SEO-first AI products: Tattoos.ai and the keyword-difficulty method

- Tattoos.ai was his first of these products. A user types a tattoo idea (example: "a bunny with a black hat"), picks a tattoo artist's style, and gets a flash sheet. A real person got one of the generated tattoos on his leg.
- Pricing: $9 for 14 days of access. He gets about one or two sales a day.
- Programmatic SEO loop: every generated tattoo's prompt becomes a search-engine-style page, auto-created (e.g. bunny tattoo, shark tattoo, wave tattoo). Users generate content, it gets published on his site, and Google can pick it up.
- He applies the same idea to LandingFolio.com (website templates). There he had to hire a developer and designer and wait about six months to have content. With AI products, user-generated output becomes site content automatically.
- Search intent argument: informational queries (like recipes) will be disrupted by ChatGPT. Solution-seeking queries (make a profile picture, get a tattoo) still go to Google, and AI can't replace the tool itself.
- He avoids AI-written copy because he fears Google penalizes it.
- Ranking view: Google rewards pages where people stay. "You just need to make things for people that they want." Blog-style content marketing is dead; tools and products are how to rank.
- He credits backlinks as the key ingredient. He builds in public mainly for backlinks: press and social links on launch day mean he doesn't wait about six months for Google to pick a new product up.

## Advice for starting: find a low-difficulty keyword niche

- His one-liner for beginners: go to Ahrefs.com and keep typing keywords for things you're interested in until you find a cluster with keyword difficulty under 10, meaning you should be able to reach page one of Google.
- Then build a product around that cluster, optimized so content can rank. Use no-code or code, either way.
- His reasoning: many people know how to build but not how to launch or market, and SEO is the easiest way to start.
- Build something to scratch your own itch; "everyone should launch something".
- He follows this on Twitter (@dannypostmaa) and builds all his startups in public.

## What failed: StockAI and the AI-quality timing lesson

- Danny built StockAI, a stock photo site fully run by AI: a model trained on stock-style photos running 24/7, generating hundreds of thousands of images. He had to shut it down.
- Why it failed:
  - Quality too low. It works for profile pictures because they don't need to be perfect and can be cropped (he hides hands in pockets or under tables), but stock photography is rejected over a single flaw.
  - Competition from free sources: Unsplash has millions of free high-quality photos.
  - Conversion: it got social press and lots of visitors, but the conversion rate to a paid product was 0.004, and AI models are expensive to run.
  - Legal risk: Getty is suing the makers of the open model (trained on the 5-billion-image LAION data set). As a solo founder he didn't want to face a lawsuit, so he killed the product rather than waste six months.
- His view: Midjourney is close for artistic styles but not for realistic photos yet. He might revisit it after seeing how Getty vs. Stability AI resolves.
- He also notes that StockAI didn't get engagement when building in public: "who the [...] likes stock photography." Boring products don't get shared.
- He thinks Shutterstock, with its huge photo database, is best placed to do it well.
