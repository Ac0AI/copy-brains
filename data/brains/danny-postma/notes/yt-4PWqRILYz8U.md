## Building Headlime: from ebook to AI product

- Danny learned to code in 2019 and started from a small idea. In 2017 he and a friend wrote an ebook with about 200 headline formulas, templates with a product name as a variable.
- Around the pandemic he turned that into a simple headline-generator tool. It was a conversion-optimization product, because headline testing is most of that work.
- After OpenAI released GPT-3 in June, he rebuilt Headlime on it. It generated headlines, Facebook ads, Google ads and landing pages.
- In January he added the feature he calls the killer one: blog generation. You type an outline and the AI writes a full blog post.
- He says he was one of the first to go live with OpenAI's API. The first non-GPT-3 version took 6 months. The GPT-3 version took about 3 months from start to finish.
- Revenue was shared publicly on Twitter: about $5K in December, $10K in January, $20K in February (about $20K a month at the sale).
- He sold a $49 lifetime deal, which he calls "totally worth it" for the earlier headline generator.

## Selling Headlime to Jasper for seven figures

- The sale was reported as seven figures. He got two offers, both by DM on Twitter. Jasper was one of them.
- He cites "normally four times the monthly revenue" as a rule of thumb for pricing a business, but notes Headlime was skyrocketing, so it deserved more. He told Jasper to double their offer, and they agreed.
- His reasons for selling:
  - Big, well-funded startups were entering the space and he was alone.
  - He was close to burnout doing support, programming and marketing. About 200 people a day were in his customer support.
  - He didn't want VC money. He says investors expect an IPO-track path and "80 hours a week", which isn't his lifestyle.
  - He grew up in a poor family, so spending his own money to bootstrap scale felt scary.
  - Hiring in a Dutch company is risky because you can't easily fire people.
  - Cashing out gave a life-changing amount of money.
- In the deal, he asked that the Headlime website stay online. Lifetime-deal customers kept access, and some flipped their accounts for thousands of dollars.
- On a customer complaining about being "abandoned": acquisitions are how a founder gets paid after "working their asses off 16 hours a day, 7 days a week", and a $49 lifetime deal was a great price.

## Indie hacking, building in public and lifestyle

- He sees himself in the second wave of indie hackers, after Pieter Levels and others. He started with about 200 Twitter followers and grew them by sharing wins, losses, revenue and learnings while building Headlime.
- He credits sharing progress publicly as a major marketing channel and a reason the sale was possible. He now thinks it has become normal.
- He prefers "solopreneur" or "indie-preneur" now. He compares it to indie music: no big label (VC), your own schedule, one project, no employees.
- He works about 6am to midday. In the first week of Profile Picture it was about 16 hours a day.
- He is "slowmading" in Indonesia (Bali) with a house, wife and dog. He says slowmading means 3-4 months in a place instead of hopping every few weeks.

## How he picks product ideas and ships

- He mostly scratches his own itch, because it stays fun and he is his own first customer.
- Landingfolio: there were no landing page galleries when he started, and he collected sites he liked. It got no traffic for a year, then Google picked it up. He didn't mind because he used it himself.
- The headline generator came from his conversion-optimization work.
- He likes to dive in and build, and accepts that a month might be wasted. Headlime was his third product to work. Profile Picture AI took 11 products.
- Caveat: this approach only works if you can ship quickly.

## Learning to code and building AI products without coding

- He says he was bad at school and only learns when something is interesting.
- He taught himself in about 6 months (4 days a week full-time, with a freelance job 2 days a week covering living costs). He learned by building Landingfolio and hitting each barrier in turn: images, file uploads, databases, servers.
- Friends in Bali pointed him to Vue.js and MongoDB, then he used free YouTube crash courses (framework, MongoDB, JavaScript). He says a curriculum might have been faster.
- He says to learn to code only if you like working with computers.
- For marketers: no-code tools (Webflow, Bubble) plus AI APIs can recreate a lot. APIs mentioned: Astria (DreamBooth), Replicate, Hugging Face, Dream Studio.
- Self-hosting is a waste of time early on because APIs are so easy. Later you want your own hosting for full control over scaling and to avoid depending on a platform. He was moving Profile Picture off the Astria API to his own hosted version.
- His view of defensibility in AI products: "marketing, a good graphical user interface, and privately trained models". He notes Headlime had about 50 direct competitors when it launched.
- He uses GitHub Copilot and says about 80% of his code is generated. He expects AI to make programmers faster rather than replace them, as in "power tools".

## Profile Picture AI, Stock AI and the image-AI explainer

- Profile Picture AI uses Stable Diffusion (open-source text-to-image) plus DreamBooth. DreamBooth lets you retrain the model on about 20 photos of your face, so prompts can place you anywhere (Santa Claus, a Roman soldier).
- It can also be trained on objects, for example a microphone.
- Prompts are found on Lexica, a visual database of images with their prompts. Dream Studio costs about 1 cent per image.
- Stock AI (stockai.com) makes unique AI stock images. A customer with a point-of-sale system for restaurants uses it to generate food photos for Uber Eats and GrabFood listings. Conversion rates reportedly went up and no one noticed it was AI.
- Pricing at the time: $19 for a Black Friday deal, and he was working on lowering fees now that GPUs were cheaper.

## Opportunities and trends in AI

- Niche specialization works because jack-of-all-trades models are not specialized. He predicts "highly specialized DreamBooth" products with nice interfaces, later bought by Adobe.
- Lexica's founder is training a private model on high-quality photography. He thinks a private, specialized dataset is a real moat.
- Ideas he flagged:
  - Summarizing long text, video and books. OpenAI had recently allowed long text input, and Whisper transcribes audio, so video and podcasts can be turned into text and summarized.
  - A personal daily podcast: pull top news items, transcribe and summarize them, narrate with an AI voice.
  - AI-generated video game assets sold as a library. He pointed to someone doing this, as game developers struggle with design.
- He also described automated TikTok channels that pull top Reddit posts, narrate them over game footage and post at scale, keeping only the top performers.
- Longer term, he thinks content could become fully tailored to each person.
