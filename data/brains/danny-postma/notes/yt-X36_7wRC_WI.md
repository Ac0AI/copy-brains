## Why Danny only builds products with existing search demand

- Danny picks product ideas by researching keywords first. He uses Ahrefs' keyword finder to see how many people search for a term and its keyword difficulty.
- His rule of thumb: with keyword difficulty under roughly 10 to 20, "you're guaranteed to go to the front page if you have like a one or two backlinks."
- He only pursues ideas with high search volume and low competition, so he never has to do marketing: "people search for it already."
- He says all the ideas on his postcrafts.com project list came from searching Ahrefs for keywords and building a product around them.
- He would not build something nobody searches for, because then "you have to create the market," which is very hard for an indie hacker.
- If a keyword has one or two competitors, he says you can still rank third. If he isn't good at SEO, he can bid on it in AdWords if the acquisition price supports it.
- He calculates the potential up front: assume a landing page conversion rate (e.g. one percent), and estimate monthly revenue at a number-one ranking.
- He says he tells everyone on Twitter to go SEO as an indie hacker, while admitting he may be biased.
- Danny calls this demand-first idea generation: start from proven search demand, then build the product.

## Domain names as an SEO lever, and lease-to-own domains

- Danny believes the domain name helps ranking. "Headshot Pro" in the domain tells Google to rank for "headshot pro" and "professional headshot," giving "a little bit of a boost." If you want AI stock photos, get stockai.com.
- He paid $3,000 for headshotpro.com and thinks it was worth it.
- His .io lesson: with Headlime (.io), many people typed headlime.com or headlime.co into Google and couldn't find the site. He notes that someone else (Damon from Testimonial) bought the .com, put an affiliate URL on it, and paid roughly 10 to 20 thousand dollars, which he earned back from the redirected traffic.
- He argues that competitors buying your .com can cost you customers, so buying it is worth it if you can afford to.
- Lease-to-own experiment: he wanted stockai.com, but the owner wanted about $25k to $30k. On a domain marketplace he could pay in installments (12, 24, or up to 60), at a higher commission (he estimates about 20 percent extra).
- He paid about $500 a month. If you cancel before it's paid off, the owner gets the domain back.
- He paid for three months (about $1,500), never found product-market fit, and stopped paying. He sees it as a cheap way to test whether a premium domain is worth owning.

## From stock photos to profile pictures to AI headshots: pivoting by conversion

- Generative AI took off in September 2022 with Stable Diffusion. Danny's first idea was a stock photo website (Stock AI), but quality wasn't there, people weren't willing to pay, and he feared legal action from big stock photo companies' lawyers.
- Another builder shipped a product that turned your face into styles. Danny and his friend Peter Levels built a competing version in about 30 hours; Peter shipped without a backend and database because he knew Danny would ship faster. Danny launched Saturday morning, it "exploded on Twitter," and he says he made six figures in sales in a week because it was a highly shareable, brand-new product.
- Revenue later dropped (competition such as Lensa boosted it for a while, then it fell to "barely any revenue"). He pivoted.
- He then launched Deep Agency (AI fashion and models) and Headshot Pro at the same time. Deep Agency got a lot of press but few sales, with about a 0.3 percent conversion rate. Headshot Pro had roughly 10 times that conversion rate.
- His rule: "Impressions and clicks doesn't pay the bills." If one product converts and earns 10 times more, focus on it. He shelved the fashion product because it was harder to build and a bigger company would likely dominate that space.
- His products (Stock AI, profile picture, Headshot Pro) are described as an evolution: reuse the same technology and templates, pivoting each time.

## Why Danny prefers B2C one-off payments over B2B SaaS

- Danny used to think B2C was bad, and still acknowledges disputes, angry customers and refunds. He is moving from software to one-off purchases.
- Headshot Pro is a one-time purchase: you upload photos and get the product back. He and his wife automated refunds and customer support. When he went to Holland for two weeks, he turned on auto-refund so any customer with an issue got money back automatically.
- His reasoning on B2B vs B2C:
  - Recurring revenue is nice, but it requires continually building features and keeping a relationship with the customer.
  - In B2C one-off, "you only have to get them once to buy," there's no need to add features, and little support or help desk, since customers don't return.
  - It scales through SEO and ads. His strength is conversion optimization: building a high-converting landing page, optimizing incoming traffic, converting, delivering, and done.
- His mantra: he doesn't build tools that rely on him always being online (like email tools). He wants products where he could be gone for a week and ignoring support wouldn't be a big deal. His wife handles support for a couple of hours a day, and the team simply lets it wait roughly 16 hours overnight.
- His working time zone (Asia, 12 hours ahead of North America) means customers sleep while he works. Early on, when Headshot Pro launched, servers went offline in the first week, so he hardened it until it auto-scales.

## Buying small B2C sites and funneling them into the main product

- Danny's acquisition logic: he acquired a site (Hashtag AI) listed on Acquire where the conversion rate was low but SEO traffic was high.
- He reasoned that if he could make the landing page convert five times better, he'd earn back the price in about 10 months (at a price of roughly four times yearly profit). At 10 times it would be about five months.
- He copied his Headshot Pro generation onto the acquired domain. He says the conversion rate is up about six times, and he expects to earn the money back in about nine months.
- He also bought profilepicturemaker.com (a 12-year-old domain). People looking for a profile picture may want a headshot, so he put a big banner linking to Headshot Pro.
- His approach: use acquired sites and tools as acquisition channels that funnel traffic back to the main product, treating it like engineering as marketing.
- He benefits from competitors' marketing. When Remini went viral with AI headshots, his sales tripled because people Googled "AI headshots," where he ranks number one, and he converts them.

## Market size, competition, and why Headshot Pro could last

- Danny sees about five big competitors in headshots, including one VC-funded company that copied his landing page and tried to copy his marketing agency. Profile picture tools had hundreds of competitors.
- He likes VC-backed or popular competitors because "they do marketing for me."
- His market view: a physical photo shoot costs around $300, and he offers one for about $39 with results that look good "nine out of ten" times. Portrait photography is a $5 to $10 billion worldwide market in his estimate, and he'd be happy with 1 percent of it.
- He admits a bigger competitor could always hurt him, and says that with AI "you could be obsolete next year." So he prefers to get revenue up front rather than build slowly, and move to another product if needed.

## Handling copycats and cloned landing pages

- Danny distinguishes inspiration from copycats: he's fine with people building the same product, but not with someone copying a whole landing page, copy, branding and screenshots, because customers get confused about who the real competitor is.
- With Headlime two years earlier, copycats were constant. He spent about two months fighting them and being negative on Twitter, and people got fed up with him. That frustration motivated him to take about three months to build version two of Headlime, a bigger product, to stay ahead. Version two was eventually acquired.
- In a recent incident a rage-bait tweet cloned his product. Peter Levels advised him not to feed the troll. Danny deleted his tweets and DM'd the person, noting he could file a DMCA notice with Cloudflare and have the site taken down. The person changed the copy, which Danny considers fine competition.
- His tactics:
  - Don't engage publicly, because Twitter promotes rage bait.
  - Block competitors on Twitter to stay sane. He kept the Copy AI founder blocked for about two and a half years and only unblocked him two weeks ago.
  - Most copycats ("99 percent") don't go anywhere.
- His view on reputation: building in public means your reputation is your asset, so cloning someone destroys trust with the community.

## Moving from solopreneur to a small remote team

- Danny posted his first job ad for a machine learning engineer. Two reasons: he is burned out on building features six months into Headshot Pro (he believes he has ADHD and hadn't built anything for three weeks), and competitors are coming, so it doesn't make sense not to invest Headshot Pro's revenue in a team.
- Headlime: he sold it about two years earlier instead of building a team, because he was afraid to hire. He had three options then (VC funding, bootstrapping with a team, selling) and chose to sell. Now with Headshot Pro the only options are to sell again or build a team.
- He argues selling would bring only about a 3x multiple because Headshot Pro has no strategic acquirers, versus 8 to 10x, so it isn't worth it yet.
- His plan: hire async contractors, ideally part-time people with other clients, in the same Asia time zone. Europe was a poor fit because his friend's 10 a.m. start meant 5 p.m. for Danny and evening questions. America is impossible.
- Style: a self-managing team with larger tasks and weekly check-ins, not constantly online, which preserves the solo-founder feel.
- He is upfront with candidates that it's his first time and tells them not to quit their job for it. He treats this as scary because shipping and killing products is easy, but shipping and killing a team affects people's salaries and families.
- To learn management, he asked on Twitter to shadow remote teams. The Gumroad founder added him to Notion, and he took notes on their processes and used them to set up his own task manager.
- The intended structure: a "Danny Postma team" or studio that can work on many products, so when something finds product-market fit, the team scales it while he keeps iterating and testing new ideas.
- His exit thinking: sitting on a beach would probably depress him, so the team lets him keep building asynchronously.

## Building in public as distribution

- Danny says most of his Twitter followers came through Peter Levels retweeting him, plus others like John Young. He went from 15K to about 87K in a few months.
- His caution: building in public attracts other indie hackers, so it only sells if your customers are indie hackers. If your audience is elsewhere, you need a different channel. He grew from 200 to 15K followers by sharing novel technology, which drew attention. For non-novel products, it may be hard to start that way, so "better just focus on a guaranteed marketing channel" such as SEO.
- Benefits: he calls Twitter his board of directors, getting feedback on things like AdWords. He is solo "only because I can ask other people." He started years ago, and says you have to start early.
- Downsides: he is addicted to Twitter, and virality brings pain. When Deep Agency tweets reached 5 to 30 million views, he got death threats. He prefers the quieter 80K size and wouldn't want to be famous.
- Life context: he lives in Indonesia (a Bali-like setup), outsourcing cooking and cleaning, which he estimates saves about four hours a day to focus on the business.
