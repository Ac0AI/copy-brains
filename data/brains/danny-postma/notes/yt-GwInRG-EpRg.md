## Building in public: how Danny started and what he shares

- Danny was a designer until about age 20, then trained as a conversion specialist (A/B testing, marketing psychology). After years of that he got bored of the "test something, throw it away, optimize" loop.
- In November 2019 he started teaching himself to program (HTML/CSS first, then backend). He worked "incognito" for about six months, rebuilt Landingfolio, launched it, and only then began sharing publicly (around February 2020).
- He defines building in public two ways: tweeting progress, learnings and builds on Twitter and Indie Hackers, and working in the open in person. At his Bali co-working space, Tropical Nomad, a group meets Tuesdays and Thursdays. At 10am each person pitches what they will finish, and at 4pm each gets five minutes to demo and take feedback on what people don't understand.
- He credits that in-person group for teaching him to program (database setup, integrations).
- He keeps notes of what he did well on each launch in a note tool (Mindwave) so he can reuse them on later launches.
- He wrote his Indie Hackers revenue post partly to celebrate and document the win, and partly to give back. He learned most things by lurking on Indie Hackers for two years. He saw his marketing background (landing page copy, pricing tactics) as the help the community lacked.

## Lifetime deals and tiered pricing for the Headlime launch

- Headlime (a headline/content template tool) was priced at $9/month, with a lifetime deal as the launch offer. Landingfolio and Headlime both came from his marketing background.
- Why lifetime deals: he thinks many people are subscription-fatigued and the tool was an MVP, so asking for monthly commitment felt hard. Up-front cash also gives runway. The lifetime price was $129 against a $9/month plan, and many monthly users churn within a year.
- Use of the cash: he hired a part-time copywriter for new copy and updates, invested in tooling, and did less freelancing. He describes it as "like a small angel investment" from customers.
- Tiered, limited-quantity pricing:
  - Tier 1: $49, capped at 200 deals, with a visible counter. It sold out in 12 hours (after a Product Hunt launch), and he had to add a new tier at 3am.
  - Tier 2: $89, 200 deals, sold out in about two weeks.
  - Tier 3: $429 (stated in the talk; he mentions $129 as the lifetime price elsewhere, so the final price tiers are ambiguous beyond the first two).
- He says there is no fixed formula: raise the price and watch whether it sells. A $49 deal selling out so fast showed it was too cheap. He estimates the true value sits between $129 and $199.
- He stopped selling lifetime deals to focus on monthly subscriptions, which he calls the most important long term.
- Platforms like AppSumo and Stack Social push lifetime deal prices between $49 and $99. He thinks $49 is "completely outrageous" for lifetime access.
- Lifetime-deal communities (AppSumo Facebook groups) were a strong early-customer source. Members shared the tool across their industries, gave free marketing, and sent feature feedback.

## Why Headlime sold: psychology, savings and speed

Danny gives three reasons for the success (about 700 sales, with roughly 10-15 refunds):
1. **Value that saves money.** Marketers always hunt for swipe files and inspiration. Over 1,000 templates (thank-you emails, survey questions, etc.) with variables for filling in company names. If it saves a marketer one hour, it has paid for itself.
2. **Scarcity (honestly applied).** He draws on Cialdini's *Influence*. With no urgency, visitors from Product Hunt, Facebook and Twitter think "I'll buy tomorrow" and forget. Limited deals with a live remaining-count push the decision.
   - He says never fake it: don't lower a price again after claiming it won't return. Customers lose trust, and earlier buyers who paid more feel tricked. His "booking.com last rooms" example: people learn to ignore fake urgency.
3. **Open, fast development.** He ships updates about twice a week (new templates, categories, requested features). Customers in the Facebook group rave about it, and he says "Danny just launches features every week."

## Letting customers drive the roadmap

- Rule: "If three people ask me for a feature, I build it." One request is a low-signal outlier; three is near-guaranteed demand.
- About 90% of features come from customers. Early requests were for email templates, other content types and examples. Zapier integration was requested a few times and went on the backlog.
- He emails customers asking things like "I've got this idea, is it useful?" or "what content would you like?", and builds the ones that pass three requests.
- Dig into the why behind a request. A customer asked for custom variables, he built them, and she requested a refund two days later. She had been using another tool whose variables worked differently, and he never asked what she actually needed. His lesson: "customers say one thing, they probably mean something else," so ask what tool they use today and ask for examples.
- Product origin: he and a friend had written a headlines book four years earlier. The first idea was just an editable headlines tool, and customer feedback expanded it.

## Getting copied: what happened and how he responded

- Over about two months, Headlime was copied about 10 times. He says they were not inspired copies but outright thefts: the whole landing page (built with Tailwind, so easy to copy), the headlines, the emails, the content, the videos, then sold at half price.
  - One copy launched on Stack Social before he did, so he lost that partnership.
  - Another translated his content into Portuguese and launched it in a Brazilian growth hacking Facebook group the next day.
  - Copycats posted in the same marketing channels he used, saying "same tool, half the price."
- Customers found most copies and asked in the Facebook group whether he had just bought the tool from CodeCanyon or GitHub. He spent a week of evenings defending that he wrote it. He felt it was damaging to his name, not a compliment.
- Why his product was exposed: its value is mostly copy, which is trivial to copy and paste, and the code took him about a month. He says the real work was months of writing content.
- DMCA takedowns worked, with no lawyer and about five minutes per form:
  - Use BuiltWith.com to see the copy's tech stack and hosts.
  - Search "DMCA" or "abuse" plus the provider (Amazon, Namecheap, Cloudflare) and submit the form.
  - Amazon took down about 90%; Namecheap banned domains; Cloudflare was good; Stripe was not.
- He distinguishes competitors who write their own copy and design (fine, "competition fuels me") from straight theft.
- Aftermath: he accepted it will happen, took a one-to-two-month social media break (deleted apps, a week offline), and decided to rebuild the tool to be more unique and harder to copy. He plans to share nothing until it is finished, then launch to customers first before returning to working in the open.
- Trolls on a post about the copying told him he was crying, and asked why the tool earns money if it is so easy to copy. That pushed him to step back.
- He took down his Indie Hackers revenue post because it attracted too much attention, saying money brings copies. He thought it had been too specific on his earnings.

## What to share and what to keep private when building in public

- Danny's verdict: working in the open has been "net positive totally." He grew Twitter from about 500 to almost 5,000 followers, and most of his sales came from it.
- Benefits he lists:
  - Meeting great people (e.g., a conversion-optimization mentor who DM'd him about the tool).
  - Free ongoing marketing: when he did not tweet for a week, sales dropped drastically.
  - Opportunities such as this podcast.
  - Inspiration for others, like his own start after seeing Pieter Levels earn from a site.
- Revenue tweets gave the most engagement and roughly doubled his followers, but also attract copycats.
- Guidance when your product is easily copyable (especially copy-based products):
  - Be careful sharing revenue numbers.
  - Don't share inner knowledge that is your competitive advantage.
  - Keep sharing learnings and what you are working on.
- Products that are harder to copy (tech-heavy ones like Roam Research or Notion) can be more open.
