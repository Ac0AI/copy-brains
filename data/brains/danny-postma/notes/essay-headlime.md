## How Danny validated and built Headlime into a $1M+ exit in 8 months

- Danny started Headlime in 2020 during lockdown by writing down ideas that were "easy to build and validate before wasting years on them."
- He picked the idea that turned an asset he already owned into a tool. Three years earlier, he and a friend wrote a book called Headline Formulas, which sold a few hundred copies.
- He chose to turn the book into a tool for two reasons: books are worth less than tools, and he already had all the content, so building was easy.
- Version one took one month. It was a simple "content spinner" with 200 headline formulas that users customized by filling in variables, outputting 200 customized headlines.
- The timeline ran from launch (about October 2020), through version 2 in December 2020 at $1K MRR, to $20K MRR in February 2021 and acquisition by Jarvis.ai in March 2021.
- He says: "Make something you'd like to use yourself. Even if no one buys it, it's still worth something to you. Furthermore, you are the most knowledgeable customer and know exactly what you want."
- Landingfolio, his earlier design gallery, started the same way. He couldn't come up with solid landing page ideas for clients as a freelance conversion optimization specialist, so he built the tool he needed.

## The lifetime-deal launch that made $60,000 in the first few weeks

- The version one launch did over $60,000 in sales in the first few weeks, and it was his first successful product launch.
- Most of it came from a limited deal that gave customers access for a one-time fee instead of a subscription.
- Before Headlime, he ran Inspireframe, launched in June 2020. There he tried many launch techniques, documented them, and reused them all for Headlime.
- The launch lesson he draws is to put the product in front of customers early rather than working for a year without launching.

## Listening to customers and shipping fast after launch

- Customers' vision of the product was "beyond my wildest ideas." They wanted more than headline templates: emails, Facebook headlines, Google Ads and other content types.
- He went on a "shipping spree," launching new features and content and updating the tool to match what users asked for.
- His rule: "Ship often and quickly. The only way to see if there is demand for your product is by putting it in front of customers."
- He advises putting out a minimum viable product: "If it has potential, your customers will give you valuable feedback on how to shape it further."

## Betting on GPT-3 and rebuilding as Headlime v2

- About two months after Headlime launched, OpenAI released GPT-3, a neural network that writes content.
- He messaged the CTO of OpenAI to get access and got it.
- He quickly saw the AI was very good at producing marketing copy. With proper training, you could explain your business and it would output near-perfect copy for ads, blogs and websites.
- His read was that content spinners were done and AI-generated content was the future, so he went all in.
- He went "off the grid" for five months and used all his working hours and free time to build version 2, an AI-powered copy generator.
- Version 2 launched in December 2020. Revenue went from $1K MRR at the start of December to $20K MRR in February 2021, roughly doubling every few weeks.
- The rebuild also answered copycats. He says he outran them by being "innovative enough" with version 2.

## Growth through building in public on Twitter

- Most growth was organic. Danny is part of the "build in public" group on Twitter that shares learnings, numbers and progress.
- While building v2, he dropped occasional teasers on Twitter showing how powerful the new tool was, so the December launch arrived with hype already built.
- Many of his tweets went viral, which created a flywheel. Bigger accounts saw them on their timelines and learned about Headlime, and that led to a TechCrunch feature (March 2021).
- His case for building in public: "A product won't market itself," and sharing what you're working on is an easy way to market it.
- On copycats: "Yes, others might copy you, but is your product actually good if no one wants to copy it?"
- Pricing was $29 a month, rising to $59. He frames it as a no-brainer for a tool that does all your copywriting, because copywriting is hard and creative ideas are harder.

## Why he sold: solo-founder overload and the exit decision

- He ran Headlime alone. He spent hours a day on customer support, had to ship features fast as many companies cloned Headlime's success, and did all the marketing at once.
- He was too overwhelmed to train or hire anyone.
- He saw three options: take venture funding and work 80 hours a week for years, bootstrap and risk being overtaken by funded competitors, or sell.
- In February 2021 he got two acquisition offers from two prominent tech startups. He accepted one, and Jarvis.ai acquired Headlime in March 2021 for a 7-figure sum.
- He calls it the right decision: "I like to start things, not manage things."
- He describes the stress of running a high-growth solo business: churn messages in the morning could ruin his day, and a competitor raising funding made him fear his days were numbered. Selling calmed his mind.

## Handling copycats, negativity and imposter syndrome

- Entrepreneurs plagiarized Headlime, stealing the code and content and relaunching it under another name. This made him cynical at first.
- His conclusion is that a successful product will get copied, and the answer is to work harder to stay more innovative than the copycats. Launching v2 was how he did it.
- Being in the spotlight brought negative comments from customers and competitors trying to sabotage him.
- A supportive group of fellow entrepreneurs cheered him up and helped with imposter syndrome.
