## What Naval looks for in founders: intelligence, energy, integrity

- Naval screens founders on three traits.
  - **Intelligence:** They know what they're doing, have insight and specific knowledge, and have thought deeply about the problem. "It's not about the age it's not how many years they've spent but just how deep is their understanding."
  - **Energy:** Founding is "brutally difficult" and takes a long time, and the people who succeed are the ones who persevere. Red flags are founders who hesitate, look for constant positive feedback, or are easily thrown off course.
  - **Integrity:** Without it you get "a hardworking smart crook." Startups move fast and people are independent, so anyone who wants to screw you over will find a way.
- He defines ethics as "what you do despite the money. If being ethical was profitable everybody would do it."
- Integrity red flag: a founder offers something slightly unfair to another shareholder, employee or co-founder to make him happy. "If they can do it to them they can do it to me."
- Integrity is the hardest trait to judge because it needs longitudinal, long-term relationships.
- He adds a personal test: do you genuinely like them? Investing in or starting a company with someone means signing up for a decade of them in your life. If they are downers or exhausting, "no amount of money is worth it."
- His favorite founders are ones he learns from. When they call for help he jumps on it, because an hour walking around the block with them leaves him smarter.

## Signals of a long-term founder, and the cost of wavering

- Ryan Breslow (Bolt, a financial payments company) impressed Naval before he invested.
  - He had a crack team of engineers from Square, Twitter and Stripe.
  - He ran thorough, professional diligence on Naval: asked for references, did his own back-channel, moved fast and was transparent.
  - He then compiled the feedback he got and gave it to Naval, "as if he had done a peer review of me."
  - Naval says he did more diligence on Naval than Naval did on him, and it was not brute force. A founder who pitches by bombarding 30 of an investor's friends comes across as a turnoff.
- Naval reads care as a signal. Great founders treat every early move as "putting bricks in the foundation of a skyscraper."
- Founders who treat the company as a flip ("if I get a good offer I'll sell") are not in it for the long haul.
- Returns in this business come from huge outcomes, so he becomes hypersensitive to meticulous founders.
- Founders with a clear true north do well. Those who say "if the wind blows this way then this" don't.
- "In this industry you get paid for being right when everybody else is wrong." Many of these founders run full speed into a wall, then get up and run at something else.
- It is very hard to tell a madman from a genius. The madman ignores feedback from the environment and lives in a fantasy.
- Examples of founders he admires: Corey and Michael of One (a protocol for after school). They were young, but he calls the depth they put into social products "mind-blowing."

## Conventional wisdom in venture is always eventually wrong

- Great new companies look strange and don't resemble previous ones. This traps investors into thinking there is one way to do things.
- Past "rules" that were broken:
  - Before Netscape, little money was thought to be in internet products.
  - Before Microsoft, the money was thought to be in hardware.
  - Before Apple, the money was in mainframes and enterprise, not consumer.
  - Before Uber, the money was in virtual software, not real-world work like taxi dispatchers and unions.
- Don't write off a space after one failure. He cites Sequoia: it invested in Webvan, the failed late-90s grocery delivery company, and still backed Instacart. It re-evaluated each opportunity on its merits and knew it might have been the right idea at the wrong time.
- "Each great business looks weird and there's no such thing as a perfect deal." Many VCs miss great companies by looking for the perfect deal.
- Founder-structure myths:
  - "Don't invest in married couples" would have missed Cisco and others. Eventbrite was also founded by a couple.
  - "Two founders is ideal" is not a rule. There are great one-founder (Oracle, Salesforce) and three-founder companies.
  - Even dual-founder companies tend to end up with one dominant founder, such as Jobs at Apple and Gates at Microsoft.
- His takeaway: any formula is a set of guidelines that is probably wrong. With a new company "you have to forget everything you know" and "shut up and listen."

## Angel investing: portfolio math, misses, and guarding time

- Startup investing is the opposite of Buffett-style value investing.
  - Buffett buys later with more data, in bigger amounts, betting on 17-30% compounded annual returns.
  - In startups you bet that about 90% of companies go to zero or return your money. The other 10% post huge multiples that overwhelm the losses.
- Examples he gives:
  - Twitter is "probably now up 400x."
  - Uber is "probably up around 4,000x."
  - His fund is on track to return 10x or 20x its capital.
  - He has made north of 150 investments.
- Because of this he focuses on the upside and says most of his regrets are mistakes of omission, not commission.
- Misses he lists:
  - Twilio: founder Jeff Lawson gave him every chance to invest.
  - Pinterest: Ben Silbermann offered him the investment three times and he passed because "it's images, like Flickr." His fiancée, who was obsessively using it, told him to do it.
  - YouTube: he helped early and took no stock.
  - Square: he passed on the first round as too expensive.
  - Twitter: he invested much less than he was allocated because it seemed too expensive.
- He also praises the "anti-portfolio" (a record of your biggest misses) and Buffett's practice of reviewing the previous year's mistakes at annual meetings, as ways to stay intellectually honest.
- Mistakes of commission usually come from lacking time to get comfortable and investing out of fear of missing out. "That's actually the worst reason to invest." He notes great companies raise very quickly, so time is often short.
- On failing investments, he learned from Ron Conway to tell a founder to keep the money, land on their feet and start the next thing, and that he will invest in that too. Reputation matters.
- His real regret is signing advisory roles.
  - They eat time and the founder calls constantly.
  - Or the founder doesn't call and feels guilty.
  - The stock looks free, but "nothing in life is free."
- Rule: "Guard your time more carefully than you guard your money." Time is the non-renewable resource.
- Emotions dominate investors: you decide in the first five minutes of a meeting, and if a company turns down your money in round one you must undo the annoyance so you can help in round two.

## AngelList, startups and the shrinking firm

- Naval describes his path as scratching his own itch.
  - Venture Hacks came from the black box of term-sheet negotiation: "boy I wish I'd had that information."
  - AngelList first solved how to find investors.
  - A few years in, he saw the bigger problem was getting help building the business and recruiting talent.
  - That led to the syndicates product, where lead investors do deals and accredited backers invest behind them, and to the jobs product.
- Scale he cites at the time:
  - Over 10,000 companies were recruiting on it.
  - It had hundreds of thousands of candidates.
  - It made over 10,000 mutual introductions every week.
  - The company had only 30 people, mostly former or future founders.
  - Former engineers had gone on to start companies like Instacart and Cover.
- His framing: AngelList "helps Founders fund other Founders" and helps founders recruit other founders. It stands for founders and underdogs.
- He thinks the information revolution is reversing the Industrial Revolution.
  - The optimal firm size is shrinking from thousands to hundreds to dozens, and maybe to one.
  - He predicts people will eventually get weekly job and contract offers on their phone, pick by social connections, pay and profile value, get rated, and take weeks off if they want.
  - The future is individual brands, such as New York Times reporters whose Twitter brands exceed the paper's.
  - He calls Tim Ferriss an independent brand and says "almost everybody on this planet will work for themselves."
- He says that to succeed in tech you should move to Silicon Valley: "half of it is just showing up."

## Habits, incentives and self-hacking

- Naval says habits are everything. A daily light workout was the first thing that showed him how much a habit can change you. Each six-month period he aims to add one good habit or drop one bad one, and treats fitness as a 10-year project.
- He wants habits that are sustainable and effortless. His method is to change his tastes and substitute healthier, tasty foods, not to ban foods and suffer.
  - He builds habits that are more pleasurable and easier than the ones they replace.
  - Examples: a roughly $50 tabletop teppanyaki grill with food straight from grill to mouth, and a workout with 20 lb dumbbells at home.
- Quitting through a crisis: a bacterial infection at 41 forced him off alcohol, caffeine, dairy, red meat and carbs in one week. The body's tight feedback loop made the changes stick. He calls suffering a teacher: "that moment when you see reality exactly as it is."
- Commit publicly and use incentives.
  - Self-control is overrated.
  - Telling everyone he was going to start a company (1996, while at @Home Network) embarrassed him into founding his first one.
  - He cites Scott Adams repeating "I am going to be a great cartoonist" in a mirror daily to force consistency with himself.
  - Principle: "every time you find a weakness about yourself you can actually turn it to the positive."
- If you are not ready for a big change, set a smaller goal, commit externally and move up later.
- He rejects the "epiphany theory" of self-development. Skills (investing, happiness, diet) are built over decades with feedback loops.

## Learning, reading and persuasion

- "If you learn how to learn it's the ultimate meta skill." Reading lets you pick up any skill.
- His reading method:
  - Treat books like throwaway blog posts.
  - Buy any book someone mentions.
  - Read 10 to 20 at once.
  - Skip around.
  - Drop any book that is boring or contains errors.
  - Don't read what you don't enjoy, so you form the habit.
- For old problems, read old sources: "the older the problem the older the solution." He recommends source books such as Darwin's Origin of Species, Marcus Aurelius' Meditations and Poor Charlie's Almanac. He calls the Almanac probably the best book on business he has read, and says he avoids most business books as "very simple ideas wrapped up in a lot of pages."
- He drops sources that show clear rationality errors or bias and filters news heavily.
- Reading list items he names: Sapiens, The Rational Optimist and Origins of Virtue (Matt Ridley), Influence by Cialdini, Scott Adams' blog post "The Day You Became a Better Writer," and Kevin Simler's Melting Asphalt blog.
- Mental models he leans on: evolution as a "binding principle" for explaining human behavior, and basic game theory (prisoner's dilemma and iterated prisoner's dilemma).
- On Cialdini's influence levers (consistency, liking, authority, social proof, scarcity, reciprocity), he says to use them with care. Being dishonest while persuading means being dishonest with yourself, which disconnects you from reality and leads to poor decisions.
- Writing tips from Adams: surprise, a strong headline, brevity, no adjectives or adverbs, and active voice.
- He uses a written vocabulary when speaking to sound smart.
- Honesty is a core value for him. Lying creates "a second thread in your head" that pulls you out of the present moment.
