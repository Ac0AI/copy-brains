## How Naval says wealth is built: equity, leverage, and specific knowledge

- Naval says you do not get rich renting out your time. Even lawyers and doctors charging "three, four, 500 an hour" don't get rich, because their lifestyle ramps up with their income and they don't save enough to retire.
- The first requirement is to own a piece of a business. That can mean being an owner, an investor or shareholder, or building a brand whose value accrues to you.
- He says this is the point of his "How to Get Rich Without Getting Lucky" tweetstorm (about 36-38 tweets). He came up with the principles around age 13-14 and has lived by them for about 30 years.
- He argues that "we live in an age of infinite leverage". Your actions can be multiplied a thousandfold by broadcasting (a podcast), by investing capital, by having people work for you, or by writing code. Good decisions therefore matter more than they used to.
- The most powerful money makers are individual brands, such as Joe Rogan, Elon, Kanye, Oprah and Trump. Their media works for them while they sleep.
- Specific knowledge: he says Joe has "specific knowledge" nobody else has, plus accountability through his own name, plus leverage through media. Joe could lose everything, start over, and be rich again quickly.
- The way out of the competition trap is to be authentic. Find what you do better than anyone because you love it, map it to what society wants, put your name on it, take the risks, and keep ownership and equity.
- Naval says he gives all his material on making money away free, because charging would make him "another huckster". He says "there are no get-rich-quick schemes".
- Wealth is positive-sum. Two people trade things and both end up with higher utility. Status is zero-sum.

## Naval's hourly-rate rule for protecting time

- When he was young, his overwhelming desire was to make money. He reasoned that no one would pay him more than he thought he was worth, so he picked an aspirational hourly rate.
- The rate should be "a little ludicrous". He started at $500 an hour and upgraded it to $5,000 an hour.
- He applied the rate in two ways:
  - If something costs less than his hourly rate, he throws it away or gives it away. His example is standing in line to return an item.
  - If a task can be hired out for less than his rate, he hires someone.
- Work, in his definition, is things you have to do that you don't want to do. If you're working, it had better be at your hourly rate. Otherwise don't do it.
- Rest, leisure, and time with friends and family don't count against the rate.
- Meetings: he says they are very costly ("one person is talking, seven people listening"). Drop non-urgent meetings or make them efficient. Meetings should be phone calls, phone calls should be emails, and emails should be text.
- He once owned the domain "idontdocoffee.com" and admits it was "a little bit of a jerk move".
- Business travel: about five years before this recording, he resolved never to travel for business. He travels only if the trip is complete in itself, such as seeing friends or visiting a place he wants to see.
- When you're high-visibility, inbound requests (events, business plans, advice) leave no time to start over. He says to "ruthlessly disappoint everybody", clear your schedule, and not respond.

## Why the optimal company is shrinking, and why he wants people working for themselves

- Naval says anyone who can be told when to show up, what to wear and how to behave is not a free person, however rich.
- He uses Ronald Coase's theory of the firm. Company size depends on internal versus external transaction costs. If a transaction is hard to do externally, you bring it in-house. If it's easy, you keep it outside.
- Information technology makes external transactions easier through gig apps, small payments, and ratings. So the optimal firm is shrinking, and he calls this an "atomization of the firm".
- Startups keep shaving pieces off big companies. His example is the small vacation-rental market on Craigslist, which became Airbnb.
- His prediction (10, 20, 50 or 100 years out) is that high-quality work, not just driving an Uber, will come in gig form:
  - Your phone buzzes with jobs from past colleagues and referrals.
  - You take or decline each project.
  - The contract and pay are settled on the spot, and you're rated.
  - Then you take months off.
- He compares this to how Hollywood already organizes projects.
- He tells employees at companies he runs that he will help them start their own company when they are ready. He calls that "the highest calling".
- A 10- or 20-person company beats a 1,000- or 10,000-person one. The smaller the company, the more human the relationships, the fewer the rules, and the more flexible and creative the work.
- The internet also creates one giant aggregator per category and a long tail of individuals, and it hollows out the middle:
  - Instead of seven Hollywood studios there is Netflix.
  - Instead of Walmart, Costco and Kmart, there is Amazon plus many small individual brands.
  - In media he expects a few gigantic outlets and millions of independent people.
- He says the most powerful people today are those writing the algorithms for Twitter, Facebook and Instagram, because they control the spread of information.

## Working like a lion: leverage, feedback loops, and nonlinear output

- He argues that output is nonlinear. "Who you do it with, what you do, how you do it" matters far more than how hard you work. The corner grocer works as hard as he does but gets far less output.
- Humans are "meant to hunt like lions", not graze like cows. A modern knowledge worker should work like an athlete:
  1. Train hard.
  2. Sprint.
  3. Rest.
  4. Reassess and get feedback.
  5. Repeat.
- Nine-to-five is for machines, not people.
- Naval picks a clear, calm mind as the key advantage. He says a calm person decides better, and that matters more when leverage multiplies decisions. He cites Warren Buffett, who plays bridge and walks in the sun rather than constantly loading his brain with information.
- Early stage: he makes and invests in businesses because it's play. He now passes on great investments when the product is boring or he won't learn anything.
- Competing with play: he says "I'm playing 16 hours a day". Anyone competing with him by "working" loses.
- Retirement, as he defines it: "when you stop sacrificing today for some imaginary tomorrow". Three routes:
  - Enough passive income to cover your burn rate.
  - Drive your burn rate to zero.
  - Do something you love so much it isn't about money.
- Traps:
  - Working for things as rewards.
  - Upgrading your lifestyle with income. He quotes Taleb on "two great addictions: heroin and a monthly salary".
  - Keeping up with peers. If you can work remotely, he suggests living somewhere cheaper, such as Thailand or Bali, and saving.
- Naval says it was really hard for him. He grew up poor, first-generation, in Queens, and started working at 11. He was $400 short for college. He calls making money "the hardest thing you will do" but says the tools are all available.

## Learning: understand the basics, don't memorize

- He says he reads for genuine curiosity. He flips through books, doesn't finish them, and has 50-70 books open on Kindle and iBooks.
- Books-read counts are a "vanity metric" and signaling. He would rather read the best 100 books over and over until absorbed.
- He grew up in a library because his single mom used it as daycare, and he read everything.
- Basics beat advanced material. He says knowing arithmetic cold helps in business (counting change, valuing a podcast business, probability math) more than memorized calculus.
- Memorizing indicates you don't understand. "You should be able to rederive anything on the spot, and if you can't, you don't know it." He cites Feynman's four-page chain from counting to calculus.
- He uses Twitter to force understanding. Writing or talking fills gaps in your thinking. He writes things down as a basic hook that points to deeper understanding.
- He reads one page a night, then spends the evening thinking about it or chasing references.
- Test of real interest: "would I still be interested in learning this if I couldn't ever tell anybody about it?"
- Pre-Google he admits he made up what he hadn't memorized to seem smart. Fact-checking improved him.
- Retraining: he rejects the idea that adults can't be re-educated, and points to online bootcamps and coding schools, some of which pay you to attend. He proposes a paid year off every 4, 6 or 10 years to retrain into a creative profession.

## Happiness and a calm mind as a performance skill

- Naval treats happiness as a choice and a skill, like fitness or nutrition. His challenge: "If you're so smart, how come you aren't happy?" He says he was miserable and is now "happy as a clam", a shift that took about eight years and is ongoing.
- He says he was happy before the money mostly.
- Desire is "a contract that you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want". Pick one overwhelming desire, accept suffering over that one, and drop the rest. He believes you will get it if you focus on one thing.
- The social contract trick: announce who you are to others, and consistency keeps you honest. He used it to start his first tech company by telling colleagues he was leaving.
- Techniques he lists:
  - Reframe: look for the positive side of each event until it becomes second nature (his example: a friend sending hundreds of photos). Positive thoughts fade quickly while negative ones linger.
  - Get more sunlight, smile, hug, and spend time in nature.
  - Watch your mind all day without judging it.
  - He tried an SSRI once. It turned him from a pessimist into an optimist, but he disliked the side effects and stopped.
- Meditation: "the art of doing nothing". Sit, close your eyes, and let whatever happens happen. He compares it to clearing an email inbox of unprocessed issues and calls it self-therapy.
  - It takes at least an hour a day and preferably 60 days.
  - He prefers not to use apps (he allows that Sam Harris's is good).
  - He says that once chatter fades into the background, you can reach moments of silence.
- Peace is "happiness at rest", and you can convert peace into happiness anytime.
- He says the peace we seek is "peace from mind". External problems are unlimited, so peace has to come from inside.
- Material possessions: being poor can make you unhappy, but being rich doesn't make you happy. Hedonic adaptation means you return to baseline within a year.
- Why he got rich first: he starts with "let's get you rich first" because money buys the time and energy to pursue inner peace.
- Resisting addictive tech: he describes social media, news, sugar, porn and video games as engineered to addict. The "only solution is to turn it off".
- He blocks easily outraged people on social media, and avoids politics because it makes you a muddled thinker. His line is that you want to be "rich and anonymous, not poor and famous".

## Automation, UBI, AI and capitalism

- Automation: he calls UBI "a non-solution to a non-problem". Automation has always created new, usually better, jobs, and nobody predicts them in advance (he cites podcaster and video-game commentator). He points to record-low unemployment and says the challenge is retraining.
- His problems with UBI:
  - It's a slippery slope to socialism, since the bottom 51% could vote themselves the top 49%'s money.
  - He says $15k a year for everyone would be three-quarters of current GDP (an estimate he saw), and GDP would shrink if entrepreneurs left.
  - It lowers recipients' status and doesn't solve meaning. Meaning comes through education and capability.
  - It should be means-tested, which becomes welfare.
- Alternative: provide basic services (housing, food, transportation, high-speed internet, a phone) in abundance through technology-driven automation.
- Capitalism: free exchange is intrinsic to humans. He sees monopolies, crony capitalism and bank bailouts as real problems. The right criticism is unequal opportunity, and the response should be equal opportunity, not equal outcome. Equal outcome can only be enforced through violence.
- Scale of the group: citing Taleb, he says the larger the group, the more you need capitalist incentives. Be a socialist with family and a capitalist with strangers.
- Test of a good system: build it, then hand it to your enemies to run for ten years.
- AI: he says general AI is not near in our lifetimes. Today's AI is narrow pattern recognition on closed, bounded problems (chess, Go). Reasons:
  - We don't know how the brain works.
  - We have never modeled a paramecium or an amoeba.
  - Computation also happens inside cells.
  - He cites estimates of about 50 years of Moore's Law to simulate a cell and about 100 years to build a brain.
  - He doubts general intelligence exists, since intelligence is contextual to an environment.
  - He expects automation eventually to replace non-creative work, leaving humans free for creative work.
- Environment: he says progress comes from lowering the price of clean technology until it is cost-competitive, and he credits Musk. For the Amazon, he suggests ecotourism and granting pharmaceutical patent rights over its plants so companies have an incentive to conserve it. He prefers private-property solutions.
- Energy: he wants iteration on nuclear fission and fusion, and suggests testing on the Moon or Mars. He notes Gen 4 reactors are passive fail-safe.
