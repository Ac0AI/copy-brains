## Virtue as long-term selfishness, and avoiding cheap dopamine

- Naval says "the modern devil is cheap dopamine." The seven deadly sins are all cheap dopamine: something that feels good now and screws you over later.
- He argues virtues are values that are good for you individually over a long enough time frame (long-term selfish). They also produce win-win outcomes for society, because they let people play iterated prisoner's dilemma and stag hunt games in a positive equilibrium.
- He calls a good life "the ultimate marshmallow test" (while noting the original experiment doesn't replicate).
- Modern traps he lists: politics, drugs, screen addiction and scrolling, porn, alcohol. On legalized weed, he says it "sucks the energy out of young men" and "you can't be ambitious if you're stoned all the time."
- He argues modern libertarianism took the wrong half: "free to do anything" became "celebrate anything," so everyone is a hedonist with no long-term stake, no shared ethos and no shared rules.
- He says atheism reaching the masses is a problem, because most people can't build their own operating system. Without a shared one you get anarchy or a state-imposed one, and he doubts the state's ethics beat the old religions'.
- On religion: "big religion causes a lot of problems but little religion is good." He favors village churches, broad unifying principles, and personally exploring the teachings of whichever religion resonates.

## Help locally first, and build a mission bigger than yourself

- Naval prefers local charity (people down the street, people who work for you or with you) because it is practical, actionable and selfishly rewarding. Your money isn't wasted, tricked or redirected.
- He criticizes modern activism: "your family is broken, but you're going to fix the world." Agitating for distant causes you've never seen, while you can't fix local problems, is a disease. "You get the right to solve global problems after you solve local problems."
- Society gives more trust, leverage and influence to people who take care of those around them.
- On Elon Musk (before he was the richest man): when someone devotes their life to giving society what it needs, society has no choice but to give them what they want. You can't count that closely or do it purely for the payoff.
- A mission larger than yourself makes you a Schelling point (a rallying cry). "I want to make humans a spacefaring species" assembles builders who share that vision and now see a vehicle for it (SpaceX).
- The more universal the mission, the more people it pulls together, regardless of whether it is effective. He cites Martin Luther King's "I have a dream" speech and the appeal of Marxism.

## Ideas spread by spreadability, survive by correctness

- Naval's version of the memetics point: an idea spreads according to its spreadability and survives according to its correctness.
- A civilization that adopts a bad idea because it spreads well can wipe itself off the map, as the Soviet Union did.
- Example: under Mao, the Great Leap Forward killed more Chinese people than all casualties of World War II (all nations combined, per Naval). China survived by adding a state-backed capitalist section: private farming, then private enterprises. "The more privatized they became, the faster they came up."
- Bad ideas get removed less by people learning than by the entities that hold them being eliminated. "Skin in the game will eliminate you." So you want to be an entity with correct ideas.
- His pseudo-spiritual take on David Deutsch: truth is a crystal in the multiverse. Falsehoods are infinitely variable, while true explanations are few and hard to vary, so the most truth-oriented versions of you are the most similar to each other.
- The goal is to be "the Rickiest of the Ricks" (Rick and Morty's Rick C-137), the most truth-oriented version of yourself. That means the least motivated reasoning, the lowest ego and the least self.

## Authenticity: no mask, no false humility

- Naval defines authenticity as "not putting on a fake persona, not having a mask." We wear masks because we care what others think and want to fit in.
- Inauthenticity includes signaling status, using jargon to sound smart, and claiming to know what you don't or not to know what you do.
- False humility is also an ego trip: "if you're actually low ego, there's no such thing as humility anymore, and there's no such thing as pride."
- When he started tweeting, VCs messaged that he was "not backable" and had gone off the rails. The cost of authenticity is risking embarrassment.
- The payoff is long-term. It removes people around you who only like a persona, ends the walking on eggshells, and gets you out of situations you'd have to force or pretend to stay in.
- He says he is lazy but won't accept bad outcomes: "I have an ability to walk away from suboptimal relationships or situations even if it's painful. I just won't stay in them."
- He dislikes VCs who call themselves "the team behind the team." The founder built the company and took the risk. Few VCs genuinely roll up their sleeves, and they're not the famous ones.

## Explore, then invest: how to choose a career, place and partner

- Naval says modern life reverses the ancestral setup. In a tribe, rejection and bad choices were costly, reputation was local and people married early. Now it's a search function: you can fly anywhere, enter any career and meet almost any people. That is daunting but means you don't have to stay in a bad situation.
- If you get rejected, "it's like a bad Google search result. Hit back and just search again." You'll meet 10-20,000 people in your life, out of eight billion.
- There are two phases, and you can't skip either:
  - Explore widely until you find the fit.
  - Then invest, because "all the benefits in life come from compound interest."
- What to invest in: a place, people, a business, knowledge, a career, your physical health. Find the work that "feels like play to you and looks like work to others," and a partner who makes you happy by being who they are, as you do for them.
- Failure modes:
  - Too little exploration: people spend 3 weeks job hunting and 5 years in the job.
  - Too much exploration: staying single until 50, then deciding to settle, or never investing in anything. "If you never invest in anything, it's an empty life."
- Weather matters more than you'd think, and he calls it prosaic but real.

## Judgment comes from iterations, not 10,000 hours

- Naval says Malcolm Gladwell's 10,000 hours is wrong. It is really about iterations, "not repetitions."
- An iteration means: do something, honestly reflect on the outcome, make a change, try again. Do that enough in any endeavor and it develops judgment.
- With judgment you stop trying everything and start rejecting most options, until you know the kind of person, place or work you want. Then you can start investing.
- He notes this is all ex post facto reasoning: easy to see looking back, hard to see moving forward.
- Advice only resonates if the listener has had the experience and now has a word for it. General principles are mental hooks. Specific help needs a back-and-forth (he likes Air Chat and Clubhouse for that), because he can't answer well without knowing someone's situation.
- He says he doesn't want to be only an advice giver. A good life means writing for yourself and your children and doing things that are "useful and beautiful and true." He gives advice only when asked something specific.

## Choose your environment, choose what to want

- Naval says environment is upstream of your thoughts, and an enlightened mind chooses its own environment. Choosing it consciously is the first step of adapting.
- He holds that you don't owe it to a relationship to struggle through it (unless you have kids), and that you can curate and change relationships.
- His tweet: "The only true test of intelligence is if you get what you want out of life." It is a two-part test: choosing what to want, then getting it. The wants should be achievable, at the edge of your range, and not make the process miserable.
- The first IQ test is whether you stay where you were born. The better place might be the next town or another country.
- If you pass that test, there is little to be envious of. He says no one on earth is someone he'd swap lives with.
- He recommends choosing inspiration over envy. Pull the inspiring part from people, such as Elon and Peter Thiel ("you should do important things, create the definite future you want to live in"), and ignore the rest.
- Steve Jobs gave him license to mix the spiritual and scientific (calligraphy, Zen, LSD, Pixar, NeXT, the fall and return). Jobs embodied his philosophy in products rather than lecturing, which Naval appreciates.
- He thinks Silicon Valley lacks a single cultural leader since Jobs, but there is plenty of mini-inspiration. Pick heroes and traits deliberately.
- Everyone is a "universal explainer" (Deutsch) and capable of anything. We are limited by social scripts. After you figure out the long-term things that make you better off, you can do things that make society better off, because obsessing only on yourself won't make you happy.
