## Wealth, money and status: three different games

- Naval defines wealth as "assets that earn while you sleep": businesses, factories with robots, code running at night, money reinvested in other businesses. A rented-out house counts too, though he sees it as a lower-productivity use.
- The purpose of wealth is freedom. He means not wearing "a tire like a collar around your neck", not waking at 7 a.m. to commute, and not having to be anywhere or do anything you don't want to. He calls Ferraris, yachts and Gulfstreams boring fast.
- Money is how wealth is transferred. It is an IOU from society for value you created in the past. Money "will solve all your money problems", not all your problems.
- Wealth is a positive-sum game. More houses built means building gets easier and more people can have one.
- Status is a zero-sum game. It is a ranking in the social hierarchy that goes back to monkey tribes, and politics and sports are examples. Naval says to avoid status games because they turn you into an angry, combative person.
- Many people attack wealth creation to signal status ("I don't need money"). He tells such people to opt out, but not to put down the game that keeps their supermarkets stocked and their roofs up.
- He distinguishes makers from takers. Societies with too many takers collapse, and he gives Venezuela as an example. He backs voluntary exchange between free minds in free markets, and says capitalism gets hijacked by monopolies, corruption and mispriced externalities.
- Mispriced externalities, such as pollution, should be fixed by pricing them properly. His example is raising the price of fresh water in a drought instead of running ads telling people to skip showers.

## The four kinds of luck and how to engineer your own

Naval takes this framework from a Marc Andreessen blog post. His claim is that wealth is a skill set, not luck. If he lost everything and were dropped on a random street in an English-speaking country, he says he'd be wealthy again in 5-10 years.

1. **Blind luck.** Fortune or fate. You can't control it.
2. **Hustle luck.** Persistence and energy create opportunities, like stirring a petri dish. The cliché is "fortune favors the bold".
3. **Preparation luck.** Skill and knowledge in a field let you spot lucky breaks others miss. The cliché is "chance favors the prepared mind".
4. **Reputation luck.** You build a unique character, brand or mindset so that opportunity finds you.

- His example for the fourth kind is the world's best deep-sea diver. When someone finds a sunken treasure ship, they come to him and give him half. He calls this "destiny", and quotes Disraeli: "we make our fortunes and we call them fate."
- Reputation works as a deal-flow engine. A trusted, high-integrity, long-term deal maker gets approached by strangers who want to do deals. Warren Buffett gets deals other people can't because of his reputation.
- Eccentricity helps because the world is efficient and the obvious places have already been dug. You need to work on a frontier and dig deeper than seems rational. He quotes Sam Altman ("extreme people get extreme results") and Jeffrey Pfeffer ("you can't be normal and expect abnormal returns").
- His own path was slow. He lost his first little fortune in the stock market and was cheated by business partners on the second. The third time worked. His wealth came from many small things stacking up, not from one giant payout.
- Don't take advice from people who got rich quickly: "they're just giving you their winning lottery ticket numbers." Survey enough advice and it cancels to zero, so form your own view.

## Why renting your time fails, and how to own equity and leverage

- He says you won't get rich renting out your time. You have to own equity, meaning a piece of a business, product or IP.
- Salaried work has three problems:
  - Inputs match outputs, so you don't earn when you sleep, vacation or retire.
  - The owner carries the risk, the IP and the brand, so they pay you the minimum needed.
  - You are replaceable and not creating anything original, and set roles can be taught or automated.
- Doctors who get really rich do it by opening a practice that builds a brand, or by owning a device, procedure or process.
- Look for work where inputs and outputs are disconnected. In software, one great engineer can create Bitcoin, while another can ship code for a year that nobody wants. A lumberjack's best-to-worst ratio is maybe 3x.
- Tools and creativity create that disconnect. Employees at tech companies can start by earning stock options, but the real wealth usually comes from starting companies or investing.
- Don't upgrade your lifestyle as income rises, because it raises what you think of as wealth and keeps you in the wage trap. He wants income in "discrete lumps separated over long periods".
- He argues high marginal tax rates punish creative, risk-taking work. That work can lose money for a decade and then pay off once in year 11 or 15.
- The oldest apprenticeship exception: renting your time is acceptable only while learning and saving, ideally in an apprenticeship business. Buffett offered to work for Benjamin Graham for free, and Graham said "you're overpriced."

## Specific knowledge, accountability, leverage and judgment

Naval gives four things to arm yourself with.

**Specific knowledge**
- It can't be trained. If it could, it could be mass-produced, coded or roboticized, and you'd be paid only the cost of training plus a return on it.
- It's found by following your innate talents, genuine curiosity and passion, not the hottest field. It sits at the edge of knowledge and is built through obsession and time on the job.
- It is often observed by others. His mother told him at 15 or 16 that he'd go into business, not astrophysics, because he critiqued pizza parlors on the street.
- A sign you have it: when asked what you do every day, you can't give a straight answer, because every day differs.
- On sales: a psychology degree helps a natural salesperson but not an introvert trying to learn sales.
- Compounding and leverage mean the person right 90% of the time gets paid hundreds of times more than the person right 80% of the time.
- Combine natural skills, as in Scott Adams's skill stack. Top 25% at three or more things makes you rare through combinatorics, but don't assemble skills too deliberately.
- Some specific knowledge is timeless, like persuasion, and some is timely, like machine learning, which lasts decades.
- He trains apprentices through Spearhead, which gives young founders a checkbook. He found classes and talks largely worthless after about an hour, while office hours on real deals are very useful.

**Accountability**
- Take business risks under your own name. Society rewards you with responsibility, equity and leverage.
- It means being willing to fail publicly. It doesn't mean being successfully accountable.
- Good teams are small with clearly delineated owners for product, messaging, fundraising, pricing and advertising.
- Equity is the riskiest claim, paid after salaries and debt, with nearly unlimited upside. Taking accountability is like taking an equity position in your work.
- Downside is limited in modern society. Bankruptcy and the failure of an honest effort get forgiven, but integrity failures such as Madoff's do not.
- He moved into philosophy under his own name around 2014-2015 despite warnings that he was ending his career.
- Companies measure inputs because they can't measure outputs, so make your outputs visible. Give credit away generously, because smart people will still know who was responsible.

**Leverage**
- Labor is the oldest form. Society overvalues it, and managing people is messy and "the worst form of leverage".
- Capital was the dominant form of the last century. It is hard to obtain, and you get it through specific knowledge and accountability.
- Products with no marginal cost of replication (code and media) are the newest form. They are permissionless and need nobody's permission.
- Examples are Joe Rogan reportedly making 50 to 100 million dollars a year, and PewDiePie.
- Tech startups combine the three: minimum-headcount, high-output labor (engineers and designers), capital for marketing and scale, and code and media.
- Learn to code. The "robot revolution" already happened in data centers, and coding lets you command that army.
- Code and media products are egalitarian. Jeff Bezos gets the same Google as everyone else.

**Judgment**
- Leverage is a force multiplier for judgment, so once you have leverage, slow down.
- He defines judgment as knowing the long-term consequences of your decisions. It comes from experience, short iterations and low emotion.
- A CEO whose judgment is 10-20% better on a hundred-billion-dollar ship earns hundreds of millions more.
- Buffett could be handed 100 billion dollars tomorrow on reputation alone.
- Outrage on someone's Twitter feed is a bad sign for judgment.

## Productize yourself: the two-word summary

- Naval compresses the whole tweet storm into "productize yourself". "Yourself" carries uniqueness, accountability and specific knowledge. "Productize" carries leverage and specific knowledge.
- Ask whether the work is authentic to you, and whether you are scaling it with labor, capital, code or media.
- The entrepreneur's job is to bring the high end to the mass market:
  1. Create something because you want it and know how to build it.
  2. Predict that society will want it.
  3. Scale it profitably, as Uber did with the private driver.
- The internet connects everyone to everyone, so any niche obsession can find an audience. Reaching just 50,000 passionate people can be a business.
- "Escape competition through authenticity." No one can compete with you on being you. Competition drives copycatting and the wrong game.
- Naval's opinions.com case: his early product review site was independent of Amazon and drifted into price comparison. It merged with DealTime and fought Shopzilla, PriceGrabber and NexTag, and that space went to zero when Amazon won e-tail. Local reviews, the area that became Yelp and TripAdvisor, was where he says he should have stayed.
- Too much competition can mean the masses are already there. A completely empty market can mean you are too authentic and lack product-market fit. He wants balance.
- Be the best in the world at what you do, and keep redefining what you do until it's true.
- Find "product-market-founder fit" (extending Andreessen's product-market fit), meaning how well you personally suit the business.
- For avoiding get-rich-quick sellers, he says there are no get-rich-quick schemes, because that is "someone else getting rich off you."
- That's also why his podcast carries no ads and sells nothing.
- Learn about wealth from people who made it elsewhere, not from those who sell the how-to.
- He tells his own hires they can leave to start companies, and he supports it. He won't ask anyone to be at a desk by 8 a.m., because he doesn't want to be there himself.

## Picking partners and industries: long-term games with long-term people

- All returns in wealth, relationships and knowledge come from compound interest. Most of the benefit arrives at the end, and a few deep compounding relationships beat many shallow ones.
- Play iterated games. Tit for tat only works when you play repeatedly. Silicon Valley works because people expect to meet again.
- Pick an industry where you can build a network and keep it. Hopping industries resets your trust to zero. Politics is an example of a transient field. A freshman class that turns over every two years cooperates less than long-term senators.
- Long-term games feel like everyone is making each other rich. Short-term games feel like cutting up a pie.
- Pick people for three traits: intelligence, energy and integrity. Integrity matters most, otherwise you get a "smart and hardworking crook".
- Read signals, which are what people do, not what they say. Someone who mistreats a waiter or is vindictive to an enemy will eventually treat you the same way.
- People who say they are honest probably aren't. Loudly claiming integrity is a conman's trait, the same as bidding for status.
- Self-esteem is the reputation you have with yourself.
- You can't keep people motivated by talking them into things. Motivation must be intrinsic.
- Don't partner with cynics and pessimists. Be a rational optimist with an action bias. He says it's better to be an irrational optimist than a rational cynic, because the downside is limited and the upside is unlimited.
- Never risk ruin. Stay out of jail, protect your health, and don't bet all your capital in one go. He says the common way to get ruined in modern business is cutting corners and unethical or illegal acts.
- Ethics is a selfish imperative over the long run. Honesty leaves you with a clear mind. Fair deals make you a hub that others route deals through.
- If the other side holds negotiating leverage, convert a single-move game into a repeat game through reputation, a second project or referrals. His example is hiring a contractor. "Negotiations are won by whoever cares less."

## Business model leverage and microeconomics

**Network effects, scale economies and zero marginal cost**
- Scale economies make unit cost fall with volume, which builds a barrier to entry.
- Zero marginal cost of reproduction applies to software and media. Rogan makes little on early episodes and much more on episode 1100 for about the same work.
- Metcalfe's law says a network's value goes with the square of its nodes. A network of 10 has a value of 100 and a network of 100 has a value of 10,000.
- Network effects create natural monopolies. His examples are Facebook, Uber (better economics than Lyft with more drivers and riders), Google, Twitter and YouTube.
- Define a network effect as each additional user adding value to existing users, with language as the classic case.
- Design hooks so customers add value to each other. He calls that the ultimate leverage.
- Think big, because "it takes just as much effort to create a small business as a large one".

**Pricing**
- Price discrimination means charging by willingness to pay, and you must offer something extra in return. Business class costs 5 to 10x economy but only perhaps 2 to 3x to provide.
- Enterprise freemium software often charges 10, 100 or infinite times more for secure, hosted or multi-user admin versions.
- Consumer surplus is the value customers get above the price. His example is his morning coffee, which he'd pay $20 for. All businesses generate it, and Amazon generates trillions of it.

**Other concepts**
- The principal-agent problem is the one he applies most. Owners and employees have different incentives, so give top lieutenants generous ownership.
- Get aligned partnerships, not deals where you win a lopsided advantage.
- If you are an employee, think like a principal. He prefers boutique firms or solo lawyers and bankers, because at big firms the principal sells and an agent who cares less does the work.
- Net present value discounts future payoffs for startup risk. Founders who say a 0.1% stake in a billion-dollar company is worth a million dollars should discount it. If the company was valued at 10 million dollars in the last round, he says the package is worth about $10,000.
- The Kelly criterion and ergodicity mean you shouldn't bet everything even with an edge.
- A Schelling point lets people coordinate without communicating, such as two competitors converging on $10 inside an $8-$12 range.
- Pareto superior solutions are better in some ways and no worse in others. Always take them in a negotiation.

## Time, hard work and learning to learn

**Valuing your time**
- Set an absurdly high hourly rate and stick to it. If it doesn't feel too high, raise it.
- He used $5,000 an hour before he had money, and looking back he says it was really about $1,000.
- Outsource anything you can for less than that rate, including cooking.
- Paul Graham's advice for YC founders is to work on the product and product-market fit, exercise and eat healthy, and little else.

**Hard work**
- He frames the "should you work hard" debate as two different games. Lifestyle businesses are one, and "the Olympics of startups" is the other.
- The order of operations is: first, pick what to work on, where the market, your specific knowledge and your interest overlap. Second, pick the best people. Third, work as hard as you can. They are three legs of a stool.
- For choosing a startup to join, pick the one with the best alumni network, like the PayPal mafia.
- Work like a lion hunting, not a marathoner. Sprint, rest, reassess and repeat. Claims of 80 to 120 hour weeks are mostly status signaling.
- Inspiration is perishable, so act on it right away. Be "impatient with actions, patient with results".
- Keep a clean calendar but stay busy on the highest-impact thing. Decline meetings ruthlessly, push calls to emails and emails to texts, and use walking or standing meetings if you must.
- Famous or busy people take meetings if you bring proof of work, like product progress, which is "the unshakeable, unfakeable resume". A busy mind and calendar destroy great work.
- Early-career networking leans on blind and hustle luck.
- Prefer smaller organizations, which have more accountability, visibility and flexibility. He suggests moving from large companies to progressively smaller ones.

**Learning**
- "Read what you love until you love to read." The foundation is reading, writing, arithmetic, persuasion and computer programming.
- Read foundational originals, not recent summaries:
  - Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations*
  - Darwin's *Origin of Species*
  - Feynman's *Six Easy Pieces*
  - Watson and Crick (he names *The Eighth Day of Creation*, a book he attributes to them)
  - Taleb
- Study microeconomics, game theory, psychology, persuasion, ethics, mathematics and computers. He says "there is no skill called business". He treats macroeconomics as politics, and calls business-school case studies "anecdotes".
- Learning curves run on iterations, not hours. Run new marketing experiments, change the inventory and branding, and talk to other owners. Accept frequent small failures.
- Learn game theory by playing games, not from books. Run your own lemonade stand.
- Ignore business journalists, economists and academics on private companies, because they've never built anything.
- He treats advice as Maxims that become mental hooks. For example: "if you can't see yourself working with someone for life, don't work with them for a day."

## What wealth won't give you, and who the advice fits

- When you're finally wealthy you'll realize it wasn't what you were seeking. You're still the same person: if you were unhappy, you stay unhappy. Many rich people he knows are out of shape or have bad family lives.
- His favorite tweet: "a calm mind, a fit body and a house full of love. These things cannot be bought, they must be earned."
- He plans a separate tweet storm on happiness, written for everyday people who want a calmer internal state.
- Common failure modes he sees:
  - Misunderstanding specific knowledge.
  - Thinking accountability means being successfully accountable.
  - Reading it as "quit your nine-to-five". You can apply accountability, leverage and specific knowledge inside a current job.
  - Agreeing only with the parts that match existing biases. The parts you disagree with matter most.
  - Thinking it only applies to tech. The real estate ladder (day laborer, general contractor, developer, REIT, then a real estate-tech company like Trulia, Redfin or Zillow) shows each rung adding specific knowledge, accountability and leverage.
- Tech is pushed because it supplies free leverage.
- He says the principles are minimum viable ones. He has put out the bedrock he's solidly sure of and has seen no tweet contradict it.
- Mid-career is the hardest pivot, but the ideas can guide weekend projects, self-education and the roles you take.
- Look up the value chain at who gets leverage from your work. Aim to manage capital, products, media and community, not people.
- Avoid repetitive drudgery. Even a barista can innovate. Founders struggle to find people with a founder mentality.
- If you're stuck in a job, find the part with the steepest learning curve and take on accountability. Offer to solve the owner's biggest problem, like raising capital to keep the factory running.
- Accountability comes first, then specific knowledge. Judgment and leverage come later.
- His long-term vision is that everyone works for themselves, and what works for you is software, and eventually physical robots.
- Eventual outcome is roughly distinctiveness of specific knowledge × leverage × how often your judgment is correct × how singularly accountable you are × how much society values it, compounded over time. He warns that this is false precision and that really it's philosophy, not math. Better to pick the one or two biggest variables: what you're good at, and what the market values.
