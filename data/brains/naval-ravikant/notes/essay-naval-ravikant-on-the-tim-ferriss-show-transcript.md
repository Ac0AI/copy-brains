## Happiness is a skill and a choice, not an outcome of success

- Naval says worldly success pulled him away from happiness. He got what he wanted, then noticed peers with similar success "just didn't seem all that happy." He describes strong hedonic adaptation: he got used to anything quickly.
- His conclusion: "happiness is internal," and all real success is internal. External effort still has to be done because humans are biologically wired for it.
- He argues more worldly success builds the ego, increases fear of loss, and deepens concern about what others think.
- He treats happiness as largely the absence of suffering. It comes from peace, which comes from understanding yourself and watching desire, judgment and reaction. He separates it from pleasure, which he sees as momentary.
- Core claim: happiness is "a skill that you develop and a choice that you make." You decide it matters, prioritize it, read everything on it, and work at it like building muscle or learning calculus.
- He thinks Buddhists have done the best work here and modern science has weak answers. He calls the modern world "full of distractions," and says Twitter and Facebook make people unhappy through dispute, comparison, jealousy and anger over things that don't matter.
- Practical hacks he uses:
  - Insight meditation, aimed at understanding how his mind works.
  - Catching himself judging and finding the positive interpretation (once took seconds, now sub-second).
  - More sunlight on skin.
  - Telling friends he is a happy person, so consistency bias keeps him living up to it.
  - Asking "Is it so important to me that I be unhappy unless this goes my way?" when he catches himself desiring something.
  - Dropping caffeine and working out daily ("If you have peace of body, it's easier to have peace of mind").
- He says these hacks won't fix severe depression but can upgrade happiness slightly.
- He quotes a monk, via his brother Kamal: the secret to peace is saying "Yes" to everything that happens. Judging separates you from things and people, leading to loneliness and suffering.

## Not fearing death, and not taking yourself too seriously

- Naval says an insight he holds that most people don't: "I'm not afraid of death anymore."
- He argues much of life's struggle (building lasting works, businesses, books, even obsession with children) comes from a fear of death and a quest for immortality.
- His reasoning: the universe is vast and old, humans are like "monkeys on a small rock," and our lives are "like a firefly blinking once in the night." Whole civilizations are remembered in a word or two ("Sumerian. Mayan.").
- Whether or not you believe in an afterlife, he says the conclusion is the same: life is short and precious, so "there's no excuse for spending most of your life in misery."
- This is not a license for nihilism or bad behavior. He says to be moral, take care of others, sleep well and enjoy yourself, but "don't take yourself so seriously."
- Stated life philosophy (he says he may change it next year): keep growing and learning, seek truth, accept things as they are, see the world as it really is, and live your life. He thinks deeper goals and ideologies lead to desire, belief systems, disappointment and conflict.
- On planning: he avoids life reviews, quarterly plans and micro-control of himself. He focuses on one or two important things and surrenders the rest, which leaves him able to appreciate everything he already has.

## Habits: the meta-skill of making and breaking them

- Naval calls learning to break habits "a very important meta-skill that can serve you better in life than almost anything else," and says you only learn it by actually breaking one.
- His cadence: break one bad habit every six months, and pick up one good habit every six months to a year. He says this keeps you from sliding backward and compounds into gradual improvement.
- Daily exercise is his anchor habit. Do the same activity at the same time, seven days a week, early or on waking. It forces other fixes (no late drinking or partying, less caffeine) and teaches how habits work. He says the specific workout matters less than the daily consistency.
- Techniques to break a habit:
  - Substitute something else.
  - Go cold turkey.
  - Wean off gradually.
  - Use social proof by telling others.
  - Stack other habits that leave no time for it.
  - Remove the triggers.
  - Tone down the rewards.
- He lost weight over about a decade through slow habit changes (going paleo, low-carb, cutting processed food), not one epiphany, and says he has almost never slid backward.
- He rereads The Power of Habit to keep in mind that "humans are basically habit machines." Habits give efficiency but also allow addiction and unconscious living.

## What Naval would teach in school and how he approaches learning

- Curriculum he would run for children: happiness, nutrition, diet, exercise, building and breaking habits, relationships and finding a spouse, meditation, basic skills over memorized facts, and old books that have withstood the test of time.
- Practical add-ons: run a lemonade stand or small business to earn money, do charity work, visit the third world to see real suffering, learn public speaking, business writing, basic persuasion and some programming.
- He would cut chunks of geography, history, second or third languages, and music (unless there is an inclination). Reason: we live in the Wikipedia era, so memorized facts are largely irrelevant; kids will likely not need to drive; and aptitude should guide emphasis.
- On his own education (computers and economics): microeconomics was very useful, macroeconomics mostly useless. Theoretical computer science (math, algorithms) was most useful because it doesn't change, while learning Java or Fortran faded. He'd add more math and physics, plus psychology and evolution, to understand people.
- Principle: "focus on theory and principles over facts." The most important skill is knowing how to learn; with good math and a love of reading, "there's nothing you can't learn on your own."
- Reading habit: he keeps around 50 books on Kindle plus several physical books, reads by mood and by chapter rather than sequentially, and finds this makes him read regularly. He says rereading the greats beats reading something not as great.
- Books he named:
  - Matt Ridley (Genome, The Red Queen, Origins of Virtue, The Rational Optimist, The Evolution of Everything).
  - The Lessons of History (Durant).
  - The Prophet (Gibran).
  - Perfectly Reasonable Deviations (Feynman).
  - The Power of Myth (Campbell).
  - Mastering the Core Teachings of the Buddha (Daniel Ingram).
  - Total Freedom (Krishnamurti).
  - Bed of Procrustes (Taleb).
  - Science fiction such as Greg Egan.

## Working in a post-corporate economy and building an independent brand

- Naval applies Coase's 1937 paper: the industrial revolution pulled people into hierarchies because factories required scale. Information technology now lowers communication and transaction costs, so people can work through computers as independent contractors.
- Examples he cites: Uber driving (a weaker example), freelancers finding work through Twitter and online sources, and AngelList startup jobs. Marketplaces he names include Pick Crew, Gigster, Elance, Craigslist and oDesk.
- Example of the better version: a world-class journalist no longer needs the New York Times. Start a blog or Twitter in spare time, build an independent brand, and late in the curve (YouTube star or popular blogger) charge for access and earn from books and newsletters, working from anywhere.
- His advice for the next 20 years:
  - Find something you love and have a shot at being among the best in the world at it.
  - Build an independent brand around your own name, not a company's.
  - Do creative work, because anything not creative can be replicated by society and then underpaid.
  - Get comfortable with boom-bust work (a few weeks busy, a few weeks off).
- Timing: "It will be gradual, and then it will be sudden." The best preparation is to not give up your independence in the first place.

## Startup careers, locations and long-run effort

- To enter startups without money or connections, Naval's answer is to move to San Francisco or another startup hub: Austin, LA, New York, Boston, Berlin, London, Bangalore, Shanghai, or parts of Delhi or Beijing. "All the other people who are in startups are in these places, so you have to get in the flow." The same logic applies to any industry: actors go to Hollywood, finance people go to New York, London or Hong Kong.
- Ways in once there: learn to code at App Academy, Hack Reactor or General Assembly; volunteer for startups; start in customer service or operations. He says startups are moved forward by people willing to do the work, and with that attitude "you'll figure your way out within a couple of years."
- On choosing between big tech, early-stage startups, bootstrapping or a VC-funded company: it is "highly, highly contextual."
  - If you know the company, the cofounders and the space, go do it.
  - If not, get as close to it as possible by joining a very early startup.
  - If you want lifestyle and steady money for family, join a later-stage startup clearly on the path to success.
  - Startups are a young person's game. He suggests taking at least one shot before 30 or 35, though older founders exist.
- Treat advice from investors as self-serving. "Incentives are everything... Incentives are superpowers."
- Long-run view, paraphrasing Charlie Munger: get up early, work hard, learn something every day, and if you live long enough you eventually get what you deserve. Investing taught him how much randomness there is. He notes "individual entrepreneurial efforts often fail, but individual entrepreneurs over their careers rarely fail" as long as they keep taking shots and getting back up.
- He made a small fortune in the 1999 dot-com bubble and lost it by holding too long. He says that was a good lesson, because later money was respected as rare and hard to make. He says people who win early often learn the wrong lessons.
- For people on US visas wanting to start a company while keeping a job, he points to Unshackled (unshackled.co), an accelerator he has a small investment in.

## Guarding time, saying no, and choosing who you work with

- Naval calls inbound requests for coffees, meetings and calls "the bane of my existence." He once owned idontdocoffee.com and replied from it, but now calls that rude and petulant. He has since become skilled at avoiding time-sucking meetings.
- Core claim: "Time is all you have in this world." Being busy kills productivity and happiness. As you age you have more obligations, so you must be ruthless.
- Heuristic he endorses from Derek Sivers: it's "Hell yes" or "No." If he's not excited, he doesn't do it.
- Keep empty space on the calendar. He recommends at least one day a week, preferably budgeting two, with no meetings, just thinking time. "It's only after you're bored that you have the great ideas." Also leave room for serendipity from new people.
- He says people generally don't disrespect you for declining; they often want your time more because you are discriminating with it.
- His most popular tweet: "If you can't see yourself working with somebody for life, don't work with them for a day." He doesn't say to refuse people literally; he says to treat early time as a search function across jobs, partners and hobbies, then go all-in on the people and things you love, because "all the greatness in life comes from compound interest, whether it's in investments or it's in relationships."
- Five chimps theory: a chimp's mood and behavior can be predicted by the five chimps it spends the most time with, so "choose your five chimps carefully," ideally happy people.
- On conflict: he admits he handles it poorly and gets angry, so he tries to notice the anger and its bodily signs before reacting. His best advice is not to associate with high-conflict people, because someone always fighting others will eventually fight you. He drifts away from them without announcing it.
- Money has a steep discount rate with age. His test: how many years of youth would you trade for losing all your money and job while keeping friends and family? For him it's about two to three years, and he says the number shrinks as you get older.

## Artificial intelligence: general vs specific

- Naval doubts general-purpose AI is close. He says people who talk about Skynet-style AI don't write much code, since no fundamental breakthroughs have been made; current work is similar code run faster or on more data. The brain works very differently from computers.
- On the Turing test: he thinks we are "barely any closer" than 20 or 30 years ago.
- Possible exception: an emergent "social AI" arising from all the world's computers networked together. He expects it to emerge slowly and softly, not self-modify like a classic general AI, and to be built to serve humans and woven into the human fabric.
- Investing view: he is not worried about general-purpose AI and does not think general-purpose AI companies have much of a future. Specific AI companies solving narrow problems (computer vision, self-driving cars, self-piloting drones) "could be very real," using lots of data, processing power and good code.
