## Why software is commoditized and AI research is the remaining chokepoint
- Naval says hardware used to be "the moat that buys you time to build a software castle." Software was the high-margin layer because it had network effects and lock-in. Patrick Collison's line was that software locks you in.
- VCs historically avoided hardware as a commodity business. Even hardware founders got asked what their software lock-in was.
- Claude Code "burned software down." Once you can specify a piece of software, an AI can one-shot or two-shot it in hours.
- China commoditizes hardware. It subsidizes production up front, becomes the global supplier, drives rivals out, then controls the supply chain. Naval thinks no one beats China on hardware in the next decade. Rebuilding US manufacturing "is going to take a full generation," though he supports it.
- What is left uncommoditized is AI research itself, the new software engineering. It is not democratized. Doom and Quake were built by a small team that could compete with EA and Activision. AI needs massive GPU clusters, proprietary data sets and a few researchers.
- Regulatory capture makes it worse. Value and choke points are moving into a few AI companies.
- His read on China's strategy: the Chinese government funds labs and tells them they don't need to make money. They can be number two or three and open-source the models so others can learn. If software is commoditized, China wins on hardware.

## Why open-source models are catching up
- Naval says the gap to the frontier has gone from about 12 months to 9 to 6, and some now say 3. He calls it "very jagged," caught up in some domains and not in others.
- He lays out several theories and says he has no settled view:
  - China does its own pre-training. It has compute and data, and it crawls YouTube, Reddit and the rest with less copyright constraint.
  - Distillation of US models. He says Anthropic's complaint is rich, since it distilled the open web. Data brokers resell subsidized Claude Max tokens at about 80% off, and KYC is "a dime a dozen."
  - Real algorithmic breakthroughs, such as the DeepSeek R1 paper. He guesses most AI mathematicians and researchers are Chinese or of Chinese origin. Researchers share schools and housing and hop between labs, taking data with them. No conspiracy is needed.
  - Leaked weights. AI labs lack the security of a national secure compartment facility, and a leaked model can be used to distill and train at high speed.
  - The commoditization dynamic above, with China subsidizing open source.
- He proposes a policy that would be popular: companies that trained on the open web and open data must open their models after about 12 months.
- Open source tends to hold a lead once it takes one. An ecosystem plugs into it, especially in enterprise. A closed lab then has to spend scarce resources jumping meaningfully ahead before its cash runs out.
- He says the AI race went from five kings to two (OpenAI and Anthropic) because only they earn revenue directly from models. They have active user bases that feed reinforcement learning. He adds "don't bet against Elon" because of the SpaceX war chest.
- On Google, he says it never got the basic app working, and calls the company bloated with overlapping PM-driven products. He says it was a mistake to reach it only through Google One or Workspace upsells.
- He sees two or more competing platforms as healthy for startups, as with Apple and Android after 2007. A single monopolist at the top of the chain is "a very bad situation for startups."

## Paying for frontier intelligence vs. cheaper models
- Naval says he will always pay for more intelligence. He would rather be right more often, his time is valuable, and he applies leverage to judgment calls.
- He might choose differently for highly repetitive tasks.
- His argument is that errors compound. Take one model with a 99.9% success rate and another at 90%. That is a 100x difference in error rate. When they loop recursively over many steps, the 90% one degrades far more.
- He complains that frontier models railroad him, with refusals and tone-policing ("I shouldn't be telling you that").
- Product idea he would like to see, "truth.ai":
  - Take the best open-source model.
  - Put it in a very good harness with all the tools.
  - Ship it as a polished desktop app.
  - Jailbreak it so it never pushes back or tone-polices, and it just tells the truth.
- He says even Grok is "a little neutered."
- On models in general, he says he used to assume AI was like fusion, something that would never arrive. He missed 2020 to 2022 and is now "AI maxing" to catch up.
- He says the best way to predict the future is to live in it. Everyone should be AI maxing, because otherwise you have no sense of where it is going.
- He agrees Claude Code was the practical tipping point, in December 2025. Before it, AI was a better Google search. It unlocked real use cases.

## Using AI for writing, and respecting the reader
- Naval does not use AI to produce output that a human is expected to read. He says that is a disservice and wastes the reader's time.
- He sees code differently. Code is consumed by a computer, so using a computer to write it is fine. If something is meant for a human, he wants to understand it, "ruminate, marinate within it," respect the reader's time and give them the insight nugget.
- Good writing and speaking are outputs of good thinking. If you outsource the writing, you lose the muscle.
- AI is too verbose and too clinical. It is also bad at summarizing and at novelty. Good writing is unexpected, and next-token prediction struggles with that. When co-writing, any good turn of phrase usually came from somewhere else on the internet.
- He does use AI for brainstorming: synonyms, associated ideas, tangential information. He also argues with it.
- His practical alternative is "just send me the prompt." He prefers short, compressed communication over AI sludge that the recipient's AI then reads.
- He sees AI-written posts and emails as a lower-class signal. He complains that he wants his own AI to read them, so neither side is in the loop.
- He also pushes back on the view that quality will be indistinguishable soon. Even if it were, he says he would lose interest on learning that something, including art, was made by AI.
- His line on human desire: robots may have desires, but they cannot replace human desire. As long as humans want things and AI helps fulfill them, humans stay in the loop.

## Staying employed, AI anxiety and robotics
- Naval says the real unresolved question is whether AI reaches ASI through recursive self-improvement. He says AI is now "the pro at everything" but he does not yet see broad creativity, the "last creative mile" of creating something new rather than recombining training data.
- If AI stops at expert level and still needs human guidance, taste and creativity, people become "Pokemon trainers": AI and robot handlers.
- He says people who use AI are more productive than ever and are not being displaced. In his view the only people displaced are those who refuse to use it.
- He frames the real risk as social. People fear being replaced and want off the train, so they attack data centers over a pretext like water, which he says was debunked. He compares this to a thundering herd that can run toward a cliff, and you are inside the herd.
- He says rapid change is more disruptive. This wave displaces white-collar work, government, managers, academics and journalists.
- He says concentrated control is scarier than broad access. He worries about national approval gates for models, as with the reported slow rollout of a new model to government-approved partners first.
- On learning, he says AI teaches him better than his college classes. It meets him at his exact level and he can ask dumb questions repeatedly, but "I just have to care."
- On college, he calls it mostly socializing, babysitting and credentialism, with a little education in STEM.
- His line on jobs: "We don't want more [bad] jobs. We want better [bad] jobs."
- On robotics, he proposes "UBR," universal basic robot. He says boosters see robots 2 to 3 years away and skeptics 5 to 10, and no one thinks they are impossible.
- He joked about his own tweet from a couple of years ago, that soon everybody would have AI anxiety.

## Trade, tariffs and China
- Naval says he sees no reason for the US to go to war with China. He thinks China is internally focused and wants to keep its empire, and he calls the CCP closer to capitalist-fascist than communist.
- He argues that Taiwan's elites avoid conscription and that US defense of Taiwan is unrealistic because of missiles and drones. His view is that Taiwan will slowly reunite with China over 10 to 20 years.
- He says a war would be foolish when the US has domestic problems: low growth, high inflation, and fights over spoils.
- He supports tariffs and barriers against Chinese subsidies, especially in network-effect and scale-economy businesses. The Ricardo "bananas and watermelons" model is too simple. A producer that gets better becomes cheaper, and a network effect creates lock-in.
- You need a level playing field: aligned currencies and no subsidies.

## California and the US as a talent magnet
- Naval says the US stays ahead because of a thin layer. It takes the best and brightest from all over the world and incentivizes them to compete with each other. Silicon Valley and California collect disproportionate rewards.
- He argues California has a monopoly on warm, dry, coastal land. It holds 30 to 50% of US GDP in one state and should be five or six states.
- He calls California's direct democracy, where 50.1% can vote for anything, a bad design.
- He says he likes the US as the last bastion of freedom. If it fails, he predicts it would fail like a Latin American country, with cartels and crime, not like Europe's retirement-home model.
