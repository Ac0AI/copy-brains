## Judge by reality, not by peers
- Naval agrees with Taleb that people judged only by peers drift away from reality. Examples are academics and journalists.
- Tech has the same problem. Companies track inputs (hours, being in at 8:00 a.m., 40-50 hour weeks) instead of outputs.
- He argues a great engineer can create a billion dollars of value, and a bad one can destroy value. Neither outcome depends on hours put in.
- Satoshi Nakamoto is his example of that scale of output.
- In markets, put your money where your mouth is. Online arguments about Bitcoin's price are pointless when you can short it or take a position on an exchange.
- Experts who called Bitcoin "rat poison" or a Ponzi at low prices were wrong while it kept rising. Without a P&L they pay nothing for being wrong.

## Macro is a distraction; polish is a poor signal when investing
- Naval says he got an economics degree alongside his computer science degree and calls it a mistake. In 20 years of venture investing, "macro has been completely worthless" to him.
- He calls macro entertainment and "another branch of politics". He says even micro isn't judged by contact with reality.
- On early-stage teams, he avoids founders who look incredibly polished: good PowerPoint, well dressed, presents well.
- He prefers the person busy at the computer who gets flustered at the whiteboard and over-explains, but has real substance.
- He goes further and argues against business plans. Plans are too well written and full of buzzwords, and rejecting them on that basis is reasonable.
- Awards like "VC of the year" or "angel of the year" are nonsense to him. The reward is making the money, like simply owning the Bitcoin.

## Asymmetry, optionality and anti-fragility in crypto
- Naval describes Bitcoin as an asymmetric bet. If you lose, you lose your money. If it becomes digital gold, you could make 50x or 100x.
- He calls it anti-fragile. Each hack, attack or ecosystem failure it survives gets fixed by developers, so the system improves and is better prepared for the next shock.
- He treats shorting crypto as the wrong side of the trade: a concave position with limited upside and large downside. His example is shorting at $40 and watching it run 100x.
- He notes trial and error is a main source of asymmetry, because you keep what works and discard what doesn't.
- Competition between cryptocurrencies makes them anti-fragile, as competition between issuers did in ancient times. People pick the currency least likely to be debased.
- He sees crypto as an attempt to separate money from the state, like separating church and state. If it works, he expects it to be just as impactful and to take a long time.

## Lindy and what survives
- Naval defines Lindy as stands the test of time. A book that has lasted thousands of years is likely to last thousands more, and survival so far is the best predictor of survival.
- Applied to technology, new things are more vulnerable to newer things. He treats old tools such as the fork as the model for what lasts.
- A commercial transaction coupled with a financial one is a Lindy concept, and blockchain inherits it.
- Bitcoin is the most Lindy cryptocurrency because it has survived longest.
- He says it won't be digital gold until it has survived long enough. The longer it survives, the more faith and money people can put into it.

## Minority rule and intransigence
- Naval calls the minority rule counterintuitive and says it changed how he thinks about many things. The idea is that a small intransigent minority can set the rules for a flexible majority.
- He first hoped the most outraged people on Twitter, on the extreme left, the extreme right or in crypto, were simply ineffective and off in a corner. The minority rule shows that if they are intransigent enough, they can control society.
- In elections he rejects the view that the median voter decides. If a few percent of voters won't compromise, for example Bernie voters who refuse the mainstream Democrat or hard-right voters who refuse Jeb Bush, candidates must appeal to them.
- He compares revolutionaries: small bands who won't tolerate the status quo, while most people in the middle just go along.
- For crypto, he imagines 10-20% of good Silicon Valley developers refusing any pay except crypto. Payroll systems would then switch to offering it.
- He says this lesson applies when you tolerate intolerance: "you're going to live with the consequences".

## Science, survival and dynamic risk
- Naval states that science can only disprove, not prove. "Proven by science" misreads the process.
- He says the result isn't what matters; the process is. Consensus and counts of Nobel winners don't matter, because one counterexample can destroy an argument.
- On path dependence, he explains with Russian roulette. Six people each playing once for a billion dollars is very different from one person playing six times in sequence. Confusing the two gives the wrong answer.
- Loss aversion, he argues, is rational, because if you go bust you can never recover. In crypto, going all-short or selling everything can take you out of the game for good.
- His rule is to "stay in the game".

## Courage, skin in the game and conferences
- Naval says courage is the one virtue that cannot be faked. On Twitter you can fake other virtues, but not taking on someone powerful under your own name rather than a troll account.
- He credits Taleb with naming the "intellectual yet idiot" and the Bob Rubin trade by name. In his view Taleb's work will last a thousand years.
- He offers a test for any proposed censorship mechanism: build it, then hand it to your enemy to run. If you're unwilling, you just want to be in charge.
- He distinguishes equality of opportunity from equality of outcome. Equality of outcome is communism and coercion, while equality of opportunity is what you strive for, narrowed through education.
- He generally doesn't travel for conferences, because they lean toward signaling, like looking busy for your boss. Sitting in an audience has little value when you could watch it on YouTube.
- The exception is meeting the right people when they are small.
- He recommends reading Taleb's five Incerto books, including Skin in the Game, Black Swan and Antifragile, instead of reading anything else that year.
