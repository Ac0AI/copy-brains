## Success factors: how Naval rates hard work, network, intelligence and skills

Scott Adams asked Naval to score success variables from 1 to 10.

- **Hard work:** "3 or 4 maybe," and about 7 or 8 at some point early on.
  - Silicon Valley winner-take-all races (the next $50 billion company) are the exception. Only one company can win, so everything scores 10.
  - For most people the reason is leverage: "technology capital media make it so easy to multiply your work."
  - What you do and how well you do it matters more than how hard you do it.
- **Network:** Early on it didn't feel important to him. Looking back, he rates it about 8 or 9.
  - Moving to Silicon Valley was a network decision, because you join the network by moving there.
  - His college network mattered little. He built work that people would see and recognize, and the network emerged from that.
  - Where you start out means fewer hops to where you want to end up.
- **Intelligence:** He calls himself biased and rates general intelligence as the number one trait.
  - Pair it with open-mindedness and intellectual curiosity.
  - A smart, open-minded person who questions everything will work out the rest at a good enough level. That includes nutrition, workouts, habits, making money, sales and persuasion, and development skills.
  - He calls this "the meta thing" that makes you pretty good at everything.
- **Skills:** He agrees that the right combination of skills matters (the "skill stack" idea Scott raised).

## Backing entrepreneurs versus businesses

- **Entrepreneurs:** Many businesses fail, and good entrepreneurs chasing bad businesses fail too. But the entrepreneurs themselves don't fail over a long enough period.
  - "When you meet the right entrepreneur you know they're gonna be a winner." The open question is how many attempts it takes.
  - His implication for investors is to back the person a few times.
- **Early stage versus mature:** Early on it is much more about the people.
  - As a business gets larger and more mature it becomes "like a fortress," and the business matters more than the operator.
- **Buffett's approach:** Buffett says to invest in businesses any idiot could run, because one eventually will.
  - Naval says Buffett invests in natural monopolies (moats).
  - His Coca-Cola example is physiological and psychological addiction. Cola has "zero taste build-up," so a heavy drinker never tires of it and can swap out water entirely.
  - He says products that go in your mouth form deep bonds, and he compares Coke to toothpaste and Gillette.
  - His point is that if your business is not a natural monopoly, then "it's all about the people."
- **Packaging as delight:** Amazon replaced cold packs with water bottles in its food delivery. A cold pack annoys the customer, but a free bottle feels like an extra. He compares this to Apple's packaging, where you fall in love before you touch the product.

## How Naval got started: Stuyvesant and the network effect of a credential

- He was an unknown kid in New York City from an immigrant, single-parent household. He wasn't a good student.
- Passing the Stuyvesant High School entrance test, an SAT-style exam taken in eighth grade, "saved my life."
  - Stuyvesant was the hardest public school in New York City to get into. Bronx Science and Brooklyn Tech took the next-highest scorers.
  - The school brand opened the door to an Ivy League college and then to tech.
- He says it started with no network but quickly became a network, and that a test-based credential gave an instant validation network.
- His first tech startup was in the dot-com boom. His team treated hard work as heroic and competed to spend time at the office. They slept under their desks and ate the packing foam from Amazon shipments.
  - He frames this as young founders not knowing better.
  - It fits his current view that how hard you work matters less than what you work on.

## Blockchain as a new form of network governance

- **Core framing:** Humans win as cooperators. English, the US, the dollar and Uber are all networks.
  - Humans can cooperate across genetic boundaries, unlike ants or bees.
  - Every network needs control over cheaters: who gets in, who gets paid, who pays, and who is not doing their job.
- **Four historical governance forms:** individual rulers (kings, tyrants, emperors), elites or aristocracies, democracies ("mobs"), and companies such as Uber or Facebook.
- **The fifth form:** Blockchains bring market-style governance. It is code running in the cloud that nobody owns.
  - Example: a storage network where anyone who contributes storage gets paid and anyone who uses it pays.
  - The blockchain is the ledger of debits and credits. It punishes cheaters and rewards reliable contributors.
- **Permissionless, programmable, uncensorable networks:**
  - Today you must go through Visa, MasterCard or PayPal to move money in code. That means big fees, no micro-transactions and permission requirements.
  - Finance has been shielded from programmers. Programmable finance lets engineers take over a privilege held by bankers, which is the "money craze."
  - Uncensorable means no gatekeeper in the middle taking 30%, and no shutdown of access in places where Twitter or Facebook are blocked.
- **Uses beyond money:** energy grids and bandwidth allocation.

## Why Bitcoin is hard for governments to stop

- Bitcoin "is basically insurance against politicians." It has value only because it isn't politically controlled.
  - A cryptocurrency controlled by a dictator, a democracy, a mob, an elite or a company is "an oxymoron" and has no value.
  - The US dollar already offers a government-backed option, so a government coin offers nothing new.
- **Legal angle:** Code is speech, and the First Amendment protects it. He points to the Clipper Chip and PGP export fights under Clinton. He also cites 3D-printed gun schematics (Cody Wilson, Defense Distributed) as not illegal.
- **Technical angle against a ban:**
  - Crypto traffic can be hidden inside other traffic.
  - Other traffic will eventually hide inside crypto traffic.
  - Blockchains can be synced by satellite broadcast. A hidden computer in a remote area could validate for its community.
- **Market view:** He says all the Bitcoin in the world was then worth about one twentieth of gold. He reads that as the market pricing a low probability that it survives. He says that if it survives it will go "20 X from here."
- He declines to give investment advice and says to do your own homework.

## Reproducing ideas, and the Golden Age view

- **Ideas as reproduction:** He says having an impact can matter more than having kids. He suggests that "memetic replication is more effective than pure genetic replication." Scott's books and his own writing are the example.
- **Golden Age:** He agrees with Scott's definition. Things are moving the right way on the big issues, and the remaining problems are psychological rather than resource problems.
  - Technology and capitalism have created abundance. Infant mortality and poverty are falling, and diseases such as malaria are being eradicated.
  - Progress is unevenly distributed. The faster it happens, the more extreme the gaps. China has both billionaires and people on a dollar a day.
  - He says perception that things are getting worse persists even though the statistics are improving.
  - Prosperity continues "as long as we don't blow up the engines that create it, which are basically capitalism, technology and trade."

## Cooperation, opponents and what to teach

- **Cooperation:** "Cooperation is the human advantage." Anytime you can cooperate with more people, you win.
  - This is why people move to Silicon Valley for tech or Hollywood for acting. You connect with like-minded people in one ecosystem.
  - The more people you can work with, the richer you will be.
- **Opponents:** His first step is not declaring anyone your opponent. Often the people we call enemies don't see us that way, and as ego softens we see they never were.
  - He argues it is better to assume you have no enemies. Two companies that find a way to work together are probably better off.
- **Teaching (his curriculum):**
  - Math and applied science, taught through building and experiments. He says all high-value advancement comes from technology, which is applied science and applied math.
  - Reading and writing, plus persuasion.
  - Nutrition by logging what you eat and seeing what works for you, rather than dogma.
  - Fitness by finding a sport you love and learning the basics.
  - Basic programming and how hardware and chips work.
  - Strategy. He learned it from strategy war games as a kid, and he maps business and politics onto them. He says he has barely read game theory books.
  - History, geography and literature should be left to reading in spare time. Forced reading makes kids hate it.
