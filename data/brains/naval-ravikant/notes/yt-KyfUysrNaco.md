## Wealth games versus status games, and why Naval says to get rich first

- Naval says hunter-gatherers had no stored wealth, only status games. Status is a ranking ladder: one person's rise is another's fall, and it needs "direct combat."
- Wealth creation is positive-sum. You build a product or service, scale it, and provide abundance for many people without fighting anyone. It also has concrete returns: "If you have more money, you can buy more."
- He argues there has "never been an easier time to create wealth" because of leverage. A few hundred years ago a serf stayed a serf.
- His advice: build following and fame first and then get rich off it, and you take the much harder path. "Getting rich first" and going for fame afterwards is easier.
- Rich people often turn to status (Hollywood, nonprofits, Davos). People want what they don't have, and we are evolutionarily wired for status.
- A person can be satisfied at a certain level of wealth. People will always want more status, so it is harder to "win and be done with" a status game.
- Platforms like YouTube and iTunes put you on daily leaderboards, which keeps you on a treadmill.
- On fame, he says it has real perks (parties, restaurants, status) but high costs: no privacy, weirdos, forced consistency with past statements, haters. Pursue it only as a byproduct of useful work, not for its own sake. "Hollow fame" is fragile and forces you to perform.

## Productize yourself: play that looks like work to others

- Naval's two-word summary of success: "productize yourself." Find what you naturally do that the world might want, then scale it into a product. It becomes effortless and feels like play.
- His heuristic: find what "feels like play to you but looks like work to others." You outcompete people who treat it as work, and you escape competition through authenticity.
- Modern society lets you work like a search function: change cities, careers, jobs and people to find the people and work that need you most.
- Explore before you exploit. The biggest mistake in a world of choices is premature commitment, such as locking in as a lawyer or doctor and discovering 30 years later it was wrong. "The best time to figure that out was before, but the second best time is now."
- Early in life, say yes to everything while exploring. Once you've found your thing, say no to everything by default.
- Understanding beats discipline on mental things. Once you see the truth of something you can't unsee it, and behavior changes immediately.
- Memorization is losing value. He names judgment, taste and understanding as what matters, and says they come from solving real problems and finding the commonalities.

## Decision rules, iteration and the secretary theorem

- His three heuristics for hard decisions:
  1. "If you can't decide, the answer is no." For any new opportunity, the default is no.
  2. If two options seem equal, take the one more painful in the short term. The brain overstates imminent pain and discounts long-term pain.
  3. Take the choice that leaves you more equanimous in the long term (credited to Kapil Gupta).
- Three decisions matter most early in life: who you're with, what you do, where you live. People spend far too little time on them, especially the city. He says to spend about a year thinking through a four-year decision.
- Secretary theorem: spend roughly the first third of your candidates seeing the field and setting the bar, then take the first that meets or beats it. He stresses it is iteration-based, not time-based, so you need many shots and must bail out quickly.
- On gut versus head: the gut is "refined judgment," taste aggregated through evolution and experience. Ruminate on pros and cons, then sleep on a hard decision until the gut answer appears with conviction. Don't go against it.
- He reframes the "10,000 hours" idea as 10,000 iterations. Repetition is the same thing again, while iteration modifies it with a learning. That is error correction.
- Pride is the "most expensive trait" because it keeps you in local maxima. Great artists and entrepreneurs will "start over."
- He cites Elon Musk, who he says put about $100M of his roughly $200M PayPal proceeds into SpaceX, $80M into Tesla and $20M into SolarCity, then borrowed for rent. Creating something great requires zero to one, which means going back to zero.

## Optimism, search and the barbell

- Naval says we are hardwired for pessimism because early humans who ignored the rustle in the woods got eaten. Modern society is safer and more forgiving of failure.
- Upside is nonlinear. A short can return at most 2x, while the next Nvidia can return 100x or 1,000x.
- Treat life and business as a search function. Once you find the one thing (spouse, business) it compounds, so "50 failed dates" or "50 small failed ventures" don't matter.
- Be skeptical of any specific opportunity, since each is probably a fail, but optimistic in general. A failure is a win if you learned something.
- Strategy is a barbell: explore and iterate fast, cutting losses quickly, then go all in. "Move your chips to the center of the table." The gray zone of being half in is the trap.
- Don't adopt labels like pessimist, introvert or "I have trauma." They lock your identity to the past, and motivated reasoning is "the worst kind."

## How he analyzes businesses and spends wealth

- Naval says his specific knowledge is analyzing a business, especially a technology business, taking it apart and predicting what is likely to work. It isn't happiness or philosophy.
- Business knowledge is contextual. Abstract advice has to be timeless, so it is thin, and it gets useful only with a specific business and entrepreneur in front of him.
- Red flags he looks for:
  - The founder is doing A just to get to B (so go to B).
  - The founder is in it for the money when a rival who loves the product will beat them.
  - Investors are in it for the wrong reasons.
  - The wrong co-founder.
  - A wrong vesting schedule.
  - Starting in the wrong place or from the wrong angle.
- Venture is a power law: number one returns more than two through n put together. You're wrong most of the time, so you only need to be right a few times. His example: Peter Thiel started PayPal but made his big money on Facebook.
- Where he puts his money: investment over consumption. He says Elon-style reinvesting in your own businesses beats most philanthropy, because many nonprofits are "grifty" or lack tight feedback loops. He is self-funding a new company, said to be about six months from a possible reveal. Being self-funded means starting businesses "without having to ask other people for permission" or having his vision warped by investors' return demands.
- He says to get help that frees your time, "always overpay and expect the best," and not to retire. "If I'm doing something I enjoy, then I'm already in perpetual retirement."
- He calls a good business one that "creates a product for people that they voluntarily buy and they get value out of."

## Time, attention and scaling yourself

- Naval keeps no calendar, and his email autoresponder says he doesn't check email or text. He now just "ghosts." His wife doesn't schedule him for anything. He says the worst thing is an hour on the calendar that your past self committed you to.
- The life is arranged so serendipity decides. "If I'm in the mood" calls get done on the fly, by text when both are free.
- Rough daily rhythm: first 4 hours for hard problems, next 4 for outdoor activity, working out or last-minute meetings, last 4 to wind down with the kids.
- Freedom increases productivity because you can put the right task in the slot at that moment. A 2 p.m. meeting forces rushed emails at 6 p.m.
- "Inspiration is perishable." Act immediately on a post, a tweet, a problem or a book. Learn at the moment of curiosity (download the book, use Google or ChatGPT).
- Scaling: "You have to be able to delete emails and text messages without flinching if you want to scale." Every interruption breaks flow. His test: if you wouldn't ask someone else for this, you can dismiss the request without responding.
- The real currency of life is attention, not money or time. Money can't buy time, and time is wasted when you aren't present.
- He defines wasted time as time you aren't present for. Stress is two conflicting desires. Anxiety is a pile of unresolved stress points, and the fix is to find and resolve them.
- Choose your problems: "Do you really need that many problems in your life?" Focus on one overarching problem. Cultivate indifference to things you can't control, and treat the news as a delivery mechanism for memetic viruses.
- Desire is "a contract to be unhappy until you get what you want," so be choosy about desires. Wanting something badly enough means "you lie awake at night thinking about it."

## Character, trust and who to partner with

- Virtues are strategies for win-win outcomes. He frames most social games as stag hunts (two equilibria) rather than prisoner's dilemmas. A high-trust society lets you hunt the stag together.
- If you signal ethics over the long term you attract ethical people. "If you are a shark, you will eventually find yourself swimming entirely amongst sharks."
- He distinguishes being wrong (fine, learning is error correction) from lying to look good, which traps you in a hall of mirrors. He criticizes memorizing his own old tweets to prepare as "just performance."
- Self-esteem is a reputation you have with yourself. Live up to your own code, and do things for others. He says his proudest moments were sacrifices for people or things he loved.
- On people: you can't change other people, only yourself and your reaction. The only effective way to shift behavior is genuine praise when they do something you want, never criticism.
- On picking a partner: "Show me your resume items" is a bad sign. Align on values and the ineffable. Choose a happy person, since "the secret to a happy relationship is two happy people." Temperament shows up in kids too.
- On high-status friends: he spends less time with friends once they become famous because they get surrounded by people who want something. He says he doesn't want transactional relationships.
- He says the rule for the capable is that the more you have, the more is expected. The order is yourself, then family, extended family, local tribe, then bigger. "The alpha male eats last." Society should reward it with status, and he calls a lazy, able man the worst in society.

## Technology and market predictions

- AI: LLMs are "natural language computers," a fundamental breakthrough (they solve search, translation, transcription, simple coding, and English as a programming language). He says they are not AGI and he sees no direct path to it. ASI is "a fantasy," and he is not a doomer. Their poems and fiction are poor, and they are bad at distilling what matters.
- Hallucination means they won't yet run mission-critical systems; programmers get more leverage as they "use AI to replace everybody else." Self-driving will be solved because it's a bounded problem.
- On Tesla versus Waymo: Tesla's camera-only approach is stronger long term and scalable, but Waymo works now and is ahead on deployment.
- Predictions he flags as under-noticed: biology is still "in the Stone Age" with few good explanatory theories and too little experimentation because of regulation, and drones will be the future of all warfare ("autonomous bullets"), with no aircraft carriers, tanks or infantry.
- GLP-1s: he calls them the most breakthrough drugs since antibiotics, possibly more important than statins, with benefits beyond weight loss (addiction, cancers, dementia, cardiovascular, metabolic aging). He says about 10% of people are already using them. His political prediction is that whichever politician makes them cheap and broadly available "is going to be richly rewarded."
- On fertility: he isn't convinced declining fertility must be fought. He cites Scott Adams' law of slow-moving disasters, where incentives solve what everyone can see coming.
