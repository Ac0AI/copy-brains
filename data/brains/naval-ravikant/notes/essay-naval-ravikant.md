## Naval on decision-making as leverage and avoiding mistakes

- Naval argues "decision-making is everything." Someone right 80% of the time instead of 70% gets valued and paid hundreds of times more, because of leverage.
- Example: managing $1 billion and being right 10% more often than someone else is worth $100 million on a judgment call. Technology, large workforces and capital leverage decisions more every year.
- He sees the brain as a memory prediction machine. "X happened in the past, therefore X will happen in the future" is a poor method. He wants principles and mental models instead.
- His best mental model sources are evolution, game theory, Charlie Munger, Nassim Taleb and Benjamin Franklin. Complexity theory taught him the limits of prediction, so he works in the face of ignorance.
- He does not try to say what will work. He eliminates what will not. "Being successful is just about not making mistakes."
- He rejects checklists for investors and entrepreneurs. He thinks Gawande's *Checklist Manifesto* suits pilots and surgeons, not founders.
- He asks three questions: can he trust this person, what are the real odds this works, and how much margin of safety he has if it does.

## Naval's portfolio system: see many, bet small, double down on winners

- Naval says he does not believe in specific goals. He quotes Scott Adams: "Set up systems, not goals."
- His approach is to find environments where he can thrive and build a system so success is statistically likely. He wants to be successful in 999 of 1,000 universes, not to be the most successful person.
- His investing system: look at 10,000 companies, pick 500 that have a shot at being huge, and keep the option to double down on the 5 winners.
- He does not think he can look at 100 companies, pick 10 winners and go all in. He says he lacks that ability.
- He likes angel and venture bets for their asymmetry. The downside is 1x, and the upside can be 10,000x. "I've tried to rig the game."
- He calls origin stories about success ex-post-facto reasoning. He judges skill by who is consistently successful, since Silicon Valley has many one-hit wonders.
- By his own account, he is no longer in it for the money. He wants to create something new that the world values, that fits his morals, and that he would buy himself.
- Money has steep diminishing returns for him. He calls it "a boat anchor around my neck": something to fear losing, argue about, and have others ask for.

## What Naval looks for in founders and business partners

- The best founders are long-term thinkers. They are laying "bricks in the foundation of the skyscraper," not building a house, so they fix small things early.
- He wants someone who knows the space well, understands how hard it is, does not care, and commits for the long haul.
- He cites Steve Case: "vision without execution is a hallucination." Passion and vision are not enough. The founder also needs execution and domain depth, deep enough to be contrarian.
- Ideas are not new. All ideas have been thought of, so the value is in idea plus execution plus passion.
- His Steve Jobs example: Jobs did not invent the smartphone idea. He had a very high design bar, understood manufacturing tolerances, rallied people and resources, and took $1 salary at Apple.
- Luck matters in tech: timing, market forces, regulation, platform shifts and open source. He says you cannot predict outsized successes, but you can predict failures, such as a founder who is out of their depth or a short-term thinker.
- Short-term thinkers should find something they can commit to for 10 years. He says that is the minimum time to a good outcome.
- Great people tend to have great outcomes in Silicon Valley, but only on a long timescale. Everyone he judged very capable 20 years ago "almost without exception" became extremely successful.

## How Naval tests integrity and trust in deals

- He uses Buffett's criteria of intelligence, energy and integrity, and says integrity is the hardest to judge.
- Long-term signal: you have known the person a while. Short-term signal: watch how they treat other people.
- Red flag: someone who is self-dealing toward a partner while saying "I'm taking advantage of that person because they deserve it. You're my friend and I would never do that to you."
- Red flag: someone who talks a lot about how honest they are. He also cites *The Art of Manipulation*, which says con men push the deal too hard and talk up their honesty.
- High-integrity people have an internal moral compass and will not make unfair deals, because it would soil their view of themselves. They are easy to negotiate with and give things they do not need to.
- He argues unhappy deals get unwound and become short-term relationships with no compounding benefit.
- His rule when someone he respects behaves badly: warn them once, then distance himself because "nobody changes." His saying: "the closer you want to get to me, the better your values have to be."
- He adds Buffett's rule, "praise specifically, criticize generally," so egos work for you, not against you.

## Naval's foundational values for business and relationships

- He defines values as things he will not compromise on.
- Honesty: he never wants to be somewhere he has to watch what he says. Disconnecting thoughts from words creates "multiple threads" in the mind.
- Long-term thinking: he refuses short-term thinking or dealing, and will stop doing business with someone who deals short-term with others. "All the benefits in life come from compound interest" (money, relationships, health, habits).
- He works only with people he expects to stay around for life, and only on things with long-term payoff.
- Peer relationships only: no one above him, none below.
- No anger, and no anger around him.
- He argues ethics is "long-term selfish" but costs in the short term: "If being ethical were profitable, everybody would do it."
- He quotes his trainer: "Easy choices, hard life. Hard choices, easy life." It applies to values, saving for a rainy day and relationships.
- When values line up, small things stop mattering. Quarrels usually mean values do not align.

## Naval on time, commitments and priorities

- His "two-factor authentication on your calendar" idea: present-him is tired and hungry, while future-him is imagined as high-energy, and present-him keeps promising on future-him's behalf.
- Fix: write a commitment down and check in 48 hours later with a clear mind. Better: commit only to what he would do right now, which matches Derek Sivers' "hell yes or no."
- He says he is in the exploitation phase, not exploration, and still finds it hard to say no.
- "I don't have time" means "it's not a priority." A fuzzy basket of 10 or 15 priorities gets none done.
- He made his own health first (physical, then mental, then spiritual, then family), so the daily morning workout always happens. It was "a complete game-changer."
- He avoids rigid schedules and wants to drop 40, 60 or 80 hour weeks, 9-to-5s and fixed roles. He asks only whether he is doing what he wants, productive and happy.
- He reads 1 to 2 hours a day and says that alone accounts for any material success and intelligence he has.

## Naval on specific knowledge, reality and where returns come from

- Each person has specific knowledge, capability and desire that no one else has, from the "combinatorics of human DNA and development." Do not copy others' checklists or frameworks.
- "Your goal in life is to find out the people who need you the most, … the business that needs you the most, … the project and the art that needs you the most."
- Returns come from leaving the herd. Best sellers serve social approval. He recommends original sources: Adam Smith, Darwin, Franklin, Munger.
- The smartest and most successful people he knows started out as outcasts, which pushed them to find their own path.
- Real understanding is first principles. "If you can't explain it to a child, then you don't know it." Complicated language is "the mark of a charlatan." Memorizing without being able to rederive from basics is useless.
- To see reality, get the ego out of the way. The stronger the desire for an outcome, the less likely you see the truth.
- Business failure is often a delayed reality check. To avoid self-delusion, he admits problems publicly to co-founders and coworkers.
- He gave up macroeconomics as "junk science" but calls microeconomics and game theory fundamental to business: supply and demand, labor versus capital, tit for tat.
- He prefers micro over macro generally: change yourself, then family and neighbors, before changing the world.
