## Naval's specific knowledge is analyzing technology businesses

- Naval says his specific knowledge is not happiness or philosophy. It is taking a technology business "apart at the seams" and predicting in advance what is likely to work and what is not.
- He says business is "very, very contextual". You have to look at the particular business, understand what is being done, why and how, then tear it apart and reassemble it properly.
- He admits he will be wrong most of the time ("Clubhouse notwithstanding"). It is like playing the lottery, except you know one or two of the ticket numbers in advance.
- He says this is why talking about wealth in the abstract is hard. Abstract advice has to be right in every context, so it shrinks to timeless statements. Specific advice, applied to a real business and a real entrepreneur, is where the real knowledge is.
- He admits "How to Get Rich" is a deliberately clickbait title. The thread was short and crafted, and left "10,000 things" on the cutting room floor.

## Power law outcomes: you only need to be right a few times

- Naval says you operate in a "highly leveraged intellectual domain", so outcomes are nonlinear.
- In any power law distribution, number one returns more than two through n put together. Number two returns more than three through n put together.
- His example is Peter Thiel. He started PayPal but made his big money on Facebook, which was the big winner.
- Implication: you only have to be right a few times, or even once, to get the big score.

## Red flags Naval looks for when taking a startup apart

When he has a specific entrepreneur and business in front of him, he checks for these:

- **Wrong reasons:** "You're doing A so you can get to B. Just go to B."
- **Money vs. love of the product:** if the founder is in it for money, the person who builds it because they love the product will beat them.
- **Wrong investors:** raising money from people who are in it for the wrong reasons.
- **Wrong co-founder:** a co-founder who is not in it for the right reasons, or the wrong kind of co-founder.
- **Structure mistakes:** a wrong vesting schedule.
- **Wrong place:** starting the business in the wrong location.
- **Wrong angle:** approaching the problem from this angle instead of that one.
- He grants he will be wrong sometimes too, but says he has seen a lot of data and has his theories.

## Understanding, judgment and taste beat memorization

- Naval's heuristic: "If you have to memorize something, it's because you don't understand it. And if you understand something, you don't have to memorize it."
- Real understanding lets you re-derive what you need from first principles. Each piece of knowledge works like a Lego block that locks into a steel frame.
- Memorization is worth less every day. ChatGPT has memorized the internet, and you will not beat it, a library or any 10 books at memorization.
- What matters now is understanding, judgment and taste.
- Those come from having real problems, solving them, and finding the commonalities across them.
- This is why he prefers podcasts to tweets. Deep understanding lets you talk about a topic for hours. Aphorisms are now copied by thousands of people and by ChatGPT.
- He does not want abstract talk. He wants to work through an actual business with an actual problem and solve it.

## Mastery in any field leads to philosophy

- Naval defines philosophy as finding the hidden generalizable truths among specific experiences, then using them as heuristics for future ones.
- Any subject pursued deeply enough ends in philosophy, and mastery of anything makes you a philosopher. You find unity in anything if you go deep enough.
- This is why timeless advice keeps looking like cliché. It is cliché "for reasons".
- Philosophy can still progress. Scientific advances changed outlooks, such as a geocentric universe vs. a vast one, or gods vs. computable laws of physics.
- Moral philosophy has also advanced. He cites the near-universal abandonment of slavery.

## Answer questions at the level at which they are asked

- Naval says many philosophical paradoxes come from switching scale. This idea draws on David Deutsch and his friend James Pierce.
- **Meaning:** asked by Chris, here and now, there is meaning. Answered from the universe's view over infinite time (heat death), there is none. Do not ask as a human and answer as God.
- **Free will:** asked by an individual, yes. Your mind-body system cannot predict what you will do next, and everyone treats each other as having free will. From the block-universe view of particle collisions, no.
- His rule: "Answer at the level at which it was asked." Many paradoxes dissolve when you do.
