## Wealth as knowledge: Naval's two definitions of wealth

- Naval's own working definition: "assets that earn while you sleep." Examples he gives are capital, a piece of a business, a machine (a 3D printer, a GPU cluster training an AI model), a team operating in different time zones, intellectual property, and a piece of media that keeps circulating.
- He wanted to break out of the 9-to-5 trap where input is tied to output. His stated aim is to be "the most successful guy for doing the least work in every aspect of my life." He calls laziness "a form of leverage," and the point is to free time for health, children and learning.
- He now prefers David Deutsch's definition: wealth is "the set of physical transformations that you can effect." Stored capital matters, but most of the capability comes from technology and leverage.
- Knowledge, not capital, is the multiplier. Ten cavemen can't change as much as ten modern humans. This is why he says Marx was wrong: value isn't in the factories. If you removed Elon Musk from SpaceX you couldn't "capture his wealth," because the knowledge would leave with him. A company is "a group of very intelligent people held together by a common mission," not a pie to divide.
- Gold is only "a pointer to actual value." The value is in the people doing things.
- His own earning power is at its highest because of knowledge. It comes from stored capital (he can fund his own projects), reputation (people trust and fund him), and know-how about what to go after, how to structure a company and how to recruit.

## Ethical wealth creation, skin in the game and small competing groups

- If you don't believe ethical wealth creation is possible, you "get bitter too early" and reject the whole game. He says the cynic's belief is self-fulfilling.
- Most effort around wealth goes into siphoning and defending it. He notes there are six times as many parasite species as standalone ones. Societies with too many people slicing the pie rather than baking it collapse.
- He argues crony outcomes such as bank bailouts are government intervention, not free-market capitalism. Capitalism is the minimum set of rules that channels energy into creating property instead of stealing it, and its basis is respect for private property.
- He says this is the greatest period for wealth creation in history, with more knowledge, capital and leverage than ever. Someone moderately intelligent, hardworking and flexible can do very well. Luck exists, but "life is long. People are very consistent. Compounding does work."
- The payoff comes on a 10-to-30-year timescale, "not two years." He says "there's no get-rich quick schemes. That's just someone selling you something."
- On Taleb's skin in the game: if you take a risk, you bear it. Good risk-takers survive and spread the benefits, and bad ones are weeded out through lost capital, lives or reputations. He says the most ethical thing you can do is be an entrepreneur who bears risk.
- He agrees with Buffett and Munger's unease about investment banks going public. The partnership structure made partners bear the risk, while a corporate liability shield lets risk be pushed onto society.
- He sees size as the biggest impediment to progress. Groups seek consensus, not truth, and "don't admit mistakes." The best conditions are small teams, small companies and city-states competing non-violently, each bearing its own results. He cites US federalism and market competition as the model, and says the system breaks with monopolies, captured regulators and central control.
- On alignment: incentive alignment, such as giving someone equity, is real. Other forms of alignment among strangers boil down to coercion, so he calls AI alignment "a joke."

## Good products are hard to vary, and technology has winner-take-all dynamics

- Applying Deutsch's "hard to vary" test to products: a good product's parts interlock so that you can't change or remove one without breaking it, like a puzzle piece that could only have fit one way.
- His examples are the iPhone's form factor, largely unchanged since its 2007 launch, and the laptop. Cars converge on one shape because of wind-tunnel testing.
- Simple means elegant, not simplistic: remove what's unnecessary. He quotes Saint-Exupéry on the airplane wing, perfect when there's "nothing left to take away."
- Emergence comes from constraints, not freedom. Locking parts together creates a new capability, as with a company or a contract.
- His rule on variants: "if you're selling multiple versions of your product, you better have a really good reason for that."
- Technology is "the automation of knowledge." You figure something out, automate it, scale it and distribute it. The embedded knowledge is persistent because it rests on a good explanation. Once battery knowledge existed, electric cars became close to inevitable.
- Good products have surprising reach. The iPhone's creators didn't plan it for oil companies. Blackberry/enterprise went the same way: Steve Ballmer-era skepticism said enterprise wouldn't tolerate a phone without a keyboard. But employees brought iPhones from home and "the IT guys got outvoted."
- All technology has deeply winner-take-all network effects and scale economies. The best product amortizes development over the largest user base. Even Amazon was not expected to have a network effect.
- Internet markets end up with one huge winner plus a long tail of small players, and they "destroy the mediocre middle." The middle relied on geography or regulation.
- Money can't buy a better phone or, in his view, a better car than the Tesla Model Y (as of 2025). Expensive cars are "just weirder," and trying to buy status is the worst way to signal it.

## Judgment, taste and learning from the specific to the general

- He favors learning from the specific to the general, citing Seneca. Do things in reality, test them, then generalize. This tells you when a maxim applies and when it doesn't. Academia does the reverse, producing what Taleb calls an "intellectual yet idiot": overeducated and underpracticed.
- "If you want to be a philosopher king, first be a king." Elon's principle of speed came from doing, with concrete implementations like engineers setting their own requirements and unblocking everything.
- His reading habits changed. He used to read hundreds or thousands of books and now thinks most of that was worthless. He reads deliberately and in small amounts, to spark thinking. He rereads *Skin in the Game* because it generalizes things he's noticed, such as the minority rule. He doesn't use Taleb's antifragility because he finds few real applications.
- Judgment is the key skill in an age of infinite leverage. His example: two Apple CEO candidates, one right 80% of the time and one 85%. You'd pay the second billions more per year because he steers a multi-trillion-dollar ship.
- Judgment comes from experience plus honest reflection. Eventually it becomes so good it can't be articulated, and that is taste: "it doesn't feel right to me." Examples are Rick Rubin, Steve Jobs, and AI researchers deciding which experiments to run.
- On the "10,000 hours" idea, he says it is really about iterations, not repetitions. You do something, honestly reflect on the outcome, change something, and try again. Do that enough and your judgment develops.
- His investing is now "almost entirely" taste. He passes when he doesn't want to take a walk with the founder, isn't curious about the category, or doesn't want to be associated with how the money is made. He wants to work with people who are capable, low-ego and intelligent.
- His line on diligence: what you do and who you do it with matter more than how hard you work, but you still have to work as hard as you can.
- Deep problems take intense focus and time. He describes 24-to-36-hour compiler-writing sessions in college, and breakthroughs that come an hour after he's ready to hang up with his co-founder. His technique is to load a problem into the subconscious so insight arrives later. He quotes John Cleese: "let your mind rest against the problem in a friendly, persistent way."
- He says that if work feels forced, you lose to someone for whom it's fun.

## AI, leverage and entrepreneurs

- AI is "the ultimate leverage information retrieval tool," not a source of judgment. It gives the conventional correct answer for solved problems and is good "when wrong answers are okay."
- Because everyone gets the same AI answer, there's no edge in it. The edge comes from early adoption and from using it on bleeding-edge problems.
- His quip: "It's not that AI is going to replace software engineers, [it's that] AI is going to let software engineers replace everybody else." He says people claiming programming is dead are "idiots." As evidence he cites Mark Zuckerberg paying hundred-million-dollar packages to individual machine learning engineers.
- Structured thinking still matters. Jobs's team built the iPhone because they understood what was possible across materials, manufacturing, supply chains, costing, batteries and bandwidth. Even with AI doing the vibe coding, building something new requires understanding the abstraction the AI is hiding, where it errs and how the pieces fit.
- AI handles what's been done before, and "you can't get paid in any meaningful way for things that have already been done before."
- Good entrepreneurs hate doing the same thing day after day. He calls that "a failure of automation and a failure of imagination." No entrepreneur is replaced by AI; they may be replaced by another entrepreneur who uses it better.
- He hopes for 7 billion companies, one per person, as tools make entrepreneurship broadly accessible.

## Building the new company and funding

- His new company is called Impossible Inc. (impossiblecomputer.com), still in stealth. He says it is probably the hardest thing he's tried. Four technical experts he spoke to each called it impossible, "for four completely different reasons."
- It is funded with his own money, burning "a million and a half dollars a month." He says that cost is a motivator: "a big carrot and a big stick."
- He has never learned faster, because he's motivated by a concrete build. His tactics: pull up the papers, interrogate ChatGPT (o3 Pro) and Gemini 2.5 Pro, and run whiteboard sessions with his co-founder, asking the "idiot" questions.
- He describes the co-founder as brilliant, low-ego and hardworking ("if he's awake, he's working").
- Early recruiting lessons from his experience:
  - Don't recruit high-ego people even if they're good.
  - Don't oversell, because it comes unstuck later.
  - Hire more technical builders early and fewer marketers, since otherwise you create too many requirements too early.
- On funding, he argues the biggest companies were mostly self-financed or broke out early. Apple, Tesla and SpaceX were self-financed. Google, Microsoft and Amazon took small checks after reaching escape velocity, and Facebook got profitable quickly. Round after round of venture capital rarely builds iconic companies. He calls this a heresy.
- On Elon's story (which he says may be apocryphal): after PayPal he put roughly $100M into SpaceX, $80M into Tesla and $20M into SolarCity. He cites Fred Smith of FedEx and Steve Jobs funding Pixar and NeXT as similar cases. His takeaway is "you have to have the courage of your convictions when nobody else does" and cross the gap from zero to one.
- Naval says he is in "endgame content" mode: with little to lose, he can play without fear. He thinks Jobs and Elon built from inspiration, not fear.
- He criticizes VCs who call themselves "the team behind the team." In his view the founder takes the risk and built the company.

## Pursuing outcomes indirectly and choosing what to want

- He says the elusive goals are elusive because they are best pursued indirectly:
  - Wealth: someone who is really into the work outcompetes someone who isn't. Purely selfish industries such as porn, gambling and crypto casinos don't attract the best teams.
  - Happiness: direct pursuit of pleasure leads to addiction and dopamine burnout.
  - Status: it is a hidden hierarchy, and claiming it lowers it. Trying to tear others down is covert status-seeking.
  - Love: giving love is what you want, not needing to receive it.
- He applies Deutsch's "fun criterion": if you're not having fun, don't do it. He doesn't follow it all the way, for example with teaching children math.
- Explore, then invest. Explore widely to find the fit, because there's near-infinite optionality. But "all the benefits in life come from compound interest," so once you've found the fit you must invest in a job, place, people, business and health. People often under-explore on jobs (three weeks looking, five years in the role).
- His test of intelligence: "the only true test of intelligence is if you get what you want out of life." It has two parts: choosing what to want, and getting it.
- Choose inspiration over envy. He draws on Elon and Peter Thiel without swapping lives with anyone.
- Virtues are long-term selfishness. His line is "the modern devil is cheap dopamine." He recommends starting charity locally: help people near you, because "your family is broken, but you're going to fix the world."
- A mission larger than yourself pulls people together as a Schelling point, as with SpaceX's Mars goal, but only "on your own terms." He later says the tweet to follow is "live for something larger than yourself, but only on your own terms."
- His summary routine tweet: "Fast, lift, sprint, stretch, and meditate. Build, sell, write, create, invest, and own. Productize yourself. Build or buy equity in a business. Read, reflect, love, seek truth, and ignore society. Make these habits. Say no to everything else. Avoid debt, jail, addiction, disgrace, shortcuts, and media." The sign-off is "Relax. Victory is assured."
- His view on the ordering of wealth, health and happiness: pursue wealth when young (teens learn and do, 20s work hard, 30s harvest), then protect health and work on happiness. In importance the order reverses. He says an amazing life is 70 to 80% in each category rather than 150 in one and zero in another.
