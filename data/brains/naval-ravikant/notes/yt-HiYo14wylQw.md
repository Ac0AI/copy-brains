## How Naval says to get rich: own equity, productize yourself, use leverage

- Naval wrote his pinned "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)" tweetstorm (May 31, 2018) as principles he first framed for himself at age 13. The aim is wealth made deliberately and repeatably, not by luck, privilege or the right circles.
- Wealth means freedom, not a high hourly rate: "when you wake up when you want you go to sleep when you want you know you live where you want." A law firm billing a couple hundred dollars an hour is still tied to the clock.
- He calls "You're not going to get rich renting out your time. You must own equity" the most important line in the thread.
- The best in the world at something can now serve the whole world through leverage, distribution, accountability and specific knowledge. Distribution costs close to zero online.
- The tactic: productize yourself into a business, then own that business. He cites Tim Ferriss's eponymous brand (podcast, books, followers) as an example.
- Lower rungs of ownership are public or private equities, being a partner in a private business, or holding startup stock options. Without equity he calls wealth "nearly impossible."
- He also says: "if you secretly despise wealth it will elude you." Ethical wealth creation is possible.
- Lotteries are "attacks on people who can't do math," and get-rich-quick schemes are "other people getting rich off of you." There are no shortcuts.

## Naval's own wealth path: specific knowledge, many medium wins, optimizing for independence

- His specific knowledge is understanding deeply technical concepts and communicating them. He acts as an interface between great programmers, developers and designers on one side and capital markets and consumers on the other.
- He uses that position to spot trends early. He invests in or helps start those companies, owns equity, and advises on fundraising, exits, recruiting, culture and technology development.
- He has also invested early in public markets and crypto, and has started funds.
- His results are "consistent" and "varied": a meaningful payday every couple of years, not one giant exit. By Silicon Valley standards he says he hasn't had one gigantic payday that set him up forever.
- He keeps a brand plus deep relationships with about a dozen high-integrity, capable people he can do business with for life. He says money "kind of makes itself" once you become the kind of person who makes money in long-term situations.
- He doesn't monetize his Twitter or podcast. He says he no longer follows his own principles in that sense, because he isn't trying to make money anymore.
- He optimizes for independence. He says he could have made more by raising a huge fund, joining a big VC firm, or becoming an exec at a large company early on. He wants no boss, no direct reports and peer relationships only.
- The ideal is to "make money with your mind not with your time." One good decision a year that earns what you need is "perfect."
- He says we live in an age of infinite leverage: code, capital, community and labor multiply your judgment. Working hard is "the last least important thing."
- You still must pay your dues: "ten thousand iterations not ten thousand hours."
- Judgment comes from clear thinking, which comes from time to reflect and follow real curiosity. He says great investors mostly sit and read, and Warren Buffett plays a lot of bridge.
- He says making more money no longer changes his life, and he has more influence through podcasts than through money.

## Learn the fundamentals, not the jargon

- Naval cites Feynman: "I learned very early the difference between knowing the name of something and knowing something."
- He wants understanding over memorization. You should be able to explain a thing in many ways from the ground up, and re-derive what you need.
- In business he says jargon protects knowledge, and he sees it often in accounting. Someone who grasps the underlying principles has an edge in negotiating deals, because they see how the pieces move rather than what they are called.
- You need only basic accounting: a few days with a decent YouTube tutorial or book. He adds that running a business, even a lemonade stand, will teach you accounting better than a textbook.
- He says arithmetic, statistics and probability are all the math most people need to succeed, not calculus.
- Jargon is useful as compressed communication, but harmful when it replaces understanding.
- He cites Feynman's line that "nature uses only the longest threads to weave her tapestry." A few basic principles recur across fields. He gives the example of Solomonoff induction and Feynman path integrals, which he sees as the same idea in different fields.
- His advice is to keep rereading the basics.

## Unlearning, pain avoidance, and starting over

- Naval says people over-focus on the tactics and miss the hard part: unlearning, not learning.
- He defines suffering as the moment you see things as they clearly are and don't like it.
- Sunk costs, such as a PhD in a social science, keep people from picking up new skills like coding. People also avoid moving or disappointing existing relationships.
- "Everybody wants to start where they are nobody wants to go back down the mountain to find the path going to the top."
- He quotes Charlie Munger's joke about wanting to be rich "like you except quicker."
- What he recommends is beginner's mind: be willing to hit reset and go back to zero. What you already know and do may be the impediment.
- He says he is guilty of this too, and that these principles suit young people best, since they can set their trajectory earlier.

## Why calm beats anxious: the effectiveness case

- Naval's tweet: "Imagine how effective you would be if you weren't anxious all the time."
- He says money solves money problems but does not make you happy, so you must take responsibility for your own mental state.
- He admits he can't separate the counterfactual, since he is also later in his career. Still, his effectiveness "has gone through the roof" as he became calmer.
- He compares two high-conflict business situations. The earlier one left him sweating, angry and sleepless. In the recent one he was calm, even enjoying it as craft, and it felt "very mechanical," so he could be effective while handling many other things.
- He argues anxiety is the mind's fear-based scenario planning. Most feared outcomes never happen. If anxiety is your only motivator you have a problem, but he says pure motivation does not come from anxiety.
- He gives samurai duels, the Terminator and *Unforgiven* as examples of the calm one winning.
- The superpower is staying calm while going about your business, provided your motivation is intrinsic.

## Self-examination practices Naval uses

- Naval says pervasive anxiety comes from an unexamined life: unresolved decisions, unmet desires, contradictions and feeling trapped.
- He says real examination "should ruin the life that you're currently living." It should lead you to change relationships, boundaries, your job, eating, books and friends. If it doesn't, it isn't real examination.
- He does three things, and says none should become a rigid routine:
  1. **Reading philosophy at night.** He reads Schopenhauer, Osho, Krishnamurti, Anthony de Mello, Rupert Spira, Kapil Gupta, the Bhagavad Gita and the Tao Te Ching. His starting suggestions are de Mello's *The Way to Love* or *Awareness*, Krishnamurti's *Think on These Things*, Osho's *The Great Challenge*, and Kapil Gupta's *Direct Truth*. He also listens to Seneca's letters to Lucilius.
  2. **Watching your thoughts.** He treats thoughts as unbidden and holds them to the same skepticism he'd give an outside speaker. He uses Michael Singer's "crazy roommate" analogy from *The Untethered Soul*.
  3. **Sitting meditation.** He learned a system from an Indian teacher: sit 60 minutes a day for at least 60 days, first thing in the morning, with a straight back. Let the mind do whatever it does and don't force anything. It takes 30 to 40 minutes to get past the initial chatter, which is why he says 60 minutes.
- The result he describes is reaching "inbox zero" in the mind. He has done it for about two and a half years, missing roughly a dozen days, and sometimes sits two hours or more.
- He says he is now self-contained, drinks less, isn't drawn to drugs, and doesn't fear mortality, aging or solitary confinement.
- Philosophy and meditation are useful only if they make your life better. If they have no utility for you, he says to drop them.
- He also warns that news and Twitter try to make every problem your problem. He favors the timeless over the urgent.

## Choose long term over short term, and pick games you can win and leave

- Naval's tweet: all effective self-help boils down to choosing long term over short term. He quotes Jerzy Gregorek: "hard choices easy life, easy choices hard life."
- He calls "cheap dopamine" the modern devil. Video games, drink, weed and porn substitute for simpler pleasures like sunlight, walks and real relationships.
- Compound interest applies to relationships, money and health.
- The good hacks make the long-term choice palatable. Examples:
  - Work you enjoy that "feels like play to you but looks like work to others."
  - Healthy food you find tasty, by learning to cook.
  - "Read what you love until you love to read."
- He says the best relationships and friendships don't take work, because chemistry and temperament match. He screens for kindness, and for how someone treats their worst enemy or a server. High-conflict people will eventually treat you as an enemy too, so he avoids them.
- The people he wants around him are self-aware, don't get angry or unhappy easily, don't take themselves too seriously and have no victim mentality.
- On games: "The reason to win the game is so that you can be free of it."
  - Multiplayer games such as school, money, career and fame pull you into anxiety. Single-player pursuits like meditation and yoga don't.
  - Two ways out: choose very few games, or win a game and stop. His hack is to define "winning" early, so you notice when you've passed it.
  - He cites the Socrates market story ("so many things that I do not want") as freedom. His own version is that he has gotten what he wants and takes care not to pick up new desires or upgrade his expectations along with his circumstances.
- He lists activities that don't create their opposite later: meditation, yoga, art, reading for fun, writing and journaling. Building a business also qualifies if done for the craft and flow rather than the outcome.

## Crypto as a hedge and what stablecoins really cost

- Naval calls cryptocurrencies "probably one of the greatest inventions in human history." Tech works in unregulated frontiers, and crypto sidesteps Wall Street's regulatory capture. It makes wealth creation, storage and protection independent of the state.
- Bitcoin is a store of value that is digital, hard to seize, easy to verify, divisible and portable. Its weaknesses are limited privacy, a short track record (since 2009) and extreme volatility. He calls it a speculative asset that "could go to zero."
- Each challenge it survives makes it more valuable. He pictures a Swiss bank account with finite space, where you buy a box from an existing holder, so price rises while demand to get in exceeds supply of people leaving.
- He says Bitcoin's edge is its die-hard, wealthy believers, who will always trade it for hard assets. Money is a consensus story, "the bubble that never pops."
- His stablecoin tweet: "choose between blow up risk, censorship risk and fraud risk." There is no free lunch in turning a volatile asset into a stable one.
  - Fraud risk: Tether, where you must trust that dollars back it.
  - Censorship risk: USDC from Coinbase, which can freeze accounts.
  - Blow-up risk: Maker, backed by volatile collateral whose peg could break.
- His starting advice is to build your own conviction first, because that tells you when to sell. Read up on Bitcoin, Ethereum and the privacy coins Zcash and Monero, and listen to his interview with Nick Szabo.
- Buy through Coinbase or CoinList, and hold with a custodian such as Anchorage or BitGo unless you really know what you're doing. A hardware wallet (Ledger or Trezor) is the self-custody option.
- Naval says he must use custodians because he is a public figure and crypto is a bearer asset. His reason for holding it is as a hedge against dollar printing and loss of reserve-currency status, and you "can't wait till the last second" to buy.
- He says crypto will likely be the native currency of the internet, where machines will transact with each other. The internet gave digital abundance, and crypto gives digital scarcity.
