## Happiness as a skill, and why Naval doesn't chase it

- Naval says happiness is "a highly personal skill" and that his "latest thinking" is that there's "no such thing as happiness". It is a construct of the mind, a thought that comes and goes.
- He says he cares more about peace and being okay with things as they are. He frames this as his own exploration ("I'm not an enlightened being"), subject to change.
- Happiness and unhappiness do not exist in the outside world. Events are impermanent and out of your control, and the world "can't make you any more unhappy than you let it."
- Exception: physical pain demands a response, which makes the mind unhappy.
- Desires should be few and consciously chosen.
- Higher motives help. If you work on something larger than yourself (a mission, children), it hurts less when outcomes go against you, because it isn't personal.
- His tweet: "the more you think about yourself, the less happy you're going to be."
- He recommends strategic indifference to problems outside your control, such as politics and distant wars.
- He doesn't want pure peace either. He says doing nothing gets boring, so he prefers to do what he does without overthinking it, "row, row, row your boat, gently down the stream."

## Meditation, self-observation and deconstructing emotion

- Naval says meditation is not for getting enlightened or calming down. Its purpose is to become self-observant, so you catch your mind doing things against your long-term interest.
- When you catch it, he suggests asking:
  - Is this true?
  - Is this desire important?
  - Do I need to suffer over this?
  - Is it easier to reinterpret this or to deal with the underlying problem?
- In his own sessions, the first 20 minutes are chaotic. After that the mind calms, and 30 to 50 minutes in it goes blank and blissful.
- Deconstructing an emotion:
  - Notice the bodily sensation (for example, a hollowness in the chest). Often it isn't actually unpleasant.
  - Notice the thought attached to it. Thoughts can't feel pain.
  - Ask who is labelling it unpleasant. That is just another thought.
- During one very unhappy period, part of him watched the unhappy part and saw it as an act "to feel important", with nothing actually being attacked.
- He credits no single silver bullet. His stress relief came from reading (Krishnamurti, Osho), some psychedelics ("you get the message, you hang up the phone"), meditation, introspection and thinking.
- He says no mental technique from someone else's mind works reliably. The right idea can change you instantly, while the wrong practice done for 30 years won't.

## Stay healthy, get wealthy, seek truth, give love, create beauty

- Naval's framework: stay healthy, get wealthy, seek truth, give love, create beauty.
- Health is the baseline. "You're born healthy, you don't finish healthy."
- Wealth: "Money doesn't solve all your problems, but it solves all your money problems." Nobody wants less wealth.
- Truth:
  - He wants to know what is true and eliminate what is false, spiritually, scientifically, technologically and about human nature.
  - He says truth and love are the two things you'd take even if they made your life worse. He uses Neo versus Cipher in The Matrix: nobody wants to be Cipher.
  - His line: the closer you get to truth, the more silent you become inside.
- Love:
  - "It's better to be in love than to be loved." Being loved can feel clawing; being in love is the high.
  - Everyone needs something they love more than themselves, such as a mission, family, kids or religion.
- Beauty and creation:
  - Citing David Deutsch, he says humans are "universal explainers" meant to solve problems and advance knowledge.
  - Beautiful things are hard to vary, embody deep knowledge and make people want to use them.
  - He calls the iPhone "among the greatest works of art ever created": beautiful like jewelry, useful and knowledge-dense.
- He says to avoid cultivating unhappiness: "acute unhappiness is real or is useful but chronic unhappiness is an ego trip."

## Identity, depression and reinterpreting the past

- Identity is what stands between you and reality. When something contradicts your identity, you fight it (his example: racism). Keep identity thin, because any chosen identity is a straitjacket.
- He cites psychologist William Glasser's choice theory: depression can be a series of childhood choices that became an unconscious, self-perpetuating habit, a way to get attention by hurting yourself.
- He accepts there are chemical components, but says "chemical imbalance" is too reductive an explanation. He says long-term drugs often cost you something fundamental.
- People can wrap an identity around being unhappy ("makes me special, interesting"), which makes it hard to undo.
- He is optimistic: there's no limit to how fast or how positively someone can change. "The most important thing with getting wealthy and being happy is just realize you can do it. It's your choice."
- Memories:
  - "You don't store memories of your past, you store interpretations of memories of your past."
  - Reinterpreting them is painful but useful, because it requires an ego hit (for example, admitting some of what went wrong was your own choices).
- Forgiveness is selfish in a useful way. Anger spikes cortisol, ages you and makes you miserable, so you forgive to clear your own mind.

## Order of pursuit: health, wealth, happiness, and seasons of life

- His tweet: the three big ones are wealth, health and happiness. We pursue them in that order, but their importance is reversed.
- He holds the order loosely. Naturally happy people have the greatest gift. Health outranks wealth, per Confucius: "a healthy man wants 10,000 things, a sick man only wants one." He wouldn't give up any of the three.
- Wealth is best pursued young because of energy, flexibility, fewer obligations and optimism. It can be pursued later, but it's harder.
- His seasons of life:
  - Teens: learn and do.
  - 20s: work very hard "in the arena."
  - 30s: start harvesting, with contacts and energy.
  - 40s: harvest, watch health, and get happy "because there's not enough time left."
  - 50s: give back (philanthropy, civic contribution).
  - 60s: be a philosopher.
- Failure modes:
  - People overshoot wealth and sacrifice health and happiness.
  - Many wealthy contemporaries are unhappy and low agency outside work. He cites billionaires who won't travel because of school schedules or won't hire help, and who attend tedious obligatory events.
- Compounding: citing C.S. Lewis, small choices compound over decades into heaven or hell, so take responsibility for each one.
- Health: pain forces a quick feedback loop, so most people fix it eventually. He notes GLP-1 drugs are tackling obesity, the biggest mass-market driver of unhealthiness.

## Agency over your own life and the "lazy" 80/20

- Naval says his life is "really good": at any time he is doing what he wants, nothing is obligatory, and if he isn't enjoying something he changes it fast.
- He wants the "fractal 80/20" in everything: the most output for the least effort across every area of life.
- "Lazy" means working on things that don't feel like work. Thinking about big questions feels less like work to him than not thinking about them.
- He'd rather learn from people who enjoy their lives (Osho, Steve Jobs) than from miserable truth-seekers like Schopenhauer.
- Jobs is his model of entrepreneurship because he was also an artist and a seeker, and tried to solve the big questions in life.
- Early-adopter note: he says his contribution on Twitter was being among the first, not unique.

## Relationships: no obligations, peers, and two happy people

- Naval says he doesn't stay in relationships that make him unhappy. He fulfills duties to family, but not false ones.
- Every relationship besides blood ones is a value exchange, and needy relationships make you unhappy. "Neediness destroys a relationship or creates a fake relationship."
- He skips obligatory events (anniversaries, destination weddings, friends' birthdays unless fun). His rule is "no obligations", since obligations drive pain.
- His circle is people who are "similarly free, similarly low ego," who see each other voluntarily and don't take each other for granted. He invests in the relationships he values.
- Family: he has no estranged relatives, and the family functions well.
- "The secret to a happy relationship is two happy people." Happiness is personal. Telling someone to "cheer up" doesn't work, and putting relationships ahead of your own work and happiness means you "get neither".
- He wants peer relationships, even with employees, to avoid ritual and falseness.
- Parenting: he doesn't make his kids say thank you ("they're not an animal"). Genuine gratitude comes out on its own.
- On rituals:
  - He sees Valentine's Day, weddings and awards as forced ritual.
  - Genuine celebration is spontaneous.
  - He compares it to COVID breaking the commuting habit. If you don't feel like doing it naturally, fix the relationship or move on.

## Enlightenment, self-inquiry and "no self"

- Naval says the self is "just a thought". Self-inquiry means looking for the "Eric" who suffers or feels pride. You can't pin it down, and the thoughts pointing at it die down once you see it isn't there.
- He says enlightened people have a persistent experience of no self. He treats it as binary rather than a path, and says it is not a state of perpetual happiness.
- Routes he mentions: some are natural, some have mystical experiences or psychotic breaks (he cites Krishnamurti's "process"), some meditate for decades, some devote their lives to service, and some use self-examination.
- Why it's now accessible: thanks to the internet, you can find living "enlightened" people on YouTube and Twitter. He has met close to a dozen and named Rupert Spira, Mooji, Nithyananda-style teachers, Steven Belcher, James Pierce, Effortless Stoicism, Kapil Gupta and Ascendor as examples.
- His test: spend enough time around them to see something genuinely bad happen, and watch for any perturbation. Another test: ask whether they'd be okay if they had never been enlightened. If the answer breaks, it was part of their identity.
- They still work, run companies, date and have desires, but without ego. He argues they may be more capable, since an enlightened "Terminator" is harder to beat than one wrapped in ego.
- On motivation: he says he hasn't met an enlightened person as motivated as him, but would take the trade of slightly less motivation for enlightenment.
- Happiness is a "strictly dominant strategy". No one chooses to go back to being poor or less happy, so "I don't value happiness" isn't true in practice. Chronic modern suffering often comes from feeling things chronically that were meant to be felt acutely.
