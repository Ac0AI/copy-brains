## Control matters more than valuation in late-stage rounds

Naval's rule from Venture Hacks: "Valuation is temporary. Control is forever." Whoever holds control can later dictate your valuation too, so he says never give it up.

- Control leaks out through protective provisions. They were meant to shield preferred holders as minority shareholders, but they hand over real control.
- A veto on future fundraising gives that investor a lock on the company. If you need money, they have to agree, "which means they run the place."
- The same applies to vetoes on option pool expansion and new share issuances to employees.
- M&A is "the big one, where the biggest fights happen". Preferred holders can push for the opposite outcome from what founders want.
- Late-stage rounds are increasingly done by mutual funds, strategics and family offices, not only VCs. Naval says this lets a hot company build a custom bundle and keep control.

## Selling common stock and the fallback ladder of concessions

For a hot, high-growth company with multiple bidders, Naval would sell common stock ("the ultimate hack"). That means no board seats and no vetoes on M&A, option pool issuance or future fundraising. He notes this only works when there are multiple bidders. If the investor won't take that, he steps down through these options:

1. Common plus a liquidation preference. "That's it. I would be leaving out all the other junk."
2. Protective provisions only for non-arm's-length transactions, such as self-dealing, stock issued to a friend, or selling the company to a brother. Arm's-length, bona fide deals need no approval. He says investors should always keep a veto on insider self-dealing.
3. A veto that belongs to the preferred class as a whole, not each series. If the other investors go along, the later-stage investors must too. Earlier investors are usually people you chose carefully and trust more.
4. Founder shares with extra voting power. This works only up to a point.

Some rights cannot be contracted away under Delaware and California law. His examples: under California law (as of 2003), each series must separately approve an M&A. And in Delaware, even a small shareholder can demand your financials through inspection rights.

## Why liquidation preference fades after the early stages

Naval explains the original purpose with an example.

- A company raises at a $9M pre-money valuation. An investor puts in $1M, so post-money is $10M and the investor owns 10%.
- If the company then just divvied up that $1M among shareholders, the founder would get $900,000 and the investor $100,000.
- The preference exists to prevent that. It protects the investor on a distribution of cash, not against a general downside.

As preferences stack on in later rounds, they become "a giant freebie." Naval's evidence is that public markets are all common stock. If a company is really worth $900M and someone invests $100M, nobody will shut it down and hand back the $100M, because that would throw away $900M of real value.

- "You'd be a fool to do a seed round buying common stock." The preference is "really, really important at the early stages."
- It makes no sense at later stages and is gone at the public stage.

## Secondaries and founder liquidity

Naval says secondaries are becoming more common, and "secondary markets are here to stay."

- Founder and investor incentives diverge more over time. Funds keep getting larger, so a $100M exit can make a founder happy while disappointing the investors.
- Odds of success have fallen. He guesses a venture-backed company's odds were about 1 in 10 in 1999 and might be 1 in 50 now. There are many more companies, the markets are winner-take-all, and the half-life of winners is shorter.
- He quotes a friend who called incubator graduates "the locust swarm of startups," so every business is under constant assault.
- Founders get "a couple of shots on goal" in life. Good VCs who ask for a billion-dollar outcome must let founders take some off the table along the way.

When to ask:

- Never take substantial liquidity if the investors end up losing all their money.
- Start asking once you are convinced, "almost beyond a shadow of a doubt," that the company's value exceeds the stack of liquidation preferences. Evidence is enough revenue or cash flow, or acquisition offers above the preference stack.
- The conversation usually starts with an acquisition offer that investors want you to refuse. A smart investor will then let you take something off the table.
- Secondaries are rare at seed, A and B. They are common from C onward, and sometimes at B if the company has made substantial progress.

## Companies need less money than they think, and raising more makes you slower

Naval calls this "the single most important elephant in the room." Companies don't need as much money as they used to, though he says hardware and local expansion are exceptions.

- Software is open source, hardware sits on AWS, marketing runs on Google, Twitter, Facebook, the App Store and similar channels, and many engineers are outsourced.
- His examples of small teams with huge value: Satoshi Nakamoto, WhatsApp (about 50 people, bought for $19B), YouTube (probably under 60 people when bought), and Instagram (a few people).
- He cites Slack, which said it had 50 or 100 years of money and was still raising. He guesses much of that went to secondary or acquisitions and calls those "not the classic reasons."
- Enterprise companies are heading the same way. Slack barely has a sales force. Bigger companies like Slack and Uber still need fewer resources than the version of them five or ten years earlier.

Why founders raise anyway: money is cheap, and it works as insurance, a scorecard, an acquisition tool, and a way to pay for hiring.

The costs:

- "When you raise more money you do spend more money," however disciplined you are.
- You move slower, meetings grow, and more stakeholders must be coordinated.
- You lose focus and take on too many projects. Cash-constrained companies tend to do better.
- Example: Pierre Omidyar's eBay. He credited his success to being the only competitor without much outside financing for a long time. Rivals put people into every transaction. With no headcount, he built an automated rating system and scalable processes from day one, and ran away with the market.

## Choosing investors and new capital sources

Naval thinks family offices, hedge funds, PE and foreign funds are good for entrepreneurs.

- Venture capital is "a bundle of advice, control, and money." More capital sources let you unbundle it: advice from people you want, money from the cheapest source, and leave the control behind.
- VCs calling these players "dumb money" is like a laundromat owner angry that another one opened. "Always filter venture advice through venture incentives."
- Downsides: they can be hot money and may not do their pro-rata. They may also try to veto things they shouldn't, and corporate contacts can get swapped out. Fix this by not giving them control and not counting on more money from them later.
- Control who sits on your board, or who the relationship person is, if you give a seat.
- "You cannot take someone's money without having a moral, ethical obligation to them." Avoid getting sued by investors, because that makes you untouchable later.

How to vet them:

- "People's true motivations and behavior are revealed, not said." Someone who spends ten minutes telling you how honest they are is dishonest.
- Reference-check hard and spend time with them.
- Watch how they behave in the term sheet process: quick, easy and sensible is a good sign. An exploding term sheet or inflexibility means "ten times worse once the money is in."
- Don't be afraid to call off the close. "I've done it and I've never regretted it." You are effectively "married" to investors, and the dating period is a week to a year.
- Ask an early-stage investor or trusted friend with a good nose who says unpopular things. Be careful of investors chasing a brand-name markup.

## Hiring slowly and rethinking equity

On headcount:

- Hire extremely slowly, "only after there's a burning need." People tire and hire someone to do their job, and it takes two hires to replace them. That is how a simple web app ends up with thousands of staff.
- Be ruthless about firing and trimming. Founders must watch waste closely, and "there's always waste." He says this has worked for him.

On equity:

- Stock is better than no stock but outdated. AngelList uses six-year vests, and VC firms use ten-year vests.
- As a company matures, grants should shift toward profit sharing, revenue sharing or bonuses. RSUs are diffuse in a large company, since one person can't move performance much.
- A hack: give small standard grants at first, then either substantially raise a person's grant next year or let them go.
- Profit sharing can be tax inefficient. AngelList is an LLC with one layer of taxation, which suits it.

Early employees:

- Naval says it is the lowest-risk time ever to be a founder, yet early employees take founder-level risk without founder-level equity.
- The old split was a founder at 40% and the first employee at 0.15% to 0.25%. He says those days are ending.
- Early employees before product/market fit are "really just hiring late founders." He suggests 1-4% instead of 0.1-0.4%.
- The problem isn't a shortage of engineers but "a surplus of founders," so early engineers have a high opportunity cost.

Who wins in the Valley, risk-adjusted, besides VCs: people who join companies just after product/market fit and help scale them, such as the next Dropbox or Airbnb.
