## Entrepreneur vs. investor temperament

Naval says entrepreneurs and investors need very different emotional temperaments. He has done both.

**Entrepreneur**
- Passionate, "almost irrationally optimistic," and highly committed to the single thing they are focused on.
- Has to "break down doors and pull trains and do whatever it takes" to get people to follow the vision.
- Needs focus and wants fairly constant feedback from the environment.

**Investor (angel or VC)**
- A little cynical, a little pessimistic, rational, and constantly re-evaluating each opportunity. All day long passionate entrepreneurs are selling to them.
- Must context switch very quickly across many different things.
- Must stay dispassionate. People call investors with bad news, not good news, and the investor has to work through it without it ruining their day.
- Feedback cycles are very slow. "You find out 10 years later if you were any good at what you're doing or not."

His background at the time (2010):
- Founded epinions.com (went public as part of shopping.com) and vast.com, a white-label classified service that powers parts of AOL, Kelley Blue Book, Vehix and Yahoo Travel.
- Co-author of Venture Hacks and launched AngelList.
- Runs a small fund with dozens of investments, including Twitter, Disqus, DocVerse, Mixer Labs and Social Gold.

## "It's the people, stupid": pick partners on intelligence, energy and integrity

Naval says the advice he ignored early on was the one every veteran gives. It holds in any business, whether the speaker is Warren Buffett on public investing, an experienced VC on private investing, or an older entrepreneur on starting a company. All of them say "it's the people, stupid." People nod, but he says you only understand it about 10 years later, "once you've worked with enough of the wrong people."

His rule is to find a great partner, whatever you are doing, and look for all three of these:
- Very high intelligence.
- Very high energy.
- Very high integrity.

"You can't compromise on any one of them." The failure modes are:
- A partner who isn't smart does you no good.
- A partner who isn't hard working does you no good.
- The worst case is "a smart hard working crook who ends up working against your interests."

Integrity takes a lot of time spent with someone to judge.

He also says most companies fail. That does not always mean falling apart. Many fail to execute, fail to deliver, or fail to realize their full potential. The cause is interpersonal conflict, either between founders or between a founder and an investor. "At the end of the day, it's people issues, which is surprising and ironic."

## Why consumer web founders should be in Silicon Valley

Naval started in Boston and found it hard to build a technology company there. Boston's definition of technology was "a little more backwards-looking." He moved to Silicon Valley.

Obvious reasons:
- Universities are there.
- Other tech entrepreneurs are there.
- Investors are there, so raising money is easier.

Non-obvious reasons:
- Partners are there, and most networking and partnerships "happen serendipitously, not by design."
- People use the latest gadgets, so you can see what will be mainstream a year or two out.
- "It's in the air, it's in the water."

Evidence from his own experience: at Vast he built a team of 100 people overseas. It was a great team, but it "took us a year longer and we lost a lot of time doing it."

Why it matters most for consumer web:
- Consumer web businesses are mostly winner-take-all. "There's only room for one Twitter and one Facebook and one eBay and one Google," and second place loses big.
- So founders need every advantage. Silicon Valley may be one item on a checklist of 10, "but if you want to win, it needs all 10."
- He grants that you can succeed elsewhere. He names a thriving New York scene, a smaller one in Boston, and opportunity in Seattle and Austin.
- He still expects that when a company gets big and "goes for the gold," it will probably move to Silicon Valley.

## How investors evaluate founders: the product is a resume

- When an entrepreneur shows a product, Naval is "evaluating it as a resume for the person." He asks what they are capable of.
- He wants to see how much they have done, not how much they talk.
- Silicon Valley, like Hollywood or New York, is a magnet place that attracts many good talkers. To separate talkers from doers, look at what they have built and how much they accomplished with very few resources.
- On the people side, he spends time with them and asks four things:
  - Are they good people?
  - Do they work really hard?
  - Are they very smart and thoughtful?
  - Can they accomplish things on their own?

## How to pitch busy investors (or any large buyer)

Naval says investor time is "assaulted from all directions." The same is true of any buy-side role, such as a buyer at a Fortune 500 company who gets sales calls all day. The same techniques apply to any large buyer. The principle is to respect their time as much as you respect your own.

Tactics:
- Don't insist on in-person meetings.
- Qualify the leads first.
- Send something in writing first.
- Get a very good introduction from someone trusted.
- Don't browbeat them into an in-person meeting or a call.
- Let them screen first via text, a summary or a business plan.
- If they ask for it, follow up with an in-person meeting.

## Secrecy: ideas are not worth hiding

- First-timers are "too secretive." Experienced founders "don't care."
- After a few companies, you see how much execution difficulty and risk there is, and how hard it is to get people to listen and believe you.
- Eventually founders "shout your idea from the rooftops just in the hope that somebody will actually use the product." The problem flips to getting anyone to pay attention.
- If an idea would lose all its value once someone else figures it out, "it's not something you can execute on as a business. It's not a real idea."

## Why angels won't fund small or niche ideas

Naval calls this a large misconception that disserves entrepreneurs. Many founders think they can carve out a small niche with little competition, aim for a $20 or 30 million exit, and raise angel money on that basis.

His reasoning:
- He cites Keynes's "beauty contest." An investor is betting on the company most likely to be selected in the next round, not the most beautiful company.
- The real risk is that the company runs out of money. "Every company needs more money than it thought it did," so future financing risk is high.
- VCs only fund companies with potential large exits.
- Because of that, angels will only fund companies with potential large exits. Otherwise they risk funding a company that no VC will touch later.
