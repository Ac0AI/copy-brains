## Wealth, money and status are three different things

- Naval defines wealth as "assets that earn while you sleep": businesses, code running at night, money reinvested into other assets. A house counts only if it can be rented out.
- Wealth buys freedom, meaning no tie, no 7 a.m. commute, no boss. He says Ferraris, yachts and jets "get really boring and stupid, really fast."
- Money is how wealth is transferred. He calls it social IOUs for value you created in the past, which governments debase by printing more.
- Status is rank in a social hierarchy and is zero-sum. Wealth is positive-sum: one person having a house does not stop another from having one.
- People who attack wealth creators are usually playing the status game. Journalists attacking rich people or the tech industry are "bidding for status." He advises avoiding status games because they make you angry and combative.
- Society can only carry a small share of takers: "If you get too many takers, and not enough makers, society falls apart." His example is Venezuela.
- He means ethical wealth: voluntary exchange, not monopolies, crony capitalism or mispriced externalities.

## Making money is not luck: the four kinds of luck

- Naval wants to be wealthy in 999 of 1,000 parallel universes, not in the 50 where he got lucky. He claims that if dropped on a street in any English-speaking country, he would be wealthy again in 5 to 10 years.
- Four kinds of luck (from Marc Andreessen's post):
  1. Blind luck, which is fate.
  2. Luck from hustle, where lots of motion stirs things up.
  3. Luck from preparation, where skill lets you spot breaks others miss.
  4. Luck from your unique character and reputation, where opportunity finds you. Example: the best deep-sea diver gets paid when someone finds a sunken treasure ship.
- Type 4 is the one to aim for. Trusted, high-integrity deal makers get approached by people who need deals done safely (his example is Warren Buffett).
- Quotes he and Nivi cite: "extreme people get extreme results" (Sam Altman) and "you can't be normal and expect abnormal returns" (Jeffrey Pfeffer).
- His own wealth came in "chips," not one payout. His first fortune was lost in the stock market, and in his second he was cheated by business partners. The third attempt worked, built from many small businesses and investments.
- Networking and coffee meetings mostly rely on Type 1 and Type 2 luck. He says a busy calendar and a busy mind "will destroy your ability to do great things."

## Equity, not hours: why you can't rent out your time

- "You are not going to get rich renting out your time. You must own equity, a piece of the business."
- Salaried work ties inputs to outputs. You do not earn while asleep, and employers pay "the bare minimum" to get the job done. Even rich doctors get there through a private practice, brand or IP.
- Look for work where inputs and outputs are disconnected. One engineer can create Bitcoin, while another ships a year of unused code. A lumberjack is at best about 3x better than the worst lumberjack.
- Tools and creativity create that disconnect. Replaceable, teachable roles will eventually be automated.
- Live below your means. "The most dangerous things are heroin and a monthly salary" (he attributes this to Taleb, unsure). Lifestyle upgrades reset what you think wealth is.
- Ideally money arrives in "discrete lumps" years apart, as in tech: a decade of losses, then a payday in year 11. He argues high marginal tax rates punish that pattern.

## Give society what it wants but can't yet get, at scale

- Naval says you get rich by giving society "what it wants, but does not yet know how to get, at scale."
- Technology is whatever doesn't work yet (he credits Danny Hillis, after an in-text correction). Rockefeller's oil, Ford's cars and Jobs's smartphone were once technology.
- Step 1: find something natural to you and within your skills that society will want. Step 2: figure out how to scale it so everyone can have one.
- Nivi's framing, which Naval extends: the entrepreneur brings the high end to the mass market. Rich people had chauffeurs, then Uber, then scooters.
- The internet connects everyone to everyone, so any niche obsession can find 50,000 passionate people. His examples: Joe Rogan reportedly near $100M a year, PewDiePie's reach, e-sports, and miniature-cooking channels.
- "Escape competition through authenticity." Nobody can compete with you on being you, so don't copy.
- Pick a business model with leverage:
  - **Scale economies:** widget 10,000 is cheaper than widget 5.
  - **Zero marginal cost of reproduction:** Rogan's podcast number 1,100 pays far more than number 1, with no extra effort.
  - **Network effects:** Metcalfe's law says value grows with the square of nodes (10 nodes = 100, 100 nodes = 10,000). The number one player wins everything unless you are number two. Examples: Facebook, Uber vs Lyft, Google, Twitter, YouTube, Amazon Prime.
- Network effect defined: each additional user adds value to existing users. Design hooks so customers add value to each other: "You're sleeping at night and your customers are adding value to each other."
- Cautionary tale: Epinions drifted into the comparison-shopping fight with DealTime, mySimon, PriceGrabber, NexTag and Bizrate. Amazon won e-tail and that space "went to zero." He says they should have pushed into local reviews, which became Yelp and TripAdvisor, because a review of a scarce item like a restaurant is worth more than the 1,000th camera review. Lesson: "play stupid games, win stupid prizes."

## The four types of leverage, and why code and media win

- Archimedes' line, "Give me a lever long enough," frames the point: fortunes require leverage.
- **Labor** is the oldest and most overvalued. People ask "how many people work there?" He calls it "the worst form of leverage": messy, requires leadership, and a short hop from mutiny. Use the minimum labor needed to access the other types.
- **Capital** dominated the last century (bankers, Buffett types). It is surgical and scales well, but you first need specific knowledge and accountability to be given any.
- **Code and media** (products with no marginal cost of replication) are where the new fortunes are made: Rogan, PewDiePie, Bezos, Zuckerberg, Page, Gates, Jobs.
- Code and media are permissionless. Nobody has to follow you or fund you. Coding, writing, podcasting and YouTube need no one's approval.
- "The robot army is already here": servers running code while you sleep. Coding is the superpower for commanding them, and the bottleneck is deciding what to tell them to do.
- Tech startups combine all three: minimum high-output labor (engineers, designers), capital for marketing and scale, and code/media.
- Code and media products are egalitarian. Bezos has no better search or Netflix than you. Labor- and capital-based goods are not; status goods like a Rolex only work if few have them.
- The best products target the middle class, where scale amortizes cost. A Toyota Corolla or a Tesla Model 3 beats a Lamborghini for daily use.
- Wealth built on code and media is more ethical because the output reaches everyone.

## Specific knowledge, accountability and judgment

- To get paid at scale: accountability is why you, leverage is the scale, and specific knowledge is why you rather than someone else.
- **Specific knowledge cannot be trained.** If a class could teach it, others could be trained, then software, then robots. It is found by following genuine curiosity and natural aptitude, "the thing that feels like play to you but looks like work to others." It usually sits on the frontier of technology, art or communication.
- Naval's mother told him at 15 or 16 that he would go into business, after watching him critique local pizza parlors. He had planned to be an astrophysicist.
- It is often learned through apprenticeship. Buffett offered to work for Benjamin Graham for free; Graham said "you're overpriced." A cheap apprenticeship in a skill nobody can teach yet is worth it.
- Don't assemble skills too deliberately. Follow obsession and keep an eye on commercial aspects. The Scott Adams skill stack idea (top 25% at several things) works because combinations make you scarce.
- Two forms:
  - **Timely:** machine learning, which may become second hat.
  - **Timeless:** persuasion, which you pick up early and is always valuable.
- Learn to sell and learn to build; "if you can do both, you will be unstoppable" (Musk, Jobs). Bill Gates: "I'd rather teach an engineer marketing than a marketer engineering."
- If forced to pick one: start by building, since it cuts through the noise, then transition to selling because sales skill and reputation scale better and building burns you out.
- **Accountability** means putting your name on things. "Accountability is reputational skin in the game," and people need to be able to tell what role you played. It is like taking equity in your own work: you are paid last but get unlimited upside.
- Fear of public failure holds people back. People who can fail in public gain power. Ruin in business now comes mostly from integrity failures (Madoff), not economic failure.
- A well-functioning team is small with clear owners for product, messaging, fundraising and pricing.
- **Judgment** is knowing the long-term consequences of your actions. In an age of near-infinite leverage it is the decisive skill: a CEO who is 10 to 20% better steering a $100B company is worth hundreds of millions.
- Judgment needs experience. Intellect without skin in the game is "often worse than useless," and the best-judgment people are the least emotional. "The more outraged someone is, the worse their judgment."
- Buffett's credibility (accountable, right in public, high integrity) means "nobody asks him how hard he works."
- Top investors read philosophy and history, not investment books, because investing is real-world and constantly competed away.

## Choosing partners, markets and how you work

- **Play long-term games with long-term people.** All returns come from compounding in iterated games (tit-for-tat in an iterated prisoner's dilemma). Switching industries resets your network and trust to zero.
- Pick partners with high intelligence, high energy and high integrity, with no compromise on any. "A smart and hard-working crook" is the failure case of skipping integrity.
- Judge integrity by actions: how someone treats a waiter, how they treat enemies. People who loudly advertise their integrity are suspect, like con men.
- His example: a founder kept putting personal money into a failing company through three pivots without wiping out investors, and said "I didn't do it for you... it's my own self-esteem." Naval's gloss: "self-esteem is the reputation that you have with yourself."
- You can't sell someone into lasting motivation; it has to be intrinsic. He won't hire or start a company with someone who isn't into the task.
- Partner with rational optimists. Cynics and pessimists are self-fulfilling and want a fourth option beyond lead, follow or get out of the way. Successful people have an action bias. We descend from pessimists who survived tigers, but modern downside is limited and upside unlimited.
- Avoid ruin: stay out of jail and avoid total capital loss ("it's never worth it to wear an orange jumpsuit"). The Kelly criterion idea: don't bet everything on one gamble, since averages across 100 people are not what one person playing 100 times gets (his Russian roulette example).
- Ethics is "long-term greedy." Honesty frees mental energy and filters out people who want pretty lies. Fair dealers become market hubs that people route deals through; unethical deals pay off only in the short run.
- Work as hard as you can, but what you work on and who you work with come first. Think "product-market-founder fit," three legs of a stool. Pick the startup with the best alumni network (the PayPal mafia).
- Nobody really works 80 to 120 high-output hours. Work in sprints "like a lion hunting," with rest. "Inspiration is perishable," so act when it hits. "Impatience with actions, patience with results."
- Be too busy to "do coffee" yet keep an uncluttered calendar. Decline meetings ruthlessly: push to a call, then email, then text. He says "I don't do non-transactional meetings." Busy people meet you if you show proof of work, like product progress, "an unfake-able resume."
- Reject most advice. Founders who get rich quickly are "giving you their winning lottery ticket numbers." The best founders listen to everyone, then make up their own minds. Avoid anyone who makes money selling how-to-get-rich courses, since "there are no get rich quick schemes."

## Hourly rate, learning and microeconomic concepts

- Set an aspirational hourly rate that feels absurdly high; his was $5,000/hour before he had money. Outsource or skip anything that costs less than your rate (errands, returns, cooking), and don't penny-pinch if wealth is your top priority.
- Paul Graham's advice for startups: work on product, exercise and eat well, and not much else.
- Learning:
  - "Read what you love until you love to read."
  - Start with foundational, original works (Wealth of Nations, Origin of Species, Feynman's Six Easy Pieces).
  - Mathematics and logic come first. Better to read a great book slowly than fly through a hundred.
  - He names reading, writing, arithmetic, persuasion and programming as the five core skills.
- "There is no skill called business." Skip business school case studies and magazines. Study microeconomics, game theory, psychology, persuasion, ethics, math and computers. Skip macroeconomics ("a branch of politics").
- "Doing is faster than watching." Learning scales with the number of iterations, not hours. Run lots of small experiments, and tolerate frequent small failures. Entrepreneurs "bleed every day" for a big win later.
- Pricing and economics concepts he uses:
  - **Price discrimination:** charge by propensity to pay with a real extra. Business class costs 5 to 10x for perhaps 2 to 3x the airline's cost. Enterprise software does it with freemium tiers charging 10 to 100x for security and admin features.
  - **Consumer surplus:** he'd pay $20 for his Starbucks coffee. All businesses generate it, and Amazon likely generates trillions of dollars of it.
  - **Net present value:** a startup grant of 0.1% of a "$1B" company is worth $10,000, not $1M, if the last round priced it at a $10M valuation.
  - **Negotiation:** "won by whoever cares less." Turn short-term games into repeat games with references, follow-on projects and reviews.
  - **Principal-agent problem:** "If you want it done, Go. If not, Send." Be generous with lieutenants on ownership, prefer boutique firms (a lawyer or banker of one) for accountability, and as an employee think like the owner.
  - **Mispriced externalities:** price them high (water, pollution) instead of using feel-good bans.

## Building a career: ownership, size, and the end goal

- Real estate ladder showing more specific knowledge, accountability and leverage at each rung: day laborer ($15 to $75/hour, no leverage), general contractor (takes risk and equity), property developer (capital leverage), famous architect or large developer, REIT, then a real estate tech company (the Zillow/Redfin/Trulia type) with all leverage types combined.
- "Productize yourself": scale something authentic to you with leverage. Find hobbies that make you money, keep you fit, and make you smarter. His are reading and making money.
- Ceteris paribus, you will do better in a smaller organization (more accountability, battlefield promotions). Move from large companies to progressively smaller ones; the reverse is much harder.
- If you must rent out your time early, only do so while learning and saving, preferably in an apprenticeship-type field (chief of staff to a founder). Seek the steepest learning curve and take on accountability, which "you can take on immediately." Compensation follows once you've stopped waiting for it.
- Companies measure inputs because they can't measure outputs, so work in a way that makes your outputs visible. His investing school Spearhead found that generic classes were worth little, while office hours on specific deals were very valuable.
- On the long haul: "on a long enough timescale, you will get paid," but it can take 5 to 20 years. He says those he judged brilliant and hardworking two decades ago are almost all successful.
- Keep redefining what you do until you are the best in the world at it. Entrepreneurs only need to be right once.
- Wealth is not the final goal. "A fit body, a calm mind, a house full of love. These things cannot be bought, they must be earned."
