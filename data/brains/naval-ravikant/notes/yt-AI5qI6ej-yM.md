## From investor to hardware builder: Naval's career and what Air Chat taught him
- Naval now calls himself a builder of things. He treats investing as a side hobby and says "every so-called career is an evolution" toward whatever interests you most.
- His path: Venture Hacks, then AngelList, with early investments including Twitter, Uber and Notion. He says he is not especially proud of being an investor.
- He built Air Chat, an audio-first, Twitter-like social product with AI transcription and translation. It never took off, and because it was a social product it needed to catch on to work.
- He is still proud of the product and the team. The team was paid, and the investors he brought in got their money back.
- Lesson he took: he enjoys designing every pixel of a product and getting it to market. His next company is harder, combining hardware and software.
- He now wants to take more technical risk than market risk. Elon Musk is his inspiration for building hard, useful things. He cites Peter Thiel's idea that the future has to be built.
- He is cautious about the new product. He says it is "really hard" and he is not sure he can pull it off.
- He says the new company depends on AI, and so did Air Chat. Without AI transcription and translation, Air Chat could not have existed.
- AngelList is also adopting AI heavily.

## Why Naval started tweeting, and what made his writing spread
- The AngelList idea of matching investors to startups did not work as a business. It had started as an email list that was popular early and then stalled.
- The SEC then sent a letter saying he was acting as an unlicensed dealer. He says he earned no money from it and treated it as a public service.
- He went to Washington and got the law changed. He says this paved the way for ICOs, incubators and demo days.
- The period was highly stressful, and he began tweeting the realizations he was having under that pressure.
- His recurring themes were science (he is an amateur physics fan), philosophy and making money. His Twitter bio joke was "truth, love and money".
- Why it resonated: everyone thinks about everything, not just their narrow lane. People deal with their relationships, with the universe and what is true, and with the practical business of earning a living.
- Before this, people expected someone in his position to talk only about money and stay in his lane.
- Many of his best pieces were notes to self, reminders such as "don't forget this".
- The "How to Get Rich" thread was one of his earliest and is still among Twitter's most viral threads.
- He prefers timeless topics and philosophy. He compares it to "compound interest in ideas". He avoids news when he can.

## Naval's view on AI policy and the Vance speech
- He found Vance's speech humble, optimistic and forward-looking. He thinks America should win and should not regulate AI.
- The speech marked a break because earlier events were framed around AI safety: Bletchley Park, the EU regulations and Biden's executive order. It put opportunity first.
- Naval does not believe AI will destroy the world. He compares the doom narrative to a religious belief in the same family as climate, asteroid and Covid doom. He says that kind of fear captures everyone's attention.
- The incentive problem: companies that are ahead want to pull the ladder up behind them. He thinks they probably believe in the safety risks, but they are also motivated to believe them and spread the message.
- He points to a contradiction in their position: it is too dangerous for open source, yet not too dangerous for one private company to own it. If AI were really a Manhattan Project making nuclear weapons, you would not want a single company owning it.
- He cites Sam Altman's remark that AI would capture the "light cone" of all future value. If that is true, open-source and small-tech AI matter a great deal.
- The hard part is that training is naturally centralized because it needs supercomputer clusters. It is unclear how a decentralized model competes.
- His framing of the real problem: how to advance AI without handing control to a few people.
- His biggest fear is not AI itself. It is what a handful of people controlling AI would do to everyone "for our own good", since that is what always happens.
- On OpenAI's structure, he says the company needs to raise money and incentivize staff. But he does not see why it has to buy out the nonprofit entirely.
- His alternative: keep the nonprofit with a truly independent board, and add an incentive package for staff and investors plus a fundraising plan.
- If you want to capture the value of superintelligence, you know it is worth far more than the number being offered.

## AI, jobs and opportunity
- He agrees with Sacks that there is no evidence yet of mass job destruction from AI. He expects it to create jobs and opportunity at least as fast as it destroys them.
- He objects to the word "jobs". A job means someone else hands you work, which frames it as a zero-sum game. He prefers to talk about opportunities.
- Examples he gives: YouTube streamers and influencers, which were not professions before. Since Covid, many people earn money from home online in ways that are hard to categorize.
- Forecasting trap: he says the European approach wrongly assumes you can predict the future precisely enough to regulate against its risks today. "We don't know enough yet."
- He describes current AI as natural-language computers. People who feared computers no longer need to program in arcane languages or learn obscure math, because they can simply talk to the machine. "The new programming language is English."
- He says many of the jobs at risk are paperwork jobs, often make-work that just shuffles paper. He uses the federal government as his example, joking that a third of it digs holes with spoons and another third fills them in.
- Startups: he notes that new companies are possible that could not have existed without AI. He dismisses Twitter claims like "AGI is here, I fired my mid-level engineers, I stopped hiring" as foolishness.
- The two real stories he sees are entrepreneurs scaling wildly with AI, and employers who want to hire people who are fluent in AI.
- On AGI: he does not think anyone knows how to build it yet, and he sees what exists today as language models used mainly for search and homework.

## Open vs closed AI, DeepSeek, and who wins
- DeepSeek shows the stakes. You cannot stop the flow of chips and talent, because constrained competitors simply become more efficient.
- He notes China sent America's best open-source model back to it while US labs were going closed source "for safety".
- He agrees America should win, but says the best way to win is to be as open, broad and innovative as possible. One company controlling everything will innovate more slowly than a dynamic, open ecosystem.
- Network effects mean being open still lets the leader win. His example is the internet: it is an open technology, yet the dominant companies are almost all American because the US led.
- He applies the same logic to AI. Open models will spread to China and India, but the leader still benefits.

## Tariffs, network effects and strategic industries
- Naval's argument is that the textbook Ricardian view (produce what you are best at, trade, and keep tariffs low even if others tax you) ignores that most modern businesses have network effects or economies of scale.
- His thought experiment, with himself as China and the other side as the US:
  - Subsidize the industries that have network effects or winner-take-all dynamics: TikTok, semiconductors, drones, BYD and autos.
  - Ban the other country's social media while spreading yours.
  - Once you win, you own the market and can raise prices. A latecomer cannot start a competitor.
  - Or, with economies of scale, cut prices to zero to drive rivals out, so nobody sane invests, then raise them again.
- His conclusion: these industries are high-margin and sticky. If someone subsidizes them or raises tariffs against you to build them, you have to respond. "You can't just stand back completely."
- Beyond money, he argues tariffs protect a functioning middle class with good jobs. In a world without tariffs the gains might flow only to the upper and lower classes.
- Strategic example: DJI is arguably the world's biggest defense contractor. Both sides in Ukraine source drone parts that trace back to it, and the US lacks that industry.
- He says drones are autonomous bullets and the future of warfare. A drone supply chain means motors, semiconductors, optics and lasers, so there is a strong case to bring at least parts of these industries home.
- He thinks the US is lucky to be nearly self-sufficient, with oil, gas, fracking and nuclear fission, and should be energy self-sufficient with a large surplus.
- He argues that building hard things at scale for national security and innovation is better work than writing essays or doing paperwork, and even better than driving.

## Immigration, knowledge and copyright
- Naval is a first-generation immigrant who came at 9. He supports skilled, legal immigration where people assimilate, and he distinguishes it sharply from open borders.
- His reasoning: technology drives economic power, military power and even culture. Wealth is capital combined with knowledge, and knowledge is the biggest component. Bringing in the best and brightest made the US the center of knowledge creation.
- He says part of the DeepSeek outcome comes from the US educating talented people and then sending them home.
- On AI copyright, he takes a "riskier" position and largely agrees that scraping the open web to build direct substitutes goes too far. Users now start and end everything inside the model.
- His rule: if you train on the open web, your model should be open source.
- He sees only two stable outcomes:
  - Pay copyright owners. He thinks this won't work on its own, because someone in China will crawl and release weights, which are compressed data.
  - If you crawl, keep the result open and contribute to open source, and let those who don't want to be crawled protect their data.
- He notes that the lossy-compression view of LLMs, attributed to Ilya Sutskever, is central to the fair-use question of whether a model learns or just regurgitates.

## Personal operating habits Naval mentions
- Parenting: he follows David Deutsch's idea of taking children seriously and giving them the same freedom as an adult. He credits Aaron Stupple as an explainer of the approach but says Aaron's near-total version is not his own position.
- His own rules: each child does 1 hour of math or programming and 2 hours of reading a day, and after that they are free. Everything else is persuasion, not control.
- He homeschools his children, and says that to raise capable adults you want them to make their own decisions.
- Sleep: he sits in bed and meditates. His reasoning is that the mind dislikes meditation so much that, forced to choose between meditating and sleeping, it falls asleep.
- Naval avoids mentally heavy or existential conversations before bed.
