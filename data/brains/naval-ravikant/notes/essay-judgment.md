## Leverage comes first, then judgment becomes the decisive skill

- Naval says all great fortunes are created through leverage, and the first job is to obtain it.
- Permissioned leverage: getting people to work for you, or raising capital.
- Permissionless leverage: learning to code, or becoming a good communicator through podcasting, broadcasting, video and writing.
- The first part of a career is spent hustling to get leverage. Once you have it, he says to "slow down a bit, because your judgment really matters."
- His analogy: you go from steering a sailboat to steering an ocean liner or tanker. You have more at risk and more to gain, and you carry a higher payload.
- His conclusion: "In an age of infinite leverage, judgment becomes the most important skill." Leverage is a force multiplier for judgment.

## Why judgment is what the market pays for (Buffett and CEO pay)

- Naval says everything else you do is setting you up to apply your judgment.
- Warren Buffett is wealthy because of his judgment. If you took all his money away, Naval says investors would "come out of the woodwork" and hand him $100 billion to invest, and he would keep a big chunk of it.
- On CEO pay: Naval grants that crony capitalism exists, with CEOs controlling boards. But some CEOs earn their keep.
- If you steer a $100 billion ship like Google or Apple and your judgment is 10 or 20 percent better than the next person's, society will pay you hundreds of millions more. Being on course more often compounds across hundreds of billions of dollars, so the pay is dwarfed by the results.
- Nobody asks Buffett how hard he works or when he wakes up. They say "Warren, just do your thing."

## Demonstrated judgment needs a public track record and accountability

- Naval says demonstrated judgment and credibility are critical for getting people to put leverage behind you.
- Buffett wins because he has:
  - massive credibility
  - high accountability
  - been right over and over in the public domain
  - a reputation for very high integrity, so people can trust him
- Naval's formula: "Judgment, especially demonstrated judgment, with high accountability, clear track record, is critical."
- Nivi's summary, which Naval builds on: you are accountable for your judgment, judgment is the exercise of wisdom, and wisdom comes from experience that can be accelerated through short iterations.

## Defining judgment and wisdom

- Judgment is knowing the long-term consequences of your actions, then making the right decision to capitalize on that.
- Naval's definition of wisdom is the same thing: knowing the long-term consequences of your actions.
- Wisdom is judgment applied to the personal domain. Judgment is wisdom applied to external problems. He calls them highly linked.

## Build judgment through experience and many fast iterations, not intellect alone

- Judgment is very hard to build, and it takes both intellect and experience.
- Naval agrees with Nassim Taleb's criticism of ivory-tower intellectuals, who have no skin in the game and no real-world experience.
- Intellect without experience is "often worse than useless." It gives you confidence and some credibility, but with no skin in the game or accountability, "you're just throwing darts."
- The real world is far more complex than we can intellectualize. This is especially true of fast-moving edge domains, where you can't get there without experience.
- His tactic: if you are smart and iterate fast, it's "not even you put 10,000 hours into something, but you take 10,000 tries at something."

## The best judges are the least emotional

- Naval says the people with the best judgment are among the least emotional. Many top investors seem almost robotic, and he wouldn't be surprised if the best entrepreneurs often come across as unemotional.
- Entrepreneurs have to care about what they're doing, but they also have to see clearly what is actually happening.
- Emotions are what stop you from seeing reality, in investing, running companies, building products or being an entrepreneur.
- The cost of this: you resist the truth until it becomes too sudden, and then you are forced into suffering as the fantasy you built breaks.
- His outrage test: the more outraged someone is, the worse their judgment. Someone constantly tweeting political outrage and getting into fights is not someone you would hand "the keys to your car, let alone the keys to your company."

## Study broadly, including philosophy, to build judgment

- Naval notes that top value investors such as Warren Buffett, Jeremy Grantham and Michael Burry sound like philosophers, or are philosophers. They read a lot of history and science.
- He says investment books are "the worst place to learn about investment." Investing is a real-world, highly multivariate activity, and every advantage is always being competed away. It is always on the cutting edge.
- What you need instead is "very, very broad-based judgment and thinking," and the best way to get it is to study everything, including a lot of philosophy.
- Philosophy makes you more stoic and less emotional, so you make better decisions.
