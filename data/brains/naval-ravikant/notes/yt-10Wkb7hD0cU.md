## What Naval looks for in founders: intelligence, energy, integrity

Naval screens founders on three traits, plus one personal test.

- **Intelligence.** Founders must know what they are doing. This is a fuzzy judgment, formed from conversation. He asks whether they have insight and specific knowledge, and whether they have thought about the problem deeply. It is not about age or years of experience, only the depth of their understanding of what they are about to do.
- **Energy.** Being a founder is "brutally difficult" and takes a long time, and in the long run the people who succeed are the ones who persevere. Red flags are founders who run out of energy, who work in a hesitant, preliminary way, who constantly seek positive feedback, or who are easily thrown off course.
- **Integrity.** Intelligence and energy without integrity gives you "a hardworking smart crook". Startups are fast-moving and people are independent, so anyone who wants to screw you over will find a way. His definition: "ethics and integrity are what you do despite the money. If being ethical was profitable everybody would do it."
- **Integrity red flag.** A founder who offers to do something slightly unfair to another shareholder, employee or founder in order to please Naval. If they will do it to them, they will do it to him. Integrity is the hardest trait to judge because it takes longitudinal, long-term relationships.
- **Personal test.** Investing in or founding with someone means having them in your life for about a decade. Naval only works with people he genuinely likes. If a call or meeting with them is exhausting, or they are negative or difficult, "no amount of money is worth it". He and Tim "will both die with money in the bank", so the question is whether he wants to spend scarce time and mental energy on these people.
- **Favorite founders.** The ones he learns from. When they call for help he jumps on it, because an hour walking around the block with them leaves him smarter.

## Honesty as a core operating value

Naval treats honesty as a foundational value, for three reasons.

- **Physics.** Feynman said "you must never ever fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool". In physics you don't negotiate or soften things to make people feel better, because a wrong equation simply won't work.
- **Street honesty.** Growing up in New York, he saw members of the Russian mob take each other's word even while ready to kill each other. That showed him how much relationships depend on honesty.
- **Presence.** Withholding or lying, even a white lie, creates "a second thread" in your head that must keep tracking what you said versus what you think. That pulls you out of the present moment and creates stress and distraction over time.
- **Recommended book.** *Lying* by Sam Harris, on deceit in general, including white lies meant to protect people.

## Investing is an emotional skill built through internal work

- Naval says the way to improve at investing is to work on your internal state until you are free of as many biases and conditioned responses as you can be.
- Investors only pretend to be rational. They decide whether they want to invest in the first five minutes of a meeting. If a company doesn't take their money in the first round, they get annoyed, and they have to undo that emotional state so they can still be a positive force when the second round comes.
- He rejects "the Epiphany theory of self-development". Reading one book or phrase and pinning it to your screen doesn't change you. Happiness, nutrition, diet, investing and self-awareness are all skills, built over decades through feedback loops.
- Books that shaped him: Seneca and Marcus Aurelius' *Meditations* (an emperor's private diary showing that power and success don't fix your internal state), Sam Harris, Jiddu Krishnamurti's *The Book of Life*, and Bruce Lee's writings. He pays attention to "rational Buddhists" and ignores the mysticism and guru worship.

## How Naval reads: treat books like disposable blog posts

- **The problem.** Twitter and Facebook gave him "little dopamine snacks" without real learning. Books felt sacred and had to be finished, so people got stuck on them and stopped reading.
- **Old problems.** For old problems, such as health, calm, values and raising a family, older sources are more reliable, because a book that survived 2,000 years has been filtered by many people. For new problems, such as driving a car or flying a plane, read modern sources.
- **Go to the source.** For example, *Origin of Species* is cheap on Kindle and an easy read, and almost nothing in it is incorrect.
- **The hack.** Treat books as throwaway blog posts or tweets and feel no obligation to finish any of them.
  - He buys any book that someone mentions.
  - He reads 10 to 20 books at a time and flips through them.
  - He skips ahead, starts in the middle where a paragraph catches his eye, and deletes books that bore him or contain errors.
- **Result.** Books are back in his reading library, and with them a lot of ancient wisdom.

## Habits, skills and building a sustainable life

- Naval says we are "a collection of thousands, maybe tens of thousands of habits" running subconsciously, leaving a little extra neocortex for new problems. "You become your habits."
- A light daily workout from his trainer showed him how much habits change you physically and mentally. His view is that "to have peace of mind you have to have peace of body first".
- **Operating rule.** In any six-month window he is either picking up a good habit or dropping a bad one. Real change such as getting fit is a 10-year journey, not a three-month fix.
- **Sustainability.** He wants everything and doesn't want to suffer through deprivation. For diet, he changes his taste buds to like healthier foods and substitutes tasty healthy foods for unhealthy ones. He wants a life that is effortless, so a habit must be sustainable with no effort.

## Success, desire and the three options

- **Success.** Naval admires creators and commercializers (Steve Jobs, Marc Andreessen for Netscape, Satoshi Nakamoto, Elon Musk). But he thinks the real winners are the ones who step out of the game entirely and need nothing from anyone. A test he cites: if you can sit quietly in a room for 30 minutes and be happy, you are successful.
- **Desire.** "Desire is a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want." He chooses desires carefully and tries to hold no more than one big desire at a time. He sees desire as the axis of his suffering.
- **Three options in any situation.** You can change it, accept it, or leave it. Wishing you could change it or leave it, without doing either and without accepting it, causes most misery. The word he tells himself most often is "accept".
- **Advice to his younger self.** Chill out, don't stress so much, and be more yourself. Don't live up to other people's expectations. Say no to more things and protect your time, because the marginal value of an extra day rises as you age.
- **Love.** Love can't be bought, but it is free to give. Quoting Charlie Munger's "be worthy of a mate", he says to work on yourself until you no longer need things, and then they appear.

## Choiceless awareness: his meditation method

- Naval says most people claim to meditate but few actually do. The form that works for him is "choiceless awareness" (non-judgmental awareness), which he pieced together from Krishnamurti.
- You walk around your day, ideally near nature and not in conversation, and accept the moment without judging anything or anyone. He runs his brain "in debugger mode" and watches his thoughts.
- Enlightenment is "a space between your thoughts", which you can achieve moment to moment.
- When he traces his thoughts, about 90% turn out to be fear-based and the other 10% desire-based. Desire is fear by another name. Once a thought is recognized as fear it fades, the mind quiets, and he stops taking his life for granted.
- Ten to fifteen minutes of this leaves him peaceful and grateful.
