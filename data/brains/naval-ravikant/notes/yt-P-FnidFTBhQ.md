## Naval on how the cost of starting a company collapsed

- Naval argues the thing that disrupted venture capital was "the cost of building a business has collapsed," not Angelist or Y Combinator (he credits YC for pioneering the new incubator model).
- His 1999 company Opinions raised $8 million in its first round. It took 6 months to ship the website. The team bought its own Sun servers and Oracle databases, hosted them in its own office, and paid for a T1 line. There was no MySQL, no Git, and no data centers.
- Today the same work is rented:
  - AWS for hosting.
  - Google for acquiring customers.
  - Twitter for customer service.
  - Facebook for marketing.
  - Heroku for deployment.
  - Elance or oDesk for part-time labor.
  - Mechanical Turk for cleanup tasks.
  - Kickstarter or Angelist for money.
- He says "a single person with a lever can truly move the earth." Great businesses can be "built entirely through leverage," talking to the world through APIs with very little headcount.
- He thinks Instagram is not an aberration and expects "a lot more Instagrams." He disagrees with Marc Andreessen that Instagram needed 200 people to monetize; he says five more people would have been enough.
- Seed rounds are mostly $750K to $1M, but many companies can get far on $250K. Incubators prototype and test for $25K in 3 months, and "that's the competition."
- Consequence for hiring: before product-market fit, employees are really "late founders." They can start their own company or join an incubator, so the old pattern of raising $1M and giving the first engineer a quarter point no longer works.
- Consequence for VCs: the first-check role moved from Sand Hill Road to super angels around 2007, then to incubators. Companies now brand themselves as "YC backed" or "Techstars backed," not as a Sequoia company. That pushes VCs further back in the feeder chain, which is dangerous because brands are built by forming early relationships with entrepreneurs.

## Company size shrinking and the future of work

- Naval's contrarian belief: the efficient size of a company is "shrinking very rapidly." The industrial revolution created economies of scale that pushed people into factories of 5,000 to 10,000. Information technology has reversed that.
- He expects many small companies loosely coupled through APIs and processes, and more contract and gig work. People will take a job for 2 or 3 days, get rated, then take the next. "In the future we're all Uber drivers."
- He expects more billion-dollar businesses built by four or five people. The VC industry still believes you must put a couple hundred million into a company to scale, and he thinks that is wrong.
- Pieces that look non-outsourceable today will be outsourced: sales, revenue collection, bookkeeping reconciliation, and the early stage of hiring.
- He says any good entrepreneur could build Facebook's feature set today with a few hundred people. Large founders know 80% of staff aren't needed but can't tell which 80%.
- Productivity gap: people comfortable with information technology have a "10x advantage in productivity or greater." He says "the smart are getting richer" and society doesn't know how to handle it.
- His definition of work: "a set of things you have to do that you don't want to do." If you want to do it, it isn't work. Startups feel harder but are done "your way."
- On Yahoo, he says it needs to exit entire businesses and focus. "Betting on mobile" is too broad; you have to pick which part of mobile.

## Seed economics, experiments, and failing fast

- Naval says the seed-to-Series-A crunch is mostly a pricing and expectations problem. Many seed companies are "cheap, low-cost experiments" and should be treated as such.
- Prices must reflect a hit rate of 1 in 5 or 1 in 10, not 1 in 3. In his day, a company that raised its first few million had a 1-in-2 or 1-in-3 chance of reaching the next round. Today a seed company has about a 1-in-10 chance of reaching a Series A.
- Early investors who forget it's an experiment may push you to raise a "seed prime" or a premature Series A. He says that traps you "like Sisyphus," pushing a rock uphill when there may be no market or no right team.
- Advice: "if you're going to fail, fail fast." Acknowledge reality, then try again, as YC companies that go back into YC do.
- He thinks the soft-landing acqui-hire is overrated by press coverage because failed companies stay quiet. Few companies get one. Acquirers separate investors from talent by paying retention and earn-out to the team, so investors often lose money.
- Investors who add multiples on acquisitions to term sheets have no real leverage. The acquirer can offer 10 cents on the dollar, and the company's alternative is zero.
- Not every company should be funded. He doesn't want unfundable companies funded, because many good businesses are ruined by funding: expectations rise and they "have to do stupid things to generate return."
- Lifestyle businesses are a put-down from the investor side. He says everyone would love one: you set your hours, keep the profits, and aren't up at night trying to turn it into a billion-dollar business.
- Timing and luck matter. A photo app with a dozen predecessors and a dozen successors won only because of timing (pre- or post-iPhone). Opinions (1999) couldn't become Yelp because too few local businesses and users were online.
- Network effects need density. Social networks failed in 1999 because not enough of your friends were online. Naval says path and product outcomes are hard to predict, and Twitter turned out differently than its founders intended.

## Raising money: terms, relationships, and choosing investors

- Naval says term sheets were a "black art." Investors see 10 to 20 a year and have institutional memory. A founder negotiating a first term sheet knows nothing, and the lawyers get more business from VCs than from founders.
- Old-style traps he lists:
  - Liquidation preference multiples, or getting paid back and also participating in the upside.
  - Anti-dilution handling of down rounds.
  - Board control and who can fire the CEO.
  - Investors pushing out the founder, installing a friendly CEO, then recapping in a down round until they own all the equity.
- His Venture Hacks blog explained the game theory of term sheets and led to Angelist. Terms are now far more standardized and often founder-friendly (convertible notes, founders with strong voting control).
- Contracts are negotiated for the worst case, not the best case. Game theory "boils down to power; there is no concept of fairness." Use power to get a fair outcome, or at least know the consequences if you lack it.
- Founders can overreach too. He cites uncapped notes and sees notes that cap conversion but return only the money if the company sells for a lot.
- Negotiation rule of thumb: "negotiate hard, get what you're fairly due, and at the end give something back." Money has karma. Strong hand means a strong outcome is deserved, but leave something on the table.
- Compound interest: "all the returns in life come from compound interest," meaning the length and strength of relationships. Examples: the PayPal mafia, marriage, Warren Buffett.
- Spotting short-term players: ask whether you like them, whether you trust them, and whether you'll be doing business with them in a decade. Check references and how considered they are. Trust shows once "the money hits the table."
- He has dropped investments because a founder fought for a tiny piece, which signaled over-optimizing for the moment.
- Iterated prisoner's dilemma: cooperation pays when you'll play again. Newcomers in a hot market are tempted to cheat for a $20M to $50M win. He says people with the wrong motives win "probably more than half the time," but you only have to get it right once.
- On choosing investors: "go for whoever trusts you, likes you and will support you over who you think is going to add value." Value-add is now available from many places. Good advisers are cheap; he asks whether you'd pay $2M in valuation for it, when advisers can be paid in stock.
- Brand reflects how nice a VC is once they have control. If you raise a lot, you will lose board control, and if you hit a hiccup you must get a bridge from insiders.
- Mix and match instead of one bundle: advice and network from the incubator, a first big bet from a prolific seed investor, then one or two angels as sherpas or board members. That gives you a counterweight when the VC arrives. He notes incubators "unionized" entrepreneurs.
- He estimates one founder spends about half their time over the company's life on investor issues: pitching, keeping them happy, searching for the next one, or negotiating.
- Online fundraising is more objective than offline. Investors judge metrics, product quality, who else is in the round (social proof), and famous advisers. If you can only tell instead of show, it works less well. If you can show, you reach thousands of investors at once and can run a competitive round.

## How Angelist evolved and its Craigslist model

- Naval says the vision evolved daily. They started with fundraising intros, then tried Series A (done dozens of times but a higher bar and relationship business), then small business equity (failed because there are few angels outside tech), then going global (only works in a few towns; China is a black box).
- The model is Craigslist: connect people to services, "charge for very, very few of them," and keep it seamless and no-hassle. The name was no accident.
- Talent product: launched a couple of months before this talk and "quite a bit bigger" than fundraising by volume. About 300 startup-investor intros last week, and roughly three times that in talent. 2,000 startups are recruiting (Quora, Kickstarter, Path, Khan Academy, Pocket, Climate Corp, Yelp). About 250 vetted candidates are added each week. Spread mainly by word of mouth plus one email to the existing user base.
- Talent rules: no recruiters, transparency on equity and salary ranges (companies that refused were removed), and candidates talk to a founder or senior person, not an HR screen. Recruiters charge $25K to $30K per placement. Angelist paid one $25K to build the system that routes around recruiters. It didn't charge yet and planned a model where small companies could pay in equity or notes, far below market.
- Pricing principles:
  - Never charge startups and investors for meeting each other, to avoid adverse selection.
  - Never knowingly screw over startups ("don't be evil").
  - Startups can't pay, so find someone else in the ecosystem who will: big companies recruiting, jobs and real estate analogues, vendors who want to reach startups at funding time (such as payments companies), and out-of-market capital.
  - Investor fees of "$1,000 a year" are "not even worth it."
- Network is an information good: "doesn't cost much to give away." Naval plans to make it widely available and charge cost-plus, far below other options. Services embedded include lawyers, press, M&A groups (over 30 actively use it, including Square, Yahoo, Google, Groupon), PR, back office, and early customers.
- Docs: free closings on Series Seed docs (Ted Wang's), with law firms such as Wilson Sonsini and Cooley doing the closing online for free. It was a loss leader for them. Cost came from negotiation over pet clauses. Online, changes cost $20,000, so social pressure standardizes. Standardized docs removed "expensive lawyers" and weeks of negotiation.
- Incubator integration: sent 500 Startups 600 companies in two weeks with structured, ranked data. Plans for Angel Pad and Techstars Boston the next day and dozens of incubators by Q1. Some VC funds use it to find initial $100K seed checks. All processing queues are open to everyone, with no preferential access.
- Adverse-selection rebuttal: Uber, Pinterest, Branch, Rally, Kaggle, Recurly, and Flashsoft were on Angelist. About 2,000 companies on the done-deals page raised over $1.3B trailing, probably closer to $2B. Out-of-market VCs (such as a Brussels VC with three deals) say they wouldn't be in business without it. One company chose a London term sheet over a local one.
- VC friendliness: top-tier VCs like it best because they are the bottleneck and it lets them view more deals efficiently. They have embedded associates who sit in on Tuesday queue reviews.

## Deal flow, access, and what investors actually fund

- Naval says "proprietary deal flow is dead." In 2007 about 90% was proprietary and closed; he estimated under 50% and falling. Drivers: lower capital needs, platforms like Angelist and YC, and the ability to launch a product first and raise later, so there is less to hide.
- Founders who stay proprietary "are self-limiting the market for their shares." Older entrepreneurs do this; younger ones know it's open and transparent. There are dozens (maybe over 100) of great VC firms and thousands of great angels you can't reach by knocking on doors.
- What VCs still have is proprietary access: the ability to win a hot deal. That comes from brand, access, and know-how. Top brands such as Andreessen or Sequoia can outmuscle anyone. New brands (super angels, Google Ventures, Yuri Milner, YC, True Ventures, SV Angel) were mostly built on being entrepreneur-friendly.
- Example: SV Angel let a founder keep the last $75,000 in a shutting-down company so he could ride out until his next job.
- Super angel definition: willing to write small checks, decide quickly, and forgo normal control terms such as a board seat. He sees them becoming the next branded Series A firms, with seed extensions that look like old Series A amounts.
- Investor-facing advice: what investors fund is teams, so fill out team bios. The four things investors look at: traction, a unique product, a stellar team, or social proof. Listing early has no downside, but with 100,000 profiles you won't get noticed without something spectacular or a promotion.
- Social proof: someone worth listening to commits their time (adviser, key hire like a Rails core contributor) or money.
- Traction: "quantitative evidence of customer demand." Enterprise: two or three paying early customers. Consumer: possibly hundreds of thousands of users. Mobile: download rate. The bar is a moving target. Naval's examples: two years earlier a daily-deals startup funded pre-traction; 18 months earlier none funded at any traction; 12 months earlier a mobile app with 10,000 downloads funded, but now it takes a few hundred thousand and rapid adoption. Use trending, featured, and done-deals lists to see the current bar.
- Incubators outside hubs are hard because funding markets develop backwards (public markets first, then late stage, then earlier). Naval says some incubators, such as Startmate (Australia) and Founder Fuel (Montreal), skim top local talent and bridge them to US investors.

## YC's model and how Naval ran the Jobs Act campaign

- Why YC worked, per Naval:
  - Paul Graham's reach and writing pulled in talent that otherwise had no path into Silicon Valley.
  - It took a much smaller stake than older idea-lab models (which took 30 to 40%) and the company, not the incubator, had the idea.
  - It overlaid a college template: roughly a 3-month semester, a project to ship, weekly meetings, office hours, and a demo day.
  - Economics are great with minimal capital: a hit like Dropbox is enough, and the founder doesn't need to raise from LPs repeatedly. It overturned the VC rule that more money raised means more made.
- Other incubators are specializing (Angel Pad for ex-Googlers, Lemnos for hardware) and by geography. He expects "MITs and Caltechs" next to YC.
- Jobs Act (securities law) campaign:
  - Securities laws date from 1934. Much of what happens at demo days was technically illegal. Angelist could not offer docs without being a broker-dealer.
  - They hired "gumshoe lobbyists" on a contingency success fee, found through friends and Twitter. He says "all the lobbyist can do is open a door; you still have to make the pitch."
  - The pitch to Congress: Angelist was spreading investment around the country in every state, so they could show investors and startups in each district willing to stand up.
  - He spent about 6 months calling in favors: calling congressmen, telling them about raising or investing through Angelist. He estimated about 100 favors, and a random five came together. He started believing there was a 10% chance of success and judged expected value worth it.
  - Approach it like a startup: success fees, and when told a Word-document petition was enough, they built a viral Twitter petition in a 36-hour all-nighter. It had login with Angelist or Twitter, photos and bios, and got 5,000 signatures from investors and entrepreneurs, sliced by location, influence, investments, and senator. A union petition had 3,600. The data could say "I see that guy; he's one of your big campaign donors."
  - Branding: the bill was a bundle named the "Jobs Act." "What congressperson can vote against something called the Jobs Act?" He advises rebranding immigration reform; mixing skilled and unskilled is "the wrong brand."
- Securities law warnings: don't get ahead of securities law. Raising from unaccredited investors or handling money through an unlicensed broker-dealer can void the entire transaction, give investors rescission rights, and carry personal liability. Uber, Lyft, and Airbnb can route around local laws because local rules are easier to change once people are galvanized. Securities laws are "radioactive." The right order is to get the law changed first, then act.
- Crowdfunding experiment: small-dollar online investments via SecondMarket (a registered broker-dealer), accredited investors only, with suitability and Patriot Act checks. He called it a test.

## Saying no, meetings, and networking

- Naval's "mediocre superpower": saying no without feeling guilty. Most startups aren't ready to be funded, can't all be met, and require triage.
- Meeting policy: he owns the domain idontdocoffee.com. The ladder is email first (so you can help 30 companies instead of one), then phone, then Skype, then a meeting, bundling meetings (four in an hour) and staying efficient. He cites Harj Taggar of YC: shortening office hours made them more efficient, because there's no time for pleasantries.
- Advice to startups: avoid meetings unless necessary. "Networking is overrated." Instead, "go do something great and your network will instantly emerge." With a great product or customer base he said "we will get you funded." Meet people to recruit or learn from smart people, but only if you have something to offer.
- Lawyers: the best act as cheap advisers, and he urges founders to seek founder-friendly ones. Names he gave: Buddy Arnheim (Perkins Coie), Jorge del Calvo (Pillsbury), Eric Benisek (Venture Law Group), and Silicon Legal Strategy. He notes lawyers must brand and prove their value more than investors, since investors can "write you a check." He thinks no startup could operate if it followed every law, but you still need a lawyer.
- On patents: he hasn't been hit by patent trolls. He suggests the bar of "obvious to those skilled in the art" be enforced, software and business-model patents be removed or held to a higher bar, terms be shortened from 17 years, and an open expert-panel review be used.
