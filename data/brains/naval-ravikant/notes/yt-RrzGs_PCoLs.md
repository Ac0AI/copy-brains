## Choosing where to work: Bay Area, industry, then who you work with

- Naval says "earn with your mind, not your time" does not mean working less. Hours are capped (168 a week), so the lever is direction. Life compounds, so the big decisions have to be right.
- He treats the Bay Area and tech as "cheat codes". It is the highest concentration of high-IQ, motivated people. He compares it to Athens, Rome, industrial-era Manchester or London, and Paris, where the best people gathered.
- After place and industry, the next decisions are what to work on and who to work with.
  - What to work on: a natural inclination, a deep field, and a secret or specific knowledge (the Peter Thiel sense).
  - Who to work with: the absolute best people.
- He says the biggest early shortcoming is staying limited to whoever happens to be nearby. "Life is a search function", and curating who you work with never stops.
- He dislikes giving advice ("like people telling you their winning lottery ticket numbers"). He does see two consistent themes from successful older people:
  - Who they worked with was the most important factor.
  - They enjoyed the work, because "if you don't enjoy it you're not going to be good".
- These principles are hard in practice because they mean rejecting people and walking away from good situations toward great ones.

## Naval's motivations over time and what actually worked

- Naval says his bar for who he worked with and whether he enjoyed the work was too low early on.
- His first founder attempts were driven by wanting the "founder" label, not by the company or the people.
- Later he was driven by money and power. He calls these fundamental drivers that nobody wants to admit, and not a great motivation either.
- His best outcomes came from following genuine curiosity. Asking "what's behind this?" repeatedly produced insight, which led to good investments, startups and outcomes.
- Now he doesn't need money or status. He asks:
  - What product does he want to exist?
  - What beautiful thing wouldn't exist without his effort?
  - Which people does he want to spend all day with?
- On meeting material needs: he says that once you don't have to hustle you get bored. Meditating in a corner gets boring, and hedonism is "a death trap". The answer he gives is self-actualization through creating: "it's better to create than to consume".
- He argues that talented, curious, hardworking, high-integrity people he met in his 20s all became successful 20-30 years later, "without exception". He wishes he had invested in or stayed close to every one of them.

## Networking and finding collaborators through work

- Naval doesn't believe in "empty networking" (mingling, name tags, meeting everyone). Be discriminating and look for earned respect: talent, curiosity, good nature, low ego, high drive and vision.
- Don't hunt for the one future star (the "Dylan Field problem"). That is impossible. Look for like-minded people you learn from, enjoy, and want to stay in touch with.
- He says the best way to match is through work, not talking. Hackathons and small projects are good.
- In any group project, one person usually carries the load. Hopefully that's you. If not, find the person who is number one and make yourself useful to them. "It'll pay off. It just takes a long time."
- His new company, the Impossible Computer Company, was assembled this way.
  - He has worked with one person since they met 20 years ago, and another since his last company about four years ago.
  - His co-founder has strong technical taste. He finds weird, idiosyncratic products, papers or code, understands them deeply, then reaches out. He compliments the creator, offers help, asks good questions, and contributes to the code. Over time this has surrounded him with brilliant, artistic people.
  - The team members see themselves as creators, not as "engineer" or "AI person". They pick up whatever skill is needed, and AI makes that easier. He calls AI an incredible learning engine for autodidacts.

## How Naval runs his company now

- The team is under 20 people, based in person in SF. A few trusted, long-known people are remote.
- His rule is not to ask anyone to do what he wouldn't do. He wants:
  - The office in a location he likes.
  - Nice private offices, not cubicles or crammed desks. He calls cramped desks inhuman and says hustle-culture desk grinding is mostly "pretend work".
  - Good pay, lots of stock and high salaries.
  - People always working or thinking about work, the same bar he holds himself to.
- Being older, he says, lets him be more idiosyncratic and do things his way, which he always wanted but lacked the confidence for.
- Unpopular CEO decisions:
  - Fire much faster.
  - Hire much more slowly and resist hiring.
  - "Pay twice as much but expect 10 times the output". He means real output, not hours, because creative domains reward the right decision "infinitely" more.
- On Keith Rabois's barrels versus bullets: barrels decide direction and when to pull the trigger, and bullets get aimed. Big companies split management from execution. Naval says in a small company everyone must be a barrel.
  - They hired a 19-year-old as a full peer: no one tells him what to do, same pay, same output expectation.
  - His co-founder is 23, started working at 15, and has the same stock and authority as Naval.
- On decisions and disputes:
  - No real argument has happened yet, which he admits may be a honeymoon.
  - The person doing the job, especially technical work, usually has the best insight. Disputes get resolved through execution, the way Elon Musk does it ("I'm going to sleep underneath my desk, I'm taking over your job").
  - For fuzzy topics like the product name, he picks the three people with the best taste and has them vote. He warns that too many "marketing droids" make a company go in circles.

## When to quit, and starting deep tech

- On burnout, Naval rejects the idea that it is temporary and that a two-month break will bring you back better. He says burnout means you no longer enjoy the work and should probably move on.
- He has seen people work around the clock without burning out when they enjoy it and see clear progress in product or deployment. People "burn out" when good people leave and direction is unclear.
- Founders increasingly start deep tech later: Elon (Zip2, X/PayPal, then SpaceX, Tesla, SolarCity), Kyle Vogt (Cruise, then robots), Sam Altman (Loopt, then OpenAI). Naval started in hardware with linear optical amplifiers and is now back in hardware.
- Reasons he gives: software feels saturated and AI makes it easy, SpaceX and OpenAI inspire people, and supply chains make hardware easier than before.
- Upsides of deep tech: a steep learning curve, less competition, and possibly more meaningful work. It also gives a better moat in the age of AI.
- Downsides:
  - It is much harder and the odds of success are lower.
  - Some great-looking products left founders and investors with nothing, because they needed too much money and got wiped out.
  - Products get cloned in China or Korea within a year or two, so there is no way to monetize.
- His advice: go where you are most inspired. If you go deep tech, start early, because failures are more forgivable (he says before about 25), the learning is huge, and you meet hardware contacts for later.

## Taste, strengths and leverage as moats

- Naval says design is always a moat. Good design needs taste and a good eye.
- AI will fill the world with average, then with extreme, until every permutation exists (he cites Borges's "Library of Babel"). The core task then becomes editing, selection and curation, so develop judgment and taste:
  - Learn what makes things great.
  - Don't dislike something just because someone else made it.
  - Pair taste with hard work to build.
- His own taste came from a youth of reading, including poetry. Twitter's 140 characters forced him to choose words like poetry, and the taste paid off in a way he couldn't have predicted.
- On finding your strength: "I want to work with people better than me" is not enough, because you must bring something unique. Almost everyone is good at an intersection of two or three things (Scott Adams's skill stack).
  - A signal: it feels like play to you, looks like work to others, and impresses them.
  - Don't cover weaknesses. Ally with people strong in them and double down on your strengths.
- On leverage: leverage forms went from labor to capital to media, then code ("permissionless" leverage), and now AI, agents, robots, supply chains and 3D printing.
  - One-person billion-dollar companies already exist: Notch with Minecraft (sold to Microsoft for $2 billion) and Satoshi's Bitcoin.
  - Returns will be non-linear and society isn't built for it. He says it's not the rich getting richer but "the smart and leveraged".
  - Earning power is downstream of knowledge creation and the ability to leverage it.

## Education: what to study and which degrees are worth it

- Naval says the most useful parts of college were the really hard things, like writing a compiler, which gave him confidence that no computer science problem was beyond him. He wishes he had stuck with physics and math to get the same confidence.
- Study low-level systems: networking, computer architecture, semiconductors, chips, physics. Understanding the layers of abstraction underneath software is useful, and he calls "you don't need to study software engineering" nonsense.
- People who understand AI and software well are paid more than ever. Pay for software, electrical and industrial engineers in the Bay Area keeps rising.
- He regrets not making enough connections with smart people at school. He was very introverted and didn't stay in touch with the talented ones, and thinks he could have met his current co-founder there.
- Should you finish school? "Probably", if the degree gets tested by nature or free markets, meaning feedback from reality (building something that works, compiling code that executes, selling something people buy).
- He is skeptical of degrees where feedback comes mainly from graders or award committees, such as social sciences. He calls this "not real" and says you may only be having a good time.
- He recommends engineering for the students there and says to consider switching if you aren't studying it.
