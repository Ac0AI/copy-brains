## Naval treats reading as a daily habit, not a book count

- Naval reads one to two hours a day. He says this puts him in the top 0.01% of readers and "alone accounts for any material success" he has had.
- He argues the habit matters more than what you read, the same way exercise works. The best book is the one you are excited to read every day.
- He started with comics, mysteries and sci-fi, then climbed to science, math and philosophy. His point is that nobody should forbid "junk food" reading, because taste matures on its own.
- He spends heavily on books and treats it as an investment. He buys roughly ten times more than he finishes, and says a $10-20 book can change your life.

## Treat books like blogs: skim, skip, and reread the great ones

- After the internet, Naval started handling books the way he handles a blog archive. He skims many, jumps around, and reads only the parts that matter right now, with no guilt about finishing.
- Most non-fiction has one idea padded with examples. Once he has the gist he puts the book down. He avoids the self-help category for this reason.
- If a book does not grab him in the first chapter, he drops it or skips ahead. "I don't believe in delayed gratification" with reading, because there are endless good books.
- He drops a book if the author states things that are plainly false, since he cannot separate truth from fiction afterward.
- He quotes a tweet from Ilya Service: don't read everything, reread the 100 great books over and over. He rereads often, and says deja vu midway through a book means you were ready for it.
- He reads original sources (Adam Smith, Darwin, Franklin, Watson and Crick) rather than the bestseller that regurgitates them. Bestsellers are "designed for social approval", while returns come from leaving the herd.
- He does not take notes. He highlights lightly. His "note-taking" is Twitter: distilling an insight into an aphorism.

## Decision-making: mental models, avoiding errors, and rigging the downside

- Naval says decision quality compounds with leverage. Being right 80% instead of 70% of the time is worth hundreds of times more when managing capital or large teams.
- He thinks the brain is a memory-prediction machine, and "X happened before so X will happen" is a weak method. He wants principles and mental models instead (evolution, game theory, Charlie Munger, Nassim Taleb, Franklin, complexity theory).
- Complexity theory taught him the limits of prediction. So he tries to eliminate what will not work rather than pick winners. Success is "about not making mistakes".
- He prefers systems over goals and does not use checklists, which he thinks suit pilots and surgeons more than investors.
- As an investor, he wants asymmetric bets. You lose 1x on the downside and can make 10,000x on the upside.
- His portfolio approach: see 10,000 companies, pick about 500 with a shot at being huge, and keep the option to double down on the few winners. He does not pick ten and go all-in, because he doubts his ability to make individual calls.
- His goal is to be successful in 999 of 1,000 universes, not to be the most successful person.
- He says to stop emulating others. Each person has specific knowledge and desires no one else has. Find the work and people that need you most.

## Evaluating people: integrity is the hard one

- He uses Buffett's three traits: intelligence, energy, integrity. Integrity is hardest to read.
- Test it over the long term, and in the short term by watching how someone treats others. Someone who cheats a third party while saying "but you're my friend" will eventually cheat you.
- High-integrity people have an internal compass, so they avoid unfair deals because it would soil their view of themselves. They are easy to negotiate with and give more than they need to.
- Red flags: someone talks too much about how honest they are, or pushes a deal slightly too hard. He recommends the book "The Art of Manipulation" (written by someone who spent time with con men).
- He lets nobody close without good values: "The closer you want to get to me, the better your values have to be." His trainer's line: "Easy choices, hard life. Hard choices, easy life."
- His foundational values include honesty (say what you think, no filtering), no short-term dealing, only peer relationships, and avoiding anger. He pairs honesty with Buffett's rule: criticize generally, praise specifically.

## Who to back as a founder, and why patience pays

- The best founders think long-term. They treat early decisions as foundation bricks for a skyscraper. They know the space deeply, understand how hard it is, and commit anyway.
- Passion and vision are not enough. He cites Steve Case: vision without execution is a hallucination. Luck and timing also matter, so he screens out failures (short-term thinkers, shallow knowledge) rather than predicting winners.
- Ideas are not new. The value is in idea plus execution plus passion. He points to Steve Jobs's high design bar, his grasp of manufacturing tolerances, and a $1 Apple salary.
- You need roughly 10 years minimum for a good outcome, so choose something you can commit to and enjoy.
- Late lesson: nearly everyone he met 20 years ago who seemed capable and dedicated became very successful. It just took longer than anyone wanted. Be patient and keep relationships long-term.
- Money matters less than people assume. He says he is not in it for the money but to create something new and congruent with his morals.

## Habits and priorities: the morning workout

- Naval's biggest positive change was a daily morning workout. He says "I don't have time" really means "it's not a priority".
- He made his own health priority number one (physical, then mental, then spiritual, then family), above work. He works out first, however long it takes, before starting his day.
- The workout also checkpoints his drinking. A hangover makes the same morning routine harder, so he stays in at night and cut back to mostly red wine, which self-limits.
- He also dropped caffeine and approximates a paleo diet without beating himself up.
- He rejects the claim that you cannot break habits, only replace them. He says strong motivation can break them, though it takes work.
- His fix for overcommitting: a "two-factor authentication for the calendar." Write down any commitment and recheck it 48 hours later, or only commit to what you would do right now. He cites Derek Sivers's "hell yes or no".

## Micro over macro, and why education is obsolete

- He no longer trusts macroeconomics, which makes no falsifiable predictions and is easily cherry-picked. Microeconomics and game theory (supply and demand, labor versus capital, tit for tat) are essential for business.
- The same logic applies to charity, environmentalism and world-changing: start with yourself, family and neighbors.
- He drops labels like "libertarian" because identities lock you into packaged beliefs, and he distrusts any set of beliefs that line up neatly.
- He calls the singularity "religion for nerds." He sees no real progress toward general AI (only data-driven narrow AI) and says hope for the future pulls you out of the present.
- Schools exist as daycare and a legacy of medieval institutions. The internet makes learning tools abundant, while the desire to learn is scarce.
- He criticizes memorization and the one-pace model, where missing one math rung leaves you memorizing calculus without understanding it. He wants basics learned deeply and advanced work only in what you love.
- He says to teach what schools skip: nutrition, cooking, relationships, happiness, meditation, building technology.
- A project idea: a cheap, rugged Android tablet with adaptive learning apps, inspired by an account of kids teaching themselves from unopened tablets in a Pakistani village. He says you change a system by building something new, not reworking the old one.

## Happiness, the mind, and doing less emotionally

- Naval defines happiness as a default state when nothing feels missing, not as positive thoughts. Every positive thought implies its negative.
- The common mistake is expecting external circumstances to make you happy ("I'll be happy when I get that thing"). He uses his own anticipation of a new car as an example.
- He treats the mind as a tool, not a master. His practice is to run his brain "in debug mode," watching thoughts and asking whether a problem needs solving right now.
- Looking back on his decisions, his advice to his younger self: do the same things with less emotion and less anger, and take a much longer-term view.
- Jealousy faded when he asked whether he would swap places wholesale with the person, not just take their body or money.
- Seeing reality clearly requires a small ego. He admits business problems publicly to co-founders and colleagues so he cannot hide them from himself, and he separates what he wishes from what is true (he correctly predicted a Trump or Bernie win despite not wanting it).
- He calls his philosophy "rational Buddhism": keep what he can verify (meditation, awareness, evolution as the binding principle), and discard unverifiable claims like past lives or chakras.
- On meaning, he gives three answers: it is personal, there is no intrinsic meaning so you create your own, and in physics life locally reverses entropy while accelerating it globally.
