## Specific knowledge is the part of your pay that training cannot reproduce

- Naval frames earning money as three pieces: getting paid at scale (leverage), being the one who gets paid (accountability), and being paid instead of somebody else (specific knowledge).
- He calls specific knowledge the hardest idea in his tweetstorm and the one people confuse most.
- His core claim: "everything can be learned," but not everything can be taught, and "the most interesting things cannot be taught."
- Specific knowledge tends to come from one of four places:
  - Innate traits in your DNA.
  - Childhood soft skills that are very hard to teach later.
  - Brand new fields where nobody else knows how yet.
  - On-the-job training that builds judgment by pattern matching in complex environments.
- His examples of domain judgment: investing, running a fleet of trucks, weather forecasting.

## Why anything you can be trained for pays only minimum wage

- If a class can teach the skill, others can be trained too, and then people can be mass-produced, programmed into computers, and eventually into robots.
- Naval's conclusion: a trainable person is "extremely replaceable," so the pay falls to the minimum needed to attract the many other people who could be trained.
- Returns on trainable skills "devolve into your cost of training plus the return on investment on that training."
- Training and schooling still matter, because you need them to capitalize on specific knowledge. But the part the market pays you for is the specific knowledge, not the training.

## Find specific knowledge through curiosity and talent, not hot fields

- Specific knowledge comes from your innate talents, genuine curiosity and passion. It does not come from "going to school for whatever is the hottest job" or entering whatever field investors say is hottest.
- Example: a psychology degree is leverage for someone who is already a formidable salesperson. For a lifelong introvert who was never good at sales, psychology will not make them great at it.
- It often sits at the edge of knowledge, in things just being figured out or very hard to figure out.
- After roughly age 20 to 22, he says you almost don't get to choose your specific knowledge. You look at what you have already built and build on top of it.

## Why you must be 100% into it: leverage and compounding punish the second best

- "If you're not 100% into it somebody else who is 100% into it will outperform you," and not by a little, because ideas are a domain where compound interest and leverage apply.
- His numeric illustration: at 1,000 times leverage, someone right 90% of the time versus someone right 80% of the time gets paid "hundreds of times more by the market."
- This is why genuine curiosity matters so much. It is what gets you to be correct more often than everyone else in the field.

## Your specific knowledge feels like play to you and looks like work to others

- Specific knowledge is usually found by observation, not reasoning. You look back on your own life and see what you are actually good at.
- Naval's own case: he wanted to be a scientist and built his value system around scientists, whom he sees as the top of the production chain for humanity. But what he was uniquely good at was making money, tinkering with technology, and selling and explaining ideas.
- His specific skills, in his words:
  - Sales skills.
  - Analytical skills about how to make money.
  - Absorbing data, obsessing over it and breaking it down.
  - Tinkering with technology.
- "All of this stuff feels like play to me, but it looks like work to others."
- Test: if you are not already good at something, or not really into it, it may not be your thing. Focus on what you are really into.

## Other people often spot your specific knowledge before you do

- Naval's mother identified his real specific knowledge at about 15 or 16. When he said he would be an astrophysicist, she said from the kitchen, "No, you're going to go into business."
- She had seen him critique local pizza parlors as they walked down the street: why they sold slices a certain way, with certain toppings, and why the ordering process should be different.
- That business-curious mind combined with his obsession with science and led him to technology businesses.
- His takeaway: your specific knowledge is "observed and often observed by other people who know you well and revealed in situations rather than something that you come up with."
