## The "big five" B2B SaaS marketing strategies and how Rob picks among them

Rob has invested in more than 230 SaaS companies. He says five B2B SaaS marketing strategies work "over and over and over again":
- SEO (search beyond Google)
- Pay-per-click advertising
- Cold outreach
- Integration marketing (plus partnerships)
- Content marketing

AI changes how each one works, but it does not remove the fundamentals. The full SaaS Playbook lists 20 B2B SaaS marketing approaches. The other 15 are used less often or depend on market and annual contract value (ACV). The playbook is the basis for the video.

## SEO: rank in the search engines where your customer actually searches

- Rob says Google is one of many search engines. YouTube is number two. Amazon is number three or four, depending on the source.
- Other search engines where an ideal customer may be searching, often with easier ranking than Google:
  - iOS App Store
  - WordPress plugin repository
  - Reddit
  - Wikipedia
  - Shopify App Store
  - Adobe App Store
  - Chrome Web Store
- Examples:
  - At Drip, his last startup, the team deliberately built two WordPress plugins and ranked them for email marketing terms in the WordPress plugin repository.
  - Tiny Seed founders build Chrome plugins to rank in the Chrome Web Store for terms that fit their ICP. The plugin extends the app's functionality and is not a random side project.
  - He has seen founders write Kindle or physical books to rank in Amazon search.
- Key insight: find where your ideal customer is searching. Many of those places are easier to rank in than Google.
- Impact of AI:
  - Anyone can now build a WordPress plugin with Claude Code in a few hours, which could crowd the repo.
  - AEO (answer engine optimization, also called AI SEO or GEO) is now a thing. LLMs like ChatGPT and Perplexity pull content to answer queries directly.
  - The Google SEO principles still work for AEO. Quality content that directly answers questions still wins. Structured content and clear answers matter more than ever.
  - Google still processes hundreds of times more queries than ChatGPT. He says traditional SEO "is not dead and will not be anytime soon."

## Pay-per-click ads: price point and payback period decide whether they work

- PPC is the fastest way to get traffic. It is also the fastest way to burn budget if you don't know what you're doing.
- Major platforms:
  - Google
  - Facebook
  - Instagram
  - YouTube
  - Amazon
  - Capterra
  - LinkedIn
  - Marketplaces with their own ads, such as the Shopify App Store
- Ads work when you:
  - need speed
  - have budget to test and refine
  - can measure customer acquisition cost (CAC) against lifetime value (LTV)
  - charge enough per month for a reasonable payback period
- Payback rule for bootstrappers:
  - They usually need a 3-6 month payback period.
  - A one-year payback only works with a lot of money in the bank or venture backing.
  - At $20 a month, that implies a maximum of $60-$120 to acquire a customer. He says you are unlikely to find an ad network that cheap today.
- Price rule of thumb:
  - He would not consider ads below roughly $40-$50 a month unless he could find extremely cheap clicks of 10, 20 or 30 cents that also convert at a workable rate.
  - He is not saying $10-$20 a month can't work, but he hasn't seen costs in a long time that allow break-even at those prices.
  - B2C plays at $4, $10 or $15 a month are limited by low average revenue per account and generally can't run ads.
- Upside: if ads work, they are "like a spigot you can just turn on and off." He grew more than one SaaS company almost purely on ads and added tens of thousands of dollars of MRR. He did it by investing money and time, carefully testing and tweaking creative, copy and targeting. He says ads are difficult with SaaS but not impossible.
- Impact of AI:
  - AI generates ad copy and more creatives to split test faster and cheaper.
  - Everyone has the same tools, so competition gets noisier.
  - Targeting, the offer, the landing page and human oversight matter more than clever AI copy. "AI won't save you from a lack of strategy."
  - New AI-native ad platforms are emerging. ChatGPT ads are coming, and maybe Perplexity. He expects products that keep prices low or offer free tools to monetize through them.

## Cold outreach: the signal is timing plus fit

- Rob says most cold outreach deserves its bad reputation, but when it works, "it really works."
- What separates outreach that converts from outreach that is ignored is the signal. The signal is the prospect's timing plus the fit of your tool. You need businesses that need what you built and a sign that they need it now.
- Drip example:
  - Infusionsoft (now Keap) was a big competitor, and its customers signed one-year contracts.
  - Drip kept an ongoing list of new Infusionsoft customers and contacted them about eight months into the contract, when renewal was coming up.
  - The message asked whether they wanted to explore other options. In many cases they did.
- Key insight: "cold outreach without a signal is spam and cold outreach with a signal is helpful."
- Impact of AI:
  - AI handles personalization at scale: custom intros, prospect analysis and automatic sequences.
  - Everyone has the same tools, so the signal matters more than ever. "AI can personalize, but it can't create timing."
  - Gmail and Microsoft now reject non-compliant bulk email, so deliverability infrastructure matters more.

## Integration marketing and partnerships: build only when both sides promote

- Rob says many founders sleep on this. It improves the product and brings in customers, and when it works it produces leads for years.
- How to find opportunities: ask what steps happen before and after someone uses your product.
  - Example for a podcast show-notes app: before it, recording, editing and inviting guests; after it, posting to WordPress or uploading to YouTube or Spotify.
  - Unless you will build those features yourself, find tools already doing the before and after steps. Ask them to integrate and to promote the integration.
- Rule: "Don't build the integration unless both sides agree to promote it."
- The seven promotion options he offered partners at his last startup:
  1. Blog post
  2. Email to both partners' lists
  3. Tweet or social post
  4. Knowledge base article
  5. In-app mention
  6. Webinar
  7. Listing in an add-on marketplace
- His pitch was to do all seven as long as the partner does all seven, and otherwise to match whichever ones they do.
- Results and process at his last SaaS:
  - The team built roughly 30-35 integrations in about 18 months. Integration marketing was a key growth driver.
  - Start with an MVP "1.0" integration, such as customers pasting in an API key instead of a full OAuth build.
  - Launch, co-promote and see whether it drives leads and usage.
  - With a couple thousand customers, some integrations had only a handful of users. Those were not built out further.
  - Track which integrations are popular and drive new customers, then build V2.0 and V3.0 versions. Add OAuth, make setup easier and support more actions.
- Partnerships are the same idea with no code written. It is reciprocal promotion to each other's audiences using one or more of the seven options, recommending each other's tool.
- Partner assets (articles, forum posts, knowledge base articles) keep converting long after launch.
- Impact of AI:
  - Integrations are technically easier to build, with faster development and better documentation.
  - MCP (Model Context Protocol) is emerging as a standard for connecting AI agents to APIs. Vendors increasingly ship MCP endpoints, much as REST APIs spread in the 2010s. He says MCP-native is expected to be core product functionality for many launches by the end of 2026.
  - The marketing side (partnerships, co-promotion, relationships) "is still a human game" that the founder must do.

## Content marketing: virality versus audience building

- Content is not only blog posts. It can be a book, a podcast, a YouTube channel or a free course. The key question is whether you are building for virality or for an audience over time, which he calls "two very different games."
- Content often pairs with SEO but can also work purely on people sharing it.
- Virality: aim for the top of Hacker News, Reddit or other social news sites to drive visitors who convert to trials.
- Audience building: slow, steady growth over time.
- Warning:
  - Building a media brand alongside a SaaS product is only for very well-funded companies or people exceptionally gifted at it.
  - He thinks only about 10%, at most 20%, of SaaS founders should do founder-led marketing with video, audio or YouTube.
- Execution: most founders produce content themselves because they lack money. Hire production help later as revenue grows.
- Impact of AI:
  - AI helps produce content faster: outlines, drafts and repurposing, such as turning a video into an essay or blog post.
  - The bar for standing out is rising sharply because of the "AI slop tsunami."
  - Original thinking, proprietary data and real expertise become more valuable. The human touch wins through original research, customer voices, and first-hand experience and opinions.
- Rob mentions a MicroConf talk on the five stages of customer awareness and how to match content to each stage.

## The three-factor framework: speed, cost and scalability

Rob uses this to choose which marketing approach to focus on. Price point changes everything.

1. **Speed:** how long until results arrive (weeks, months or years) and how long you can afford to wait. Early on, favor fast approaches, though they may not scale. As the company matures, add slower approaches.
2. **Cost:** hard dollars plus your time.
   - Early on, think in dollars rather than your time.
   - Higher ACV means more money to invest in marketing, and more approaches open up as you raise prices.
   - With a very low price point, only about four or five of the 20 playbook approaches are available.
   - At $30,000 a year, all 20 are available.
3. **Scalability:** whether you can reach more people by turning the dial.
   - Low scalability: one-time events like Product Hunt launches or Quora posts.
   - High scalability: SEO and pay-per-click ads.

Practical advice:
- Work one fast approach and one slow approach at the same time.
  - Fast only means you are always chasing leads.
  - Slow only means you starve while waiting for traction.
- A common pairing is cold outreach (fast) plus SEO (slow).
- You don't need dozens of approaches. SaaS companies growing to seven or eight figures can often do it on one or two channels that scale well.
- Find what's working and double down. Adding two or three new approaches scatters you too much.
