## Rob's view on bootstrapping vs. funding (Tiny Seed)

- Rob started Tiny Seed because he "wished I had been able to do this 10 years ago" while grinding on Hitail and Drip.
- Back then he worked a 40-hour day job plus about 20 hours a week of nights and weekends on his products, for years. It was hard to think about quality of life or growth when the question was "can I make the bills this month".
- He says funding is "just a tool": know what you're getting into and use it to grow the business.
- Funding doesn't have to be Tiny Seed. It can be a small raise, or being "mostly bootstrapped".
- He did not assume the guest would say "bootstrap only". He is comfortable with bootstrappers who take bootstrapper-friendly capital.
- He endorses the guest's view that financial breathing room makes it easier to be creative and to think clearly.

## Mission, vision and values must be communicated deliberately as the team grows

- Rob says many founders start companies partly to avoid mission, vision and values exercises. At past employers they were on a plaque and nobody cared.
- Once a team reaches a certain size, and especially as new people join, you can no longer pass on culture by osmosis. You have to do it deliberately.
- That can be a values statement or any document that captures "this is who we are, this is how we think about things".
- His example is frugality at a self-funded company. If that isn't written down, a hire from a big corporate HQ with a large budget will mismatch with how the company spends.
- He asks where the culture is written down when a company's founders are frugal but a new hire isn't.

## Afford the next hire: "How can I afford it?" instead of "I can't"

- Rob heard the delegation advice in his first few years of bootstrapping and thought: "this sounds great, but I don't have the budget to hire anybody".
- His answer, to his past self and to anyone thinking this now: ask "how can you afford it?" Carve out the monthly dollars for the next position only, not all the positions.
- Ways to fund that hire:
  - Launch a new product.
  - Increase pricing.
  - Add expansion revenue or premium support.
  - Run an annual special and sell a bunch of annual plans.
- He calls "I can't afford it" a trap: you come up with ideas that solve a problem, then find excuses not to implement them. The fix is to drop the excuse and ask "how can I".
- His reasoning is that the alternative, the founder doing everything, "is not sustainable".

## Founders hit growth "speed bumps", and solving them is the job

- Rob calls problems like not being able to take a vacation "speed bumps". They feel like roadblocks at the time, and you only meet them if you've grown the company.
- He says much of being an entrepreneur is "troubleshooting and creativity".
- The guest's concrete case: the majority of the team reported to the founders and he could not take a day off. The first fix was an executive assistant who handled admin work, ahead of any big hire. The guest then added a CTO, a head of customer support and a trainer, plus QA through internal promotions.

## Shifting customer base and listening to customers (as discussed with the guest)

- Rob asks how the guest spotted that the customer demographic changed after COVID, and how he got the team to believe it. The answer he gets is to use actual customer words: exports of webinar Q&A and support chats, plus support tickets that engineers and marketers read.
- Rob notes that a founder often sees the data first, and the team then has to be convinced with data.
- He also endorses the broader point that launching a bundled product is a way to solve more of a customer's problem: the guest described customers stitching together landing pages, carts, Zoom and membership software, and Rob agreed that more tools will bundle functionality.
