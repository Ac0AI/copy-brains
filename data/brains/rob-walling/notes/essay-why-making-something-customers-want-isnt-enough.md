## Rob's fourth ingredient: customers must pay more than they cost to acquire

- Rob extends Paul Graham's three startup requirements (good people, something customers want, spend as little as possible) with a fourth: "Customers must give you more money than what you spend to acquire them."
- Building something customers want is necessary but not sufficient. If it is your only success metric, "you may be unpleasantly surprised when you find out you can't acquire a new customer for less than $250 when your LTV is only $150."
- Customer lifetime value (LTV) is the revenue a customer generates over the whole relationship. Acquisition cost has to stay below it.
- You cannot know in advance what acquisition will cost unless you know how you will do it. Founders usually think about technology, customer conversations and market size, but rarely about how they will reach prospects and what that costs. The answer is often scary or hard to measure.
- Rob's advice is to estimate LTV and cost per acquisition (CPA) before launching. Better, try the marketing approaches yourself "before writing a single line of code."
- Market sizing: use top-down if you are seeking funding, bottom-up if you are bootstrapping.

## How to calculate LTV

- One-time purchase product: take a conservative LTV equal to the purchase price minus payment processing fees. Example: DotNetInvoice sells for $329. Add-ons, upsells and maintenance plans give only a small bump unless they convert well.
- Recurring product: you need the price point and churn rate for each plan. Formula: LTV = price / churn.
- Worked example: $19 per month at 8% monthly churn gives LTV = $19 / 0.08 = $237.50.
- Churn varies by company stage and industry. Early on, expect monthly churn of roughly 5-10%. Some larger SaaS firms get below 1% per month.
- Of the three numbers that decide profitability (LTV, cost per click, conversion rate), you can only measure one before you start marketing. You make educated guesses at the other two.

## Google AdWords math: why paid search often fails

- Rob's example: bidding on "invoicing software" costs about $4 per click to rank in the top 3.
- At 1% conversion, you need 100 clicks per sale, so CPA is $400. At 0.5% conversion, CPA is $800.
- Against the $237.50 LTV above, AdWords loses money. Rob compares it to "buying high and selling low."
- This is also why "you can't make money charging $1 per month."
- Even at a $100 CPA, the LTV takes 12.5 months to arrive as cash. You would need a pile of cash to finance acquisition, which Rob calls a case where venture funding makes complete sense.

## Estimated CPA for other acquisition channels

- **Facebook:** Clicks typically cost $0.80-$1 with no effort. Using cheap-advertising tricks, Rob says you can reach $0.10-$0.20. At 1% conversion and $0.40 average cost per click, CPA is $40. At 0.5% it is $80.
- **SEO:**
  - It pays out over time instead of sending a few clicks and stopping.
  - Example: a top-3 ranking sending 200 clicks per month with minimal maintenance, costing $1000 (or a lot of up-front time).
  - At 1% conversion that is 2 sales per month. With a $250 LTV, that is $500 in future revenue per month.
  - It is a good investment if you have the cash to fund it.
- **Cold calling:**
  - Make 10 or 100 calls yourself to develop a script, then hire a telemarketing service to stay scalable.
  - Services charge $15-$20 per hour after setup fees.
  - At 20 calls per hour and 1 close per 100 calls, CPA is $75-$100.
  - Rob says he has never used a telemarketing service and these numbers are estimates only.

## Word of mouth and referral bonuses

- Word of mouth is free unless the referral needs a high-touch sale.
- If you know your LTV, you can afford a healthy referral bonus. With a $250 LTV, paying $50 or $100 per referral is "an easy sell."
- HostGator pays up to $125 in cash per referral because it knows its LTV.
- Companies can give a $50 gift card for attending a webinar because they know their LTV and webinar conversion rates. The gift card value is simple math on what still leaves a profit.

## Takeaways for bootstrappers

- Before building, estimate LTV and CPA for at least one realistic acquisition channel.
- Work out the financial nuts and bolts of marketing first, then build.
- Do not rely on add-ons or upsells to rescue weak LTV.
- Check whether you can fund the gap between spending on acquisition and receiving the LTV as cash.
- If acquisition costs more than LTV, you will be out of business quickly, unless you are doing "dotcom math" and have venture funding.
