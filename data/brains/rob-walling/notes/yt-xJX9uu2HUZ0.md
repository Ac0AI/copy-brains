## Real cost of starting a SaaS from $0: marketing and distribution, not building

- Rob says first-time founders overestimate what the product costs to build and underestimate what it costs to get anyone to care.
- The real expense, in time or money, is finding people who will pay. He calls this marketing, sales or "distribution".
- He says that without a paying customer "you actually have a hobby, not a business."
- He says "Revenue validates the business, everything else is theater."
- In applications to Tiny Seed, his B2B SaaS accelerator, he sees founders spend 6 months perfecting the product and setting up bank accounts, with zero customer conversations.
- He says founders pick easy tasks over complex, uncertain ones such as customer conversations.
- He says the money needed to get a SaaS off the ground has never been lower.

## Skip the formalities and use free or cheap tools until revenue arrives

- Rob says he could cross about 80% of the expense list off. Before revenue, don't form an LLC, set up a bank account, order business cards or obsess over a logo.
- He ran a US sole proprietorship for five or six years. He made six figures of revenue as a consultant and through products without an LLC.
- He gives this as his own experience, not legal advice. Most people formalize a corporate entity after they have real revenue.
- Payments: Stripe is free to set up, and you pay only when you get paid.
- Education is free or cheap:
  - His podcast Startups for the Rest of Us has more than 830 weekly episodes, recorded 52 a year since 2010.
  - YouTube, free book chapters online and the MicroConf community on Twitter.
  - Most of his books are about $10 on Kindle, PDF or audio.
- Hosting: Vercel and Supabase have free tiers, and AWS and Google Cloud offer startup credits. An MVP typically runs well under $50 a month.
- AI coding tools have free tiers or cost about $20 a month. Domains are about $10-15 a year.
- Total hard-dollar cost for a functional MVP can be under $100 if the founder does a lot themselves.
- He says the barrier is not money but "knowing where to focus."

## Three ways to get a product built (best to worst)

**Option A: build it yourself, if you can code.**
- This is his preferred path. Build nights and weekends around a day job or consulting.
- With AI assistants like Cursor and Claude Code, weekend tasks now take an evening. Shipping an MVP in under 4 weeks of nights and weekends is realistic.
- The fundamentals are unchanged:
  - Find the core use case.
  - Shrink scope ruthlessly.
  - Ship, then iterate on real feedback rather than guesswork.
- Building faster doesn't remove the need to get prospects using the product. The odds of building the right thing from your own head are "very, very low."
- The advantage is that you can maintain and extend the product yourself. "Product is never done," and it isn't a one-time agency job.

**Option B: non-developer using AI-assisted or no-code tools.**
- Tools named: Cursor, Claude Code, Lovable, Bolt and Replit.
- Works well for prototypes, simple CRUD apps, landing pages with functionality, internal tools and MVPs for customer feedback.
- Gets hard with complex business logic, deep integrations, ongoing maintenance and scaling. Code becomes difficult to modify at the edges.
- Recommended workflow:
  1. Build a prototype with AI tools.
  2. Validate willingness to pay.
  3. Learn to maintain it yourself, or hire a developer once there is revenue.
- Two Tiny Seed-funded companies built a no-code product to validate it, then hired a developer to rewrite it in full code.
- Cost is $0-20 a month, maybe $100 on pricier tiers. The real investment is time.

**Option C: find a technical co-founder.**
- "I have the idea" is not enough, especially now that non-technical people can build prototypes.
- The non-technical founder owns everything but the code: customer conversations, demos, sales, landing pages and marketing.
- The best pairings come from shared context, such as the same industry, meeting at MicroConf or the same community. He has not seen many work out from a Reddit post.
- The best pairings are complementary. Two-developer teams often both want to write code and nobody sells.
- One co-founder must head up driving demand and building a marketing and sales engine.

## Marketing with no budget: pair a fast channel with a slow one

- With no budget, paid ads are off the table and time is the main resource.
- Slow, compounding channels:
  - SEO and content can take months to pay off but compound.
  - SEO isn't just Google. It includes YouTube (the number two search engine), app stores such as the WordPress and Shopify stores, Reddit, niche directories, and AI answer engines like ChatGPT and Perplexity.
  - All of these reward useful content that answers real questions.
  - An SEO engine that ranks in Google brings organic traffic month after month.
- Fast channels:
  - Cold outreach is free and fast. It depends on a signal, "an external cue" showing someone needs your product "now, not someday." Without a signal it is spam.
  - Partnerships and integrations: co-promote with complementary products. They cost time, but leads can last for years.
  - Communities and events: meetups, Slack groups, subreddits, Facebook groups and Discord where your vertical gathers. Show up and be genuinely helpful. This reaches early customers and can be a long-term channel.
- His ideal plan is one fast channel (outreach or communities) and one slow channel (SEO or content), worked at the same time.
  - Fast only means always chasing the next lead.
  - Slow only means starving for 3, 6 or 9 months while waiting.

## Using AI for content without sounding generic

- AI lets everyone produce more faster, so it isn't an edge. What stands out is original thinking, proprietary data and real expertise.
- He says "A thousand AI blog posts are probably not going to beat one piece from someone who knows the problem."
- At MicroConf and Tiny Seed, AI helps with prep, but the script is not AI-generated. This video was outlined with AI assistance.
- The team feeds its own internal knowledge, original thinking and frameworks into AI, which then fleshes out the edges.
- Asking AI to outline a video from a title alone gives something "bland," "generic" and "the average."

## Keep your day job and build nights and weekends

- For the first year or longer, the founder is the entire company: sales, support, engineering and marketing.
- The default path, and the way he and most successful bootstrap founders did it, is to keep the day job until the business replaces it or gets close.
- He says posts on X and Reddit from people who quit, ran out of runway and had to get a job again are a pattern to avoid.
- Exceptions are founders with savings or a supportive partner or spouse.
- Benefits of the paycheck:
  - It removes the ticking-clock pressure.
  - It allows patience, better decisions, less desperation and longer-term bets.
- Timeline is unpredictable: maybe 3 months, 6 months or 18 months.
- A finite cash reserve pushes founders into short-term decisions. He calls a steady paycheck "an underrated competitive advantage."

## Hard work, luck and skill, and finding problems worth solving

- Every successful startup he knows combines hard work, luck and skill in different proportions. This includes his own, the 230+ he has invested in and thousands he has watched through MicroConf and Startups for the Rest of Us.
- Luck can't be controlled, but how much the outcome depends on it can be.
- Hard work is necessary but not sufficient. Plenty of founders grind on the wrong things for years.
- Skill directs the work: marketing, product sense, hearing what customers say, knowing what to build and how to get it into people's hands.
- The goal is to minimize luck's role by building skills systematically and putting in the work.
- The most important early skill is spotting problems worth solving. Ideas usually come from paying attention, not a flash of inspiration, especially at a day job where you see broken workflows and manual processes.
- He offers a free ebook, "the 9-to-5 audit", a structured framework for finding SaaS ideas at work, at robwalling.com/ideas.
- He says none of the above matters if you build the wrong thing, and finding the idea is hard.
- He surveyed more than 200 successful SaaS founders on where they found their idea and covers eight methods in a follow-up video.
