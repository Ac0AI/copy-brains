## Rob's definition of a flywheel and why he fails fast

- Rob borrowed "flywheel" from Good to Great. He defines it as a marketing approach (or part) that makes the business run and scales, versus a "hamster wheel" where income stops when you stop (consulting is the example).
- He later concedes his usage was loose. Last year he meant "invest time once, let it keep spinning" (SEO). In this talk he mostly means "can you scale a business on this channel?"
- His portfolio is about 10 small web apps in tight niches, which together are his full-time income. He likes buying and fixing up products because it skips 600-800 hours of finding problem/solution fit, and says it is worth it at $5,000-10,000.
- He never tries to build two products at once ("I've failed at it"). He builds an app to the point of automation, with a VA doing tier-one support, then moves on.

## Failing faster: his string of failures

Rob's argument is that each failure got cheaper and taught him something.
- Flogs (a Digg for personal finance, c. 2005-06): 600-800 hours and $3-4K. It solved no problem.
- Feedshot (2006-07): about 200 hours, roughly $100/month. It solved a problem, but with a $2 LTV he could not market it.
- Startup Lens (free website-review screencasts): 12 episodes over 2-3 months, 100 subscribers, shut down. He wrote that it filled no fundamental need.
- Apprentice electrician jobs: he tested SEO first with one hired article and a few links until it hit 400-500 uniques/month. Only then did he buy an off-the-shelf job board for a few hundred dollars. Total cost was about 12 hours and $400. His apprentice lineman job site gets 80-90% of its traffic from SEO.
- His blog took roughly a year of failed posts, some 15-20 hours each for about 50 uniques, before it grew. The failures taught him to write better and what his audience wanted. It reached about 20,000 RSS subscribers.
- HitTail (bought Aug 2011): the previous owner's LTV data was off by more than 50%. A redesign he estimated at 6 weeks and $5,000 took almost 4 months and $10,000. His 6-month goal was 10x revenue. In month one he was 80% below target, then hit the February number by the end of March and was heading roughly $1,000 above that in April.
- Lessons: "failure is not the end point". Treat it as a step back and a pivot, not giving up. Always do some marketing on a test idea, because marketing is what he is testing. He frames a launch as testing conversion across Facebook pay-per-click, AdWords and SEO.
- Closing quote: "your desire for success should be greater than your fear of failure."

## The four tiers of customer need

Rob classifies products on three axes: is it sold on value (saves or makes money or time), is the need obvious, and are people actively searching for it.
- Tier 4, entertainment: games, Facebook. Not sold on value, need not obvious, not searched for.
- Tier 3, "complex/new" (formerly "enterprise"): sold on value, but the need is not obvious or the product is too complex or new. It needs high-touch or in-person sales. He has little experience here and says he will focus on tiers 1 and 2.
- Tier 2, "vitamin": sold on value and the need is obvious, but nobody searches for it. Examples: AppSumo, and salon management software (tiny search traffic, so inbound alone is hard).
- Tier 1, "aspirin": all three axes are yes. People type generic terms and "bang your door down". Examples: invoicing software, time tracking, his apprentice lineman jobs.
- The tiers are not a ranking of "best". Aspirin means more inbound and cheaper acquisition, since fulfilling demand is easier than creating or educating it.
- Aspirin drawbacks: there are only so many keywords, and these businesses plateau at a fixed number of monthly searches. To grow past that you need outbound, vitamin-style techniques.
- "Boring" products win because they tend to sit in tier 1 or 2.

## Where aspirin demand lives beyond Google

Rob's list of search-like channels where people look for generic terms:
- Google (keyword tool).
- Bing: about 29% of the market with Yahoo. For HitTail, Bing sends one-tenth the traffic of Google but converts three times higher. He is unsure why, and notes more women use Bing.
- YouTube: the third largest search engine. One person takes emailed questions, makes each a video title answered by screencast, posts weekly, and gets hundreds of thousands of page views a month after a year.
- WordPress.org: free plugins that upsell to premium. Several people in the room make a full-time living from it.
- Amazon, the iPhone app store, the Android app store, Pinterest, and Craigslist. He cites Airbnb's early growth on Craigslist.
- He says there are many untapped aspirin niches.

## Why the tier sets your LTV and pricing floor

Rob says LTV and CPA are the two most important numbers in a business, and that people rarely share them. He ties required LTV to the tier.
- Complex/new, high-touch: LTV of at least $5,000, with $10-20K "where it really starts" and up into the seven figures.
- Vitamin: moderate to low touch. If LTV is not above about $150, "you're screwed". The usual range is $150-500, with outliers near $2,000 (a niche Squarespace-style host).
- Aspirin: LTV can be as low as $1 (a 99-cent iPhone app). Most are $10-30. Apprentice lineman jobs runs about $20-25 LTV. Standalone software tends to sell at $9-19.
- You cannot run a vitamin business at $1/month.
- HitTail: he thought LTV was $165 when buying. It was about $105. It is now about $165-170, with a goal of $400 in six months.
- Pricing too low can hurt. An audience member doubled their price and made their first sale that day, because very low prices signal low value.

## Marketing tactics for vitamin and aspirin products

Rob lists what has worked recently for him and others, with CPA and whether each scales:
- Podcast interviews: 30-45 minutes with no prep, CPA effectively zero, 10-100 signups each, several thousand dollars per hour of his time. It does not sustain itself because traffic dies off, and the audience is too small to grow a business.
- Joint mailings and partnerships: swap emails with someone who has a similar audience, at no cost beyond writing. Expect 10-100 signups. If you convert 30-50% with an LTV of $100-150, the hourly return is high.
- Guest posts.
- Integration marketing: BidSketch builds integrations with big names, makes a landing page with a video, and emails the partner's list. CPA is $5-100, covering code or contractor cost.
- SEO: very low CPA and scalable.
- Facebook ads: his earlier cost-per-click was 20 cents. At a 1% close rate, CPA is $80-100 ("20 cents times 100 is 20 bucks" shows how his formula works, with the 1-in-100 conversion assumption). He has gotten 15-cent clicks with special techniques.
- AdWords: scalable but Google makes money by pushing bids up. He compares today's Facebook to AdWords five years ago.
- Cold calling is possible later.
- The first four or five tactics get the first hundred customers. SEO, Facebook, AdWords and cold calling carry you beyond that. "Scalable", not "self-sustaining", is how he now defines flywheel.
- He wishes he had understood CPA five years ago.

## Doubling LTV: the math and the three levers

- LTV is total profit (expenses subtracted) over the customer's lifetime. To be profitable, LTV must exceed CPA.
- Backend example: a $19 product where 20% also buy a pricier second offer has an LTV near $85. That lets you cut the front-end price toward cost or even give it away, as big firms do. He cites $50 payments to attend a webinar, and HostGator paying $125 per referral at 20+ per month.
- Three levers: raise retention, raise price, sell more to existing customers.
- Retention via a customer happiness index (borrowed from Dharmesh Shah of HubSpot): a weighted score per customer, reverse-sorted, so you contact the lowest-scoring before they cancel. For HitTail he sketched 12 factors in five minutes and built the report in a couple of hours. Four examples: tracking code installed, referrers actually collected (with a pivot at about 50 uniques/day), recency of last login, and long-term organic traffic growth. Install rate went from 25% to 65-70% via about four emails in the first week and free code installation (VA at about $2 versus about $170 LTV). For proposal software the factors might be last login, proposals created, sent and won. Start with equal weights and tweak. Rob says a coder needs four hours at most.
- Pricing, wholesale: .NET Invoice was a $98 one-time fee. Mike Taber told him to quadruple it. Rob went to $295 and sold the same number of copies, roughly tripling revenue. Had it failed he would have tried $195, then $149.
- Pricing, restructure: BidSketch had $9 and $19 plans, with 15-20 person firms on the $19 plan. Ruben roughly doubled to $19/$29, added an agency plan, and tied tiers to value (such as logins). LTV rose 100% or more with no new traffic, and existing customers were grandfathered. Rob added graduated usage tiers to HitTail by visits and made one tier the best, which was a big part of a roughly 50% LTV gain. Ask whether you are underpriced and whether price rises with the value a customer gets.
- Sell more: an annual support/upgrade fee of 20% on .NET Invoice, and add-on modules (QuickBooks module at $99 on a $329 product, which also lifted core sales). For SaaS, put niche features in a higher plan or sell them as a monthly add-on. HitTail is adding one-click articles via an outside writer. Ask what hard-to-build thing customers keep requesting that they would pay for.
- Estimating SaaS LTV with little data: use rules of thumb and talk to others in the niche. Churn often starts near 20% monthly, and HitTail's went from 30% to 8% in two months. Aim for about 5% (5-10% is acceptable for small SaaS), while enterprise SaaS runs near 20% per year. LTV needs only price and churn.
