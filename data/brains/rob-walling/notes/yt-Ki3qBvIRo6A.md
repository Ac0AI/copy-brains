## Market first: market before you code, and don't confuse it with building an audience

- Rob argues founders should start marketing before they start coding. He says this is repeatable and predictable, so you learn from it. Luck teaches nothing: "It's like buying a lottery ticket and winning."
- He separates marketing from "building in public". Marketing means talking to prospective customers, not to other indie hackers or founders on social media.
- Of his 191 investments in B2B SaaS companies, fewer than 5% had any kind of audience. That count includes social media, podcast and YouTube audiences.
- His advice: "Build your network, not your audience." Learning marketing as a skill matters far more than building an audience.
- He calls treating X (Twitter) as a marketing strategy, or building a following to become a SaaS founder, a big beginner mistake. A handful of people succeed that way and tens of thousands try. He compares it to saying "I want to play in the NBA" or "I want to be Taylor Swift."
- Marketing includes content people want to consume, SEO, pay-per-click ads, integrations and partnerships, direct outreach, in-person events and podcast appearances.
- He has listed 20 marketing approaches for B2B SaaS in his book "The SaaS Playbook".
- Learning to market repeatably carries over to every product you start afterward.

## Setting up before the first conversations: landing page, early-access list and ICP

- Rob starts with a basic landing page and an early-access list.
- How hands-on he is depends on whether the product will use high-touch sales and demos or a self-serve approach.
- He picks an ideal customer profile (ICP), maybe two. If you think your product suits everyone online, "good luck." He says you are in for a really hard time.
- Self-funded founders especially need a tight ICP, which ties to going niche.
- An ICP can be an industry (construction company owners, gym owners, martial arts schools). It can also be a job title in a company (HR manager, software development manager, engineer).
- The test is who is most likely to need, want and buy the product.
- A list of interested people is an asset. You can survey it, for example:
  - Ask what they want the product to include.
  - Send a screenshot of what you're building, or a value proposition you're considering, and ask what they think.
- You can also poll visitors on the landing page. Collect anonymous feedback first, then ask for their email address after they give it.
- He calls embedding short surveys in the flow a somewhat advanced approach, but one he sees successful founders use.

## Four ways to start conversations with potential customers (founders, network, audiences, communities)

- **Talk to other founders** who sold into the same space or to the same ICP. Talk to those who succeeded and those who failed.
- **Talk to sales and marketing people at somewhat bigger companies.** He means not huge ones. His example is a software company making $50K a month in the same space or selling to the same customers. Find someone on LinkedIn and say something like "Hey, I'm trying to do this thing and I need some advice."
- **Use your existing network.** Reach out to people you know in the industry for insights and ask them to introduce you to people who might want the product. If you know no one in the industry, he says you've made a mistake. You should build your network in the space you plan to sell in.
- **Find an audience online that you can reach.** He stresses finding one, not building your own, because building takes years. Examples are:
  - Podcast appearances.
  - Someone in your network willing to tweet about you or share your ideas with their audience.
- **Lurk in online communities.** Join Facebook groups, subreddits, Slack channels and other relevant forums. Watch for common problems and complaints people have with existing solutions in your space.

## Direct outreach (warm or cold) as a core early tactic

- Rob counts direct outreach as the fifth tactic. It can be warm or cold, by email, LinkedIn or Twitter DMs.
- He says few aspiring founders do it. They assume everything is about inbound traffic, which can work. He leans toward outreach himself because he is a developer turned marketer.
- His example is Tally (tally.so). Co-founder Marie appeared on his podcast in episode 717. She cold-contacted thousands of people to get started.
- He pushes back on copying success blindly, such as "they had a free plan, so I'll have a free plan." In his view what made Tally succeed was:
  - A free plan in a very broad market at low cost to serve.
  - Hard work.
  - Things like cold outreach that probably felt uncomfortable.
- His point is that it will make you uncomfortable too, and that is fine.

## Using early feedback to shape the product

- Rob warns against launching a landing page with a fixed picture of what you'll build. Founders are makers, so they just build.
- Feedback arrives in conversations on Reddit, Zoom or email. Some of it reinforces your vision and some says you're heading the wrong way. Typical asks are "Can you add this?", "Can you pivot to this?" and "Can you support my use case?"
- He calls learning to take feedback from prospects and others a skill you must develop to last as an entrepreneur. This means learning who to listen to and how to adapt your product vision and marketing copy to what the market demands.
- Without that, the odds of succeeding with your first idea are very low.
- The goal of the first 10 customers is sales, a launch list and learning (what to build, how to adjust course, how to improve it). He says it is all of these.
- A common first-time-founder mistake is not knowing which feedback to heed and how to adjust your messaging and vision.
- Decide whether you want to find a unique niche or compete in an established market. If you pick a unique niche, validate with customers early that anyone cares.
- If you can't find your people online, you may need traditional marketing, which is hard. It takes lots of cold outreach and in-person events. Or you start over in a space where people look for solutions online.
- Even with a low-touch path, you must talk to people to learn what you're building and where the market will lead you.

## Sixth tactic: run ads to find early adopters

- Rob's sixth tactic is running Facebook, LinkedIn and Google ads to start those early conversations and find true early adopters for your first 10 customers.
- The goal is not positive ROI. It is to get people into the funnel and find interested prospects who might fit your product.
- It doesn't work for everyone. You need a budget and have to learn to run ads. He says many free resources and inexpensive video courses teach it.
- Finding the first 10 is not the end. You then need a marketing strategy that brings in new customers on a regular basis.
- He promotes his SaaS Launchpad video course (SaaSLaunchpad.co): 27 modules and about 10 hours of video, built on lessons from thousands of startups he has watched and worked with over 20 years.
