## Why cutting price from $99 to $19 backfired (Neil Magnuson example)

- Rob cites Neil Magnuson, who cut his SaaS price from $99 to $19 a month hoping to grow faster. Churn nearly doubled.
- Customer lifetime fell from 8.8 months at $99 to 3.1 months at $19. Lifetime value fell from $683 to $68, a 10x collapse for the same product.
- Neil's conclusion, which Rob endorses: "cheap prices brought browsers, not operators."
- Replacing 100 customers at $99 with $19 customers at the same revenue takes about 11 times as many customers.
- Rob's experience is that cheaper customers also cost more to support. The $19 business is more expensive to run and makes less money.
- Lower prices can lift sign-ups or trials in the short term. In "almost every case" Rob has seen in 20 years, they make the whole business worse.
- Rob's context: he has invested in more than 240 SaaS companies and has seen this pattern repeatedly.

## Price is a filter: who you attract at low vs. high prices

- Rob's core claim is that "your price isn't just a number. It's usually a filter." Set it too low and you filter in the wrong customers.
- Low prices attract price-sensitive buyers who want cheap software, not an in-depth solution. They churn as soon as a competitor is a dollar or two cheaper.
- Higher prices attract buyers who have already convinced themselves it is worth it before signing up.
- Higher prices also attract business buyers. A $99 a month tool is a "rounding error" for a business. At $19, many businesses won't take the product seriously and more consumers sign up.
- Rob repeats his advice not to sell to consumers. Businesses mean lower support, higher payments and longer retention.
- At $19, "a lot of people are just going to come along and kick the tires." At $99, the price adds just enough friction that buyers commit more. They onboard properly instead of logging in, poking around and leaving.
- Lower-paying customers are almost always a higher support burden. They are less technical and need hand-holding. A business customer may have a developer or IT person to learn the tool.
- A higher price usually leads to better customers and fewer complaints.

## Underpricing patterns Rob sees at Tiny Seed

- Rob runs Tiny Seed, a B2B SaaS accelerator. Pricing is one of the first things reviewed at the kickoff retreat, held twice a year.
- Most founders anchor their price too low from day one, often "out of fear, not out of strategy."
- Many anchor to their own cost of doing business instead of the value delivered to the customer.
- They assume a lower price removes friction. Rob says it actually removes "the signal that the product is serious."
- About 80% of incoming batches have some pricing issue. Either the price is too low, or the value metric is wrong. The value metric is the number, such as subscribers or seats, that rises as customers pay more.
- The advice is usually, but not always, to charge more. Higher prices also fund more marketing and sales.
- Across hundreds of Tiny Seed companies, underpricing is "one of the most consistent and correctable problems."
- Raising prices is usually on the table earlier than founders think, and founders who do it are usually glad they did.

## The price ceiling and how rarely founders hit it

- Rob acknowledges there is usually a ceiling within a particular customer type.
- In his experience almost no founders reach it, because they are too scared to raise their monthly price.
- Of the hundreds of companies he has seen raise prices, he knows of only two, maybe three, that went too high and later dropped them.

## Case study: Jimdo's price increase

- Rob names Iran Gerpott, founder of Jimdo, as an example of raising prices and changing the business. Jimdo is a mostly bootstrapped company whose only outside money came from Tiny Seed.
- Gerpott raised prices around 2021 or 2022. Rob recalls an increase of between 50% and 100%, though he doesn't remember the exact numbers.
- Gerpott had not raised prices in the previous 5 years. Rob says fear and anxiety, more than the numbers, kept him from doing it.
- After a few calls with Rob, Gerpott raised prices and worked with some existing customers to keep it affordable.
- The change reshaped both the growth trajectory and the churn trajectory of the business.
- Gerpott later sold a majority stake in the company for $32.5 million.
- Rob writes about this in his book *The SaaS Playbook*, which also covers Auren Galperin raising prices for Gymdesk.

## Two paths: raise prices within a segment vs. go upmarket

Rob says to decide first which move you are making. They are two different moves.

**Path one: raise prices within the current segment**
- This is the same ICP and use case with more money. The increase might be 10%, 30%, or 50% if you are underpriced.
- Every segment has a ceiling. You push toward it, not past it.
- Run it as an experiment:
  - Change the pricing page and leave existing customers alone.
  - Watch conversions for a few weeks or months.
  - If you are sales-led, quote the new price to new prospects first and see how they respond.
  - If the new pricing works and the business is healthier, revisit existing customers later.

**Path two: go upmarket**
- You are changing customers, not just raising prices.
- Rob's example is a podcast tool. A hobbyist pays $25 a month and a business podcaster pays $75. Moving to $500 and $2,000 a month means targeting broadcasters and large podcast studios.
- When you raise prices 10x or 20x, it helps to already have one or two customers in that tier, because their needs differ from your $25 and $75 customers.
- Going upmarket means being more sales-led, and the product may need to evolve in ways you didn't anticipate.
- The payoff is a healthier business with lower churn, higher lifetime value, and more ability to pay for marketing and sales.
- Rob points to a separate video on executing a price increase, including grandfathering existing customers and communicating the change.
