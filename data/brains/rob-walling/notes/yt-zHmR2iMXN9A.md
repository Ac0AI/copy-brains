## Free trial vs. freemium: the definition and why freemium usually fails

- Rob defines the difference this way: a free trial ends, either by time ("a 7-day free trial") or by usage (for example, 100 emails through the account). Freemium, which he also calls "forever free," renews automatically. You might get 100 emails every month and only pay when you go over.
- Freemium gives people much less motivation to convert to paid, which is a big reason it is hard to make work.
- In the 2024 State of Independent SaaS Survey, about 29% of bootstrapped and mostly bootstrapped SaaS companies offer freemium. About 51% offer a free trial.
- His default advice is that freemium doesn't work "90, 95% of the time." He suspects many of the 29% freemium offerings are not actually working.
- His data for the survey and for his advice comes from 224 investments and nearly 700 startups surveyed for the State of Independent SaaS Report.

## When freemium might work: Ruben's five requirements

Rob cites Ruben, founder of SignWell, as one of the most knowledgeable people on SaaS freemium. Ruben's five minimum criteria are:

1. Self-serve with simple onboarding.
2. Customers get to value quickly, without weeks of onboarding.
3. Almost zero cost for each new user.
4. Some element of virality.
5. A very large market, not a small niche.

Rob says that meeting all five still doesn't guarantee freemium will work. These are the minimum criteria he would check before even considering a freemium plan. Generally he tells most founders to avoid it.

## Should the trial require a credit card? Start with who is signing up

- The key question is whether the person signing up is likely to have a company credit card.
- A software engineer at a very large company might not have one. If that person is your end user, you may not want to require a card.
- Engineering managers are very likely to have a card.
- Rob's Drip experiment: screen one asked for email and password, and screen two asked for a credit card. If someone bailed before entering a card, Drip emailed them about 12 hours to a day later and offered a trial without a card.
  - Roughly 0% of those people became a good long-term fit. There was a trickle, but lead quality was "10 to 20 times worse" than with the card up front.
- Without a card you also get many unqualified people. That can swamp a small team and raise the support burden.

## When to require a card early, and when to drop it early

- In his last bootstrapped SaaS, Rob required a card up front. He had a launch list of 3,400 people who wanted to try the product, and he didn't want unqualified signups swamping the team. With a big list like that, he would ask for the card early.
- The case for removing it early: you have no brand, only a trickle of visitors, few trial signups, and you need feedback. Letting people in without a card and trying to talk with them can be worth it. He warns you will get a lot of unqualified people and you need to be ready for that.
- His old default was that all founders should ask for a card up front. Now he sees a case for removing it early when you aren't getting enough feedback on the product.

## Experimenting with removing the card once you're established

- Once the funnel works and you're at roughly $500k, $1M or $2M a year, he suggests experimenting with removing the card, as long as you can roll it back.
- At that stage you have historical numbers and good knowledge of the business, so you can see the impact quickly. It may take a month or two, and you must also look at churn.
- He calls it a worthwhile experiment because it is a big bet, and "sometimes you have to take big bets to really change the trajectory of your business."
- One data point he often thinks about: a company that removed the card after reaching about $1M a year saw absolutely no difference. He notes this doesn't mean the same will hold for your business.
- Check the competitive landscape too. If competitors allow trials without a card, or offer freemium, there is a pull to match them, though that doesn't mean they are making it work. You may need it to win customers.

## What the State of Independent SaaS data shows

- Share of free trials requiring a card: about 73% in 2020, up in 2021 and 2022, then down by more than 10% in 2024. About three quarters of mostly bootstrapped SaaS companies still require a card.
- Bootstrapped founders require it because it cuts noise and support burden. The downside is that it may turn away a small number of qualified prospects, especially if competitors don't require a card.
- Asia Orangio, founder of Demand Maven, analyzed growth and churn for the report:
  - Month-over-month growth for a trial with a card required is double that of a trial without a card.
  - It is about 40% higher than growth for a freemium plan.
  - Churn is lowest with a card required.
- Rob's read is that requiring a card is the way to go for most mostly bootstrapped SaaS companies. He adds that companies not asking for a card may be earlier-stage, with funnels not yet figured out, which could explain their low growth and high churn.

## The puzzling LTV result and what it implies

- Orangio also compared customer lifetime value. A free trial without a card showed 2.2 times the lifetime value of a trial with a card. Rob says he "genuinely" can't explain it.
- Free plans had very low LTV. Combined with high churn and weak growth, that makes freemium an uphill battle.
- His hypothesis is that the high LTV for no-card trials comes from later-stage companies with funnels that are more figured out. This fits his advice to test removing the card once you pass about $1M ARR.
