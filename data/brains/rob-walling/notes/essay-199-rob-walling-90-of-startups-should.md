## Rob's 1-9-90 rule for funding software startups

- Of all software startups, Rob figures about 1% should consider raising venture capital.
- About 9% should consider other funding: friends and family, an angel round, or non-venture money like the accelerator he runs (TinySeed).
- The other 90% should bootstrap.
- He calls the idea that "everybody who does a startup has to raise funding" a "bullshit narrative" and says "it's just not true."
- He has spent 25 years and six companies showing that raising is optional.

## The "venture industrial complex" and Rob's own change of mind

- Rob says "the venture industrial complex has brainwashed us all," and includes himself: "Me included, back in the early 2000s."
- He once assumed, like everyone around him, that building something real meant raising large sums and swinging for a huge outcome.
- The story he rejects: raise money, try to become Uber, then Facebook, and anything below that bar counts as failure.
- He is not anti-ambition. He wanted to build multimillion-dollar businesses and did so more than once.
- He is against the single story that the raise is the start of the journey and the size of the raise measures ambition.
- He describes the cost of buying the story: companies agree to chase a size they never needed, on an investor's timeline. A wonderful ten-million-dollar business can get managed into the ground reaching for a hundred million.

## The iceberg of boring B2B SaaS

- By Rob's count, there are fifty or sixty thousand B2B SaaS companies in the world doing real revenue.
- Many started as an email tool, an invoicing utility or a scheduling widget and now pay their founders very well.
- None of them will be written about or trend. He compares them to the part of an iceberg under the water.
- The visible tip is the Ubers and Facebooks, the companies that raise enough to buy attention.
- Rob's work through TinySeed (an accelerator for bootstrapped and non-unicorn SaaS founders) and MicroConf (his conference for bootstrapped founders, run for over a decade) is funding and connecting the part underwater.
- He points founders to tinyseed.com/portfolio to see boring B2B SaaS businesses he has invested in and the niches they occupy.
- Background stated in the source: he has invested in 211 SaaS companies and funded more than 200 through TinySeed.

## Drip as the proof case: a gap, not a war chest

- In 2012 Rob wanted a small email-capture widget for websites. He and co-founder Derek Reimer built it, and it did not take off at first.
- It took 14 months of grinding to reach what Rob considers strong product-market fit. Nine of those months came after the public launch.
- Product-market fit came after he made a hard call on incomplete information and turned the email tool into a marketing automation platform.
- The gap he saw: cheap email tools (MailChimp, AWeber, Constant Contact) could not automate anything. Expensive marketing automation platforms (Marketo, Infusionsoft) had floors of about $400 a month, roughly $2,000 onboarding and annual commitments.
- In between was a population willing to pay $50 or $100 a month without talking to a salesperson. Drip slid into that middle.
- No venture money was involved. Drip was sold to Leadpages in 2016, which Rob has called his "never have to work again" moment.
- On luck: "It wasn't luck," he says, then adds, "I also happened to see the gap."

## Luck as a multiplier, and the case for merely great businesses

- Rob frames luck as a multiplier, not a cause: it scales what you have already built.
- He cites Basecamp. When he asked Jason Fried and DHH why it succeeded, they credited luck and timing.
- His arithmetic: Basecamp is doing, he guesses, north of $100M a year. Strip out the luck and it is still a ten- or twenty-million-dollar business. "It'd still be a great fucking business."
- He mentions DHH describing how he and Fried split $4 million off the bottom line in a year.
- Rob's point: a venture-backed startup rarely lets founders keep a number like that, because the money belongs to the next round.
- A bootstrapped company lets you pull profit every year, sell later if you want, and keep the decisions. Nobody on a board bends your roadmap toward a valuation multiple.
