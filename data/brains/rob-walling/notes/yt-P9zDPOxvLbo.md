## Raising TinySeed and the gap between bootstrapping and venture capital

- Rob runs MicroConf and TinySeed, two sister companies serving bootstrapped and mostly bootstrapped B2B SaaS founders.
- TinySeed has raised $59 million from outside investors (LPs). It has made 239 SaaS investments (he says 219 at another point). Rob has started six companies, five bootstrapped. TinySeed is the only one he raised money for.
- It started in 2018. He did not know whether anyone would invest in bootstrappers, or whether bootstrappers would give up equity.
- The idea came while growing Drip (started 2012, sold 2016). He was cash-strapped and could have used $250k-$500k, but venture capital did not fit.
- His view of VC: "You are a bad investment if you do not hit" a billion-dollar valuation. Many investors now only chase "decacorns" ($10B).
- The gap: a founder who wants to take a little money and sell for $20-$30 million. He calls that generational, life-changing wealth for 99.9% of people.
- His "1990 rule" for startups that could grow fast:
  - about 1% should raise real venture capital
  - about 9% should consider friends and family, angel, or a TinySeed-style round
  - about 90% should bootstrap
- TinySeed is an accelerator like Y Combinator, but only for B2B SaaS. Founders take roughly $100k-$200k and get a year of mentorship.
- One portfolio exit sold for $20 million. The two bootstrapped founders had given up about 10% to TinySeed. TinySeed took about $2M, and the founders took about $18M.

## What TinySeed screens for in B2B SaaS

- The target is B2B SaaS that can reach roughly $1M to $20-30M or more in ARR. The core question: "have you built a real product for real customers that will pay you real money?"
- It does not invest in two-sided marketplaces or any B2C.
- The first numbers it checks:
  1. **MRR growth over the past 6 months.** Momentum matters. A truly flat business is very hard to move off its plateau, whether at 1K or 50K MRR.
  2. **Churn.** Logo churn is the share of customers lost. He prefers revenue churn: 5 of 100 customers lost is 5%, but losing one $5K customer out of $100K MRR is also 5% of revenue. He says churn is the "death of SaaS" and acquirers value low churn.
  3. **Average revenue per account.** Low ARPA usually means higher churn and more support. "It's just hard to build a business charging 30 or 40 dollars a month."
- Caveat: a lifestyle bootstrapper aiming for about $10K MRR can charge $20, $40 or $80 a month. His own HitTail made about $300k a year, solo, at roughly $20/$40/$80 tiers. He would not start that today, because he would now aim for $10M ARR.
- After the numbers come interviews. He is not looking for someone who will "shred their whole life." He wants a reasonable, smart founder who is easy to work with for a year and has a real reason for doing this, such as building a better life for their family.

## Pricing too low is the single biggest SaaS mistake

- He says pricing too low is the number one mistake, "probably five times more than number two."
- Makers devalue what they build ("couldn't I build that in a week with AI?"). Price follows the value delivered. It does not follow build cost or hosting cost.
- Example: Senior Place, a TinySeed CRM for senior placement agents (like realtors for retirement homes).
  - Access fee about $300-$400 a month, plus per-seat fees.
  - The value is more than code: brand in a space with only about 3,000 US customers, support, and hand-holding.
- Lower prices almost inevitably mean higher churn.
- Examples of underpricing:
  - A product at $50 and $100 plans that Rob said was a $500-$1,000 a month product. It was a tenth of what it should be.
  - In interviews he tells applicants he will lean on them to triple or 5x their price.
- Vertical software can charge more than horizontal. A CRM for jewelers or senior placement agents can charge more than a generic CRM.
- Blanket advice does not hold everywhere. In each TinySeed batch of about 15, two or three have pricing right. For 80%+ his advice is to raise prices.

## Niching: vertical, orthogonal and horizontal SaaS

- TinySeed's data across its investments shows horizontal bootstrappers growing slower than vertical or niched-down ones.
- Horizontal examples it backed in early batches: SavvyCal (a Calendly competitor) and SignWell (e-signature). He calls them good businesses, but he has since backed off horizontal SaaS and counts it as a strike.
- He defines three kinds:
  - **Vertical:** one industry (maybe two or three), such as a CRM for jewelers.
  - **Orthogonal:** a horizontal product aimed at a single job title or department. Example: an applicant tracking system for the head of HR or people ops at any company.
  - **Horizontal:** anyone could use it.
- Vertical and orthogonal perform similarly. Truly horizontal lags by a significant margin.
- The TinySeed portfolio is at tinyseed.com/portfolio.

## Founder traits and evaluating founders

- His framework: idea/product times market times founder, multiplicative, times luck. He ignores luck because he wants advice that is repeatable. The framework is still unpublished.
  - **Market:** a growing market (email service providers in 2002, marketing automation in 2013, AI now) is an advantage. A crowded generic CRM market might rate a 1.
  - **Product:** a big market is not enough. The product must solve a desperate pain point people will pay for.
  - **Founder:** can fumble even a great market.
- Strong founders do many things quickly, do them well, and are right "enough of the time" (maybe 60%, not 90%). They are usually not right the first time.
  - Example: a TinySeed founder who, after a strategy call, launched two marketing approaches in 3 weeks. One failed and one worked.
  - Contrast: founders who take 6 months to launch the first one, behind a chain of contractors, perfect copy and excuses.
  - The slow archetype tends to fail with every channel, and says marketing channels "don't work anymore."
- He says SEO, AdWords, in-person events and Capterra still work in 2026. He can name 10 portfolio companies making each of those work. The problem is lack of self-awareness: "maybe you're just not good at this."
- How he screens in interviews:
  - Does the person shoot straight, or exaggerate?
  - Does the founder make excuses? He will say "your pricing is 50 and 100, you should charge 250" or "your churn is 12% a month, tell me why."
  - Good responses: "Hell yes, let's do it" or taking responsibility. Bad: "my customers would never pay that" or "that's just what this market churns at."
  - Results: for example, going from 5K to 30K MRR in 6 months is a signal of execution.
  - TinySeed sometimes "takes a flyer" on a smart team in a space it believes in, even with weak growth and churn.

## B2B SaaS marketing: the 20 approaches and the big five

- Two fallacies: "a great product will market itself" (Apple, Basecamp), and "posting on social media builds a SaaS." He said "if you think that posting on Twitter... is marketing, I have really bad news."
  - Even with an audience he built over 20 years, he says it "really doesn't help you very much with SaaS."
  - Founders who sell SaaS to their audience typically stall at 5K MRR, sometimes 10-15K.
  - Social-first successes (Peter Levels, Marc Lou) are survivorship bias: perhaps 5-10 out of tens of thousands who tried.
  - His line: "build your network, not your audience."
- Rob identified 20 B2B SaaS marketing approaches (in The SaaS Playbook, a $10 Kindle or PDF). The big five most common among bootstrappers:
  1. **SEO.** Usually Google, but sometimes YouTube, Amazon, the iOS App Store or the WordPress plugin repo.
  2. **Pay-per-click.** AdWords, Capterra or Meta, depending on where customers search.
  3. **Cold or warm outreach.** LinkedIn DMs and email. It works less every month as inboxes get flooded.
  4. **Content marketing.** YouTube videos to rank in Google or YouTube search, or viral attempts on Reddit or Hacker News. Can be written, video or audio.
  5. **Integrations and partnerships.** Drip built about 30-35 integrations in 18 months. His pitch to partners: "we'll promote you if you promote us," and if they would not promote back, Drip did not.
- The next five include podcasts and YouTube guesting, and in-person events if lifetime value supports it (some TinySeed companies charge $20,000 a year and can afford a booth).
- His advice to resistant makers: treat marketing as a game and as learning. Decide whether you are starting a business or a hobby. Building software may be half your time, and the other 20-40% (marketing, filings, payroll) will not be fun.

## Exiting a SaaS and founder dependence

- Rob has sold two SaaS companies: a mid-six-figure exit, and Drip, a life-changing exit. Drip sold to LeadPages while bootstrapped with 10 employees and about $2-3M a year. He then ran engineering as VP, and the company grew to 125 people in about 20 months. He did not want to do sales.
- **Bucket one: under about $1.5-2M ARR.**
  - Usually priced on a net profit multiple (EBITDA or SDE, seller's discretionary earnings), roughly 4-7x.
  - Founder roles are lighter: advising a few hours a month for a few months.
  - Brokers like Quiet Light fit here. He sends them business and they sponsor MicroConf.
  - Example: his HitTail sale, where he handed off the technology and advised for a few hours.
- **Bucket two: roughly $2-20M ARR.**
  - Private equity and strategics buy on a revenue multiple.
  - Multiples he has seen:
    - 1-2x for mostly flat companies, with a $2M ARR company selling for $4-5M
    - 5-7x revenue for companies growing 60-100% a year
    - one $3M ARR company in a tight vertical, growing quickly with strong fundamentals, sold for $45M, a 15x multiple
  - The founder usually has to stay 1-3 years. Drip's buyer required both founders, negotiated from about two years down to between one and two. He estimates only about 1 in 20 founders can walk away at this size.
  - One edge case: a founder sold 70-80%, kept the rest to roll into a bigger entity, and hired a CEO to work under while staying VP of engineering and product.
- The less you are involved day to day, the better the sale. It matters less at lower ARR, and a fast-growing business can still sell with the founder embedded.
