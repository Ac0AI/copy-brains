## What Rob calls a "Toxic Customer" and why avoiding them matters

- Rob defines a Toxic Customer as a demanding, entitled customer who "feels that their purchase price buys them control over your life, liberty and pursuit of happiness."
- He has dealt with his share of them while selling invoicing software.
- Examples of what toxic customers have done to him:
  - Called his cell phone five times in 5 hours on Memorial Day, a U.S. holiday, even though the customer was U.S.-based and knew it was a holiday.
  - Demanded (not asked for) support outside standard support hours because the customer was busy at his day job during those hours.
  - Used 10 hours of support time over 2 months because he didn't follow the installation instructions or run the troubleshooting steps in Rob's first reply. After 30+ emails, Rob logged into his system, ran one check and found the issue.
- Rob says you won't meet many in a lifetime. After the first few, you learn to spot them and step away gracefully.
- The costs he lists:
  - They chew up support time.
  - They cost you money.
  - They can damage your reputation by posting to Twitter, forums and review sites.
  - They cause severe stress.

## Detect toxic customers early by stacking warning signs

- Rob watches for warning signs during the sales process.
- Any single sign is "not a big deal." Stack 2 or 3 of them, depending on severity, and you have a red flag.
- Rob's five warning signs:
  1. **Disrespectful or overly abrupt.** Some people write as if no real person is reading. They may be in a hurry, not know email etiquette, or be a jerk. Rob says it takes several emails to tell which.
  2. **Asks for a discount with no reason.** He always works with particular circumstances, especially schools and non-profits. Asking for 25% or 30% off "just for kicks" is not typically a sign of an outstanding customer.
  3. **Multiple contacts, often through multiple channels.** For example, three emails to the sales, support and info addresses plus a voicemail, all within minutes. The request isn't time-sensitive. Rob's view: if someone has to send four requests to get a reply, do they really want to buy from that company?
  4. **Unrealistic expectations.** For example, four emails in two hours with rising urgency over a non-urgent pre-sales question the website already answers. The last one was in all caps: "WHY AREN'T YOU ANSWERING?"
  5. **Multiple questions that can be answered from your website.** Common and "far from a deal-breaker." It can signal a future support burden, especially if installation requires the customer to read and follow a list of steps.

## Case study: the 80-question prospect for the invoicing software

- A prospect emailed a spreadsheet of nearly 80 questions. He said he needed them answered "in order to proceed as I can't get them all answered off your website." At least half were answerable from the website.
- He planned a flat-file data exchange between Rob's software and a custom piece his colleague had written.
- Rob said the software has a .NET API and a web service layer, and that passing flat files would not be optimal.
- The prospect replied sharply that his colleague had 25+ years of programming experience and he "just need[ed] pricing and questions answered."
- Rob expected extensive due diligence taking several hours. He also expected the support trouble to start after the sale.
- Several signs stacked up: abrupt tone, many website-answerable questions, and a push for an approach Rob advised against.

## How Rob declines a bad-fit prospect respectfully

- Rob did not fill out the spreadsheet. He told the prospect: "Thanks very much for your interest. I think you will be better off finding another invoicing solution."
- The prospect demanded an explanation and insisted on making the decision himself. He also asked why Rob doesn't use a last name.
- Rob's second reply stayed respectful without watering down the message. It made these points:
  - At the product's price point, the company can't run a manual sales process that answers a large number of questions the website already answers.
  - A text file between applications will become a support headache. "We don't recommend that approach and are not willing to support it."
  - He thinks the prospect won't be happy with the application and will be better off elsewhere.
  - "We reserve the right to sell our software (or not) to whom we choose."
  - He closed with thanks and good wishes: "Best of luck finding a solution that fits your needs."
- His stance: he was fairly sure this was not someone he wanted as a customer, and he was willing to wager a sale on it. He had been down this path before.

## Outcome and lesson: the signal can sit with one person, not the company

- The next morning, someone else at the same company wrote. He said his colleague was the wrong person to lead the project and apologized for the "monster list." He asked to hit reset because their needs were simple.
- He listed four needs, and the software handled all of them out of the box.
- After 2 or 3 emails, the company bought within a week.
- The takeaway from this story: the toxic signals came from one individual, not the company. Politely declining a toxic contact can leave room for a better buyer to come forward, and the sale can still happen.
- Rob's point is that these are warning signs to weigh together, not automatic disqualifiers.
