## Why Rob thinks B2B SaaS is the best startup business model

- Rob's core case is recurring revenue. One-off purchases mean "start of the month zero revenue," while with MRR you start each month roughly where you left off, minus churn. This is why businesses push subscriptions for underwear, razors and snack boxes.
- In 2008 he lost 80% of his revenue overnight because his software product was a one-time sale. Recurring revenue does not make a business recession-proof, but it dampens swings. A downturn might cost 20% a month rather than 80% at once, because some customers will not or cannot cancel.
- He says B2B SaaS depends less on luck than most startups. Marketplaces, social networks, medtech and biotech depend on virality, scale or invention. SaaS has a repeatable path: find a problem businesses will pay to solve, validate it, build, iterate and do customer development.
- The risk in SaaS is market risk (will anyone pay?), not "can this be invented?". He says there are only about 20 B2B SaaS marketing approaches (cold email outreach, SEO, content, integrations, events) and you work down the list.
- B2B SaaS is "boring, but much higher likelihood of success" if you follow rules of thumb. He calls it repeatability, not a guarantee, and says "There's no blueprint". He deliberately titled his book *The SaaS Playbook*, not a blueprint.

## Margins, bootstrapping and exit multiples

- At scale SaaS can reach 80-90% gross margins and 30-50% net margins. Zoom went public with more cash in the bank than it had raised from investors.
- High margin means you do not need venture funding to build one. Rob is an investor in about 220 SaaS companies and sees funding as an accelerant when the unit economics are worked out, but says you have the option to bootstrap.
- Exit multiples are the highest he knows of. Friends selling e-commerce sites get about 2x EBITDA or 1x revenue, and an agency sold for about 1x trailing recurring revenue. In SaaS he has seen 7x topline revenue exits, and a 15x exit on a SaaS doing $3M (a $45M price).
- He says the enterprise value created per dollar of MRR is unmatched, and that the predictability of the revenue is the reason.
- Buyers: as a partner at Discretion Capital (a sell-side M&A firm for B2B SaaS at $2M-$20M ARR), he estimates roughly 70-80% of exits go to private equity and 20-30% to strategics. He says not to quote him on the exact numbers.
- His theory for why PE likes SaaS is uniformity. Churn, lifetime value, ARPA and price points let a buyer judge quality quickly, whereas marketplaces, social networks and medtech have no uniform way to assess health.

## Horizontal vs vertical vs orthogonal SaaS

- **Horizontal** SaaS can be used by any company in any industry. **Vertical** SaaS targets a specific niche or industry (for example a CRM for construction firms or municipalities).
- **Orthogonal** is a category Rob coined while classifying his portfolio. The product is technically horizontal, but it targets a specific role or title, such as HR directors.
- Knowing who your customer is and where they are is a huge advantage. With a defined role or industry you can cold-outreach the right people and pick the right in-person events.
- Across his 224 investments, horizontal is a minority, roughly 20-30% (he does not know the exact figure). Horizontal companies often grow well, then hit headwinds at roughly $500K-$2M ARR from well-funded competitors. Vertical and orthogonal face less of this.
- Vertical SaaS tends to show lower churn, and often net negative churn once it has traction.
- Why vertical beats a giant like HubSpot for bootstrappers:
  - You build the handful of features that niche wants, in its own terminology, as a pre-configured product.
  - Customers do not have to pay consultants to set up a complex horizontal tool, and you can add onboarding and training services.
  - Marketing and sales cost less because you only talk to one segment.
  - You can often charge a premium. His example is a Microsoft Word license at about $75 versus a specialist Bible-language word processor at $895.
  - Retention is better.
- Rob says venture money mostly flows into horizontal markets. A founder with $100K-$500K or no funding will not have much luck competing head-on with HubSpot.

## Why niching works in B2B but not B2C

- In B2C you cannot charge much, if anything. It is ad-based or about $4-$5/month, with B2C SaaS around $100/year, so you need huge scale and a narrow niche does not provide it.
- B2B annual contract values in his portfolio run roughly $5,000 to $250,000, so you do not need many customers to build a roughly $5M ARR bootstrapped business.
- High contract values pay for cold outreach (cold DMs, LinkedIn, email). That only works when you know who the customer is, which is why horizontal SaaS cannot do it.
- Even if you know your B2C niche (tall people in Minnesota), you lack the money for outreach.
- He agreed with the host that small differentiation matters more in B2B: a lawn-care CRM that handles seasonality and other industry quirks wins on "they get me", even if 95% of features match a competitor.

## Net negative churn and net revenue retention

- Revenue churn is the MRR lost in a month divided by MRR at the start of the month. Example: $1,000 lost out of $10,000 is 10% churn.
- Contraction is existing customers shrinking. Expansion is existing customers growing (his example is an email list growing from a $25 to a $50 plan).
- When expansion exceeds gross churn plus contraction, you have net negative churn: with zero new customers, MRR still grows. At 3% net negative churn the business grows 3% a month. The host's equivalent is net revenue retention above 100%.
- It is a minority of SaaS companies that get here. It usually needs pricing on a value metric (seats in a CRM, subscribers in an ESP), very low churn and strong product-market fit. He has seen -12% net churn.
- Buyers weigh it heavily. He has seen similar businesses where one with strong net revenue retention gets 10x ARR, while one with 6% monthly churn gets 3x.

## The 2-20-200 validation framework

- He says he lands on this approach, though not the only way.
- **2 hours:** research the idea online. Do keyword research in Google, check YouTube, G2 and Capterra, look at competitors and read Reddit complaints. Run it across about 10 ideas to see which have demand, and look for an angle, such as Capterra complaints about competitors.
- **20 hours:** build a landing page with the value proposition found in research (H1, H2, email capture or a booking link such as SavvyCal). Run a smoke test, talk about it in your audience, and do cold outreach on LinkedIn and to your warm network to validate assumptions.
- **200 hours:** build an MVP and get it into users' hands. With AI this might become about 40 hours, and it is not always possible in 200.
- Purpose: many developers build for six months in a basement for something nobody wants, having skipped two hours in Ahrefs and avoided cold outreach. He asks "what are you doing with your life" about developers who fear sales.
- Start with problem-solution fit before product-market fit. The early solution can be virtual assistants in the Philippines collating spreadsheets, or Zapier-style automation, Make or Airtable.
- This is the early module of his course on early-stage B2B SaaS (the 2-20-200 framework is one of 27 modules).
- He learned from Drip (founded 2012, sold 2016) that you must learn your real users even if you are one of them. It was meant for SaaS companies, but bloggers, info-marketers and course creators outgrew the SaaS revenue, so he had to learn how they talk and work.

## AI and SaaS: not dead, five categories

- Rob rejects "SaaS is dead". He compares it to claims that SEO was dead 15 years ago and that no-code would end SaaS. No-code did have an effect: at Tiny Seed and MicroConf they run 3-4 custom line-of-business apps in Airtable that replaced apps costing $10-15K a year. He estimates no-code took perhaps 15-20% of custom vertical SaaS, not the whole market.
- His brother runs a construction firm and will not build his own software, because it would be buggy, could be hacked, and he does not want to host it. Rob warns about Dunning-Kruger and says the complexity of SaaS is not something AI will solve soon, though he allows that 20 years out is uncertain.
- The host added that maintenance is the hidden cost and that "vibe maintenance" is not a term. Rob agreed, saying AI cannot maintain code yet and that maintenance dwarfs build cost (a rough old figure was $500K to build versus about $3M over 20 years).
- Rob's five categories of AI use in B2B SaaS:
  1. **AI as the core business.** Without AI there is no business (foundation models; fiscal.ai for financial data; Rosie, an AI answering service for service businesses; he is invested in both).
  2. **AI as a feature.** The product survives without it (Notion's writing assistant, Zoom's meeting summaries, Loom's AI titles and chapters). This is the most common in his investments.
  3. **AI for building the product.** Copilot, Cursor, Windsurf, v0. He is not convinced it makes people faster, noting early research that suggests it may not.
  4. **AI for growth.** Marketing and sales (Clay for outbound, Jasper or Copy.ai for content, personalized email, ad optimization).
  5. **AI for operating the business.** Support tickets, resume screening, and checking a hotel or venue contract for non-standard terms, which saved a lawyer round and won concessions.
- Categories 3-5 apply to non-software startups too.
- Investing view: Tiny Seed does not fund many AI-as-core companies because they tend to have high churn and heavy platform risk. They prefer AI as a feature, where there is still a moat without AI and AI helps retention.
- The host's refinement, which Rob liked: don't bolt on a chatbot, and treat AI as an enabler of new features. The best AI features are baked into the workflow and not obviously labeled AI. Rob's example is a portfolio company for real estate buyers' agents that uses AI on listing photos to extract property features not in the listing, so agents can search by them.

- He has worked in this space for nearly 20 years. Each product came from a need: masterminds, funding, in-person events.
