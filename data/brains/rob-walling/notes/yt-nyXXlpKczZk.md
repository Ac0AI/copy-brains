## Rob's stair-step approach to bootstrapping

- Rob started with tiny software products in tiny niches, expecting "a couple grand a month, five grand a month". Stacked together, they let him quit his job.
- He had about five or six such products at once. Examples:
  - .NET invoicing software with source code, sold one-time for $300, doing $3-5k/month on SEO and AdWords only.
  - A job board for power line electricians ("linemen") doing about $2k/month.
  - An ebook.
- Together they reached about $10k/month, enough to quit his job and live in California. He later described it as "120k a year" and almost literally the four-hour work week.
- He wrote a long essay called "The Stair-Step Approach to Bootstrapping". The idea is to start small with a single traffic source, then parlay one thing into the next.
- He argues people think too big. Don't read TechCrunch or VentureBeat for this. Ask "how can you get to a thousand dollars a month... five thousand... ten grand a month", which is "way harder than people make it out to be."
- Ideas don't need to be huge. There are thousands, if not tens of thousands, of niche SaaS companies nobody has heard of doing millions a year.
  - Examples he cites: software for analyzing one component of a car, and payroll pension plan software.
  - Friends of his sold tiny-niche companies for tens of millions.
- Rob wants "repeatable small wins, base hits" with a roughly 50/50 chance over time, versus roughly a 1 in 10,000 shot at being Zuckerberg.

## Learning marketing one channel at a time

- Rob was a developer who learned marketing as a "tool belt". Without marketing or sales you have nothing, and "these things don't sell themselves." Great products with no awareness fail.
- Only about 20-40 marketing approaches work for this kind of software, and you need only a couple to build a good business.
- He learned them one at a time by trial and error, then reused the playbook on each new app:
  - The .NET invoicing product taught him Google AdWords and SEO.
  - He applied AdWords and SEO to the next app, then learned display advertising, then applied all three to the following one.
- His development skills were the superpower because he never had to rely on contractors to maintain, deploy and fix bugs.
- He learned from direct-response books (Dan Kennedy and similar) on copywriting, marketing and sales. He disliked the sleazy parts but kept what was true.
  - "Marketing is just math and human psychology."
  - Math is the funnel. For example, 100 visitors, 5 trials, 20 convert.
  - Psychology means leading with the pain point and benefits, not features.
- Early mistake: he spent about $400 on AdWords before it worked. He learned it isn't "set it and forget it". You must pick keywords carefully and monitor, and be capital efficient without funding.

## What to build first, and who should (not) start a SaaS

- Rob says not to start with a SaaS app as a first product, even if you're technical. It is "really, really hard," especially without funding.
- Better first products have built-in distribution:
  - WordPress plugin
  - Shopify add-on
  - Course or info product
  - App store listings, where you get discovered
- That lets you focus on code, customer support, deciding features and talking to customers, and learn those skills before building a full SaaS.
- He says a founder needs at least two of three things: technical skills, marketing/sales skills, or funding.
  - Money alone with neither skill will "crash and burn."
  - Money can buy the missing skill. Otherwise learn it, which takes longer but works.
- For non-technical founders, Rob recommends against hiring a single developer. A product role is also needed, and good product people cost over $50k.
  - Better to find a reputable agency that handles product and project management, at a premium.
  - He compares it to remodeling a kitchen: you hire a general contractor with a project management fee rather than six tradespeople.
- Hiring developers is hard for non-technical people. His advice:
  - Go to your network and communities: Indie Hackers, MicroConf (Connect has about 2,000 founders, free), Dynamite Circle, Tropical MBA, Twitter, TinySeed's Slack groups.
  - Ask for recommendations.
  - Reviewing the code is the real hard part after hiring.
  - Bad Upwork stories are common: founders burn $5-10k.

## Buying instead of building

- Rob had about $10k saved from consulting and realized he'd rather pay $5-10k for an app than spend six months of nights and weekends.
- He paid $11,000 for the .NET invoicing app, which "scared the [expletive] out of me." It was more than he had ever spent on anything, including a car.
- He calls this a "secret" early on: buy things and grow them, skipping the first 18 months of grind to find product-market fit.
- He acquired HitTail in 2011. It was an SEO keyword tool SaaS doing about $1,500/month, with technical problems. He grew it as the only employee, with some contractors.
- He once hired a contractor on Upwork for a ColdFusion app he didn't want to maintain. He preferred Canada or South America for time zones, but today would stay in his own time zone.
- He built Drip (email service provider, similar to Mailchimp) from 2012 and sold it in 2016. From his first launch to the final sale took 11-13 years.

## Validate before you build

- Rob originally wrote "start marketing the day you start coding". He now regrets that and says to start marketing before coding, through pre-validation.
- Pick a problem that is solved poorly or not at all. Then find people with it and see if they will pay for a solution. This is hard and scary work.
- Ways to find people: cold calls and emails, your network, conferences, Facebook and Slack groups. Example: talk to 20 lawyers, and if none need it, don't build.
- The alternative is to spend $10-15k over six months building, then do the same outreach anyway after launch. Better to do the hard work first.
- Another validation method: check organic keyword search volume for the space (his illustration was something like 50,000 searches a month, which he said was made up).
- Drip validation: he had a small audience from his podcast and blog. He emailed that he was thinking of building it, and about 10 or 11 people committed to trying it and said they'd pay $100/month. He took that as validation.
- On idea theft: he told lots of people his idea and "zero people stole that idea." Execution and marketing are the hard part.

## Funding, TinySeed and "build your business, not your slide deck"

- Rob never raised money for his own startups. He worked a day job or consulted, then spent 20 hours on nights and weekends. He built one product to $1k/month, used that to buy the next, and so on, until selling Drip.
- "Build your business instead of your slide deck. The best slide deck is revenue." For example, at $10k/month you can raise a round, which a slide deck rarely does.
- Most institutional investors and many angels won't fund pre-revenue ideas, since you've proven nothing until someone pays.
- TinySeed is an accelerator for bootstrapped and "mostly bootstrapped" SaaS founders.
  - First fund: $4.5M, 23 companies, about $120-180k each. A second fund of roughly $26-27M was about to close.
  - It funds people with traction. Applicants arrive from about $1k MRR up to $100k+, with a sweet spot of about $5k/month and up.
  - Advice at the end: don't apply until about $500 to a couple thousand dollars in revenue.
  - Its 42-point checklist comes down to the people, product-market fit and pricing (price sensitivity). He looks for people who ship and don't ask permission.
- He defines "venture funded" as raising roughly $1M+ from institutions, which puts you on the unicorn track of raising every two years and going big or going bust. Angels, small funds and accelerators suit a business aiming for $5-20M a year.

## Execution, velocity and psychology

- The difference between successful founders and the rest is that they "take action." Examples:
  - One founder writes versus-comparison SEO articles in two days while another takes months.
  - Derek Reimer, his Drip co-founder (now SavvyCal), launches features so fast that velocity alone makes success likely. Not every marketing approach lands, but doing more means half need to work.
- "Most things are speed bumps", not roadblocks. Don't wait for permission from investors, publishers, or a coding skill. Use no-code or do the work manually first (offer SEO manually, for example).
- Patience is undervalued: "11, 12, 13 years" from his first app to his final sale.
- He calls it the "terror of firsts": the first blog post, podcast, book, conference talk. It isn't scary for long, and by the 10th or 50th time you won't feel it.
- His regret: being too risk-averse and too worried about criticism for years. His parting advice: take bigger risks within reason, take action every day, and don't wait for permission.
- He also tells founders to ignore comparison with others and set your own milestones, since managing your own psychology is one of the hardest founder tasks.
