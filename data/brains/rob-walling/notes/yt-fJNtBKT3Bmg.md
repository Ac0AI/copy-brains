## Why Rob calls freemium a marketing strategy, not a pricing strategy

- Rob says most founders treat freemium as a pricing strategy. He credits Patrick Campbell of ProfitWell as probably the first to see it as a marketing strategy.
- His reasoning: the price is zero, so you earn nothing. Pricing strategy is about value metrics and getting the most from the people who get the most value.
- In his view, freemium is "a way for you to get more leads into your funnel."
- As a default, he believes bootstrappers should generally avoid freemium as a marketing strategy.

## Freemium vs. free trial: the definitions Rob uses

- **Free trial:** a limit, usually on time (7, 14 or 30 days). It can also be a usage cap in total, such as converting PDFs five times and then the trial ends.
- **Freemium:** he describes it as "forever free," with very limited capacity.
  - PDF converter example: one conversion per month, every month, forever.
  - Email service provider example: up to 100 subscribers on the list, forever free. The trial equivalent would be a 14-day trial or 100 total sends.

## Why bootstrappers shouldn't copy Slack, Figma, Notion, Dropbox

- Freemium looks like a good default because big venture-funded companies used it well: Slack, Figma, Notion, many no-code tools and Dropbox. It became almost the default for B2C-ish SaaS 10-15 years ago.
- Those companies have hundreds of employees and tens or hundreds of millions of dollars raised, and many are now public.
- A bootstrapper with a team of one to three can't model that. Success depends on when and how you implement freemium, not just on adding it.
- Adopting it only because others did is the real problem.

## The three risks of freemium for a bootstrapper

1. **It pushes revenue down the line.**
   - Rob cites Dropbox's line that roughly 3% of free users become paying customers within a year.
   - A founder trying to quit their day job and reach $5K-$10K a month can't wait 12 months for 3% of free users to pay. They need willing payers to pay sooner.
2. **It doesn't market itself.**
   - A free plan still has to be marketed.
   - Example: Marie at T.O (a podcast guest) grew to 300,000 users on freemium. She started by sending thousands of cold DMs to get people onto the free plan.
3. **It needs huge volume.**
   - 10,000 free users usually won't get you far. He says you need 50,000, 100,000 or more, depending on conversion rate.
   - Launching a free plan and getting some signups won't produce meaningful revenue quickly.

## Backing out of a free plan, and the 10x pricing thought experiment

- Rob says you can pull back on freemium after launch. Expect some pushback.
- Two options: remove the free plan from the signup page and grandfather existing free users, or shut it down and make everyone pay.
- His example: in 2011 he acquired an app called HitTail with a free plan that cost a lot in support, headaches and server resources.
  - He shut the free plan down and told users they had to start paying.
  - Of roughly a thousand free users (he recalls "I think it was a thousand"), only a dozen or two dozen stayed on and paid.
  - It was painful and drew many complaints, but it worked.
- When a founder says they want a free plan, he asks the opposite question as a thought experiment: what if you 10x your pricing instead, to lower churn and go upmarket? What would the product have to do to be worth 10x the price? He says this is a useful exercise.
- He discusses freemium at length with Ruben Gomez in episode 725 of Startups for the Rest of Us.

## Four conditions that must hold before bootstrappers use freemium

Rob borrows these from Ruben Gomez, whom he calls one of the world's experts on freemium for bootstrap founders.

1. Almost zero support needed per customer.
2. Almost zero incremental cost per additional user.
   - Usually fails if free users trigger paid API calls (such as AI requests) or send SMS messages, because it gets expensive.
3. Users can self-onboard and get value from the app very quickly.
4. Some measure of virality or very strong word of mouth.
   - Rob adds that if he didn't have virality, he wouldn't use freemium.

His verdict: with all four in place, the answer is still only "maybe." Without them, he would avoid freemium "at all costs."
