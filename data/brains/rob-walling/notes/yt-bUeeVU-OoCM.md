## How Rob chooses a first SaaS marketing channel: two questions

- Rob says there is no one-size-fits-all way to market a SaaS company. His book *The SaaS Playbook* lists 20 B2B SaaS marketing approaches he has seen people use. A free sample chapter is at saasplaybook.com.
- Examples he names from the 20: content, SEO, pay-per-click ads, cold outreach, partnerships and integrations (grouped as one approach), affiliate marketing, free tools, hangouts and Q&A sites.
- To pick where to start, he asks two questions:
  1. **Where do your customers hang out, and where do they look for a solution like yours?**
     - If they go to Google, try SEO.
     - If they go to YouTube, get interviewed on other people's channels, or make videos that answer the questions people ask when trying to solve the problem.
     - If you have the budget, buy pay-per-click ads instead of doing SEO.
     - Cold outreach (email, LinkedIn DMs, X/Twitter) works for finding qualified people.
  2. **Do you have an advantage with any of the approaches?** He does not mean "which do I want to do?", which he calls "a terrible question".

## Do the marketing you don't want to do

- Rob uses a weight-loss comparison: to get fit you eat spinach and work out, not ice cream. Building a business works the same way.
- If you only want to write code and post to Twitter, he says to "go find a hobby" and not start a SaaS company. Success requires doing things you don't want to do, and marketing and sales may be two of them.
- Posting to Twitter and social media "does not count as marketing". He rejects "I'm building an audience so I can someday sell my SaaS to it".
- He recommends approaches that move the needle: content, SEO, cold outreach, free tools, affiliate marketing, hangouts and Q&A sites.
- He describes the typical failure as a founder who codes for 6 months, launches on Product Hunt or posts once on Reddit, and hears crickets.

## Scoring channels with ICE in a spreadsheet

- Rob lists all candidate approaches in a Google Sheet and scores each from 1 to 10 on three measures:
  - **Impact:** how big the result is if it works.
  - **Confidence:** how likely it is to succeed.
  - **Ease:** how easy it is to execute.
- He multiplies the three scores into one number per approach.
- He does not just sort descending and pick the top one. He sanity-checks the result, since the scores involve some guesswork.
- Ways to inform the scores:
  - online research;
  - talking to someone who knows marketing;
  - reading *Traction* by Gabriel Weinberg;
  - watching YouTube;
  - talking it through with ChatGPT or Claude to learn where customers are and how they search for a solution.

## The three-factor framework: pick one fast and one slow approach

- Rob applies his three-factor framework to marketing approaches. The factors are:
  - **Speed:** how quickly it works.
  - **Cost:** usually dollars, but it can be time.
  - **Scalability:** how far it scales.
- Nothing is fast, cheap and highly scalable. If you find such an approach, "you are very, very lucky".
- Example: posting on Q&A sites or answering Reddit threads is fast and low cost but not very scalable.
- After the ICE analysis he picks **one fast approach and one slow approach**.
  - Fast approaches can get a customer "tomorrow or today", for example cold and warm outreach, including DMing people he knows.
  - Slow approaches such as content and SEO can take about 6 months. They are usually more scalable and build a flywheel.
- The aim is long-term growth from the slow channel and customers now from the fast one.

## How long to run a channel before quitting

- Rob learns the approach, executes hard, and grinds until it clearly works or clearly doesn't.
- **It's working if it brings in leads:** people he is having conversations with.
- If leads arrive but nobody signs up or buys, there are two possible causes, and telling them apart is a judgment call:
  - no product-market fit (the product doesn't solve a problem people want);
  - the wrong prospects are coming in.
- Time to invest before pulling the plug:
  - most approaches: at least 2 to 3 months;
  - SEO: 6 or more months.
- Example with cold outreach: early on the message, list, copy and maybe the product won't be great. So he doesn't send emails for a week and conclude it failed. He studies how to do it, executes for months, and tunes if results are mediocre. If he gets no results after several months, he probably pulls the plug.
- Winning channels tend to have "asymmetric upside". He says you don't need 5 or 10 approaches to build a successful product. One or two that work well can grow month after month, though you must keep innovating and pushing on them.

## The "Big Five" channels most bootstrapped founders should start with

- For founders who don't want to work it out from scratch, Rob says roughly 80% should start with his "Big Five", listed in no particular order:
  1. Content
  2. SEO
  3. Pay-per-click ads
  4. Cold outreach
  5. Partnerships and integrations
- He chose them because they are by far the five most-used B2B SaaS approaches among bootstrapped and mostly bootstrapped founders.
- His evidence is his 212 B2B SaaS investments, the tens of thousands of SaaS founders he interacts with through his books, podcast and accelerator, and what he sees working in TinySeed.
- He says he suspects content marketing will be the choice for many founders.
- He claims a content framework that has helped portfolio companies reach their first $100,000 in ARR. He says he shares it with every founder he invests in, but the details aren't given in this source.
- His book *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding* (2011, revised about 2 years before this video) is the basis for much of the framework.
