## The SaaS plateau equation: new MRR divided by churn

- Rob says "churn is the death of SaaS, but churn causes plateaus."
- Formula: a SaaS plateaus at (new MRR added per month) ÷ (monthly revenue churn).
- Example: adding $3,000 of new MRR with 5% monthly revenue churn plateaus at $60k MRR.
- Cutting churn to 2.5% moves that plateau to $120k MRR, about a $1.4M business.
- Unless the fundamentals (new MRR or churn) change, "that's your business. You're going to plateau. Predict it. Avoid it."
- He reviewed the MRR graphs of all 192 Tiny Seed investments and asked founders in Slack what caused each plateau and how they got through it.
- About 85-90% of the companies that plateaued never broke through, so predicting and avoiding a plateau beats hitting one.
- The seven plateaus apply after emerging product-market fit, from roughly $10-30k MRR up into the millions of ARR.
- The solutions are usually obvious. The useful part is diagnosing which plateau you are in.
- The first four plateaus are by far the most common. The last three are rarer.
- The examples he shows are mid-six to seven-figure businesses.

## Plateau 1: Leaky funnel (low-touch trial and signup funnel)

- Churn is excluded here. A leaky funnel is the path from hearing about you to trial to paid.
- Rob defines it as funnel metrics falling outside his rule-of-thumb ranges for low-touch funnels. The ranges are in the SaaS Playbook (sasplaybook.com/resources).
- If you are within the ranges, the funnel is probably fine. If you are below them, fix the funnel first.
- Example: a credit-card-up-front trial converting trial to paid at 20% is the weakest part of the funnel, and he knows it from experience.
- He defends rules of thumb against critics who call them "common knowledge." They tell you where to look first.
- Fix: troubleshoot the funnel starting at the bottom.
- Example, ScrapingBee (Pierre):
  - They sold a commodity and were 50-100% more expensive than competitors on concurrency.
  - Once new infrastructure let them match price, new customers per month almost doubled "overnight."
  - They kept the nominal plan price and increased what each plan offered.
  - This helped them avoid a plateau as they neared the point where churn equals new MRR.
- Rob calls this a clever way to raise pricing. At Drip he liked the $49.99 and $149 price points, so he lowered the subscriber count per plan twice. That pushed people to upgrade sooner without awkward new prices like $59 and $159.

## Plateau 2: High churn

- Rob's churn thresholds are in the SaaS Playbook. He means gross revenue churn, not net churn and not logo churn.
- Enterprise or annual-contract companies should sit about two points below the thresholds.
- About 2-3% monthly churn is fine for a non-enterprise company.
- 9% monthly churn is "almost catastrophic."
- Common mistake: founders with 9-10% monthly churn say they "just need more leads."
  - He says "You can't possibly outrun your churn."
  - At that rate you turn over the whole customer base in about eight months, so it is "not a subscription business at all."
  - More leads is the "fix all" people reach for, but it fixes nothing if the fundamentals are broken.
- Fixes depend on why people leave. You can build missing features or checkbox features.
- More often he sees founders stop serving high-churn customers:
  - Low-paying prosumer and B2C-ish customers churn the most and cause the most headaches.
  - Higher-paying customers churn less, sometimes with net negative churn, and are less of a pain.
  - Moving up market often means cancelling your lowest plan, even without raising prices.
- Examples:
  - CartHook (Jordan G): plans started at $49/month and up. He set a $300/month minimum plus a demo with him, and turned away new Shopify stores. Churn was catastrophic, and he wanted a multi-million dollar business. Rob calls this the "velvet rope" policy.
  - Gather: the bottom plan was $29/month when they joined Tiny Seed, with one demo per close. Rob called that not viable. By the end of the season the bottom plan was about $200-250/month, after about 18 months of moving up market.
  - JBoard (Marty):
    - They never hit a plateau, but their analytics predicted an MRR decline from high churn.
    - Trial users split into solopreneurs or wantrepreneurs drawn by low prices, and established businesses that didn't convert because of missing key features.
    - Marty built those features and almost doubled pricing while keeping funnel conversion rates. That avoided the projected decline.

## Plateau 3: Not enough leads

- Most people jump to this first. Rob lists it third because the cause is usually funnel or churn.
- Diagnose by elimination: if churn is good or great, funnel metrics are within his ranges, and you still aren't growing, you need more leads.
- Leads needed depend on price. $10,000/month software needs far fewer leads than $10-50/month software.
- Example, Flatplan (Kieran), enterprise sales:
  - They plateaued for a long time, perhaps about nine months. Funnel metrics and retention were strong and they closed many deals.
  - Before Tiny Seed they relied on inbound referrals from one large partner and some SEO.
  - The Tiny Seed marketing playbook call used the three-factor framework (speed, cost, scalability) to pick channels. The fast channels (cold outreach, paid clicks) didn't work for them.
  - Kieran: "I accepted it would be a slog." He doubled down on slow, scalable channels: partnerships, organic search, daily LinkedIn posting, a podcast, and recently events.
  - Rob notes you must hold your nerve when tracking is muddy. Kieran is still a bottleneck and is trying to hire a salesperson.
  - Rob: "That is almost the definition of SaaS."

## Plateau 4: Competition and shifting product-market fit

- Rob has no numbers for this one, only how it feels. You keep hearing "we went with HubSpot because they have a CRM" or "we went with Mailchimp because they're 30% cheaper."
- Product-market fit is a moving target. You can have it and lose it. The product stays the same while the market shifts.
- Example: a Tiny Seed company was growing well until a free open-source competitor arrived. It pulled leads and caused churn, and the founder felt he was losing product-market fit.
- Even a bootstrapped company at $0.5M-1.5M ARR can stop working if a funded competitor undercuts price by half and loses money for years.
- Response: admit what is happening and reposition. Rob points to April Dunford's positioning work and Anthony's MicroConf talk on positioning. Ask how you differentiate, which may mean re-entering the market with a new angle if you have the energy.
- Example, UserList (Benedict):
  - Plateau around 2023, after about six or seven years in the market.
  - Building visual workflows broke the plateau. Benedict's hunch was that it had become a table-stakes feature, and not having it made people dismiss UserList even though its linear campaigns covered most use cases.
  - Rob does not give developers permission to build features randomly: "talk to your customers first."

## Plateaus 5-7: Tapped-out market, one-segment fit, one-time events

- **5. Tapped-out market.** It is very rare, and usually an excuse to avoid hard work in marketing, sales and product-market fit.
  - Founders often respond by launching a second product or translating the app into Spanish.
  - Senior Place (Jason and JD) serves only single-digit thousands of potential customers and keeps growing.
  - If you have truly tapped out (corroborated by about five people, not founder gut feel), expand horizontally or add verticals. For a realtor product, that means real estate attorneys or mortgage brokers.
  - Postpone.app (Grant): a Reddit scheduler wasn't a seven-or-eight-figure business, so he added Bluesky, Instagram, Threads, Facebook and more. Grant credits breaking the plateau (about six months or more) to a 25% price increase and new platforms, which raised signups and expansion revenue. Users cancelled other scheduling apps to manage all social accounts in one place.
- **6. Strong product-market fit with only one segment.**
  - Blended averages of churn, ARPA and LTV are like a three-star rating that hides a pile of five-star and one-star reviews.
  - Segment customers by industry, lead source and price paid, in a spreadsheet, to find the ICP and the "worst customer profile."
  - Fix: build features to win the weaker segment, or use the velvet rope and stop serving the poor-fit segment, provided the ICP is big enough.
  - He looks at TRM (total reachable market) instead of TAM: how many people per month are switching or newly looking. A $1B TAM with five buyers a month won't grow quickly.
  - Example, Monolith Forensics (Matt Danner): segment A (governments and law enforcement) has high fit. Segment B (private digital forensics labs) plateaus because of missing case and analysis management features. He is building those features and still markets heavily to A.
- **7. One-time events.**
  - Examples: angering your customer base (for instance, raising prices repeatedly and communicating it badly), bad press, a major marketing setback such as Google changes hitting an 80% SEO lead source, or a black swan like COVID.
  - Fix: apologize and fix the problem if you angered customers.
  - During COVID some Tiny Seed applicants doubled monthly while others went to nearly zero.
- Honorable mention is seasonality (Glitch Secure plateaus yearly). He doesn't know a fix, so it isn't on the list.
