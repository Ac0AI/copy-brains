## Why funded competition is dangerous and niches are the defense

- Rob has seen funded competition crush a bootstrapped company first-hand. AdWords can go from $.50 to $5 per click overnight. A funded rival can hire a PR firm and land in *Inc.*, *Fast Company* and *Wired* in the same month. Rankings drop as competitors hire high-end SEO firms.
- He says it's possible to beat funded competition. But fighting them head to head is "like wrestling 2 weight classes above your own."
- His fix is to avoid the head-on fight. Choose a niche the funded competitor can't afford to enter.
- Funded companies can break bootstrapping rules because the money in their bank gives them "a bit of a reality distortion field." His advice is for bootstrapped companies.
- Rob frames success as a continuum, not a binary. Each item on his list lowers the chance of reaching profitability. None of them guarantees failure. The view comes from observing and working with over 1,000 entrepreneurs.

## Go vertical: why niches beat horizontal markets for bootstrappers

- Rob calls not targeting a niche (going horizontal) perhaps the most common mistake he saw around 2009. He saw it declining by 2011. He credits his own advice and Jason Cohen's, or notes that going niche may have become the obvious approach.
- Niches are easier to penetrate and cheaper to advertise to.
- They have less competition and higher profit margins.
- Word of mouth is "a vibrant force" in a niche.
- He concludes vertical niches are "the best option, by far" for someone bootstrapping.

## Build recurring revenue instead of one-time sales

- Rob calls this a more recent lesson: "one-time sales suck."
- With one-time sales you start every month at $0 in revenue and must hustle to hit your number. He says starting from zero every 30 days is "no way to grow a business."
- Successful businesses he has watched grow always had a built-in recurring revenue stream.
  - Netflix moved from one-off movies to subscriptions in the late 90s.
  - Many desktop software vendors were moving to subscription billing.
  - Dharmesh Shah promoted recurring payments in his Business of Software 2011 talk.
- If your product has a one-time fee, he encourages you to find a way to turn it into a recurring model.

## Pricing math: why $1/month and low price points fail

- Rob says you can make money charging $1 a month only with millions of customers. Reaching them takes more money and time than a bootstrapper has. Even with a gigantic market and the expertise or funds to reach it, "support will kill you."
- His worked example for a goal of $2k per month:
  1. You need 2k customers at that price.
  2. That is a lot of non-technical customers to support for little money, on nights and weekends while holding a full-time job.
  3. At a 1% conversion rate, 2k customers needs 200k unique visitors in total (not monthly).
  4. Even with hard promotion and word of mouth, he could imagine ramping to only 1k-2k uniques per month. At 4k per month, hitting 200k takes a long time.
- His advice now is to go up-market with $30/month and $50/month price points.
- He asks what you can build that delivers that much value to customers.

## Avoid freemium and two-sided markets

- **Freemium:** Rob says free plans rarely work for bootstrapped companies. He points to a guest post by Ruben, "Why Free Plans Don't Work" (August 2010).
- **Two-sided markets:** One customer group is hard enough to market to. Finding and managing two groups is "just brutal."
  - Examples he gives are Elance, eBay and Monster.com.
  - Any ad-supported website also counts, since you must sell to both advertisers and visitors.
- It can be done, but it doubles (or triples) the marketing needed to get off the ground.

## Start marketing when you start coding

- Rob lists "starting your marketing once your product is complete" as a mistake.
- Early marketing does several things:
  - It validates the idea.
  - It builds an instant beta list.
  - It gives you a stronger launch day.
  - It builds links over time.
  - It builds confidence in the idea, which you otherwise lack during six months of coding alone in a basement.
- His example is a rumor that Dropbox had 200,000 emails by launch. They reportedly got them by driving heavy traffic to a landing page with one video explaining how the service would work once built.
- He jokes that if you gather 200,000 emails instead of building a product to pitch investors, "I'll write you a check."
- He points to his post "Why You Should Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding."

## Don't do everything yourself, and expect it to be hard

- **Doing everything yourself:** Even a 1.0 launch takes hundreds of hours, covering graphic design, coding, marketing and setting up a payment processor. Insisting on doing every step yourself lowers the chance you reach launch day. He calls this need for control "one of the most common startup roadblocks."
- **Expecting it to be easy:** Running out of money is often called the top cause of startup death. Rob says that doesn't apply to bootstrappers, because "out of money is where you start." The goal from day 1 is to go from out of money to covering hosting.
- For bootstrappers, the top cause of death is running out of motivation.
  - Expecting to go from nothing to $100,000 in revenue in a few months of part-time work makes you more likely to quit when it doesn't happen.
  - The day you decide to throw in the towel is the day the startup dies.
- Starting a company is not easy, and he says that difficulty is probably why you wanted to start one.
