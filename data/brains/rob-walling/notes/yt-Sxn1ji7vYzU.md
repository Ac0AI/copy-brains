## Rob's framework: five stages of customer awareness applied to B2B content

- Rob built this after a Tiny Seed founder in 2023 wrote down 15 blog post ideas and then panicked: "how do I prioritize any of this?" Rob realized there was no framework for it.
- He takes Eugene Schwartz's five stages of awareness (1950s-60s, written for consumer ads) and applies them to B2B content. Most prior art he found was B2C.
- He says this applies even to founders who do only outbound sales. The last two stages need content (case studies, versus pages, demos) even if it is "sales literature" and not marketing.
- Rob's rule of thumb: if you have nothing, start at the bottom (most aware) and work backwards up the funnel.

The five stages, with his email-marketing-for-realtors example:
1. **Unaware**: the prospect doesn't know they have a problem (a realtor doesn't know they need more leads). In B2B this is the hardest and "you really kind of don't want to be there". It means cold outreach plus education, or inventing a category.
2. **Problem aware**: they know the problem but not the solutions. They search "how do I send bulk email" or "email marketing templates", not product names.
3. **Solution aware**: they know the category but not specific products. They search "best email marketing software".
4. **Product aware**: they know your product and compare it with competitors (MailChimp vs Klaviyo, ActiveCampaign vs Drip).
5. **Most aware**: educated on the market and close to buying, or already bought and telling peers.

His B2C illustration: running shoes. Prime Day reminded him he needed shoes, then he searched "best running shoes", then compared model numbers (Nike Streak Fly vs ASICS GT-2000), then searched "buy ASICS GT-2000" and "cheapest".

## Three ways to tell which stage a prospect is in

Rob says attribution is never 100%. If you get to 75-80% attribution of where customers come from, "you're doing pretty well", and this is harder now that Google withholds keywords ("keyword not provided"). The methods below are "good enough".

1. **Ask on sales calls** (high-touch):
   - When do you plan to buy?
   - Are you comparing us to other products, and which ones?
   - Buying soon, or comparing us to MailChimp and Klaviyo, means roughly stage 4-5. Early research with no other vendors considered means roughly stage 2-3.
   - If he were running SaaS sales today, Rob would add a guessed awareness stage to the CRM alongside budget and timeline, to see whether prospects move along.
2. **Read the keywords** (low-touch, cohorts). He maps the SEMrush search intent types to stages:
   - Informational is roughly stage 2-3.
   - Commercial investigation ("best", "top", "review", "X vs Y", "compare") is stage 3-4.
   - Transactional ("buy", "discount", "coupon", "cheap") is stage 5. A coupon-code search for your SaaS means they are ready to buy.
3. **Use tools that label intent**: SEMrush's dashboard tags keywords I (informational), C (commercial), T (transactional), N (navigational). SimilarWeb has an intent column too (nav, transact, local).

## Stages 1-2: unaware and problem-aware content

- Stage 1 content is high-level and slow to convert. His example is Session Lab (workshop agendas), with posts like room setup styles and becoming a great facilitator. It likely gets search traffic but takes a long time to turn into customers. This is why he says start at the bottom.
- Stage 2 content solves a specific problem and doesn't mention your product category. Examples from the Micro Conf community:
  - **Ruben (Sewell)**: most of his traffic is organic Google. He gives away contract templates and agreements, drawn from 25,000 sales documents. People need a template and don't yet know they need e-signature software.
  - **Session Lab**: workshop templates.
  - **Veed** (co-founder spoke at Micro Conf Europe): SEO tool pages like add subtitles to video, add music to video, add text to video. Rob's guess is that they pull visitors through with a freemium offering.
  - **UserList** (Benedict and Jane): "What is user onboarding? The Ultimate Guide for SaaS companies".
- Stage 2 content is general (contracts, blog posts) and says nothing about your product.

## Stage 3: solution-aware content and channels

- Searchers use category terms ("best email marketing software"). Sources include Google, Capterra, G2, Reddit and Quora.
- To check which review site dominates your category, search "best [category]" and "top [category]" in an incognito browser and see whether Capterra, G2 or Software Advice ranks higher. He hopes it's Capterra, "the easiest one to work with and advertise on".
- Capterra ads: people there are usually at stage 3 or 4, so even high cost per click can convert well if you test. Rob points to an interview with a founder "crushing it" with Capterra, found by searching YouTube for "Capterra SaaS marketing" plus his name.
- Monitoring mentions: Rob uses **F5Bot** and **Syften** for Reddit, Hacker News, Twitter, Lobsters, Stack Overflow, Indie Hackers and more.
  - They email or Slack him a few times a week when someone mentions his book, Micro Conf or Tiny Seed.
  - Google Alerts is "useless these days" for him.
  - Anyone naming your product is product-aware by definition (stage 4), so you can join the conversation.
- Content examples: UserList's "SaaS email marketing strategy, everything you need to know" (deeper than their onboarding content).
- Sewell's e-signature funnel walks people through three screens:
  1. What is an electronic signature?
  2. Create your own, with the signature drawn on screen. Rob calls this "engineering as marketing".
  3. "Easy and free e-signatures for all of your documents".
- Rob notes this carries people from stage 2 to stage 4.

## Stage 4: product-aware content (versus, alternative-to, case studies)

- **Alternative-to pages** target people searching for a competitor (e.g. "alternative to HubSpot, Klaviyo, ActiveCampaign, MailChimp"). Use them in your early days to piggyback on the competitor's brand and SEO.
- **Versus pages** ("your product vs competitor") only work once you have a brand people search for. In your first month or year, almost nobody compares you to a competitor. Rob says to keep these for stage 5.
- UserList has a lot of alternative-to pages because not enough people search versus terms for them yet.
- Rob's incognito test on MailChimp vs Klaviyo: MailChimp ranked 2nd and Klaviyo 3rd, each with passive-aggressive copy. Zapier ranked first, even though it is far up the funnel. Companies like Zapier, HubSpot and Honeybadger go after huge traffic terms and don't mind.
- Case studies belong here. They help close one-on-one outbound sales too.

## Stage 5: most-aware content (features, demos, trust)

- At this point you "break the paradigm" of benefits-not-features. When someone first finds you, lead with benefits. Deeper in the funnel they need features.
- Examples: Drip's feature pages (built after Rob left), UserList's feature pages, and UserList's knowledge base with a list of integrations. A buyer judges whether the documentation is good.
- **Long-form demos**: go from the 60-second homepage demo to a 30-45 minute demo. Rob says: "If I'm ready to buy software I will 100% watch a 30 minute or 45 minute demo of someone going through it at 2x." It can be pre-recorded.
- Also: versus pages, case studies, white papers (which Rob personally dislikes).
- **Security and compliance content**: Ruben at Sewell published a page on SOC 2, HIPAA, GDPR, network security and system reliability. A buyer about to purchase cares, while a stage 1-2 reader doesn't.
- HubSpot is his model of a company doing this at every stage (versus pages such as Sales Cloud vs HubSpot Sales, and case studies).

## The 2012-13 Velvet Mail (later Drip) mistake

- Rob and Derek Reichert started the codebase that became Drip, originally named Velvet Mail. Rob paid a writer roughly $5,000-6,000 for a batch of content before launch, to "start marketing the day you start coding".
- Content was released over about six months and brought "crickets".
- Why it failed: no plan, strategy or framework for prioritizing. Titles were generic and top of funnel: "Seven email marketing myths", "A beginner's guide to email marketing", "Five things to know about email marketing".
- The fix is to prioritize by awareness stage, starting with the lower stages where people are closer to buying.
