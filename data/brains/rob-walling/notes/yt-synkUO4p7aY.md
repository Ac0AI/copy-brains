## Qualities Rob looks for in a first developer

- Hire for experience if the budget allows. Rob says training developers (as he and Derek did at Drip because they were budget constrained) is "not something I would recommend."
- Derek's criteria, which Rob endorses: 5+ years as a software developer, full-stack generalist, and able to work autonomously. Not everyone thrives as a lone engineer.
- Look for a "confidence to ship": someone who cares about quality but favors "better done than perfect."
- Alignment on how the founder codes (tests, tabs vs spaces, tool choices) matters. Rob wants opinions, but flexibility to go with the founder's final call. He asks what happens if a senior dev refuses to use AI coding tools like Cursor or Claude Code.
- Small-company experience is a near-automatic screen for Rob, for any role, not just developers. If someone has only worked at big companies, he is close to a hard pass. Tiny Seed and MicroConf are about 10 people, and the culture shock is large.
  - Example: a new hire asked what the process was to buy a mouse and keyboard. Rob's answer: "buy it on Amazon, tell me the total, I'll PayPal or Venmo you."
- Rob screens every hire on three things:
  - Technical acumen.
  - Interpersonal fit (flexible, a good team player).
  - Reliability (ships on time, does what they say, shows up).
- Reliability can't be tested in interviews. It only shows up by working together, for example over a probationary period. Rob has hired people who were great technically and interpersonally but flaky.

## Hiring process and onboarding a new developer

- Rob's Drip process: an initial HR screen, one or two conversations, then a pair-programming session as the last step before hiring. Drip was in Minneapolis, so candidates came in person. Now it is done remotely.
- Take-home projects are obsolete. Candidates will use AI (which they should), so Rob and Derek moved to pair programming even earlier at Drip because it showed how candidates think and react to challenge.
- Rob's trick: throw a candidate a curveball, such as suddenly switching to spaces instead of tabs, and see how they react.
- The pairing session runs about 2 to 3 hours. Rob says that sounds long, but by that point most candidates get hired. A few were quick no's, which saved a lot of time.
- Onboarding with AI has changed. Before AI, founders drew whiteboard diagrams and walked through the code. Now a new hire can fire up an agent and ask what the major subsystems are.
  - Rob was amazed that an agent can read git history, pull requests and surrounding tickets to explain why code exists.
  - Writing code comments and hand-written commit messages now feels archaic, because agents capture context better.
- Still pair for an hour or two a day for the first week or two. Rob endorses this (Derek's practice) as the time to instill the team's ethos, build rapport, and learn how you disagree and resolve it.
- Recruiting: Rob often recommends Remote First Recruiting (Dan and Ian's flat-fee service, used by many Tiny Seed companies). It hires worldwide for developers, customer success, sales, marketers and freelancers, and handles sifting through AI-generated applications.

## Avoiding the trap of building every customer request because AI makes it cheap

- Context: a listener built a complicated feature for a new client that took longer than expected, touches a lot of other code and adds maintenance overhead. He admitted AI had made him overconfident about how easy code is to write and maintain.
- Rob's own guidance, from an earlier essay, is "why you shouldn't build everything customers ask for."
- Derek's example, which Rob called a good catch: a SavvyCal customer wanted a complex HubSpot integration with deal creation and de-duplication logic. Mapping it out with an agent showed it would expand API calls, retries and failure modes, so they sent the customer to Zapier instead.
- The cost isn't the initial build. It is the larger surface area to maintain, even with agents.
- Rob sees a trend of products taking on more scope because building is cheaper.
  - Drip example: customers often asked for a CRM, and they lost deals to HubSpot because it had CRM plus email marketing. Rob and Derek resisted because it would have taken months. If it had taken a fraction of the time, Rob thinks it would have been good for the business, though competitors might have built it too.
  - Rob's test is to know your customers and market and ask whether the feature moves the needle, or whether you are throwing in "all-in-one crap" that makes the product worthless.
  - Broader scope works best for less technical users who "don't want Make or Zapier" and want to replace four tools with one, provided competitors aren't doing the same. It can eventually become table stakes.

## Getting past social anxiety as a founder (sales, marketing, customer interviews)

- Rob's main answer is practice. His anxiety around the first blog post, first podcast episode and first time on stage was intense. After doing it repeatedly (he put IMC and similar events at the 50th to 100th time) it is easy. He admits "easier said than done."
- Options Rob mentioned:
  - Beta blockers can help for specific occasions, but not for every sales call.
  - A Tiny Seed founder with real social anxiety had a teammate join sales calls as a "wing person." That is a costly use of resources, but you improve and eventually don't need them.
  - For severe anxiety, get a therapist, book or course to find the root cause. Rob has learned a lot from therapists and says repetition can also numb the anxiety.
- Derek's tactic, which Rob called "really good advice": record sales calls, feed the transcript to an LLM, and ask what went well and what could be better. Derek cautions that LLMs are not good therapists.

## Trademarks and IP protection for a bootstrapped product

- Rob and Derek are not lawyers and say not to take their legal advice.
- Rob's record: he tried to trademark Drip and was rejected as too generic, and other filings also failed. He believes he holds the trademarks for MicroConf and Startups for the Rest of Us.
- Costs Rob cited (rough, from experience and Claude):
  - US filing fee is around $350. With a lawyer, about $1,000 to $2,000 (roughly $1,500 if easy), depending on the number of classes.
  - New Zealand is $150 to $200 DIY and about $500 to $1,500 with a lawyer.
- US: a unique name gets a "use" defense, so you can prove prior use later, but it is more expensive and weaker than having filed.
- New Zealand (per Claude, "not legal advice"): no use defense, so the first to file wins even if you have used the name for years. Rob leans toward filing there, and Derek agreed.
- Rob suggests trying AI or an online legal service to fill out the filing and cut cost.
- General principle: lawyers and insurers will tell you to do everything on day 10, but bootstrappers have limited money. Rob says "don't overdo this." That applies equally to LLC formation, E&O and cyber insurance, and IP filings.

## Hiring-adjacent notes: Rob's long-run consistency and founder community

- Rob says the way to build anything interesting is to show up over a long period. The podcast reached episode 850 by recording every week since 2010.
- Tiny Summit: a premium retreat for 15 to 20 founders past $1M ARR, Cancun, December 5 to 7, 2026, by application at tinyc.com/tinysummit.
