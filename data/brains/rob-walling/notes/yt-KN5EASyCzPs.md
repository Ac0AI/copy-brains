## Why Rob considers most "B2C SaaS" not real B2C SaaS

- Rob says true B2C SaaS is "extremely rare." Most examples either pivot to B2B or only survive at massive scale.
- Netflix, YouTube Premium, HBO Max and Spotify are subscription content businesses, "content as a service, not software as a service." Customers pay for the content, not the software.
- iCloud works only because it is bundled with Apple's hardware ecosystem. It does not stand alone as a SaaS business.
- Dropbox started B2C, but over 80% of its revenue is now B2B.
- LastPass co-CEO David Fogno said in a 2025 interview that enterprise makes up 75% of its revenue.
- The B2C SaaS examples Rob knows of are YNAB (You Need a Budget), Todoist, and fitness and sleep trackers. He knows of only "a handful or two," against 50,000+ B2B SaaS apps.
- His credibility claim: six companies started, 224 SaaS startups invested in, five books written. He also draws on founders from his podcast, the Tiny Seed and the Micro community. They often tell him "I wished I had listened to you sooner" or "I'm in B2C and my gosh, this is awful."

## Why founders are drawn to B2C SaaS (and why Rob calls it a trap)

- **Massive user base appeal:** the idea of millions of potential customers is "intoxicating."
- **Personal connection:** founders solve their own problem or build for "everyone" to reach a mass market.
- **Emotional appeal:** the wish to impact the world or be famous, inspired by *The Social Network* or the Google and Twitter stories. Rob says "very, very, very few of these succeed."
- **Perceived simplicity:** no enterprise sales cycles, procurement or red tape, so you "just launch and grow like magic."
- Rob's counterpoint: selling to consumers feels simple, but the buying psychology on the customer's side is harder than selling to businesses.

## Consumers and businesses judge price differently

- A $30/month subscription makes a consumer pause and think.
- For a business, a subscription that saves an hour a week, or makes $100-$300, is a no-brainer.
- To a business, $30 is "a rounding error." Rob jokes they spend more on toilet paper in a week.
- Consumers judge return on investment differently in their personal lives. Businesses are far less price sensitive and more willing to spend.

## The financials and unit economics of B2C SaaS

- Low price points and high price sensitivity mean you need thousands or tens of thousands of users to make real money.
- Worked example: $10,000 MRR at $8 per user needs more than 1,200 paying customers. At typical free-to-paid conversion rates, that means tens of thousands of signups.
- You can't afford to spend on marketing. Spending even "a couple hundred" to acquire a customer, which is small in B2B SaaS, puts you in the hole.
- It is hard to hold a healthy LTV to CAC ratio. Rob asks which ad platforms let you acquire consumers at a profitable cost, and answers: "for consumers, it's almost zero."
- So you "need virality in quotes." Having a free plan and hoping to stumble onto virality is "very, very, very unlikely."

## Churn rules of thumb by customer segment

- **Enterprise, large contracts:** net negative churn, or about 1% monthly.
- **Small businesses:** 2-3% monthly if the business is healthy.
- **Prosumers:** 5-6%.
- **Consumers:** 10%, 15%, 20% or more. Rob has seen B2C businesses with 25% churn.
- At 25% churn it is "not even really a subscription business anymore." It is "a four-time payment," because people pay four times and leave. The whole customer base turns over in under 4 months.
- High churn makes the business a "leaky bucket." Compounding growth is hard and plateauing early is likely.
- Churn also affects free apps. People churn out of free products "all the time," and consumers are "notoriously fickle."

## Five myths that trip up B2C SaaS founders

1. **"If I build it, they will come."** They won't. Distribution, marketing and getting people to care and click buy is the hardest part. Building the product is hard, even with AI and no-code, but it is not the hardest part.
2. **"Viral growth will save me."** True virality is rare and orchestrated. Few apps reach a meaningful viral coefficient. Well-funded teams with more experience can't find the viral loop either, so a first-time founder shouldn't expect to stumble on it.
3. **"Low price means faster growth."** Rob says it is the opposite. Low price means lower revenue per user, higher support load, often higher churn, slower growth and early plateaus.
4. **"Churn isn't a big deal."** Churn is "the death of any type of subscription business, especially SaaS." High churn makes scaling nearly impossible.
5. **"I'll monetize it later."** Bootstrapped founders, or those without lots of cash, rarely have the time and money to run an app for months or years without revenue. They need to monetize early.

## The one B2C approach Rob would bet on: the dual funnel

- Rob says B2C is very hard and probably shouldn't be a first effort.
- The approach he has seen work for bootstrapped and mostly bootstrapped founders is a dual funnel, or "B2both." It serves consumers and prosumers as well as businesses.
- His example is Castos, podcast hosting. It hosts Rob's own podcasts, Startups for the Rest of Us and the Micro Conf podcast. Castos has low-priced tiers for hobbyists and higher tiers for businesses or podcast networks.
- Why it works:
  - The brand scales from having lots of users and lots of people talking about it online.
  - Business customers provide real revenue and stability, even if the consumer and prosumer end churns at higher rates.
  - It gives "a nice sustainable smooth growth curve."
- Enterprise-only has a drawback: you might close a big deal one month and none the next, so growth is spiky. A B2B dual funnel can smooth that curve.
- Rob's alternative advice is to build B2B SaaS. He points to a follow-up video on seven ways founders find a B2B idea worth building.
