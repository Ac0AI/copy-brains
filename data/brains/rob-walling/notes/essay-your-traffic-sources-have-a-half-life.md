## Traffic sources decay at different rates, and "half-life" is the right way to compare them

Rob borrows the line "Marketing sources have a half-life" (he doesn't recall the origin) to describe how traffic sources decay over time. Every source dies out eventually if you don't keep investing in it, but at very different speeds. Half-life is the time for a source's traffic to fall to 50% of its current value.

His evidence comes from Google Analytics on his own blog (about six weeks before the 2011 essay):
- **Hacker News:** a huge, fast spike followed by rapid decay. Even where the curve looked flat (around December 13th), it was only about 65 visits/day, and about 16/day by the 20th.
- **Twitter:** a spike with a smaller peak (Y-axis max 4,000 versus 7,000 for Hacker News) but slower decay. It was still around 250 visits/day by the 13th and 298 by December 20th.
- **Organic search:** no spikes and no decay. Apart from a weekly dip on Saturday, it barely fluctuated from month to month.
- Rob's estimate for the Twitter and Hacker News half-life is about 12 hours.
- By contrast, if he stopped maintaining the blog, organic search traffic would take "years (by my estimates)" to fall to 50%.

## Spikes look bigger than they are; consistent traffic compounds past them

On a shared Y-axis, the organic search line would barely rise above the apparent "flatline" of the Twitter graph. The social peaks look far larger, since single-day totals from those spikes are 10-30x higher in any given month. But the steady organic traffic summed to about 70% of each of the other two peaks' totals over the same period. Because of this "tortoise-like consistency," organic search is always one of the blog's strongest sources despite never spiking. The lesson: judge a traffic source by cumulative volume over time, not by its peak day.

## The three-way classification of traffic sources

Rob sorts sources into three groups:
- **Zero half-life:** any kind of advertising. Traffic stops the moment you stop paying.
- **Short half-life:** StumbleUpon, Twitter, Hacker News, Digg, a direct mailing, most referral links.
- **Long half-life:** SEO, email subscribers, RSS subscribers, and (in some instances) Facebook fans.

## If you're bootstrapping, don't plan on advertising

Advertising builds no traffic "equity" and doesn't sustain itself; once payment stops, clicks stop. Rob says you can try it, but shouldn't count on it as a viable lead source unless you have "a chunk of change stashed away for this purpose." He notes that only a very small percentage of startups have any hope of a positive ROI from advertising, and a bootstrapper needs that positive ROI.

## Short half-life sources are the "sexy" ones, so use them for events and convert the traffic

These are the sources the media talks about: "new," "revolutionary," "viral." Rob sees real benefits in using them for events like a product launch or big announcement. But if a business relies on them to sustain itself, "you had better be an insanely talented buzz builder," on the order of Peldi (Balsamiq) or Matthew Inman (The Oatmeal), "1 in 100,000 at least."

He adds that viral sources demand a large amount of ongoing time and passion that most people can't maintain long term. His tactic: don't rely on them to drive sales directly. Instead, remember the number one goal of your website and find a way to turn short half-life traffic into long half-life traffic.

## Long half-life sources are the holy grail for a lasting business

Rob has owned well over 30 software products and websites, using every approach above (including advertising) as marketing lifeblood. His unequivocal conclusion: "If you want to build a business that does not require a heavy, ongoing, constant investment in finding new customers…focus on long half-life traffic sources."

In practice that means:
- Learn SEO.
- Build email and RSS subscriber lists (and potentially Facebook fans).

That is where the equity is built that keeps a business around for years. Without it, you are "perpetually chasing your next prospect, which is an expensive proposition."

## Case examples: Groupon, Facebook and AppSumo convert viral traffic into an email list

Rob points out that some companies appear to be built on viral, short half-life traffic, but are quietly converting it into a long half-life asset, an email list. The examples he gives are Groupon, Facebook and, at a smaller scale, AppSumo. His line: "Facebook and Groupon, two of the most talked-about companies of the past year, are actually highly-optimized email marketing firms masquerading as viral marketing firms." His advice is to do the same when marketing your startup: use short half-life sources to drive traffic, then capture it into a source that lasts.
