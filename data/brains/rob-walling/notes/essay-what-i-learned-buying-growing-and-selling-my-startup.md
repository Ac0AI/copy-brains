## How Rob uses stair-stepping to move from small apps to bigger SaaS bets

- Rob calls his approach "Stair Stepping": start small and move through larger opportunities as experience, skills, funds and confidence grow.
- He frames the HitTail-to-Drip move as a step from step 3 (recurring revenue) into an undefined step 4, which might be more recurring revenue in a larger, more competitive market.
- He says that without the experience, skills, funds and confidence from growing HitTail, launching Drip in 2011 "would have had my ass handed to me", and that he doesn't think he could have pulled Drip off "P.H. (pre-HitTail)".
- His view: stair stepping is an optimal way to maximize results while minimizing the chance of failure. "It's not the fastest path, but a predictable one."

## Why Rob buys an existing SaaS instead of building from scratch

- In early 2011 he wanted a bigger "next thing" but didn't want to spend 6 months coding and 12 months launching and hunting for product/market fit.
- He looked for an acquisition larger than any he had tried before, and spent almost every penny in his business account to buy HitTail, a SaaS app.
- He argues that buying an app that already has product/market fit puts you 12-18 months ahead of starting from nothing.
- He estimates acquiring HitTail condensed his career timetable by 18 months and removed much of the anxiety and stress of pre-product/market-fit thrashing.
- He grew HitTail's revenue 10x in 15 months, using steps he outlined in a MicroConf talk.
- His summary: "Buying is probably better than building."

## Why Rob keeps looking for the next thing

- He says that when he isn't learning he isn't happy. In early 2011 he had a book selling well, a portfolio of 8 or 9 small apps and sites, and a 12-16 hour work week with income comparable to his full-time contracting pay. But nothing scared or stretched him.
- He expected burnout and apathy within months, so he went looking for a bigger challenge.
- A second "what's next?" moment came partly from Google's "Not Provided" change in 2012/2013 and partly from all he had learned revamping HitTail. HitTail worked around it easily, but it scared him for a couple of months.
- That led to Drip, whose growth curve eventually made it senseless to spend even a few hours a month on other products.

## How Rob wound down a product portfolio to focus on Drip

- Once Drip took off, he divested his portfolio of sites and apps down to one:
  - He shut a few down.
  - He sold one for a few thousand dollars after an unsolicited offer.
  - He gave his share of another to a business partner.
  - HitTail made too much money to shut down or give away, so he sold it.
- He ended up with one application, Drip, and says the focus "feels great".

## How Rob sold HitTail through a broker

- Selling small web apps used to be bleak. He knew of no reputable brokers, and he compares Flippa to "selling at wholesale" with many uneducated buyers.
- Over about 4 years he watched a secondary market develop through reputable brokers such as FE International and Quiet Light.
- He chose FE International, with whom he had built a relationship over several years.
- Due diligence felt like hundreds of hours but was closer to 40-50 hours, over a few months.
- He found the sale emotionally hard. He says there's an emotional component as you wonder whether it will fall through, you sleep badly, and it's hard to let go of something you've invested so much time, effort and emotion in. He still says "sometimes it's the right thing to do."

## What more money meant to Rob

- Before HitTail he was already comfortable, so he hadn't thought about earning more after hitting a "good enough" level around 2008/2009.
- He asked his wife what more money would let them do. Her answers: be more generous, travel, save for the kids' college, have more control of their schedules, and feel more secure about the financial present and future. None involved buying stuff.
- Within a year HitTail had delivered most of that, including helping friends out of financial situations and taking the kids to Europe/Thailand for a month each year for 3 years.
- He says you don't need buckets of money to be happy, and "more stuff" is a ridiculous reason to want more, but extra breathing room lets you do generous and interesting things.

## Why Rob says it's the best time to be a solo founder

- He argues a team of one can accomplish "crazy things" in the 2010s thanks to leverage from marketplaces like Upwork, metered hosting like AWS, cheap payment gateways like Stripe, and many tools far cheaper than a decade earlier.
- He says his HitTail journey would have been impossible 10 years earlier.
- His view: today is the best time in history to be a founder, especially a single founder, given outsourcing and the global labor pool.
