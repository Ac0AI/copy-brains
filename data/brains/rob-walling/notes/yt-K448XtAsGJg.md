## SaaS is the best business model but the hardest to build: Rob's "brutal truths"

- Rob frames SaaS as having "recurring revenue, high profit margins, and incredible exit multiples", making it the best model for a multi-million dollar business.
- His view comes from about 20 years of building and exiting companies, and from investing in hundreds of SaaS companies through TinySeed.
- He still recommends it: SaaS changed his life, and he has seen thousands of founders change theirs. Even without a big exit, pulling $10-30K a month out of a bootstrapped SaaS, the satisfaction of building for customers and what you learn along the way are worth it.
- He does not recommend diving headfirst into a full-blown SaaS. He recommends his "stair-step method" as the best way to de-risk the journey.

## Build your network, not your audience, if you are building SaaS

- Rob says a social media following does "very, very little" for a SaaS: a small launch bump, then it is irrelevant.
- The exception is when the audience is exactly your ideal customer profile, which he says is almost never the case.
- His rule: "build your network not your audience" if you are going to build a SaaS.
- He pushes back on the common advice to "build your audience and then ask them what they want".
- That advice works for selling information, event tickets, books and courses.
- For SaaS he calls it a waste of time.

## You will have to do work you do not want to do

- Rob says SaaS inevitably has some level of grind. Writing a bit of code and posting on X or LinkedIn will not build a great company.
- Marketing channels he lists that founders may not enjoy: cold outreach, SEO, in-person events, partnerships and integrations, talking to humans, going on podcasts and YouTube channels, and generally promoting the product.
- Beyond marketing, the job includes:
  - launching and sticking with it long enough to take feedback and iterate
  - making hard decisions with incomplete information
  - managing your own psychology
- He compares influencers who say otherwise to telling you that you can lose weight by eating ice cream. It is fun overall, but not always.

## The long, slow SaaS ramp of death

- Rob credits the term to Gail Goodman, founder of Constant Contact, from a Business of Software talk 10-15 years ago.
- One-time sales can grow quickly. Recurring revenue at $10, $50 or even $500 a month compounds slowly, and meaningful MRR usually takes a long time.
- There should still be leading signals that people are interested. He is not telling founders to grind 5-10 years with no traction and expect growth to appear.
- His advice: "think in terms of years, not months."
- Example: a founder wanted $10K MRR in 6 months. Rob told him to give himself 2 years, and to hope to be pleasantly surprised if it comes sooner.

## Churn and product-market fit decide whether the SaaS works

- Rob says "churn is the death of SaaS".
  - If you sell a book or course and buyers never use it, you keep the money.
  - With SaaS, anyone who gets no value or finds it does not do what they need cancels.
- The best SaaS companies find extremely strong product-market fit and retain users to roughly zero churn. His definition: you built something people or businesses want and are willing to pay for.
- Churn never fully goes away and is a constant worry. Monthly churn of 8-10% erodes momentum, and you cannot outrun it with more top-of-funnel signups.
- With that much churn it "will never be a great business". It can be an interesting lifestyle business.
- It is hard to get people to try the product, and harder to get them to stay and keep paying over the long term.

## Talent, constant work and burnout

- **Engineering and other talent:**
  - Good engineers are expensive and hard to retain because they have many options.
  - Early on, founders write the code themselves, but long term he does not want founders saddled in the code.
  - Hiring in your own time zone or nearby cheaper regions helps. For a US founder that means Canada, Central America or South America.
  - Marketing and sales talent is equally sought after and knows what it is worth.
  - He names the "core four SaaS competencies" that a founding team needs. Founders usually cover development, marketing and sales early on.
- **The work never stops:**
  - Uptime, bugs, incidents and customer-impacting issues follow you everywhere, even on vacation.
  - With two founders where one writes all the code, it is "24/7 by 365".
  - It is fun for the first few months, not at 6 months, and by 12-18 months founders burn out.
- **Stress and burnout:**
  - He says stress is inevitable, and burnout feels near-inevitable. Competitors, AI and other problems keep appearing.
  - Nearly every SaaS founder he knows who sold said afterward they did not know if they would do it again. Many do launch another.
  - Many founders near burnout are trying to go it alone, so he recommends serious founders join a mastermind. It works like a personal board of advisors of peers who support you and call you on your BS.
  - MicroConf runs mastermind matching on time zones, experience, revenue, team size and industries served. It has matched thousands of founders across 20 time zones and 50 countries (microconf.com/masterminds).

## SaaS never stops changing

- Rob cites Arvid Kahl's Bootstrapped Founder podcast for this point and agrees with it.
- A dry cleaner might run for 10-30 years with little change. SaaS rarely feels safe or stable for long.
- Sources of constant change he lists:
  - technology and stack shifts
  - security changes
  - AI sideswiping you
  - new competitors
  - marketing channels that start or stop working
  - a Google update suddenly cutting your customers
  - UI/UX refreshes and redesigns to fit the paradigms of the day
  - rising customer expectations
- Even at around $100K a month, founders can feel "existential dread" about how long the business will last.
- Few SaaS companies survive 5-7 years with the founder still running them rather than someone else after a sale. He thinks the exhaustion of constant change is one reason.
