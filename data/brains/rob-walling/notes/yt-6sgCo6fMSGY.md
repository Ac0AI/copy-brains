## Rob's survey of 200+ SaaS founders: how they found their ideas

- Rob surveyed over 200 SaaS founders making at least $1,000/month. Some made well over $100,000/month.
- He wanted data on how founders actually found winning ideas, not opinions or hypothetical idea lists.
- He sees these methods across his own companies, hundreds of startups he's invested in, and thousands of SaaS companies in the TinySeed, MicroConf and Startups For The Rest Of Us ecosystem.
- His long-running line: "Don't tell me your idea, tell me what problem it solves."
- Share of respondents by method:
  - Problem at a day job: 48%
  - Copying or entering an existing product category: about 10%
  - Freelancing or agency work: about 10%
  - Spouse, friend or colleague's problem: 8%
  - Scratching your own itch: 16%
  - Building on an existing product: 4%
  - Finding a problem online: 3%
  - Emerging technology: 3%
- 72% found their idea through work. That covers day-job frustrations, expensive tools they knew they could build better or cheaper, patterns from agency clients, and opportunities found while building something else.
- His conclusion is that "your SaaS idea might be right under your nose."
- His free "9-to-5 audit" at robwalling.com/ideas walks through exercises for spotting opportunities in your workday.
- He is turning the research into a book on finding, choosing and validating an idea, finding product-market fit, and launching a profitable SaaS business.

## Method 1: Find a problem at your day job (48%)

- This is the most common route. You spot a problem as a frustrated developer or as a subject matter expert who knows the industry's pain points.
- Examples he cites:
  - **Jitbit** (help desk). The founder saw that modern tools lacked "boring" features like SSO, Active Directory integration, user provisioning and on-prem Windows hosting. The tools that had them looked like the '90s. Rob notes the saturated market was a good sign, because "the market was there".
  - **Upstream Edge** (oil and gas well valuation). A petroleum engineer of ten years built a desktop app that is 100 times faster than alternatives.
  - **Didomite** (looping video for public screens). The founder spent years in digital signage and saw the same UX problems repeatedly. He jumped 10 years later.
  - **Blue Gamma** (interest rate forecasts). Two consultants had manually pulled and formatted rate data for clients, sometimes several times a day.
  - **Loody** (formerly Metro Retro). Born from a hackathon prototype after the founder watched a PM hand-write retro notes.
- Why it works:
  - You have deep context on the problem and its workarounds.
  - You have built-in access to customers and beta testers.
  - You understand the buying process: who signs off, budget cycles, objections.
  - You may be able to validate while still employed.
- Watch-outs:
  - Make sure the problem exists beyond your company. Your employer may be uniquely dysfunctional.
  - Check your employment agreement for IP clauses.
  - Don't use proprietary information or customer data.
  - Rob wouldn't use an employer's laptop. He'd work on a personal one and be "really clean about it".
- Questions he suggests asking:
  - Which tools frustrate me or my coworkers daily?
  - Which manual processes should be automated?
  - Are the incumbents actually old and clunky, or just disliked by me?
- Tip: consider old jobs too. Didomite's problem was a decade old.

## Method 2: Enter an existing category (about 10%)

- Rob says "copy" really means entering an existing category with other players. It does not mean cloning.
- Examples:
  - **t.ly**. The founder's employer was quoted thousands of dollars for a Bitly custom domain. He built an in-house URL shortener over a weekend, then realized others would want a cheaper option.
  - **BigMailer**. The founders had 300,000+ subscribers and found email tools too expensive. They built in-house on Amazon, then productized it.
  - **Checkout Joy**. Local payment processors for LMS platforms in a market without Stripe. The first customers were live within a week, with an immediate conversion bump.
  - **Hururu.ai**. An AI phone answering service for Australian businesses with local accents, modeled on US products like Rosie AI. The founder bet US players wouldn't localize soon.
- Rob's own Drip example (acquired in 2016, a "life-changing exit"):
  - Drip began as an email capture widget plus email sequences.
  - He saw market pull for automation features that cheaper email providers lacked. Existing tools with those features were expensive, used annual contracts, had poor software and required talking to a salesperson.
  - Drip didn't copy a product, but it entered an existing category. Visitors to the homepage immediately understood what it was.
  - He only had to say why Drip was better or different, not explain a novel concept. Positioning moved from email service provider to email automation to lightweight marketing automation.
- Why it works:
  - Demand is already proven.
  - You can read incumbents' negative reviews and support forums.
  - You can differentiate on price, UX, features or niche.
  - Long-standing incumbents often raise prices and go upmarket, which leaves room.
  - In a big market, a small slice can be a great bootstrapped business.
- Watch-outs:
  - Copying in a small market means fighting for scraps. It works best in big markets.
  - "We're newer" is not a differentiator.
  - Incumbents have a head start on brand, SEO, integrations, trust and marketing.
  - "I'll just execute better is not a strategy."
- Questions to ask:
  - Why are customers unhappy: price, features, support or usability?
  - Does my differentiation matter to customers, or only to me?
  - Is there a channel the incumbent uses poorly?
  - Is there an underserved segment?

## Method 3: Freelancing and agency work (about 10%)

- Examples:
  - **Drum** (ERP for small consulting firms). The founder kept rebuilding similar functionality for clients.
  - **Civic Review** (permitting software). The founder was asked three times by municipalities and built it on the third request.
  - **StatusGator**. Built to solve the founder's own bug-hunting problem: a status page he didn't know about held the cause of a bug. It aggregates status pages and emails on changes.
  - **Dump Truck Dispatcher**. The founder ran a dev shop and kept the IP in return for charging the client less.
- Why it works:
  - You see the same problem across several clients.
  - Clients pay you to learn their problems, which is paid market research.
  - You build domain expertise.
  - Agency revenue can fund early product development.
- Watch-outs:
  - Agency work is demanding, so finding build time is hard.
  - Make sure client contracts make IP ownership clear before productizing. Many consulting and employment agreements sign away what you build, even on your own computer at home.
  - Moving from custom work to product thinking is harder than it looks. You have to learn to say no to feature requests.
  - Don't build something so tailored to one client that nobody else wants it.
- Questions to ask:
  - What functionality have I built three or more times for different clients?
  - What internal agency tools might others want?
  - Do I have a clear path from agency revenue to product revenue, or will I do both forever?
  - Do I want to serve my clients' industry long-term?

## Methods 4 and 5: Problems from people you know, and problems found online

**Method 4: spouse, friend or colleague (8%)**
- Go hunt for problems by asking your network about their jobs.
- Examples:
  - **Quill Therapy Solutions**. The founder's therapist wife suggested using his AI tool to generate progress notes.
  - **CrankWheel**. A co-founder with years in sales told him that people on a call with a prospect used nothing to show something, because screen-sharing tools were too complex.
- Why it works:
  - You have a direct line to someone with the problem.
  - You can learn without cold calling at first.
  - The relationship creates accountability.
  - They can introduce you to others in the field.
- Watch-outs:
  - Their problem may be unique.
  - They may be too nice to say your solution is weak.
  - Mixing business and personal relationships is complicated.
  - Their expertise plus your building skills isn't enough without understanding the market.
- Questions to ask:
  - What does my spouse or friend complain about at work?
  - Can they give me about five intros?
  - Am I building a business, or doing a favor?
  - Do I have the patience to learn an unfamiliar industry?

**Method 5: finding a problem online (3%)**
- Rob notes this method is often touted online but is hard. Only 3% used it.
- Examples:
  - **String Thing** (Shopify delivery-date app). The founder combed Shopify support forums for complaints. He then checked the app store for apps with many reviews and many negative reviews, which pointed to widespread but poorly solved problems.
  - **Hovercode** (dynamic QR codes). The founder had an interest, talked to a marketing friend, and used Ahrefs to find huge search volume.
- Why it works:
  - You can find problems in industries you've never worked in.
  - You can validate with search volume, complaint counts or community size.
  - Negative reviews of existing products are "a goldmine for differentiation".
- Watch-outs:
  - People complain about things they wouldn't pay for, so signal-to-noise is low.
  - You lack insider context.
  - It takes lots of time and patience.
  - Selling to strangers in an unfamiliar industry is hard.
- Sources he lists:
  - Reddit
  - Facebook groups
  - Private Slack groups
  - Support forums
  - G2, Capterra and app store reviews
  - Ahrefs, Semrush and Google Keyword Planner
  - 75+ marketplaces at microconf.com/marketplaces, if you want to build an add-on for an existing platform
- Questions to ask:
  - Is the complaint repeated by many people?
  - Would they pay, or just vent?
  - Can I reach them?
  - Will I invest the time to learn the industry?
- A real-world variant is **OrderNerd**. A co-founder saw a wall of delivery tablets (DoorDash, Uber Eats, Grubhub) in a restaurant and heard the staff's pain. The founders had five years of restaurant sales and a decade of third-party API experience. Rob's lesson is to stay curious and observant.

## Method 6: Scratch your own itch (16%), and how Rob validates it

- Basecamp (DHH and Jason Fried) popularized this in the early and mid-2000s. Rob says it's "certainly not a bad way", but many things have to line up.
- "You can't just say, 'I have a problem, therefore that's validation.'" That only validates one customer paying $0, yourself.
- Rob's own validation of Drip, which began as an itch he had:
  - He asked 17 other founders he knew whether they had the problem.
  - He got 10 or 11 yeses before building.
- Examples:
  - **Dialog Shift** (AI for hotels). The founders were frustrated as hotel guests by how hard it was to get simple information, such as breakfast times or yoga class schedules.
  - **Simple Booklet**. The founder had sold an earlier business and sailed for 11 years. He found paper brochures hard to use, and noticed that every print brochure started as a PDF.
- Why it works:
  - You understand the problem intimately.
  - You're motivated.
  - You are your own first user.
  - Your passion shows in marketing and sales.
- Watch-outs:
  - You may be a market of one.
  - You may build what you want rather than what the market wants.
  - Your use case may be too niche.
  - Founders underestimate how different their needs are from typical customers.
- Questions to ask:
  - How many others have this exact problem, and can I talk to them?
  - Am I a representative user or an edge case?
  - Would I pay more to solve it, or is it mildly annoying?
  - Can I separate my preferences from market needs?

## Methods 7 and 8: Emerging technology (3%) and building on an existing product (4%)

**Method 7: emerging or fast-growing technology**
- Rob calls this "every developer's dream", but rarely do people turn it into a business. Only 3% did.
- Examples include AI, a new API, or a new platform opening up (Facebook, Shopify, Squarespace, HubSpot).
- Cases:
  - **WA Notifier**. A former WordPress agency owner bet that WhatsApp marketing would follow email marketing, especially in India, GCC countries and South America. The timing coincided with WhatsApp opening to new API partners.
  - **Gen Text AI**. Word add-ins built right after ChatGPT launched, by a consultant and economist who used Office heavily.
  - **PodSqueeze**. Rob's podcast uses it. It generates posts, clips, summaries and transcripts from audio or video. The founder built it from his own post-production frustration right after ChatGPT launched.
- Why it works:
  - Early movers establish themselves before the market crowds.
  - New technology creates new problems.
  - You ride the attention.
  - Customers forgive rough edges.
- Watch-outs:
  - Too early means the technology or customers aren't ready. Too late means dozens or hundreds of competitors.
  - The hype cycle can mislead you.
  - The technology may not mature as expected.
  - Well-funded players move fast.
- Questions to ask:
  - Is the technology ready for real use, or still experimental?
  - Are businesses already spending in adjacent areas?
  - Do I have a unique angle, or am I chasing hype?
  - Can I build something useful before the window closes?

**Method 8: building on an existing product**
- You're working on one product and find a bigger or better opportunity. Rob says these were new products, not necessarily pivots.
- Examples:
  - **Authored Up**. The founders built a hiring product without talking to customers (no Mom Test). They then made an internal LinkedIn content tool for their own outreach.
  - **Super Data**. A YouTube transcript scraper for another product became an API. The founder monetized it on RapidAPI and it "snowballed".
  - **SignWell**. Founders saw customers' dissatisfaction with e-signature tools while running another business.
- Why it works:
  - You're already in the market, talking to customers.
  - You have momentum, skills and sometimes revenue.
  - An internal tool may have broader demand.
  - You reuse what you've learned.
- Watch-outs:
  - Shiny objects. The new idea must be better, not just more exciting.
  - Splitting focus between two products can kill both.
  - You may be running from hard problems in your current product.
  - Sunk cost can cloud your judgment.
- Questions to ask:
  - Is this genuinely bigger and better, or am I bored?
  - Can I validate quickly without abandoning the current product?
  - Do I have resources for both?
  - What would have to be true for the switch to be worth it?
- Rob says validation comes next once you've found an idea, and he covers it in a follow-up video.
