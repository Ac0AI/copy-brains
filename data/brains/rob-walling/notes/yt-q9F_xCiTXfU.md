## The "core four" SaaS skills and why missing one caps the business

- Rob says that after working with thousands of founders and investing in more than 230 SaaS companies, a stuck SaaS is usually missing one of four skills. His examples of "stuck" are $5-10K MRR, or not reaching $1K MRR.
- The four skills, in no particular order, are product, development, marketing and sales.
- Founders usually think they can fix a gap with an agency, a freelancer or a co-founder. Rob says "almost every time, this doesn't work".
- Rob treats company success as a multiplier: founder × product × market, each scored 1-10. The factors multiply, they don't add.
  - A founder scored 3 (missing sales and marketing) with a 9 market and an 8 product gives 216.
  - A founder scored 7 across all four skills, with the same product and market, gives 504. That is nearly 2.5x the outcome.
  - He calls the numbers directional, not exact.
- His example of close to 10 × 10 × 10 is Jason Cohen's WP Engine: a 9-10 founder, a growing market and a smart product idea.

## What each core skill means: product, development, sales

- **Product:**
  - It is knowing what to build, in what order, why and how. That includes how a feature integrates into the existing application.
  - It is not project management. It means taking customer feedback and understanding the space and niche. The aim is something people will pay for month after month.
  - Rob says "You can't build something people want by accident." He calls it the founder's job and says most people underestimate how hard it is.
- **Development:**
  - It is more than writing code. It means shipping fast and keeping a codebase that doesn't collapse, so technical debt doesn't hurt 12 months later.
  - Feature velocity is one of a small company's only real advantages over bigger entrenched competitors. With no bureaucracy, a small team can ship in days what takes big companies months.
- **Sales:**
  - It covers real conversations with prospects, handling objections, closing deals and dealing with procurement.
  - Purely self-serve products can skip it. But the fastest-growing companies in Rob's portfolio are almost inevitably doing some sales.

## Marketing as a skill: three layers, and strategy matters most

- Rob says marketing is not posting on Twitter, and audience building is not a core SaaS skill.
- Marketing has three layers:
  1. Execution: the individual contributor pushing buttons in an ad console or writing SEO content.
  2. Project management: keeping work moving, reviewing it and keeping people accountable.
  3. Strategy: deciding which approaches to test and where to double down.
- Strategy matters most and is the hardest to replace. It can't be outsourced to an agency or handed to ChatGPT.
- He points to 20 B2B SaaS marketing approaches, covered on his channel, on his podcast and in his book *The SaaS Playbook*.

## Four founder archetypes where a missing skill stalls growth

- **The builder (missing sales and marketing):**
  - This is the most common pattern Rob sees, because he deals with many developers.
  - Technical founders build good products but nobody knows they exist.
  - Builders think the product should sell itself, or that posting on social media counts as marketing.
- **The marketer (missing product):**
  - Typical of info marketers and audience-first founders who move into SaaS. They can drive traffic and sign-ups, but churn is through the roof.
  - In info products, the headline matters and people pay once. If they never use the course, nobody cares.
  - SaaS needs people who keep using and paying. These founders don't know what to build or how to turn feedback into improvements.
- **The outsourcer (missing development):**
  - Non-technical founders hire an agency or freelancer and expect them to handle both product and development.
  - The top struggle is too many bugs. The next agency, hired after firing the first, wants to rewrite the whole codebase. They ship too slowly and have technical debt by launch, a permanent headwind.
  - Rob believes a developer co-founder or a founding engineer who owns the codebase would make a significantly different business in almost every case.
  - Only about 10-15% of his portfolio companies lack at least one technical co-founder. Craig Hewitt of Castos and a handful of other Tiny Seed companies are exceptions, but the pain is real and ongoing.
- **The vibe coder (a newer archetype, seen over the last 12-18 months):**
  - The product is built with vibe coding, no-code, AI-written code shipped without review, or third-party platforms.
  - Rob has never seen a true seven-figure SaaS built this way. Everyone he has seen or invested in (a handful) needed a rewrite, often at $5-20K MRR when the founder realizes it won't scale.
  - A rewrite means standing still for three, six or nine months, right when a startup's advantage is speed and when it should be responding to the market.
  - His analogy: with tools and lumber but no carpentry skills, you can build an outhouse or tool shed, but a garage, house or skyscraper falls over.
  - Tiny Seed has funded several no-code tools. It now asks up front whether founders are vibe coding or using no-code, and treats it as a meaningful drag on their odds when deciding who gets offers.
  - No-code and vibe coding do get you to an MVP quicker, but there are tradeoffs.

## Three ways to fix a missing skill, in order of preference

1. **Learn it yourself (preferred, especially for solo founders):**
   - You don't need to master all four, just be good enough to "scrap and get by". The commitment is to take ownership and put in the time to get good at all four.
   - He says the founder is "a jack or Jill of all trades and a master of none. That is the job." Most successful solo founders he has seen did this.
2. **Constrain the idea with the stair-step method:**
   - Pick a business that doesn't need all four skills. A step-one business is a Shopify plugin or a tool built for an app marketplace. MicroConf lists more than 80 of these.
   - Step-one businesses need less development, so no-code or vibe coding can work, like a tool shed or small utility. They have built-in marketplace distribution, so you learn one marketing channel, and they often need no sales.
   - The ceiling is lower, but you stay in the game, build confidence, earn some revenue and learn many skills.
3. **Find a co-founder for a specific gap:**
   - Don't look for a vague "business co-founder". Fill the exact gap in your core four.
   - The most valuable skills in a SaaS are sales and marketing. Their order depends on niche and price point. Don't give away half the company to someone who can't execute in sales and marketing.
   - A non-technical founder strong in sales and marketing needs a developer who also knows product: what to build, in what order, why and how.
   - In his successful portfolio companies the four skills are spread across three co-founders, sometimes two, and occasionally one person has all four. Examples of one-person coverage: Ruben Gomez of SignWell, and Iran Gabrin of Gymdesk (a developer who learned product and then the other two).
   - He points to podcast episodes 807 and 811 of *Startups for the Rest of Us* for more.

## When to delegate each skill

- The principle: delegate based on repeatability and how standardized and certain a function is, not on a revenue number. Founders should work on the risky, uncertain areas and hand off what becomes repeatable.
- Delegate, don't abdicate: train the person and review their work.
- **Development (earliest and easiest):**
  - A technical founder can hire a developer as early as $10-30K MRR.
  - At Drip, Rob had a contract developer, Derrick, who later became his co-founder. They added a junior dev at about $10-15K MRR. Drip's market (email service providers and marketing automation, with hundreds of competitors) needed years of development just for table stakes.
  - Keep architecture decisions and tech leadership with the founding team much longer. "Delegating execution is very different from delegating direction."
- **Sales (depends on complexity):**
  - A simple, repeatable, one-call close can be delegated at $20-30K MRR. Rob handed off sales at Drip around that range because the calls were similar. His customer success person took over.
  - Enterprise sales (tickets of $35,000 a year and up, with procurement) should be held past $500K, probably into seven figures.
  - With a dual funnel (for example $500 a month and $5,000 a month), hand off the lower-end deals first and keep the high-value ones.
  - If every call is different and complex, you're not ready.
- **Marketing:**
  - Execution (running ads, writing articles, making videos) can be delegated early to contractors and freelancers. It is a black box: it works or it doesn't.
  - Project management can be delegated once you have budget.
  - Strategy is the hardest. The earliest he has seen successful founders bring someone in even to collaborate on strategy is after seven figures, around $1-2M ARR, and it stayed collaborative. At zero-to-one, while you're still finding which channels work, you can't outsource it.
- **Product (usually last):**
  - Typically about $1.5-3M ARR (he says $2-3M to be conservative). He can't think of a single company among the hundreds he's invested in that brought in outside product help earlier.
  - At Drip, he and his co-founder made every product decision until about $5M a year. They didn't have to, but it felt comfortable. Then they brought in a great product person, and it was still a three-person collaboration.
  - Good product people are hard to find and expensive, so the slow-burn approach matters most here.
