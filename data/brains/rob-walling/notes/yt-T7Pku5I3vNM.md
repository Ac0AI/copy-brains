## Stop consuming and start shipping (decision one, 2002)

- Rob started his first online business 23 years ago. In 2002 he decided to stop reading and start shipping.
- He says many early-stage and aspiring founders are stuck in a "dreamer phase". They read business books, hold a "graveyard of dormant domains", watch YouTube and plan the perfect startup without building anything.
- His own problem was analysis paralysis. He hid behind constant learning.
- His fix was to move from consuming business books to building and launching products.
- He argues there are no secret guarantees of success. "No book, no YouTube channel, no podcast is going to give you the magic secret to guarantee your success."
- Input from people you trust, who have done what you want to do, is still valuable. At some point you have to stop consuming and start shipping.
- Shipping publicly is scary, because of the fear of criticism, judgment and failure. Examples of firsts that feel scary are launching code, publishing a blog post, launching a podcast and recording a YouTube video.
- The more you ship, the easier it gets and the more confidence you build.

## Rank work by business impact, not by passion (decision two)

- Rob says embracing hard, boring, grindy and scary work "may have been the most important factor in my success".
- His mindset was never to skip a task for lack of passion or interest. He ranked tasks by how much impact he expected them to have on the business.
- He ground out unglamorous work, including:
  - SEO
  - advertising
  - copywriting
  - bug fixes
  - customer support
  - code maintenance and rewriting
- He put in "hundreds and hundreds of hours of tedious, frustrating work" to get things shipped.
- He now does much less work he doesn't want to do. Years of grinding built businesses that give him that choice.
- He credits his background in high school football and track, college track and construction work. He did things he didn't want to do to pay the bills.
- He says to keep hobbies for enjoyment. For a startup, especially in the early bootstrapping period (six months to a few years), "you need progress not passion".
- He calls "I don't want to do that, sounds boring" a huge roadblock. He criticizes founders who only do the fun work, such as posting on X/Twitter and calling it marketing. He says that won't get them there.

## Learn from mistakes and change course (decision three)

- Working hard doesn't help if it's on the wrong things. Rob had to learn to adjust.
- Lessons he learned, many the hard way, because nobody was saying them in roughly 2002 to 2007:
  - B2C SaaS is not good. After a couple of B2C apps he decided to sell to businesses. Every product he built or acquired after that solved problems for businesses.
  - Low-priced software has higher churn, so he moved toward higher-priced products.
  - Building from scratch takes a long time. Acquiring an app that already has traction and product-market fit gets you there faster. He did a mix of building, launching and acquiring.
- These adjustments grew into what he calls the stairstep method of bootstrapping (described on robwalling.com).
- He says "Failure is only a good thing if you learn from it." Repeating the same mistakes is catastrophic.
- Founders often do the same thing over and over and don't get out of their own way.
- He applies this today at MicroConf and TinySeed. They have tried several marketing approaches. For each one they ask what's working, what's not, why, and how to adjust. They don't "beat our heads against a wall".

## Make increasingly larger but manageably sized bets (decision four)

- Rob quotes comic artist Dave Kellett: over a career you should make "increasingly larger but still manageably-sized bets".
- His progression of investments:
  - **2005-2006:** about $11,000 on NetInvoice, an acquisition. He saved it by freelancing as a software developer nights and weekends for months, while working a full-time day job with a baby at home. He grew NetInvoice from a few hundred dollars a month in revenue to $3,000-$4,000 a month.
  - **2011:** $30,000 on HitTail, an SEO keyword SaaS tool, funded by the cash from his earlier products.
  - **2012-2013:** almost $200,000 to take Drip from an idea to working software with decent product-market fit. It was funded by revenue from his earlier products and his own grinding, not by wealth or rich contacts.
- He never bet the house. He took on no credit card debt and no second mortgage. He earned the money through freelancing and then products.
- Any single bet could have failed and "would have sucked", but none would have bankrupted him.
- Each step up the staircase can be bigger than the last.

## Avoid impulsive decisions during hard times (decision five)

- Founders will hit burnout, failure, frustration, money worries about paying bills, and social media criticism. They'll also feel a deep urge to quit or change course dramatically.
- Rob never acted on those emotions in the moment. He calls this a huge win.
- His method is a cooling-off period of at least weeks, sometimes months, to sit with a big decision made while emotionally charged.
- Example: years ago he was frustrated with the startup space, and the team received a seven-figure offer to acquire MicroConf. It felt like they should take it, but they declined. He thinks he would regret selling to this day.
- His principle: don't let temporary emotional states make permanent decisions out of temporary problems.
- He built cumulatively over about 20 years: blog, then books, MicroConf, the podcast, TinySeed and the YouTube channel. Each built on the last. He didn't drop one because it was frustrating at the time.
- His advice is to develop the discipline to weather temporary low periods without making big decisions.
