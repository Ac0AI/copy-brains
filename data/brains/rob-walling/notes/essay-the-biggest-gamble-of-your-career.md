## Rob's pattern of repeated career gambles (2006 to 2014)

- Rob describes three earlier leaps: leaving salaried employment for consulting in 2006, leaving consulting for product in 2008 (with a wife, kid and a mortgage), and spending most of his bank savings on HitTail, "a broken-down SaaS app few people had heard of."
- The HitTail purchase scared him: he put years of work and savings on the line "based on nothing more than confidence that you will execute."
- His claim: "every chance you take feels like your biggest gamble _while you're taking it_." He adds that most of the time, choosing the gamble is the right choice.
- All three earlier gambles worked out, and he ended up better off than if he'd never taken them.

## Why taking more gambles makes you better at them

- Each gamble improves your ability to read the tea leaves and judge whether the next one will work.
- You gain confidence in your ability to execute when your back is to the wall, so you don't crumble under the stress of the decision.
- You can become "addicted to drawing yourself out of your comfort zone."
- Rob says to embrace the large amount of learning that comes from doing something that terrifies you.

## Drip's launch: flat revenue and churn as a market signal

- Drip launched in November (2013). Month one brought over $7k in recurring revenue.
- Months two (December) and three stayed at about $7k. Growth he expected from his proven marketing efforts didn't come.
- Many new subscribers from his "tried-and-true marketing efforts" churned out after a few months. He once called this "leaking out of the bottom of the funnel." Now he calls it churn.
- Onboarding completion was close to 60%, high for an app of this complexity. Users still didn't stick.
- Rob's reading: high churn means you haven't found a market that really needs your product, or you've built something no market wants (yet). It meant he hadn't solved the problem he set out to solve, which was fixing email marketing for startups.
- This is the learning phase between building and scaling (covered in his 2013 MicroConf talk). He calls it "the most painful part."

## Listening to customers and finding the real problem

- Drip had more than a dozen requests for "marketing automation" features: sending subscribers personalized emails based on behavior. Examples:
  - Tag a subscriber "SEO" if they read many SEO articles, then move them into an SEO crash course.
  - Tag people by links clicked, URLs visited, or things bought.
- He saw that tracking customers from visitor to subscriber to trial user to customer, with tailored communication throughout, is "insanely powerful" and "the future of email marketing."
- He resisted building it. It would be "a ton of work," the space had been crowded for years, and it's "not a typical bootstrapper play, it's the kind for which you raise funding."
- The more founders he talked to, the clearer the real problem became. Most had outgrown their first email tool (static newsletter services like MailChimp or AWeber) or had tried a heavy tool like Infusionsoft that failed on high price and low usability.
- The lesson: founders didn't need "a _little_ help" with email marketing. They needed a massive step up from the tools on the market. The problem was bigger than he first thought, and that bigger problem was the one to solve.

## Results of building marketing automation

- Plan: one month of development for email marketing automation aimed at SaaS, WordPress plugin and info product use cases. It took almost five months.
- He shipped in batches, a new feature every few weeks.
- Results while shipping:
  - Revenue climbed, first a few hundred dollars, then a thousand.
  - Churn dropped by more than 50%.
  - Revenue rose almost 50% in a couple of months.
  - All of this happened while he was heads down and did no marketing.
- He took this as a sign he was onto something: fixing the product to fit the market moved churn and revenue more than marketing had.

## The Drip 2.0 gamble

- Rob invested a year's worth of company profits in Drip, "funds that could buy a house in most cities in the world," plus the bulk of his waking hours for a year.
- Drip 2.0 entered a competitive market with massive upside and players who had raised millions of dollars.
- He frames this as the biggest gamble of his career, as every one before it felt at the time.
