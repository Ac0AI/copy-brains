## Step 1: Start with a simple product and a single traffic channel

- Rob says the biggest pitfall for first-time product people is "trying to create something too complex."
- His advice is a simple product with a simple marketing plan that needs only one traffic channel.
- He advises against starting with a stand-alone SaaS product. Recurring revenue is the holy grail, but a stand-alone SaaS product is more complex because you must build it and market it on your own.
- Easier first products are add-ons to an existing ecosystem. They are small to build, cheap for customers, and come with built-in discovery through a plugin repository or app store.
  - Examples: a WordPress plugin, a Shopify app, a Heroku add-on, Magento or Drupal add-ons, a Photoshop plugin, a WordPress theme, an ebook or a course.
- These projects are not "sexy" and won't land you on TechCrunch, but they get you profitable much earlier.
- Rob says he is confident he could not have made Drip work when he first started building products 13 years earlier.
- On traffic, he says to figure out one way of generating customers rather than mastering every channel at once. Early sales are a big win.
- In his case that meant getting good at SEO on his first project instead of learning SEO, AdWords and Facebook ads together.
- Others he has seen focus on free downloads from WordPress.org and then up-sell premium plugins, or rank for keywords in the Shopify or Heroku app stores.
- This lets you start earning without being a master marketer, and you add skills one by one as needed.

## Step 2: Double down until you own your time

- Step 2 is repeating the model that worked in Step 1 until you own your time. You may grow one product to a full-time income, or you may need to combine two or three.
- Rob names a common mistake: founders abandon what already works and take on a bigger challenge "before they're making enough money to own all of their time in a given week."
- Examples of founders who stacked small products:
  - Dave Rodenbaugh sold premium add-ons to users of his free plugin, then built two more plugins with premium options instead of jumping to SaaS or Facebook promotion. He later built the SaaS app Recapture as a "step 3" product.
  - Phil Derksen quit his job by stacking the revenue of two WordPress plugins, Pinterest Pin it Pro and Stripe Checkout Pro for WP.
  - Richard Chen of PHP Grid moved from one-time sales to leaving his job.
  - David of FatCatApps has Easy Pricing Tables and Easy Opt-in.
  - Fri Davies of the Dynamite Circle did this with Magento add-ons.
- Rob's own path: in 2005, as he moved out of consulting, he acquired DotNetInvoice, an invoicing product promoted almost entirely through SEO.
  - Its revenue grew incrementally until 2008, when he began acquiring and building more products instead of optimizing the one he had.
  - The new ones were simple one-time-sale products: a job board for electrical linemen, an e-commerce store for beach towels, and a couple of ebooks. They relied on SEO or AdWords traffic.
- Stacking them built enough product income to buy out 100% of his time and quit consulting.

## Why multiple low-LTV products beat optimizing one

- Rob's explanation for why the stacking approach grew faster than tweaking each product is lifetime value.
- Nearly all of these products had an LTV too low to justify anything except "free" organic traffic, such as Google or the WordPress plugin repository.
- Rob didn't know this at the time and wasted more than a year trying to scale his beach towel store.
- With an LTV of $10-15, "you just can't make paid advertising work. Or content marketing." Those approaches suit an app with a higher LTV, which he puts at $150+.
- Rule for Steps 1 and 2:
  - Stick to free traffic channels (organic or viral).
  - Use the first channel that works and grow it until it plateaus.
  - Replace your income with the first product, or by repeating the process with multiple products.
- Rob says none of his early projects were glamorous, but together they let him quit consulting and move to bigger projects with higher LTVs that gave him room to experiment.

## Step 3: Stand-alone recurring revenue

- Once you can justify going out on your own and have a few successes behind you, Rob says to take a bigger risk with a stand-alone product with recurring revenue.
- Why recurring revenue works: instead of repeatedly selling new product each month, every customer adds revenue this month, next month and beyond. Some customers churn, but a good product retains most of them month over month, which gives strong growth leverage.
- The tradeoff is a long ramp to substantial revenue. That is why he recommends finishing Steps 1 and 2 first.
- Recurring revenue also produces much higher lifetime values (he points to what people pay Basecamp or a hosting company over time).
- The higher value of each new customer opens up marketing approaches: PPC, display ads, content marketing, integration marketing and others.
- Example, HitTail:
  - He bought HitTail and optimized its sales funnel, which more than doubled his annual revenue even though the average purchase per customer was barely into double digits.
  - Because it was cheap monthly, customers stayed long enough to build a nice LTV.
  - That LTV gave him the room in ad spend to find a profitable Facebook ad campaign, which brought more than a thousand new customers over about 6 months.
- Rob says he doesn't think HitTail or Drip would have taken off as first projects. He doesn't know that he "would have had the time, money, skills, or confidence" to give them what they needed, and SaaS also brings technical complexity, marketing complexity and competition.

## Origin of the framework and the moonshot caveat

- Rob first noted the pattern in his notebook in early 2009, began using it as a framework on his podcast around 2010, and named it the Stair Step Approach in 2013.
- It comes from watching hundreds of entrepreneurs (arguably thousands) through his blog, podcast, MicroConf and TinySeed. He noticed it among bootstrappers who replaced their income, bought back their time and quit their jobs.
- His own move from consulting to products followed the same steps. His product revenue chart shows each jump coming when he moved to the next step.
- On moonshots, he says the tech media focus on the 1-in-10,000 that work and ignore the many who fail because they "try to play in the NFL before they've learned basic blocking and tackling."
- He makes no guarantee that the approach will launch a successful product right out of the gate. He calls it "a nice, low-risk path to a successful software company" based on patterns in the bootstrapper community.
