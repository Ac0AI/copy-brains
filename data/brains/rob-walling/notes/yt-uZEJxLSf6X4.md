## Rob's stair step approach, and how Corey Haynes applied it

- **Core idea:** Start with one small, simple product, then build on it as experience, revenue and confidence grow. Rob usually describes building a small product in an ecosystem like Shopify, Heroku or WordPress, or buying one.
- **The first steps:** One or two of those small products rarely pay a full-time living. Two or three of them can "buy out my time," which frees you to work on a bootstrapped SaaS.
- **Not only software:** Rob says the early steps can be a book, an ebook, a video course, audio, or an app-store add-on. He has since updated his post to focus on software because he is "all in on SaaS."
- **Corey's path:**
  - Courses: one-time info products
  - Newsletter: free, with a paid archive
  - Community: grouped into one membership
  - Pricing: from $100 to $500 a year
  - Corey calls this "a stair step in action."
- **Rob's reaction:** He called Corey's approach "equally viable" even though it was done "a little differently" from his own software-first version.

## Choosing which SaaS to build: fit with the founder

- **Personal fit matters most.** Rob says he struggles to answer "what SaaS shouldn't I build," because much of it is personal.
- **Avoid sales-driven companies if you don't want to run one.** Rob says sales-driven orgs "can grow really quickly," but he agrees you shouldn't pick one if it doesn't interest you.
- **Avoid customer groups you aren't fired up about.** He wouldn't launch a SaaS for a group he wasn't excited to work with.
  - He runs Tiny Seed and Microconf because he loves hanging around ambitious founders.
  - Tiny Seed has funded companies selling to home improvement and electrical contractors. Rob was an electrician for a while but has no affinity for that market, and he thinks he would get bored.
- **General cautions:** He mentions "maybe don't go B2C" and that prosumer products tend to have high churn.
- **Staying close to the metal:** Rob sold Drip six years before this episode. He felt himself drifting from deep founder knowledge until he started investing in and advising Tiny Seed companies.
  - He says hands-on gigs like Corey's marketing role at SavvyCal keep your chops up.

## Learning marketing: where to get the skills

- **Advice for a would-be founder:** Rob says to find a job at a small company with a lot of autonomy.
  - At a Fortune 1000 company you do one narrow thing, such as pay-per-click, in volume.
  - In marketing you would write a lot of content without strategizing or editing it, so you never learn what you like.
- **A hard road for pure coders:** Rob calls learning SaaS marketing "a hard road to go if all you've done is coding to date."
- **Where to learn:** Join Microconf Connect, sign up for Swipe Files, and follow people who know SaaS marketing.
- **Rob's recommendation for developers:** This is the point of the stair step. A developer already has the software skill.
  - Build a Shopify add-on, Heroku add-on, WordPress plugin, or buy a business.
  - You only need to learn enough marketing to rank well in the app store.

## What a developer should build first: app-store add-ons and acquiring

- **Income range:** App-store add-ons won't become a $5 million business. Rob has seen add-ons reach $10-15k a month, enough to quit a day job.
  - Tiny Seed applicants have been in the $10k-$50k a month range.
  - They aren't venture-backable, and that isn't the goal. The goal is the first two steps, which is "to basically buy out your time."
- **Buying a business:**
  - Rob says he jumped to product-market fit about four times by buying apps.
  - Hitto, his biggest buy at $30k, already had product-market fit. Nobody was marketing it, it was buggy, and its infrastructure was going down about twice a month. He fixed all that, then marketed it.
  - His early acquisitions were NetInvoice and Wedding Toolbox, in 2005-2006. They were in bad shape and he overpaid.
  - NetInvoice cost him $10-11k, "all the money I had in my bank account," which forced him to make it work.
- **Where the gap remains:** Buying a product doesn't solve marketing. If you can't market and sell a SaaS, that gap is still there.
  - Corey notes you can buy your way to product-market fit, but a developer can't "product" their way into growth.
  - Marketers can do this well. Ryan Culp and a partner bought FOMO with seller financing, used profits to pay a developer, and focused on growth.
- **Marketing and building:** Rob agrees software has two full-time jobs, building and selling, and splitting them is hard.

## Moving from consulting to products

- **Rob's own history:** He was a contractor with a micro agency billing $120-150 an hour. He says working on stuff that made no revenue "killed me."
- **Options he gives a successful consultant:**
  - Acquire a small product to jump ahead.
  - If you have a team, fork one person off to build a product, ideally an app-store product. He calls this "such an easy opportunity these days."
  - If you want to be more ambitious, go after the full SaaS. Then someone still has to market it, and a developer working part-time on the side can't.
- **Corey's additions:** Cut back to part-time consulting, as he did with SavvyCal, or productize the service.
  - Example: content marketing consulting can become a content agency, then an AI copywriting or SEO tool.
- **The income dip:**
  - Moving from full-time work or consulting to products almost always means an income dip, unless you raise funding.
  - It can take two, three or five years to get back to what you earned before.
  - What you gain is "the freedom, you have purpose, you have relationships."
  - Rob's rule: if you optimize for money, keep consulting. If you optimize for lifestyle and recurring product revenue, build the product.

## Marketing to customers who are not online, and expanding internationally

- **Offline or niche markets (insurance, legal, small theaters, contractors):** Rob lists the options:
  - Cold email, cold calls and cold LinkedIn outreach.
  - In-person trade events, usually a few a year.
  - SEO.
  - Partnerships and business development, which he thinks is underused.
  - Writing for or sponsoring trade publications.
  - Agencies that do marketing or web work for the space.
- **Example:** The Lawyerist is an education brand for lawyers, with courses. Rob knows the founder, lives in Minneapolis, and says legal-software founders know it.
- **Find who has your customers' ear:** Ask who has at least 10 or 20 of your customers on a list. Thousands would be better.
- **Dream 100:** He recommends Chad Holmes's "The Ultimate Sales Machine."
  - Pick your dream 100 customers. For some businesses it is 1,000 or 10,000.
  - Contact them in sequence: call, email, postcard, "lumpy mail" (a calendar in an envelope), then call again.
  - You spend about a year courting them and won't close them all, but they will notice you.
  - It needs the price point to justify it. You need the LTV or ACV.
- **Corey's influence-mapping tips:**
  - Survey existing customers about where they pay attention.
  - Do keyword research in Ahrefs or Ubersuggest.
  - Do video calls with podcasters or content creators in the space.
  - Then pay to play, set up affiliates, create content together, or show up at the conferences.
  - Sometimes there is nothing to map. One Swipe Files member sells to licensed insurance agents in the Northeast, who can be found in state licensing records. DMs, email, postcards and direct mail worked.
- **International expansion:**
  - Rob's advice for a German-language product is to go English next. Spanish is probably the next market after that.
  - Don't launch six languages at once.
  - If an English product is "running out of customers," Rob says don't translate it. "Usually that's the wrong choice." English-speaking markets are plenty big.
  - A translation means the whole marketing site, email support, the knowledge base, sales, landing pages and ads.
  - He can't go on podcasts in Germany because he doesn't speak German.
  - Corey adds that only the largest SaaS companies, such as HubSpot and Salesforce, expand internationally, and that is the last step.
