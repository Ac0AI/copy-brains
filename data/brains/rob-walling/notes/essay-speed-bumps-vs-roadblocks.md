## Speed bumps vs. roadblocks: the founder mental model

- Rob (co-host of Startups for the Rest of Us, a podcast on bootstrapping software startups) coined the framing while talking with his co-host, who was struggling to get his product to escape velocity. Rob told him: "It sounds like you're taking speed bumps and turning them into roadblocks."
- A **roadblock** keeps you from moving forward. A **speed bump** slows you down for a minute while you continue along your path.
- Early in his career, Rob saw everything as an insurmountable roadblock, a "company-ending event."
- He now forces himself to view obstacles as speed bumps. He says this simple shift made him more effective and less stressed.

## How to tell you're treating a speed bump as a roadblock

- Problems are inevitable when building a company.
- If you catch yourself saying any of these, your founder lens is seeing a roadblock:
  - "This is too big to fix."
  - "If this doesn't work, the business is finished."
  - "I'm out of ideas. I can't make this work."
- Real roadblocks exist, such as a platform shutting down its API or being sued out of existence. He says they are "much, much less common" than speed bumps.
- Speed bumps masquerade as roadblocks because founder stress and uncertainty make us believe things that aren't true. In constant fight-or-flight mode, most things feel like an existential threat.

## Examples from Rob's own experience

- **Landing a large customer for Drip:** His inner voice said "If you don't land this customer, the business is going to fail." He calls this factually inaccurate. Missing the customer would have been a disappointment, but several large potential customers showed up in the inbox every month, so there were options. He let it become a roadblock in his mind, which hurt his happiness and productivity.
- **Early TinySeed:** A founder declined TinySeed's investment terms. Rob's inner monologue said "Our approach is too new and people aren't ready. It's not going to work." Six months later TinySeed had a full batch of startups he was proud to work with. He had turned a minor rejection into a mental roadblock that stuck with him for multiple days.

## The cost of manufactured roadblocks

- Roadblocks stress your mind, body and relationships.
- Rob argues this is especially wasteful for founders who build startups to improve their lives rather than rearrange their lives around the company.
- Bootstrappers don't have to grow at all costs or answer to a board. They accept the handicap of not being able to raise large amounts of funding. Rob calls this "hard-earned freedom" that shouldn't be squandered by letting your mind invent roadblocks.
- After 14 years of building companies, he realized that most of his worry was not a good thing.
- His biggest founder regret is stressing too much about things that were going to work out: "I made roadblocks out of speed bumps."

## Problem-solving through optionality

- Rob's process changed from stressing about everything that could go wrong to mapping out 3 or 4 possible options if things do go wrong.
- The options are often not optimal, but none would be business-ending. Typical options:
  - Spend more money.
  - Invest more time.
  - Turn down a lucrative deal.
  - Navigate a sticky situation.
- These are things he would prefer not to do, but none would ruin the company.
- The mental shift is from "everything will end" to "we'll switch to plan B, C or D."
- He calls this one of the biggest leaps in his own psychology and "a life-changing realization." His regret is not discovering it years earlier.
- He heard the idea in full force again in the Startups for the Rest of Us episode about Bluetick (episode 448).
