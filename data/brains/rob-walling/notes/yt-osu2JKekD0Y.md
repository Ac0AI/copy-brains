## Bootstrapping as the default funding path

- Rob says the right funding move "depends" on your circumstances and goals.
- He defines bootstrapping as using your own funds, also called self-funding, with complete control of the company. He notes a nuance between the two terms.
- It is the simplest structure: no outside debt or investment. It is how most businesses are started, including dry cleaners and bookstores, not only tech.
- He argues "bootstrapping should be the default."
- Why: once you take an investor, they expect a return. That means either pulling profits out or a liquidity event (selling for a chunk of cash that goes to founders and investors).
- Investors can add real value through advice and may become friends. There is also the risk of a "bad apple."
- Bootstrapping fits if you want to run the company forever, decide when to sell, and avoid anyone looking over your shoulder.

## Bootstrapping is harder, and small businesses don't need VC

- Rob has seen both sides. He bootstrapped several companies, then sold to a company that had raised $38 million in venture capital. He then started Tiny Seed, which has raised north of $31 million under management.
- He says "unequivocally" that bootstrapping is harder and that raising funding makes things easier.
- His earlier lifestyle businesses had no reason to raise money. He knows of no investors who want to invest in businesses that make only a few hundred thousand dollars in revenue.

## What venture capital is and how the venture track works

- Venture capital is money raised from wealthy individuals or institutions to invest in high-risk, high-growth companies. Examples he names: Sequoia Capital, Andreessen Horowitz and Bessemer Venture Partners.
- Buying real estate or car washes is not venture. Buying businesses is usually private equity, which is a less risky model.
- Investors back VC funds for diversification. Endowments (Yale, Stanford, Harvard) have billions to deploy and don't want to be 100% in public stocks and bonds, because the stock market is bumpy (he cites 50% drops). In the US, VC funds raise from accredited investors.
- Fund managers earn a 2% annual management fee on assets managed, plus carried interest ("carry").
- Carry example: raise $10M and return $20M. The first $10M repays investors. Of the second $10M gain, the managers take 20% ($2M) and give back $8M.
- The typical portfolio: six or seven companies go to zero, two or three break even or return 2-3x, and they want one 100x return.
- The venture track means going "to the moon": building a billion-dollar unicorn or bust.
- On this track you hire fast and burn money. You raise new funding every 18 months at a higher valuation. You have a board of directors and board meetings.

## Trade-offs and complexity of taking venture money

- Rob says raising money is easier: you can hire more support and move faster. But complexity creeps in.
- VCs can often block a sale, especially at a low valuation. Example: you raise at a $5M, $10M or $20M valuation and someone offers $40M. That is a great outcome for you, but the VC usually won't want it because they want you to reach a billion-dollar valuation.
- VC funds have a 10-year duration, so they want companies to sell or go public within 10 years.
- You can't pull profits out under venture funding. They want you to go, then sell or IPO.

## Indie funding as the middle path

- Rob adds a category between bootstrapping and VC: "indie funding." It means raising from places like Tiny Seed or from angels who don't expect nine of ten companies to fail.
- Typical use: raise one round of $250,000 or $500,000, usually in the low six figures, to reach "escape velocity." Then become profitable or grow to a sale around $20M.
- It is simpler than VC: no board, nobody "breathing down your neck," and usually no giving up control.
- You can run the business and pull profits out of it, which VC doesn't allow.
- He has seen this emerge over the past nine or ten years. He started writing angel checks himself and found more companies than he had money to fund. That is why Tiny Seed was raised.
- He describes Tiny Seed as technically a venture fund and accelerator, but with a different mindset. It does not expect one in ten companies to be a billion-dollar unicorn while the rest fail.

## Debt and revenue-based financing for SaaS

- Rob hasn't used debt himself, but Tiny Seed has funded companies that did.
- Revenue-based financing is available to SaaS companies once they hit about $15,000 a month (he also says "15 grand, 20 grand a month").
- Rob thinks you can borrow about 4-6x your MRR. The lender takes a percentage of top-line revenue for two or three years until they make back two or three times their money.
- It is non-dilutive: no equity given up. It works for SaaS because revenue and growth are so predictable. He says it "used to be out of the question."
