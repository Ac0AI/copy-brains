## Why Rob says founders undercharge, and why charge more is still right

- Rob has called pricing "the biggest lever in SaaS" for years. At early MicroConf events about 13-14 years ago, Patrick McKenzie (patio11) told a room of founders "charge more", and Rob says that advice still holds.
- He points to Sam Altman admitting OpenAI loses money on the $200/month ChatGPT Pro plan. His takeaway is that if a sophisticated tech company can underprice, a small founder can too.
- Pricing is the one thing that, if wrong, can create a terrible business. If right, it can make a business 2, 3, 4 or 5 times more valuable and successful.
- Getting pricing right can be an unfair advantage over competitors.

## The four fears that keep founders from raising prices

1. **"Nobody will buy if I charge more."** Founders often undercharge while a competitor charges much more, yet still lack the confidence to raise prices.
2. **"My product isn't good enough."** Rob calls this "product impostor syndrome". In almost all cases the product is good enough.
3. **"My competitors charge less."** This is only a real constraint if you are a commodity that can be swapped one for one (a gallon of gas, an orange). The fix is differentiation: features, positioning, focus, brand, or some moat. Otherwise it is "a race to the bottom". He covers this in the SaaS Playbook.
4. **"I might wreck my business."** The odds are low. Rob has seen hundreds, if not thousands, of SaaS companies raise prices. A handful had to roll back, and he has seen a business actually broken only once. In that case competitors dropped their prices and the company didn't follow quickly enough, so the real cause was failing to react to new market data.

Across the 22 B2B SaaS investments he has made, the vast majority were underpriced at some point. The vast majority went "up and to the right" after raising prices. He says you can raise prices too much, but bootstrapped and mostly bootstrapped founders mostly undercharge.

## What raising prices does: faster MRR and lower churn

- **First-order effect:** more MRR/ARR and faster growth. Rob has shown TinySeed companies that started their batch year in May and raised prices in May or June on his team's advice. Their MRR growth increased significantly, making them "a completely different business".
- **Second-order effect, churn:** he has said for over a decade that lower-paying customers churn faster.
- An anonymous TinySeed company segmented its churn:
  - Customers paying $29/month had net revenue churn of over 11%.
  - Customers paying $99/month and up had net revenue churn of negative 4%.
- Rob says 11% churn "means your business is on fire", and that this one metric can be the difference between a $4 million/year business and one doing $1 million or $1.25 million.

## Case study: Gather's price increase

- When Gather joined TinySeed, its plans were $39 and $79 per month, and the business was not viable. It couldn't run sales calls at the scale it needed to grow.
- The team encouraged them to raise prices, and they worried they would get fewer customers.
- They moved from $39 to $99 and from $79 to $159. Rob says there was "no difference in conversion".
- Since then they have gone further. Their lowest plan is still $99, the mid plan is $199, and there is a custom pricing plan.
- They lost some solo and more price-sensitive customers, but churn went down and growth accelerated over roughly 6 to 8 months (Rob doesn't remember which).

## Quick tactics for raising prices

- **Shrink the value metric threshold.** Instead of 3,000 contacts or subscribers for $49, offer 2,500 for $49. This raises the effective price on one or all plans.
- **Drop your lowest plan.** For example, with $19, $49 and $99 plans, start at $49 or $49.99. It is an easy experiment that gets you comfortable without a cheap tier.
- **Don't count on split testing.** Rob has seen approximately zero SaaS companies split test pricing. A few have, but bootstrappers lack the volume, so you take a leap of faith and watch your numbers.
- **Decide whether it is an experiment or a certainty.**
  - If it is an experiment, make it easily reversible and "watch my numbers like a hawk".
  - If it is a certainty, make the increase a marketable event. Email everyone in the trial funnel, non-customers and customers. Try to drive annual conversions ahead of the change by offering to honor current pricing for those who sign up before a set date, even though prices rise in a couple of weeks.

## Existing customers and grandfathering

- Raising prices for new customers and raising them for existing customers are separate decisions.
- You can change new-customer pricing first, watch it for a month or two or three, and decide later whether to raise existing customers.
- Grandfathering means not raising prices on existing customers. The reasons to consider it are some churn, support complaints and burden, and possible damage to brand or reputation.
- Rob says raising prices on all customers is "usually not always" the right answer, provided you communicate it well.
- Give at least 2 months of notice, but not more than 6.
- **Rob's rule of 15:** if raising prices on existing customers won't grow MRR by at least 15%, it's probably not worth it.
- Never promise to grandfather for life, because in 1, 3 or 5 years you may want to raise prices again. "Never say that anything is for life."

## Structure of a price increase message

Rob backed this structure out of several well-constructed price increase emails and blog posts:

1. Set the stage.
2. Say "we're changing our pricing".
3. Give a high-level justification.
4. Optionally, give specifics on who is impacted and when.
5. Optionally, give more justification.
6. Close with "please reach out with questions".

He points to Jordan G's price increase blog post for CartHook (viewable in the Wayback Machine) as an example.
