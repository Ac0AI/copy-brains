## The number one goal of a website is getting first-time visitors to come back

- Rob argues the #1 goal of a website is not to sell software. Unless the price is under the "impulse" point of around $20, most people will not buy on their first visit.
- Very few people spend more than about $20 on a first visit. Above roughly $30, conversion rates for first-time visitors drop dramatically.
- Heavy-handed selling creates "an inexplicable feeling of pressure and discomfort." His line: "The ineffective marketer asks you to buy too soon."
- His real goal is to get first-time visitors to come back and have repeated, "confidence building" contact with the company or product.
- It is not the marketer's job to "shove your $300 product down their throat" on the first visit. The job is to convince visitors that coming back is worthwhile.

## Five reasons people don't buy

1. They need more information (does it have the features I need?).
2. They don't trust the product or company.
3. They don't have the budget.
4. They don't need it yet.
5. They are never going to buy (just researching).

- Rob says #5 can't be changed. The first four can be changed over time through repeated contact.
- Given the obstacles, he finds it "amazing anyone buys on their first visit to your site, at any price point."

## Data: returning visitors buy far more than first-time visitors

- Rob's motto is "Data is my co-founder." As a solo founder he relies on data for most decisions.
- He says the "magical 1%" conversion rate is an average of many individual conversion rates, not a single number.
- Returning visitors produced more sales than first-time visitors:
  - DotNetInvoice, over three years: 450% more sales.
  - JustBeachTowels (a site Rob used to own), over one year: 770% more.
  - AWPCP (an Academy member's product), over about three months: 400% more.
  - CrazyEgg, over 60 days: 1585% more.
- Rob says this is not proof. It is a rule of thumb that holds "in almost every case" in his selling experience.
- Typically 80% or more of website visitors are first-timers. Returning visitors are 4-16x more likely to buy, by his numbers.
- Tactic: set up a purchase goal in Google Analytics to track new vs. returning conversion on your own site. He says it takes 30 seconds and gives some of the most actionable data in an analytics package.

## Why email beats RSS and social media for bringing visitors back

- Rob's view is that a 5,000-person following on Facebook, YouTube, LinkedIn or Twitter is worth much less than a 5,000-person email list.
- Email's advantages, as he lists them:
  - Ubiquity.
  - Less time commitment to maintain.
  - Highest click-through rate of all the options.
  - You control what each individual user sees on a given day.
- RSS is used by a very small share of internet users.
- He warns against assuming the rest of the world shares your dislike of email.
- Social media and blogging are good for building an audience if you have the time, tools and talent. He says email should be the first goal, with social approaches coming once the email list is in place.

## Three-step setup: landing page, giveaway, automated follow-up

**Step 1: a landing page that earns the email.**
- People don't like to give out their email, so trust must come first.
- Build confidence with solid design that puts the email sign-up at the forefront, or with information that shows expertise.
- Examples he cites: jumpstartcc.com and dotnetinvoice.com.

**Step 2: give something away.**
- An 8-12 page PDF report or whitepaper works, especially with a strong title such as "6 Things You Must Know Before Implementing a CRM System" or "8 Expert Tips for Working with PDF Documents."
- The key to high conversion rates is doing something unique.
- Jumpstartcc.com's PDF was so well designed that Rob downloaded it without needing the content. It spread virally and sent traffic to the landing page.
- Smart Bear gives away a hardcover book by Jason Cohen. Its cost is down to $5 shipped, and it sends out 1000 a month.

**Step 3: automate the follow-up.**
- Set up a short email sequence sent over a few weeks or months that provides information, perhaps a discount, and builds trust in the product.
- Rob recommends MailChimp after using four email providers, citing its feature set, reliability and customer service. Its free trial allows up to 1000 subscribers.

## Split-test evidence: requiring an email plus follow-up lifted revenue

**Collectorz.com** tested requiring an email before the free trial download, with a 4-step sequence:
- Day 0: welcome, $5 discount coupon, download link, Getting Started guide link, testimonials.
- Day 2: buying guide, standard vs pro, barcode scanners, iPhone app, testimonials.
- Day 6: newsletter invitation, benefits of cataloging your stuff, testimonials.
- Day 30: outright invitation to buy, more testimonials.
- Results of collecting emails:
  - Downloads: down 33.6%.
  - Sign ups: up 20.7%.
  - Sales: up 3.4%.
  - Average first purchase value: up 13.5%.
  - Profits: up 15.4%.

**SolarAccounts** sent two emails over 14 days:
- Day 0: "Thanks for downloading – here is a useful How-To guide."
- Day 14: "Did you like our software?" (if the user had not opted out).
- Results: 15% fewer downloads and an 18% increase in sales.

- Rob says these two tests do not unequivocally prove the point. But they should make you curious enough to run your own split test, which takes a couple of hours of setup.
- Tools he names: Optimizely, Visual Website Optimizer and Google Website Optimizer.
- Based on these examples and his own products, he says the odds are good you can raise sales by 10% or more with a few hours of work over a few weeks.
