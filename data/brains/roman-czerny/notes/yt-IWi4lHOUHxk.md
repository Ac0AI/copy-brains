## Why Romàn chose SaaS over infopreneurship and Web3

- He is 30 and has done freelancing, infoproducts, and a Web3 venture (a raised round for a mobile game) before settling on SaaS about 2.5 years ago.
- His reasoning: past 25, you cannot relaunch a project or switch sector every 3 years, so you need something durable.
- SaaS gives revenue that is "quasiment très prédictible" (monthly or annual payers).
- It decouples the business from personal branding: customers buy the product, not him. He is replaceable, so the company is sellable.
- He says the leverage of 3 years in SaaS is far bigger than 3 years in infopreneurship.
- On Web3: friends made fortunes, but many projects failed and he took out almost nothing. He calls it "pile ou face", hard to make durable, and dependent on raising a lot of money at the right moment.
- SaaS can be built over 5 to 10 years (some for 20, like Salesforce) with large valuations.
- He likes earning online without showing his face much.

## Cocoai: copy a validated product, pivot fast, sell at about €1M+

- First attempt: he picked a Y Combinator startup from 2023 (One Text, "text to buy" via SMS) and adapted it. It failed in Europe: customers answered "yes" and did not understand why they were billed. About 6 months were lost.
- His lesson: YC backs about 500 companies a year and can afford 450 failures, so copying one YC startup is roulette. Also, you cannot transpose a US product directly.
- Pivot: he copied competitors already winning in Europe on WhatsApp and built "the same thing, better". First customers came right after the pivot, with very little churn.
- Outcome: about $50k MRR after roughly 8 months from first customers, sold for "plus d'un million".
- Why he sold:
  - Platform risk: WhatsApp depends on Meta, which changes rules and prices about every 3 months. Marketing messages were banned in the US during the sale process.
  - Part of the team was losing interest.
- Founder-side caveat: he sees the pivot from his own instinct to "copy what works" as a reaction to Web3 trauma ("j'ai trop fait confiance à mon instinct").

## How he sold the company

- He ran a Boolean search on LinkedIn for people with "buying SaaS" in their bio and contacted them. The first to answer became the buyer.
- The buyer had been looking for a Shopify SaaS for months and partnered with a fund.
- Timeline: a call, then about 2 months of due diligence with lawyers and many emails, described as smooth.
- Terms: 90% upfront, 10% paid over 3 years from the company's profits (he calls it not a true earn-out).
- The buyer paid lawyers (about $15k) and registration costs.
- Proceeds went up into holdings, reinvested within the legal window (he recalls about 70%, and says to check the exact figure), so no immediate tax. He sees it as "des points de vie" to build other projects for 10 years.
- Post-sale lesson: the US buyer communicated in English with a base that was about 60% French. Revenue fell about 15% below the sale level before recovering. Cultural and language fit matters when selling abroad.

## Killing two SaaS fast before Gojiberry

- Gojiberry V1 (AI UGC videos): LinkedIn posts got several million views and 3,000 to 4,000 signups but only 4 or 5 paying customers, about €300 MRR.
  - He killed it within a week. It attracted the curious, solved no deep need, and he saw no brand using it long term.
  - Generation costs were high, so margins were weak.
- V2 (AI call recorder, like Fireflies and tl;dv): built in 2 weeks, sold roughly $300 to $400 MRR.
  - Every demo asked "what's different?" and he had no answer. He used Fireflies himself.
  - Those companies burn their funding on transcription, and selling $20 licences to non-decision-makers is painful.
  - He killed it as well.
- Principle: he was looking for an explosive project, not a slow grind.

## Gojiberry V3: sell before you build

- Choosing the need: the most pressing need across all his conversations was "more customers". "N'importe quel business te dira: si je peux avoir plus de clients, je suis chaud."
- Product idea: detect buying signals (for example people interacting with certain LinkedIn posts), then enrich the contact with email and phone. He copied a US YC-backed, profitable company, Origami. Many signal tools do not enrich contacts, and that is the gap he filled.
- Validation: he got on a call with Origami as a mystery client. The rep spent 30 minutes explaining why he would not sell, which signalled strong demand.
- Pre-sale: a Lovable landing page that simulated the output (his own prompts, no software). Emailed to prospects, it brought $3,000 to $4,000 in the first week, at about $500 per customer, before a line of code was written.
- Inspiration: a free course by Adam Robinson (Retention.com, Warmly-style visitor identification), who offered the idea at a conference and people handed over their cards.
- Rule: "je coderais pas une seule ligne avant qu'on me donne de l'argent", even with AI.
- Targets: B2B teams at companies with 100M+ revenue.
- Growth: about 25% per week, about 250 clients, one French. US only.
- Unfair advantage: his developer had already built LinkedIn signal extraction and multi-account management, worth 6 to 12 months of head start.
- Why V3 beat V1 and V2: sales is a need felt immediately, whereas accounting or HR tools come later.

## Gojiberry growth and sales stack

- Team: 3 full time plus 2 freelancers about to become employees.
- Early manual phase: for 2 weeks he used 5 Pakistani freelancers on Fiver, following a protocol, to find buying signals before the product existed.
- Channel mix: cold email and outreach drive about 60 to 70% of demos, and the other 30 to 40% is inbound (Reddit, LinkedIn).
- Stack:
  - Gojiberry to scrape leads who like or comment on topics like cold mailing and lead generation, and to enrich them.
  - Instantly for cold email: 3,000 to 4,000 emails a day, scaling up. AI pre-replies overnight; he reviews and sends about 500 in 2 minutes.
  - Walaxy for LinkedIn outreach campaigns on the same leads.
  - WhatsApp messages (or calls for French prospects) to hot leads.
  - Reddit posting, automated, which brings a lot of demos.
- Dogfooding: using the product to grow the company is "la meilleure preuve au monde".
- Leverage: AI lets him do the work of about five marketers from a few years ago.

## Copilot AI, not full AI, and why SaaS survives LLMs

- Copilot vs full AI: he does not believe in 100% AI sales agents ("AI SDR"). AI is better than humans at lead generation, but personalisation needs judgment.
  - Example: if one contact does not reply, a human adds their manager, takes both phone numbers, checks their comments, and phones.
  - He advises calling people with a human voice.
- Argument that SaaS is not dead:
  - ChatGPT launched a note taker and everyone said it would kill note-taking tools, yet a month later Fireflies and tl;dv were still growing.
  - Mainstream features evangelise the category, and niche buyers then look for specialised tools.
  - People who say "I can do it with n8n" ignore maintenance. A 2,000-person company would not pay someone $5k to $7k a month to maintain it, versus his $500 tool.
  - A friend's SaaS launched 10 years ago was told "WordPress does it free" and now makes tens of millions a year. DIY people do not pay.
  - He reasons that VCs invest millions in SaaS with multi-year visibility.
  - Customers still value UX, an experience, continuity, and a human to talk to.
- Warning: incumbents without AI (a friend's large SaaS) face new entrants that replicate in 1.5 years what took 10.
- Vibe-coding tools: he sees Lovable as good for demos and videos, not a replacement for a developer. He has not replaced his own.
- Founder advice: SaaS is "niveau 2". Take a technical cofounder; a paid dev will never be as committed as someone with skin in the game.
- Capital: you need runway because you won't pay yourself. He says at least €30k to €50k, since tools alone (Instantly about $500 a month, an accountant, AI licences) add up.

## SaaS ideas and niches he sees

- Pre-warmed mailboxes for cold email: a huge demand, and few suppliers (mostly India, Pakistan, some US). "Chaque personne à qui je parle de cold email me pose cette question". Instantly likely marks up its own heavily.
- Reddit keyword monitoring with AI-drafted comments (he uses Popsy, YC-backed, with competitors): a similar signals idea applied to Reddit.
- Cheaper LinkedIn outreach copilot: existing tools (Waalaxy-like) are expensive and make you write the messages. He sees room for a cheaper tool with AI credits and pre-written messages.
- Vertical SaaS in the US: depth of market lets micro-niches reach multi-million exits. Examples: a SaaS for landscapers only in Florida sold for about $20M to $30M; a vet note-taking tool valued at hundreds of millions; therapist booking software making about €120k MRR (told secondhand).
  - A niche lets you target a message, build a network, and ship features generalist tools skip. A US state is as deep as France.
- AI email assistant by niche: Fixer-style tools pre-answer a Gmail inbox. He suggests niching it for construction trades.
- Consumer apps: Cal AI (calorie scanning, a ChatGPT wrapper) is marketing-driven, using around $50/year plans. He advises against cloning it: the barrier is low (about €2,000 to rebuild) but copying marketing genius is not possible.
- Distribution check before choosing a niche: he dropped a restaurant answering-machine idea because restaurateurs are very hard to reach. Ask how you will reach the target first (for example Facebook groups for vets).
- Source of ideas: YC's twice-yearly manifestos on trends, since they now back companies already making money. He says AI agents are the main theme, in copilot or full-AI versions.
