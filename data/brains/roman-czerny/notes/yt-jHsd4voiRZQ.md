## Validate with real revenue before building: manual fulfillment, payment links, then outbound

- Romàn's rule: "Don't build anything unless people want to pay you." If people are not throwing their credit cards at you, you don't have the right product.
- Gojiberry's third version (V3) was sold before it was built. V1 (UGC) lasted two weeks; V2 (a note-taker, chasing the Fireflies trend) lasted one month and was "really hard to sell".
- For V3 the developer told the founders to sell the solution first and he would build it once customers existed.
- Romàn pitched companies on high-intent leads and delivered manually: he searched LinkedIn lead by lead, about 24 hours of work per 100 leads, at $100/month per customer.
- That manual service reached $10k MRR in about two months. Then they told every customer the service was shutting down and relaunched it as a platform, which is when growth took off.
- Revenue also motivated the developer, since it proved people wanted to buy.
- He says too many people "build something no one wants and they hide behind the fact that they are building".
- Early customers say "I'd love to use it when it's out" to be nice. The real test is to send a payment link to secure their spot. If most pay, it is validated; some will not.
- Goal for new founders in 2026: get traffic to a landing page as fast as possible and validate; don't procrastinate.

## Early go-to-market sequence: outbound first, then funnel, then volume

- Phase 1, validation: for the first 10-50 customers he does everything outbound (calls, emails, LinkedIn requests) and doesn't communicate on social media at all.
- He takes demos even for $50-$100 customers, to learn who they are, why they said yes and what they want.
- He "would never start a SaaS without doing calls at the beginning, even if it's painful".
- Phase 2, funnel: once he knows what people want, he builds a funnel for that audience and pushes volume in to see where it breaks and where people drop off. He tests several prices.
- Phase 3, scale: with a working funnel, "you only need more people". If 1,000 visitors produce X revenue, the game becomes impressions and signups.
- He is cash-conscious: Gojiberry is profitable and had not spent any YC money by the time of the interview, so he doesn't spend money he doesn't have to make things go faster.

## Reddit as a volume and validation channel

- Romàn created roughly 2,000 posts on Reddit, pushing hard.
- It brought about 2,000 visitors per day. Reddit traffic is "not so qualitative", but the volume was enough to find who converts and how they behave in the funnel.
- Posts were advice-style content on outreach, sales and marketing, run from many accounts.
- He estimates Reddit alone took them from zero to $20K MRR.
- Limit: showing up 10 times a day everywhere hurts the brand, so they expanded to X, LinkedIn and cold email once Reddit had validated the funnel and told them who pays.

## Current Gojiberry acquisition channels

- LinkedIn lead-magnet posts: the team posts many of them, and they bring many customers. LinkedIn traffic is far more qualified than X or Reddit for an outreach SaaS.
- The lead-magnet recipe: post a result ("I helped a website rank top 10 on Google in under six months"), tell people to comment a keyword (e.g. "SEO") for the recipe, reply to each commenter with a Notion link containing a video of who you are, what you solve and a book-a-demo CTA. More engagement means more Notion visits, website visits and customers.
- X: two founders have large followings and go viral about weekly. It works for founders, solo founders and SMBs of 1-10 (maybe 20) people, with higher churn but an effectively infinite lead pool. It doesn't reach heads of sales, who don't self-identify on X.
- Other channels: YouTube, a webinar three times a week, B2B influencers on LinkedIn, a bit of Meta ads, the YC exposure, word of mouth.
- Affiliates bring about a quarter to a fifth of revenue.
- Paid placement: they paid a well-known X creator for mentions and a TrustMRR listing. About $1,500 spent produced around $1,000 of new MRR per month, profitable but small at their stage; they keep it for staying top of mind.

## Webinars instead of one-on-one demos

- Every marketing asset (blueprints, SEO pages, influencer docs) links to the webinar, which runs three times a week with about 40 people per session.
- They stopped one-on-one calls because they didn't scale for customers paying $100-$200 per month.
- Format: explain high-intent leads and the results possible, demo the product for 15 minutes, answer chat questions, and invite a customer on stage to present their business.
- Romàn says he likes marketing but not sales, and finds webinars energizing because he runs the show.

## YouTube: rank for competitor "review" searches

- Strategy: publish videos titled "[competitor name] review 2026" and spend about 10 minutes reviewing their SaaS.
- Mid-video he says "we have an alternative" (Gojiberry), and viewers sign up. He compares it to Google ads, but free.
- Output: about 60 videos since January against a goal of one per day; he admits he is behind.

## Cold email and outreach tactics

- High-intent leads: Gojiberry finds leads who engaged with a competitor, a keyword or specific influencers. He says once you've tried high-intent leads you "can never go back to a cold database".
- Reply rates run two to five times those of competitors using static databases. Targeting active leads alone gives about 2x; adding personalized messages and an aligned LinkedIn profile (good picture, clear line) gives 4-5x.
- Scraping 1,000 people from Apollo or Sales Navigator yields maybe 100 who might be interested. Some are inactive on LinkedIn, changed jobs or don't care.
- Volume: about 6,000 emails per day for Gojiberry's own outreach. It is capped by how many people show intent each day. Gojiberry itself is used for low-volume warm outreach (roughly 20 personalized messages per day), and he uses Instantly for cold email.
- Best current campaign: pay B2B influencers to post lead-magnet posts on LinkedIn, then retarget every liker and commenter by cold email: "I saw you commented on this post. Maybe you didn't get the resource, would you like me to send it again?" It gets an "insane" reply rate and keeps them top of mind.
- Earlier variant: pull the commenters of competitors' lead-magnet posts (some had 3,000 commenters), email "did you get the blueprint?", then mention his own blueprint on the same subject; people usually say send yours.
- Copy rules: messages are short, about three lines. The lead should not need to think more than two seconds. Use a low-friction CTA like "I've done X for Y, I can show you how. Reply yes and I'll send it to you", not "How are you addressing sales at your company?"
- Subject lines: don't trick people ("your order is here", "your Meta ad has been banned"), which is a red flag for bigger companies. Examples he uses: "you commented, here is your blueprint", "did you get it". He admits he should A/B test headlines more.
- AI vs human copy: about 80% of users get better results from AI-written messages because they don't know what to write. In his own five campaigns, AI sometimes beats his hand-written ones, so he runs a mix.

## Free trials, paywalls and AI-SaaS pricing

- AI SaaS has real token costs, so free usage attracts users (he cites India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, sometimes South America) who use it heavily but can't pay.
- Gojiberry's setup: a paywall at signup, credit card required, 7-day free trial. If seven days isn't enough, support in chat gives 14.
- Even so, India is their third-largest nationality of signups, and only 2-3% of those pay, versus an average of 40% overall.
- His point: with free AI usage, people will use it free and you can't blame them.

## Selling a SaaS: lessons from the Koko AI exit

- Koko AI did WhatsApp marketing and AI chat for e-commerce brands and went from zero to $500K in a few months. Churn was low, but nearly all customers were French. A competitor held the upmarket, leaving them mid and low market, and two of the co-founders wanted out.
- The buyer was found when Romàn reached out on LinkedIn to people with "looking to buy a SaaS" in their bio; contacts passed the word along. The sale to an American buyer took two months. The buyer doubled the business by finding US customers.
- Lesson: when a buyer really wants you, they move fast.
- Due diligence prep: have accounting in order, because a mess slows it down. Keep personal expenses out of the company, since buyers go through every bank-statement line to check your story.
- Keep the buyer's attention during the process: send updates (e.g. "just closed a customer"), because buyers usually run several due diligences at once and you need to stay the best option.
