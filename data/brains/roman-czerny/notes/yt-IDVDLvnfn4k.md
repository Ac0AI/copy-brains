## Validating before building: customer first, then sell before code

- Romàn's core rule: "Everything starts with the customer, not with the product." A good idea comes from listening to the right people: how badly do they want the problem solved, and how much will they pay?
- His first SaaS failed. His team copied "One Text" (a YC-backed text-to-buy company), rebuilt it on WhatsApp for Europe, and spent 6 months building. Pilots stopped fast because European customers weren't ready to pay by text. He says one week of customer conversations would have shown that.
- He now treats having money before code as the signal: "having money before you code is a great sign. It means that really you are into something."
- Copying only works if the clone targets a real problem and the right audience. He warns against copying a company without knowing its unfair advantage, since its numbers may be misleading.
- Goji Berry went through V1 (AI UGC, reached about $500 MRR, no interest in evolving it) and V2 (a note taker like Fireflies, built as an MVP in about two weeks, very hard to sell) before V3. For V3 the developer waited while the two non-technical founders sold for the first month.

## Cloning a proven product and launching in two weeks

- After the failed text-to-buy product, his team looked for WhatsApp tools already selling in Europe and making real money, rather than inventing a product.
- Finding them: one founder shared numbers on LinkedIn. The Chrome extension Store Leads shows which stores install a Shopify app, and team size also signals revenue. He searched "WhatsApp" in the Shopify App Store and created an account in each competing app.
- He picked the most successful app that was easiest to clone with what the team had already built. They implemented two main features in about two weeks.
- He re-contacted everyone from the text-to-buy outreach and landed 5 to 10 clients almost immediately, because almost no one sold it in France.
- The text-to-buy build had been harder than the competing apps, so adapting a few features and rules was enough. He describes the result as not the best product, but one that worked.

## Cold outreach engine: volume, targeting and the customer-service trick

- Goji Berry's earlier product, Coco AI, got its early traction almost entirely from cold outreach, mostly in France. His daily list: about 100 calls, 1,000 to 2,000 cold emails, demos, and LinkedIn outreach.
- Demos closed at 60 to 70%, so the game was getting demos. Outside France, close rates fell to about 15 to 20% (Germany, Spain), which made scaling harder.
- ICP: Shopify stores making over $10K/month in Europe, with AI-tool users a plus.
- Lead sources: LinkedIn (people sharing results, then everyone interacting with them), Sales Navigator, Store Leads, Fox Leads.
- His scaling trick: email the customer service address with a demo video and ask them to forward it to the CEO. They almost always do, the CEO books a call, and replies come back nearly 100% of the time because support always answers, so emails avoid spam and volume can be high.
- Tools today: Goji AI (their own) with 5 LinkedIn accounts, plus Instantly for email, about 5,500 messages a day combined. At the start you don't need that much; one LinkedIn account and a few inboxes is enough.
- Why outreach ranks first: you know right away whether it works. If 5,000 emails get no answers, the offer or messaging is bad. Unlike Reddit or YouTube, it scales fast.
- Once a campaign has good response rates, he leaves the copy alone until it dies. His weekly job is adding leads and replying to people.

## The "blueprint" offer in cold messages

- Instead of asking for a demo, which he finds intrusive for strangers, he writes the same email but offers a blueprint at the end.
- Saying yes to a blueprint is easier than saying yes to a demo, so most say yes.
- The blueprint is about 10 pages, full of value, with a booking link at the end.
- Most readers don't book a call; they go to the site and start a free trial. He says this puts the offer in front of more people than a demo ask.

## Selling before building: the $7K pre-code playbook

- Trigger: Coco AI worked because outreach targeted high-intent leads, such as people commenting on LinkedIn posts about WhatsApp or email marketing. They were far more responsive.
- He emailed roughly 20,000 people across many industries in the US, offering high-intent leads, with a refund promise if it didn't work. He charged $200 to $300/month for 100 to 200 leads.
- After each call, buyers paid, and only then did he figure out delivery: manually first, then with a coworker, then Fiverr workers following an exact blueprint.
- Example blueprint: for a dental clinic hiring nurses, search "dental clinic nurse" on LinkedIn, collect people interacting, extract the right ones, find email and phone, send the lead list. Clients were happy because lead quality was high.
- He reached $7,000 before writing code. Demand grew until it was unmanageable, so they paused almost everything, then built the platform and automated it.
- Timeline: sold Coco AI in February, launched Goji Berry V3 in September. He says restarting is painful and that great products with low churn are hard to rebuild.

## Distribution: add channels one at a time, starting with Reddit

- He disagrees with the "focus on one or two channels" advice, especially early. His process: post a lot on one channel, learn how it works, then keep adding channels while time per channel shrinks.
- Goji Berry grew from 0 to $33K MRR in 4 months and reached $50K in about 6, with channels added in this order: Reddit, outreach, X, SEO, YouTube, Trustmrr ads, and most recently Threads.
- Reddit: about 11 million impressions in 4 months, driving tens of thousands of visitors. It costs nothing but ChatGPT and storytelling. He posted 3 times a week across 10 SaaS subreddits.
- Formula: tell a story, back it with proof, include a link. Examples: a post on applying to Y Combinator and being rejected after the interview (what they did, what YC said, proof, link), and a post sharing a five-influencer campaign playbook with budget and results.
- Every business experiment (customer service, sales) becomes a Reddit post.
- Hack: get about 10 upvotes fast. A WhatsApp group of friends shares each new post so everyone upvotes it.
- Reddit, YouTube and similar channels are hard to scale (three posts a week won't become 30), which is why outreach stays number one.

## Paid and partner channels: influencers, newsletters, affiliates, Trustmrr

- LinkedIn influencers beat newsletters. They have fewer deals, so prices can be cut in half or doubled in posts, and one or two customers can repay the spend. One viral post makes it very profitable, which a newsletter can't do. Finding them: keyword search and checking who sponsors competitors.
- Newsletters (French only so far): profitable over two to three months, payback in about three months, "not incredible". Attribution is hard. More competition from other SaaS.
- Affiliates: 30% lifetime commission, planning to raise to 40%, 50%, even 60%. Higher commissions attract more promoters and add visibility. New affiliates get a kit of videos to repost and text to paste, because most don't know how to promote. Affiliates are 10 to 15% of revenue, and he calls it under-worked. Recruiting is just a site link and occasional emails.
- Trustmrr ad: even if the ad didn't pay, posting about it on X would. Marc Lou retweeting the product twice brought many customers and leads. The ad itself brings a client every two to three days. A retweet from a founder with many followers beat the ad cost.

## 90-day plan if starting B2B SaaS from scratch

- Start from your skills and unfair advantage. Study how competitors market and whether they raised funds or bootstrapped, then pick your own path.
- Build 7 PowerPoint slides: logo, pain points, solution, how the product works, price.
- Message 100 to 500 people, get them on calls, and ask about their problems. Pain points usually differ from the slide. Adapt each time.
- When someone will pay, deliver it as a service, or promise to build it and then find a co-founder or paid developer. Validate with 5 to 10 people before going all in.
- Marketing: first cold outreach, with a LinkedIn automation tool and email tool at about $300 to $500 total, aiming for 10 more demos. When it can run near autopilot, add inbound on LinkedIn, X and Reddit (e.g. a Reddit post on how you're solving the problem, asking for feedback).
- Month 1: outreach. Month 2: outreach plus inbound. Month 3: more traffic sources, maybe paid or influencers, depending on money. He notes this is harder with zero capital.
- What moves the needle: do more of what works. If 10 messages yield one demo, send 100, then 1,000. He says people underestimate the energy needed.

## Selling a SaaS and building a team

- Coco AI reached $500K/year and sold for seven figures about two years after starting. Reasons: only France was reachable, other markets closed at 15 to 20%, two teammates wanted to sell, and he had no long-term vision for the product.
- Marketplaces like Acquire.com and Flippa gave offers of a quarter or third of the final price because it lacked a year of revenue. He instead contacted Shopify app founders with portfolios of apps, then answered a LinkedIn post from a buyer looking for businesses in his size range. The sale closed about two months later: 90% upfront, 10% over three years.
- He credits both work and luck.
- Team: four at Coco AI (two marketing, one product, one code), three at Goji Berry (one each). The code is not his part and he doesn't discuss tech tools.
- Goji Berry now has about 500 customers. The plan is a GTM copilot that tells you whom, when and how to contact, rather than only sending messages, with a lean team and no hiring soon.
- He knows what he wants to build because he's done outreach for five years and is the product's core user.
- Mistakes: building something customers didn't want (Coco AI), moving too fast on marketing and paying fake influencers.
