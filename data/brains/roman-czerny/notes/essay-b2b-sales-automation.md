## Automate in order: cheapest and safest first, targeting fourth

- Romàn's core claim: "Automation multiplies whatever you feed it." A good sequence sent to the wrong list reaches the wrong people faster, with better follow-up.
- His example: a three-person team buys a sending tool in week one. By week three it pushes 400 emails a month at an unargued list. The sequence runs perfectly and the pipeline doesn't move, because real work was automated in the wrong order.
- He defines B2B sales automation as handing mechanical work (capturing activity, sending, scheduling, reminding, updating records) to software. Judgment stays with the human: software decides when something happens, the seller decides what it says.
- Five jobs in a seller's week: find accounts worth a message, draft a first message, chase people who went quiet, get a time on the calendar, keep the record straight.
- His five-step order: 1) log what happened, 2) book meetings, 3) send follow-ups, 4) decide who to write to, 5) draft the first version.
- Why targeting sits fourth: better targeting only pays once you can act on it. A team that can't follow up reliably gains nothing from a perfect list, because the leads leak out through the same hole. "Steps one to three build the container. Step four fills it."

## Steps 1 and 2: logging and booking cost nothing and pay back fast

- **Logging:** connect a calendar and inbox to a CRM so meetings and threads land on the right record untyped. It removes the Friday hour three reps spend reconstructing Tuesday. No prospect sees it, so a mistake costs a tidy-up, not an account. Setup is an afternoon: connect mailbox and calendar, import the deals spreadsheet, skip workflows for now.
- **Booking:** a shared link ends the four-message exchange about Tuesday at three. Romàn calls it the fastest payback on the list. A rep booking six meetings a month saves roughly an hour of scheduling and loses fewer slots to people who cooled off. Setup is about 20 minutes: one meeting type, hours you'll defend, link in the signature.
- Prices he read on vendor pages in August 2026:
  - Attio is free for up to 3 seats.
  - HubSpot's free tools stop at 2 users; Sales Hub Starter is $7 per seat monthly billed yearly, $20 pay-as-you-go.
  - Pipedrive Lite is $14 per seat monthly billed yearly, no free tier.
  - Cal.com is free for one user; Teams is $12 per user monthly billed yearly.
  - Calendly Standard is $12 per seat monthly, $10 prepaid.
- Cal.com Teams (round-robin) is not worth buying until someone actually needs round-robin.

## Step 3: write follow-ups yourself, let the tool handle timing

- "Most deals die from silence, not rejection." A three-step sequence recovers more conversations than any new channel added this quarter.
- It is the first automation a prospect notices, so the quality bar rises. His rule: "Write them yourself and let the tool handle the timing."
- Deliverability setup before any prospect hears from you:
  - Buy a sending domain kept away from your main one.
  - Set SPF, DKIM and DMARC.
  - Warm the mailbox for two weeks (a day of technical work, then waiting; "you can't skip the waiting").
- Sender pricing he lists: Smartlead Base $39/mo (2,000 active leads, 6,000 sends, unlimited mailboxes and warmup; CRM and API only from Pro at $94). Instantly Growth $47/mo, $37.60 prepaid (1,000 contacts, 5,000 emails; lead database is a separate subscription). Lemlist Email $69/mo per workspace, $55 prepaid; Multichannel returns to per-seat pricing at $109.
- Budget floor for sending: about $39 a month. Free stops at sending because deliverability needs a separate domain and warmup.

## Step 4: work from live buying signals, not a stale list

- Replace a static export with signals: a funding round, a job change, a hiring page that suddenly wants five sellers.
- Why lists rot: he cites US median job tenure of 3.9 years and 22% of workers with their employer under a year (BLS), so a list built two quarters ago is already wrong about a chunk of it.
- Setup is about half a day to describe the ICP and pick signals.
- Data-tool pricing he lists:
  - Clay: free tier (100 data credits, 500 actions/month), Launch $185/mo or $167 prepaid. Powerful (waterfalls across 100+ providers) but needs someone who enjoys building tables.
  - Apollo Basic: $49/seat/mo yearly or $59 pay-as-you-go; free plan has 900 credits a year. An email costs one credit, a phone number eight.
  - Hunter: free 50 credits/month; Starter $49 or $34 prepaid. It's a verifier more than a list builder, so use it right before sending.
- Gojiberry, his own product, is $99 a month flat for the team, tracking 30+ buying signals across the socials and the web. He is open that it ranks first in his comparison "because it's ours."

## Step 5 and the four things never to automate

- **Step 5, drafting:** automate it last, and use the AI writer inside the sender you already bought rather than a fifth subscription. Treat output as a first pass: the tool supplies structure and a subject line, you supply the one sentence proving you read something about the company. Setup is about an hour, mostly on the prompt describing how your team sounds.
- **Never automate:**
  - **The reply to a real reply.** Someone answering is the event you paid for. Write back yourself, from your own mailbox, within the day. The second message in a thread is where trust forms or dies.
  - **Objection handling.** An automated answer treats a specific concern as a category and lands as a brush-off; the conversation ends politely and nobody notices.
  - **Pricing and negotiation.** A number sent by software becomes the anchor, and you lose the context you'd have asked about and the room to move.
  - **Anything a bad list would multiply.** Automating before qualifying the list scales the mistake. CAN-SPAM has no B2B exception, with penalties up to $53,088 per violating message. "Volume turns a sloppy list into an expensive one."
- His view of replacement: automation takes the parts of the job nobody wanted (data entry, scheduling messages, remembering day-four follow-ups). Reps still answer replies, handle objections and quote prices, which is where deals turn.

## The "rule of 7" and how many touches to send

- He calls "a buyer needs around seven contacts" mid-century advertising lore with no study behind it: "folklore with a useful shape."
- The useful version: most sellers stop after one or two messages, so persistence separates those who book meetings from those who conclude outbound doesn't work. Consistency beats the exact count: "Three touches you send every time will beat seven you send when you remember."
- A ceiling applies. A seventh message to someone who never opened the first six reads as a complaint waiting to happen. Set the sequence at three or four touches, then stop and put the account back in the pool.
- Before automating, write the process on one page: how many touches, how many days apart, what happens on a reply, what happens on silence. Otherwise three reps each follow up at their own rhythm and you get three different machines. "An hour with a document saves you a quarter of confused reporting."

## A four-week plan and stack economics for a 3-person team

- **Week 1:** Attio free plan (covers exactly three seats), connect mailboxes and calendars, import deals. Cost: $0.
- **Week 2:** each rep gets a free Cal.com account with one 30-minute meeting type. Cost: $0.
- **Week 3:** buy Smartlead Base ($39), register a separate sending domain (roughly $12 a year), connect two mailboxes, switch on warmup, touch no prospects for 14 days, and write the three-step sequence while waiting.
- **Week 4:** fix the list before the sequence goes live. Point the sequence at a signal-based list instead of an export. Gojiberry adds $99 after its 7-day trial.
- Total: $138 a month, $46 per seller.
- The by-the-head comparison he gives for the same four jobs: Pipedrive Growth $39, Calendly Standard $10, Lemlist Multichannel $109, Apollo Basic $49, which comes to $621 for three people. The flat stack still costs $138 when a fourth rep joins.
- Billing model matters more than headline price: senders bill by workspace, data tools by head or credit.
- He expects teams to feel the change in week four rather than week one, because that is when targeting lands on top of working plumbing.

## Where automation goes wrong

- **Sending before warming the domain:** a new domain firing 200 emails on day one lands in spam and stays there. Google asks bulk senders to keep spam complaints under 0.3% (applied since February 2024). Two weeks of warmup on a dedicated domain costs only patience and protects the mailbox you run the company from.
- **Unverified lists:** bounces waste the send and signal to mailbox providers that you don't know who you're writing to. Verify first; Hunter's free 50 credits a month is enough for a small batch.
- **Tools that don't talk:** a sender that can't write back to the CRM turns reps into copy-paste operators. Check whether the CRM integration is included in your plan or gated a tier higher (he notes Smartlead holds CRM and API back on Base).
- **Unwritten process:** see the one-page sequence rule above.
- **Origin of his targeting view:** before Gojiberry, Romàn prospected on the socials for his previous company, Coco.ai. He noticed the accounts that replied were the ones showing a signal that week: a funding round, a new hire, a comment on the right post. He was hunting intent by hand, one tab at a time. A small team has no operations person to maintain a scoring model and no hours to check 200 profiles a week, so he built that part, kept the price flat so headcount stops mattering, and left the reply to a human.
- Customer results he cites: Wispra gets 60% of weekly demos from intent signals; Mindflow saw a +31% reply rate after moving to intent-based outbound; GTE Localize booked 100+ meetings after swapping email blasts for intent-led outreach.
- His honesty note: he has not run a controlled reply-rate test across the 13 tools and won't invent a percentage.
