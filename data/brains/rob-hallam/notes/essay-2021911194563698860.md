## Rob's argument: the viral "Something Big Is Happening" AI article was emotional engineering, not analysis

Rob wrote this essay on Feb 12, 2026, in response to an AI startup CEO's post (Matt Shumer's "Something Big Is Happening") that reached 65+ million people in 48 hours. His core claim: "that feeling you had? That was the point. Not the information. Not the facts. The feeling." He is a software engineer who uses AI daily and a creator on X with tens of thousands of followers. He says he understands how content moves on the platform. He read the article twice and, on the second, slower read, "the feeling dissolved" and he realized he had been "worked."

## The persuasion techniques Rob identifies

- **The COVID anchor:** opening with "Think back to February 2020" links AI to a collective trauma, so the emotion transfers before any claim is evaluated. Rob calls this "a rhetorical technique as old as persuasion itself."
- **The insider confession:** "I keep giving them the polite version" frames every skeptical thought as proof you aren't "in the know." Rob calls this an "unfalsifiable setup."
- **False precision:** presenting February 5th as a single civilizational date, when Opus 4.6 was, in Rob's words, an incremental update.
- **Anthropomorphism:** describing software that "likes" or "decides" things. Rob says it runs predefined tests and iterates on programmatic feedback loops, yet the image of a digital colleague redesigning a button "is doing more persuasive work than any technical claim."
- **The singularity suggestion:** "The AI helped build itself." OpenAI's actual wording was that the Codex team used early versions to debug its own training. Rob compares it to saying VS Code built itself because Microsoft developers use VS Code to write VS Code.
- **A call to action that benefits the author:** after 4,000 words of escalating fear comes "sign up for the paid version" and "follow me on X." Rob: "It's a funnel."

## Why it spread: X's algorithm rewards emotion

- X hired Nikita Bier (known for viral apps tbh, acquired by Meta, and Gas, acquired by Discord) as head of product in mid-2025. By his own account, downloads have doubled since.
- Rob cites the public ranking weights: replies count 13.5x a like, retweets 20x. Content that holds you on-platform for more than two minutes gets boosted, and posts that send you to another website get suppressed.
- The article fit every lever: fear (your job), urgency (happening now), social obligation ("share it with someone you care about"), 5,000 words of dwell time, and a native text post rather than an external link.
- Rob's point on reach: this article reached 65 million people while "a hundred more nuanced, more accurate takes" sit at about 200 views. The cause is that it is more emotional, not more true. "The algorithm doesn't ask: is this accurate? It asks: does this make people feel something strongly enough to engage?"
- Evidence he uses: a 2025 *Science* study (Stanford, University of Washington, Northeastern) that reranked 1,256 participants' X feeds during the 2024 election. Pushing hostile, provocative content up increased polarization, anger and sadness; pushing it down reduced them.
- He adds that the variable-reward pattern of social feeds works like a slot machine.

## Where Rob says the article was wrong on AI

Rob grants that AI is improving fast and will change industries, then disputes specific claims:

- "AI can write tens of thousands of lines of code that work correctly" is like "a calculator can solve any math problem": true narrowly, misleading in practice. AI code is "a starting point, not a finished product." The gap between "runs without crashing" and "production-ready, maintainable, secure, and does what users need" is "enormous, and humans fill that gap."
- "The free version is over a year behind" is, he says, "simply false." ChatGPT gives free users its frontier model and Claude's free tier has capable models. The real gap is usage limits, not model quality.
- "AI is building itself" misrepresents humans using AI tools to speed up their own development work.
- The balanced version ("AI is a powerful and accelerating technology that will significantly change how we work") is reasonable but "not emotionally charged enough to go viral."

## Rob's playbook for resisting engineered content

- **Notice strong feelings:** catch yourself mid-scroll when your heart rate spikes or your thumb moves toward share. "That gap between feeling and action is where your autonomy lives."
- **24-hour sharing rule:** manufactured urgency is the tell. "Real information rarely expires in 24 hours."
- **Seek the boring version:** for any big claim, the most accurate version is usually the least exciting one.
- **Curate your feed deliberately:** every like, reply and extra second teaches the algorithm what to show next, so you are "training your own recommendation engine."
- **Check incentives:** ask who benefits from you feeling this way. He applies it to himself too: he builds tools for X and has his own incentives.
- **Protect kids from the pace and intensity of emotional activation,** not just from content.
- **Use AI but think for yourself:** the winners "think the most clearly about what to outsource and what to keep," not those who outsource the most.

## Rob's framing of the "two revolutions"

- Rob says there are two simultaneous revolutions: AI, which is real and worth watching, and the "algorithmic curation of human emotion at global scale," which has already changed how people feel about information.
- The incentive chain he describes: "emotion drives engagement, engagement drives retention, and retention drives revenue." He does not call it malicious design, and says the original author "probably believes what he wrote."
- He says X is one of the most powerful platforms for learning and building in public, and that the same mechanics that let a small creator reach millions also let engineered content reach millions.
- His closing line: "That feeling is the product. And you don't have to buy it."
