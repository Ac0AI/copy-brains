## Rob's view: the Fable 5 restriction is political, and it hurts defenders

Rob argues the US government's move to pull Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5 from general access is a political play, not a technical one, and that it is the wrong call. He made the video on 16 June 2026, about 64 hours after the last official word from Anthropic or the government. He notes Anthropic had promised more details "over the next 24 hours" in its 12 June 2026 release.

- The only reliable account of the backstage dispute came from David Sacks, the former AI czar who stepped down in March 2026.
- Per Sacks, the US asked Dario to fix a jailbreak, Dario refused, and "the ball is in Anthropic's court."
- Anthropic denies this. It points out that OpenAI's GPT-5.1.5 has the same jailbreak problems the US raised.
- Rob says he is frustrated by the lack of communication and by what he sees as a pretext.

## Why Rob reads it as political, not technical

Rob's evidence is the history between Anthropic and the US government.

- Earlier in 2026 the government wanted to use Anthropic's models for mass domestic surveillance and autonomous lethal weapons. Anthropic refused.
- Since then Anthropic has been in a legal dispute with the Department of Defense (the Department of War), effectively suing the Pentagon.
- Anthropic then told the world it had built a state-of-the-art, incredibly dangerous model. Rob says the Department of War took that as its opening to ban Fable.
- Anthropic's IPO is "right around the corner." Rob says it is fair to hypothesize "this is a political play and not just a technical play."
- Even taking the situation at face value (a jailbreakable, exploitable model), he says pulling the model from the public does not make sense.

## Rob's security background

Rob frames his credibility carefully.

- He finished a computer science bachelor's in 2022. His thesis was on hacking AI models, specifically extracting and distilling model parameters through side channel attacks.
- A side channel attack gets the result an attacker wants through non-traditional routes. Examples he gives: analyzing the sound hardware makes, measuring electromagnetic fields, or timing how long LLM operations take.
- The thesis took a year and was awarded best in his cohort.
- He says he is not a cybersecurity expert today and now makes videos and builds products. He spent a year living as an adversary against LLMs, "doing the very thing that the US government is afraid of."
- That is why he is certain that locking these models away from the general public is the worst thing the government can do.

## The defender asymmetry: "infinite chess pieces"

Rob's central argument is that restricting access removes tools from defenders, not attackers.

- A friend of his, Jameson, who extracted Fable 5's system prompt and applied it to Opus 4.8 to make an unofficial "Fable 5 light," gave him the analogy. It is like chess where your opponent has infinite pieces.
- The defender has a fixed number of pieces to cover the holes in the wall. The attacker has effectively unlimited vectors, as with side channels.
- Rob's point: the bad actors lose almost nothing. They lose API and Claude Max subscription access to Fable 5, but they already had it.
- What gets removed is the best tool from "the only people who are capable of defending against adversaries": the researchers who would find the holes to patch.
- His "queen and king" line: the government is taking the queen and king off its own side of the board while the attackers keep infinite kings and queens.

## The model is already out

Rob argues the restriction is too late to matter.

- Fable 5 was publicly available for about 72 hours before it was taken down. He accessed it from Vietnam as a UK citizen.
- He says that is enough time for anyone who wanted to distill it to do so. "You can't uninvent something."
- On reports that the White House suspects China has access to Mythos, he says "Of course it has." The effect of the ban is that everyone willing to defend loses access.
- Other models and capabilities are already out there, he adds, citing Mistral.
- He expects a small group of people in the US to be the only ones left with the best models.

## The enforcement problems

Rob points out the practical absurdities of the export control directive.

- The directive would bar foreign nationals from using Fable 5 and Mythos 5. He says that would include Andrej Karpathy, who just joined Anthropic, is Slovak, and holds an EB-1 extraordinary ability green card. Under that reading Karpathy could not use Mythos inside Anthropic.
- A US-citizens-only rule would be just as odd. A US citizen with multiple felonies could access the model while someone like Karpathy could not.
- He hopes the model is not released to US citizens first and everyone else later, but says "my guess is just as good as yours" on what happens next.

## The 1990s encryption precedent

Rob compares the situation to the US export controls on cryptography.

- Until 1996, strong cryptographic tools were classified as munitions in the US, effectively cyber weapons before the term existed.
- From 1996 to 1999 the Clinton administration gradually liberalized and stepped back from encryption export controls.
- He notes these are the same kind of export controls now applied to Fable 5 and Mythos. His test: imagine banning strong encryption in 2026, or classifying Telegram as a cyber weapon.
- Linked to his thesis: encryption can be mathematically unbreakable, yet side channels mean there is always a way in with enough time and power. He cites governments sitting on zero-day attacks, and Russia and China hacking the US.

## Rob's conclusion

- Restricting access to models like Fable 5 only "hand[s] the enemies a head start," whether the user is a founder who cannot build with it or a defender protecting national security.
- He calls for the model to be given back to the people who want to use it ("Free Fable") and says "hackers will always find a way."
- He expects news soon, since it was trading hours when he recorded, and says it could be pushed back about a week.
