## Rob's daily work routine: night-owl schedule and one deep work block

- Rob makes $22,000 a month and runs the whole business himself while traveling.
- He lives the "reverse typical guru advice lifestyle". He wakes around 11:00 a.m. to 12:00 p.m. because he goes to bed late. He is a night owl, not a morning person.
- He felt guilt about this for a while, but says "for me it works".
- Midday he has breakfast, gets some sun, and answers a few customer support emails.
- Around 2 to 3 p.m. he goes to the gym on training days (weights, then cardio), showers and makes coffee.
- He front-loads chores and hard errands so that nothing else is left to do when work starts.
- In the evening, once it is quiet and nobody is texting, he does a full 8-hour solid work block. He says that is how he gets into flow state.

## Rob's rule for choosing what to build: the boring stuff moves the business

- Rob keeps a running list, reset roughly monthly or every two months. It holds the top three or four features he wants to build, things to improve, and bugs. He notes items as they come up.
- When he sits down, he either continues yesterday's work or picks from the list by feel. He says he is privileged to be able to work on what he wants.
- He tries to focus on what moves the business forward the most. Building his startup taught him what that is, because he tends to want the fun, visual, flashy work.
- His conclusion: "99% of the time the stuff that moves the business forward the most is the boring stuff."
- When a list gets too long, he creates a new note and copies over only the important items.
- If a customer doesn't raise something again, or he forgets it, he treats it as not important enough to begin with.
- Triage signal: a critical bug or real issue generates several emails (for example five), so he knows it matters and gets it done. A one-off request for a small tweak or an extra setting that nobody else asks for gets dropped if he lacks time.
- He admits this may not be the most optimal way of working, but it has worked well so far.

## Building a business is like surfing: get in the water and read the waves

- Rob's analogy: building a business is like surfing.
- You spend a lot of time in the water watching waves, learning what a wave that will break properly looks like before it breaks. You only learn that from time in the water.
- A solopreneur or small-team founder's job is to pick the big waves, see them coming, get on them and ride them.
- He says this is why people who win once win again and again. The hardest step is getting in the water the first time: the board is new, you must learn the leash, everything is new and overstimulating.
- Once you have been in the water long enough to read waves, catching and riding them stops being a problem. Whether to get off one wave and pick another is up to you.
- His advice: "You just got to get in the water."

## Travel cadence: base for 2 to 3 months, not constant movement

- Rob started traveling as a backpacker trying to build startups. He says he had no idea what he was doing, made lots of mistakes and failures, and earned nothing for about a year.
- At first he moved every few days or every week, then every two weeks, then every month.
- He learned the hard way that moving constantly makes it very hard to focus on one thing. While building SuperX he moved non-stop and got "bits and bobs" done.
- He only got real work done after settling in Colombia for 3 months straight in July 2025.
- For roughly the last 6 months (as of this video), he sets up base somewhere for 2 to 3 months, ideally 3, then moves on.
- He finds this the right balance: routine for deep work, plus the travel.
- He believes going somewhere else to see how other people live, how money moves and what opportunities exist is "the best thing anyone can do", and that getting out and moving was the best thing he has done.

## Productivity follows happiness, not discipline

- Rob once thought the gap between what he had and what he wanted was discipline. He thought he needed to wake earlier, plan better and lock in harder, because self-help content says so.
- Having lived in more than 13 countries in a few years, he found the places where he shipped the most were always the places where he was happiest, with no exception. In some places he barely touched his laptop. In others he shipped more than he had in months.
- His conclusion: "The variable was never discipline. It was how I felt in that place."
- His motto: "Productivity follows happiness." He says the best things you create come from being in a good place.
- His advice is to find the environment that puts you in your best place and work out what you enjoy.
- He draws on things he loved as a kid, such as wanting to travel the world.
- Tactic: he keeps a Rubik's Cube and mixes it up between prompts to stay in a state of play. He says approaching work in a state of play makes both life and work better.

## Protecting sleep and winding down

- Rob needs 9 hours of sleep. He tried for a long time to get by on 8 and it didn't work for him.
- He spends about 9.5 hours in bed and sets his alarm for the time he goes to bed plus 9.5 hours.
- He protects sleep above everything else: without it he can't think creatively, can't stay motivated, and doesn't want to go to the gym. He says "everything is downstream of sleep".
- About an hour before bed he switches everything off. He reads or watches YouTube, may doomscroll a little, then turns his phone off for 30 minutes. He admits it isn't perfect but works for him.
