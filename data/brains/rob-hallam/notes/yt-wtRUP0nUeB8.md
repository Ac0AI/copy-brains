## Rob's core thesis: validation is money, and don't create waves, ride them

- Rob argues that with AI you can build almost anything, so the question is no longer "what can you build" but "what should you build".
- His definition: "Validation is money." Unless building is a hobby, asking a friend "do you like my product idea?" is useless. Ask "would you buy it?"
- He calls opinions "a courtesy" and not a basis for a business.
- His surf analogy: one person flails in the ocean trying to make their own waves; the other is calm and waits for the inevitable wave. The product is the surfboard, and the money is the water.
- Rob says he was the flailing person for years. Reinventing the wheel is "the worst thing you can do" if you want a business that pays your bills and gives you freedom.
- Unless you are a multi-billion-dollar fund that can create demand and move a market, creating a wave is not your job. Spend time in the market, watch the trends, then ride them.
- He ends with: stop asking people whether they would pay, and get them to pay. "Put the buy button."

## Rob's failure history before validating

- In August 2024 he posted on X about his failures. It took him 1 year and 11 months of attempts with no result. He had no agency and had made no money.
- Failed projects he lists: Coach Miro (connecting coaches with each other or with clients), Indie Hackers (a platform to help indie builders connect), and a luxury-apartments directory.
- His diagnosis: he was trying to build things that didn't exist yet (no directory for X), which means trying to make waves himself.
- The tweet that went viral was about failing repeatedly. He joked that after 26 more failures you would statistically be a winner, and says the real number turned out not to be 26.

## Playbook 1: Copy a proven idea and make one part 10x better

- Use a directory of proven ideas such as TrustMRR (made by a person named Mark on X). It lists business ideas that are proven because they earn revenue and shows how much each earns. Logging in gives more data.
- Pick one, run it through AI, and make it your own, but make one part ten times better.
- Example: Nevo David and Postiz, a social media post scheduler. Schedulers like Buffer and Hootsuite had existed for decades and make roughly $2-3 million a month. Nevo struggled at first, then changed course to improve one part of the scheduler 10x: exposing it to AI agents.
- Rob says Postiz went from about $17-20K a month roughly four months earlier to about $140K a month, within about three months, from that single change.
- He used the same method for his own product, Super X, an X (Twitter) post scheduler and growth tool.
  - Existing proven products: Tweet Hunter (his co-founder's old product, about $150K a month, and about $250K a month when his co-founder sold it), Hypefury and Typefully.
  - His "10x better" angle was improving the AI integrations 10x.
- The risk shifts. With an unproven idea the risk is whether anyone wants it. With a proven idea the risk moves to execution, which is "completely within your control."

## Playbook 2: Sell a service first, then build the product

- Example: his friend Roman and his startup Gojiberry, an AI lead-finding platform (also spelled "Goji Berry" in the source). Per Rob, it passed $3M a year.
- How it started: they ran an agency selling lead packages to other companies, validating demand before building any product. It was a normal services agency with about 10-20 clients paying monthly for lead data.
- Benefits Rob names:
  - Cash flow up front, so you don't burn savings.
  - A ready first customer base. You show the product to existing clients and ask what they think.
- Rob says the product then reached $1M a year in about eight months, and about $3M a year after roughly a year and a half. (The source says "within a year, within eight months" for the first milestone.)
- Pricing change: they cut what they charged clients from about $3,000 a month under the agency to $800 a month for the product, which let them serve far more customers.
- He describes the agency as riding a wave with clients while Roman and his co-founders could not yet ride it themselves, so they built the product to do it.

## Playbook 3: Landing page with a buy button (the Levels.io approach)

- Rob credits Pieter Levels (Levels.io), a prominent indie hacker on X. The method: put up a landing page for an idea and add a buy button. Rob frames it as "show me the money."
- He says Levels made about $100K a month before AI and about $44K a month from remote-work-related projects (the source is unclear on the exact attribution), and more at his peak.
- Rob saved this for last because it combines with the other two: you can add a landing page and a "will you pay for this?" button to a copied idea or a service offer.
- Strategies one and two give you up-front cash flow, or a proven idea, so you don't worry about running out of money.

## Rob's own run, and how he knew it would work

- About a year before this talk (the video is dated 2026-07-16), he left his home and his job and lived off savings. He tweeted that he was becoming a founder. He calls it "either the smartest or the dumbest decision" of his life.
- He was nervous about hitting his goal of $10K a month, but he knew the core idea was proven because he applied Playbook 1.
- Timeline he gives: roughly six months of hard work with no income, from January (the first post) to July, when he started publicly posting about it. The slow part was building infrastructure, which is complex for something like Super X. After about eight months he reached $10K a month with recurring revenue.
- He says demand grew over that time, because X is gaining users, so the market was expanding.

## Q&A: How do you measure "10x better"?

- Rob says you can't measure it precisely, unlike AI benchmarks (88.8%, 80%, 89%).
- For Super X he had been using Tweet Hunter and Hypefury and was frustrated. Their AI-generated tweets were "so bad".
- His test: build something he himself wants to use daily, and check whether it is actually better than the product he is comparing against.
- He cautions that "I built it to scratch my own itch" alone is not a validated product idea. Do both: scratch your own itch and others' at the same time.

## Q&A: Paid vs organic traffic, and competing as a copycat

- Super X growth was 99% organic, mainly his own tweets, because his ICP (X users who want an X scheduler) lives on X.
- He did run paid ads, and they currently lose money on ad spend. Reason: it gives a steady stream of users, so when he changes the product he can watch how trial conversion and churn shift. He pays for a consistent feedback flow, not for growth.
- To compete with incumbents when you copy, he uses a "gravity" analogy: the more mass a brand has, the more pull it creates (the Sun vs the Earth). Create mass by constantly showing up, making noise, shipping updates, announcing news, and sharing customer success stories.
- Same logic for SEO: rank by publishing more high-quality content and earning more backlinks. He says the key is volume of quality content, not time in market.
- His own cadence: posted on X daily, ran a daily video update series for 133 days, and now does it weekly.
- Why this beats incumbents: once big players get comfortable they stop posting and updating, which leaves room for small founders who can move fast and produce a lot of content.
