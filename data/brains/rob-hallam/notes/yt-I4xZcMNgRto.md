## Path to a first SaaS: wins, losses, and volume

- Rob graduated in computer science in the UK and worked as a low-level software engineer at a video conferencing company, fixing bugs in protocols.
- He says he never enjoyed it and felt like an imposter because he is creative and loves the arts rather than deep programming.
- He was laid off and treats it as a positive: paid to leave with about two months, plus a runway to experiment.
- His mindset line: "Optimists will always win."
- A friend in a crypto startup told him what to learn: Next.js, Node.js, JavaScript, Tailwind.
- He learned them from a YouTube Next.js course (about 20 videos) in roughly a month, then worked nine months at that startup until it went bust at the end of 2023.
- Building a real app taught him far more than watching tutorials.

## Early failed products and what each taught him

- Rob was inspired by Marc Lou's ShipFast (Next.js boilerplate) and flew to Egypt in September 2023 to build with his friend Mustafa.
- Failure 1: a software agency with friends, "Techia".
- Failure 2: Coach Miro, connecting coaches with coaches.
- Failure 3: Indidex (indidex.app), a "Pokedex for indie hackers", a directory of indie hackers pulled from X.
  - Validation: he tweeted the idea, got about 2,000 views when his posts normally got 20-30, then shipped an MVP in a few days.
  - Launched on Product Hunt in March 2024 and got #4, 100,000 impressions on launch day, 250 users, and no monetization.
- Failure 4: Pentest List, a directory of ethical hacking tools, built in two weeks for a family friend who is a pentester.
  - The plan was to monetize with ads, which he calls naive because ads need a lot of traffic.
  - It gets about 1,000-1,500 visits a month (about 50 a day), all from SEO, and made no money. It taught him SEO as a distribution channel.
- Takeaways he draws:
  - Building what he found cool but nobody would pay for kept repeating.
  - Review each failure, ask what went well and wrong, and fix it next time ("The lesson will keep repeating itself until you've learned from it").
  - His fixes in order: distribution (SEO, X), then monetization (a buy button, then an agency for runway).

## Validation: interest versus money

- Rob separates interest validation (people click and look) from monetary validation (people pay).
- Indidex had interest but no money. He had not monetized anything early, which he attributes to imposter syndrome or fear of using Stripe.
- "People saying that they would pay money doesn't mean they're going to pay money." The best test is putting a buy link on the product.
- Go to where your customers are (indie hackers were on X), post the idea or a teaser, and measure the interest.
- Visual demos spread well, as with the "I built cursor for X" style posts.

## The Liftoff MVP agency funded by building in public

- Rob posted a long tweet with a selfie (about August 2024) saying he had made "$0" in 11 months of savings, listing his four failures and what each taught him.
- Peter Levels replied that 26 more failures and he would statistically be a winner, which motivated him. Ian Nuttle suggested a service charging $2,000-$5,000 to ship an MVP, with an optional monthly retainer.
- Within 24 hours Rob launched an MVP-building service called Liftoff. Within about a week of the post he signed his first client at $3,000.
- Over about four months, deals grew: $3,000, then $5,000 (a couple), then $6,000, and finally two clients on back-to-back days worth $18,000 combined.
- Every client came inbound from X. They arrived already sold because they had seen his wins, losses, and proof.
- His method for those months: post everything (each client signed, each hire, each problem). Losses and struggles resonate with more people than wins, which are merely inspirational.
- Post yourself: a selfie or face content stands out against AI-generated text.
- He cites the "digital gravity" analogy (from Cody Schneider's circle): each piece of content adds mass to your personal brand, so customers orbit several times before they "land" and buy.
- He wound the agency down in December 2024 because of client complaints and contractor issues, and because it was unfulfilling and stressful ("a hedonistic treadmill"). Agency work trades time for money.

## From using AI for his own tweets to SuperX

- A fellow MVP agency owner told Rob he keeps one long ChatGPT conversation that learns his voice and uses it to write tweets. Rob tried it, tweaked the output, and got several tweets to go viral.
- Stance on AI slop: AI turns good ideas or good writing into great writing. Slop comes from bad ideas and low effort ("Garbage in, garbage out").
- He decided to productize it as an app, "Brancast" (broadcast your brand), and bought the domain. The first version was messy and niche to his own use.
- He asked Tibo (a SuperX co-founder) for private beta access to SuperX. The product was janky but had a user base and a Chrome extension with validation and data.
- In January 2025 Tibo posted that SuperX's technical co-founder collaboration was ending and he was seeking a new one. Rob sent a Loom of what he had built, saying he would otherwise compete. He built the SuperX web app in about a week, and they started working together.
- SuperX began as a Chrome extension for X analytics. The web app helps users write, plan, and schedule posts and generate content ideas.
- Rob's vision: speak an idea and have the tweet, images, and scheduling handled, with a content strategist chat that reads your analytics and explains what to change.
- He also shipped an "AI Shield" feature: the extension scans each tweet in the feed and scores 0-10 for how likely it is AI-written (em dashes, overly optimistic tone). Users can hide or label content over a threshold.

## SuperX launch and numbers

- Over about six months, SuperX grew to $1,600 MRR in private beta with roughly 30 active web app users. Private beta users also paid, covering AI and server costs.
- He got those users by screen-recording or screenshotting features and posting "reply SuperX for early access", then DMing a link to those who replied.
- Soft launch of the web app was on Wednesday the 9th (July 2025), with harder launches planned later (more videos, Product Hunt) after a couple of weeks of feedback.
- Launch method: one tweet with a short video (an old extension demo clip stitched with a screen recording, edited in DaVinci with music), then follow-up progress updates, wins, losses, and selfies to keep momentum.
- The launch tweet got about 30,000 views and 100 likes ("semi-viral"). In 24-36 hours it brought about 50 free-trial signups at $29/month on a 7-day trial. He expected MRR to rise from about $1.6K to roughly $2.5K, assuming around a 50% trial conversion.

## Principles for persistence and finding what works

- Core idea: "The more you do, the more you find out." Do more, ship more, and follow the leads that appear, like buying more lottery tickets.
- Changing his physical environment helped him break out of a stuck period (he went from Koh Samui to the UK for a month).
- Quote he credits to Alex Hormozi (as "Homozy"): figure out what you want, clear out all your distractions, and do so much volume that it is statistically improbable you will fail.
- Cut distractions: limit TikTok and reels, avoid constantly watching how others are doing, since you don't see their full picture.
- Keep a personal "why" strong enough to sustain volume. His is supporting his family, who backed him throughout, and avoiding health and money worries.
- Surround yourself with people who share your values, and drift away from those who criticize the path.
