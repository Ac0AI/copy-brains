## Open-weights models put a floor under frontier AI that governments cannot remove

- On 12 June (about 10 days before this video), the US government put an export control on Fable 5 and Mythos 5. Rob says this "effectively overnight switched off access to everyone in the world" to what was then the frontier AI model. He says he was affected too: "the best tool in the world that they were using... gone."
- His point is that this has nothing to do with whether you can afford a server. The most powerful models "don't belong to you. They belong to someone else. They live in someone else's building."
- You are "renting access to intelligence", like paying Netflix for access to someone else's movies.
- Four days before the video, ZAI (Beijing) released GLM 5.2 as an open-weights model. Rob's reading: it "can't be applied to an export control" and "can't be switched off by a single letter from the Trump administration."
- He repeats the line going around X: "you can't export control your way out of an open source model."
- Rob frames this as a floor: "A version of the AI model frontier that no company, that no government can take away." He says this holds even if you never touch these models.
- He recalls that the US put export controls on cryptography in 1999 and rescinded them shortly after. He expects the same here but notes it hasn't happened yet.

## What GLM 5.2 actually delivers: benchmarks, cost per task and the reasoning-token catch

- On a well-respected coding benchmark (score on the Y axis, cost per task on the X axis), GLM 5.2 sits "up with the big dogs" alongside Opus 4.8 and GPT 5.5, the frontier models in production at the time.
- Other models compared:
  - Kimi K 2.7 (also released June 2026) is more expensive per task and scores about 13% lower than GLM 5.2.
  - Gemini 3.5 Flash, Sonnet 4.6 High and Gemini 3.1 Pro High all sit lower.
- GLM 5.2 prices per million tokens are far below Opus 4.8:
  - Opus 4.8: $5 input, $25 output.
  - GLM 5.2: $1.40 input, $4.40 output.
- Even so, it plots as more expensive per task than Opus 4.8 high. Rob's explanation: GLM 5.2 "uses an insane amount of tokens" in its thinking to reach a comparable score.
- Trade-off he states: with enough time and tokens it scores very well, but thinking costs both time and money. It can lose to Opus 4.8 and to GPT 5.5 on medium on cost-efficiency.
- A known limitation is that it can't see. You can't drag images into it the way you can with Codex or Opus 4.8 in Claude Code.
- Rob notes Browserbase-style integrations already work around this. He cites a viral tweet and article on adding image handling for under 75 cents per task. He says "that's not even a constraint anymore."
- He flags this isn't a sponsorship, and adds that the praise isn't only from him. The CEO of Herself was also shocked at how good it is.

## Why you should care even if you never run an open model

- The obvious objection: self-hosting costs tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars in hardware. Rob compares this to buying Netflix for $325 billion instead of paying $20 a month. For about 99% of people he calls it "absurd" and unrealistic.
- Most people therefore conclude it is "a rich person's toy" for labs and big companies. Rob says "that's where most people's conclusion stops. And I think that's where people are wrong."
- His argument is that open models don't need to be used by you to matter to you.
- Hosting options exist beyond owning hardware:
  - run it locally on your own hardware
  - set it up with Fireworks and run it in Codex
  - set it up with AI Gateway and use it inside Claude Code
- His summary: "you just need to clock that the door is now open."

## Open-source pricing is what keeps frontier APIs cheap

- Current subscriptions are heavily subsidized. Rob's example: $200 a month on Claude Code buys roughly $8,000 of token compute.
- The "permanent underclass" meme says that if you don't get set up now, models will become too expensive in the next few years and you'll be locked out. Rob argues GLM 5.2 changes that.
- Open models cost roughly a fifth to a sixth of frontier prices in many cases. He says this pricing "is what is going to keep these APIs down for the foreseeable."
- He caveats that frontier APIs aren't cheap right now. The effect is competitive pressure, not low prices today.
- As open models keep improving ("which they will"), that pressure continues. His logic: "Every market is a competitive market. It's capitalistic."

## Models commoditize labor, not building

- Rob's claim: as frontier-level intelligence gets cheaper and more accessible, "the value no longer lies in who has access to these models", it lies "with what you actually go and do and build."
- He rejects the idea that nobody will build things because AI can build everything from a spoken prompt:
  - People building with these models know there are still caveats.
  - Even with artificial superintelligence, it "is not going to understand what you want. It's not going to understand taste without humans in the loop."
  - He ties this to Andrej Karpathy's partial-autonomy idea and says he wants to make a separate video on it.
- He cites Cursor's success as the example: SpaceX acquired Cursor for $60 billion in stock.
- The reframe he recommends: stop thinking "models commoditize building." Think "models commoditize labor." That should let a builder "think bigger, to create more things, and to create bigger and better things."

## The newsletter analogy: own your distribution, own your model access

- Rob calls this the "newsletter moment" of models, and prefers it to the common "ChatGPT moment of open-source models."
- His reasoning: platforms like Twitter, Instagram and YouTube own your audience. If they ban you, "it's gone and you can't do anything about that." The same applies to Anthropic or OpenAI controlling access to a model.
- By contrast, if you collect your audience's emails in a newsletter, "there's nothing anybody can do about your control of your audience."
- The parallel: if multiple companies can own and host open-weights models, "there's nothing anyone can do about that."
- His closing frame: it's a race and "not just about America."
