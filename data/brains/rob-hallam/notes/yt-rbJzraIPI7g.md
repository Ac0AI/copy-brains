## The $1 challenge: what Rob set up and how he framed it

- Rob picked three people who had never made a dollar online, gave each a $100 budget and 6 weeks, and set one goal: build something from scratch with AI and make a first dollar.
- He ran weekly check-in calls and asked them to talk to their end users very early (week one or two). One participant credited that push as her biggest takeaway.
- He framed the finale as proof that a first dollar is "not the end. It is the beginning." Starting, shipping and selling is what most people never do.
- Rob's own background: he once had a 9-5 he "absolutely despised" and felt stuck in. He argues getting what you want comes from "taking one step at a time, putting one foot in front of the other."

## Building around a 9-5, burnout, and pacing

- Kate built a mood-tracking app (Mood Mark) before and after her 9-5. She made her first dollar in week two but burned out by week six.
- She took the 9-5 evenings easier to rest, and came back in a better place. Rob called this the most realistic story of building online for the first time, especially around a job: "She's human just like you and I are."
- Her app logs mood plus habits. After 14 days of logging it surfaces "early patterns" from a statistical calculation, with habit streaks and a calendar view.
- Her takeaway: the six weeks strengthened her wish to do entrepreneurship outside the 9-5, and she plans to keep going "with or without" the show.
- Rob's point: progress is incremental, so rest and keep going rather than quit.

## Pivoting when nobody buys: Davis's story

- Davis had built products before but never made a dollar. His first app (Daily Sensei) got no buyers, so he pivoted to a productized, automated service: hosting an OpenClaw server for people who want one without setting it up.
- Pricing used Rob's launch method: $19.99/month for the first 10 customers, with the full price set higher at $100/month. Only one plan.
- He had an insight to pre-build servers in advance rather than build one each time someone pays.
- He stopped posting online and DMed people in communities and people asking for OpenClaw help. More than 50 conversations produced 1 customer ($19.99/month). He called it "a painful sale" of manual, brutal outreach.
- The one-on-one conversations showed why people did not buy. The biggest reason was trust (what happens to my server if you shut down?). Others: not ready to pay $20/month, and people not replying.
- Posting on X got attention but no payment; direct outreach got a sale.
- He launched two products in six weeks, and the original app was still stuck in App Store review.
- Rob's takeaway: the first idea usually does not work. People say they want something but will not put money behind it. Listen to the market, talk to users, set your ego aside, pivot, and stick with it.

## Cold outreach to local businesses: Meraj's sales process

- Meraj, 18, built a tool that turns a website URL into a finished social carousel (Super X is a similar product), fully on autopilot. The only input is the URL.
- He sourced leads from Google Maps. He filtered out businesses unlikely to pay and kept ones that already had an Instagram page or website, since those owners care about marketing. He also chose businesses whose offerings are pricey enough to justify marketing spend, even at about $10 in the Indian context.
- He did not ask for the sale in the first message, only for a yes. He told owners he had already made a sample post and full plan for them and asked if they wanted to see it. Saying yes costs them nothing; the worst case is they dislike it and block him.
- Most did not reply. One engaged, and he got 1,500 rupees (about $15) as his first sale. Within weeks he made $64.90 total, including a $50 sale from one person and then a second $50 sale.
- Rob called this "a master class": find leads, open with value, then show a result built from their own business. Rob noted the carousels looked so good he first assumed they were made by hand.
- His early setback: a gym owner rejected him, he improved the product and came back. Rob said Meraj showed up with force after a sick period and heavy online criticism.

## Persistence and confidence

- Meraj lost accountability after the weekly calls stopped, doubted himself, and feared not winning his bet against himself. His advice: "it's okay to be also a bit delusional as long as you're not like too delusional," and "stay delusional." If he had quit three weeks earlier because of the gym owner, he would not have this story.
- Rob's closing lesson: if you keep going you will achieve what you want. All three were rejected several times and still started, shipped and sold.
- Rob's observation on audience: some viewers follow the series closely, and he is reminded of that when strangers approach his participants at events.
