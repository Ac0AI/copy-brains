## Bootstrapping three SaaS companies to $1M ARR

Adam co-founded Robly (email marketing) and Retention.com (originally Get Emails), then started RB2B. He got to $1M ARR faster each time: about 17 months, then 27 weeks, then 16 weeks.

- **Robly:** launched Jan 1, 2014. It stalled at a mid single-digit ARR and then shrank. He sold it to private equity in 2021 for an eight-figure exit, and the founders owned 100% (no outside funding).
- **Retention.com:** founded Nov 2019. It is at $21.8M ARR and targets e-commerce brands.
- **RB2B:** started March 2024, aimed at SaaS companies with sales teams. It went from $1M to $2M ARR in six weeks.
- **Starting capital:** he says you need some money to start, even if tiny (about $50-100K). It can come from a day job, an agency, info products, or friends and family. He had saved from 10 years as a trader at Lehman Brothers and lived frugally.

## Robly: a channel with no competition makes customers cheap

- **Core belief:** "What makes acquiring customers expensive is competition." If you have a channel nobody else works and a good offer, acquisition is cheap.
- **The list:**
  - Adam's team crawled websites for Constant Contact signup widgets. That was a list of paying customers.
  - About 250,000 domains were enriched into phone numbers by a team in Pakistan.
  - A call center then pitched "50% more opens for half the price", and about 5,000 customers switched.
  - Nobody else was calling this list, so human sales worked on $15-50 a month subscriptions.
- **Onboarding:** the magic moment was a customer hitting send on their first campaign. Customers who did stuck 100% of the time, but busy small-business owners wouldn't get there alone. The team first hand-built templates for each customer, then ran one-to-many onboarding calls. Those had a better show rate, and a $25 incentive got people to show up.
- **Why it stalled:** once the list was burned through, the product wasn't competitive. Mailchimp had a better product and a free tier, and Robly wasn't cheaper.
- **Layoffs:** a second Constant Contact-style list failed when nobody picked up. He had scaled sales around it and had to cut from 38 to 6 people.
- **Hindsight:** "I wouldn't start it." Email marketing is a brutal space, and he copied UI as a first-time founder.
- **Why it still mattered:** its cash flow funded the experiments that led to everything after.

## Retention.com: fast payback ads, then cold email to a better customer

- **Origin:**
  - He spent about a year and a half learning how to identify anonymous website visitors by email.
  - It started as a feature inside Robly. Users kept downloading the file and putting it in the email app.
  - He spun it out as Get Emails.
- **Before it:**
  - A Constant Contact "authorized local expert" reseller model failed. He wasted money and got sued over a non-compete.
  - A build aimed just above Mailchimp's buyer failed because he built before selling. About ten others were already doing it.
  - He then spent two years and hundreds of thousands of dollars on things that didn't work before landing on Get Emails.
- **Selling first:** he asked people at a conference, "What if I could get you the email address of people on your website who don't fill out a form?" Every one of them wanted it.
- **Facebook ads:**
  - He and his wife made weekly one-minute videos that were inflammatory about privacy and also answered sales questions. The goal was to have people anticipate the next ad.
  - Targeting was by interest (Shopify, entrepreneur, Ezra Firestone, Klaviyo, Mailchimp).
  - Ads paid back fast. A self-serve $5,000 a month plan sold about once every two weeks and covered the prior ads.
  - He kept pouring money back in. After eight weeks with no big customer, he had spent about $100K.
  - The customers were poor: high-churn wannabes, around 15% monthly churn.
- **The pivot:** he stopped ads and built features for high-value users. His co-founder Diana prospected Shopify and Klaviyo lists by cold email. MRR went from about $200K to $600K over the next year.
- **Cold email:** it worked in 2020-2021 with five people in the Philippines, half the day on list building and half sending manually from Gmail inboxes. He says it would not work the same way today.
- **Churn:** he calls it a high-churn category with a high-churn buyer. He says you are always trying to outpace churn. The next step is larger deals with omnichannel retailers, because the Shopify audience has topped out.
- **Market shift:** he says the addressable market went from about 50,000 stores to about 1,400, with more competitors. A third layoff was planned. In his words, no founder has regretted making that move.
- **Mindset:** after hitting $3M ARR twice and stalling, he wondered if he was "just a 3 million ARR guy."

## Building a LinkedIn audience from zero

- **Starting point:** zero followers in Sept 2022; over 92,000 at this interview.
- **Year one:**
  - He posted daily video and general business thoughts to sell Retention.com to Shopify owners. It didn't work.
  - He kept going because he believed deeply in social media's power. He cites the Kardashians as an example.
- **Work in public:**
  - His angle was sharing financials and failures.
  - When his 2023 sales scale-up failed and he cut the team by about 75%, that post exploded.
- **The pivot:** around Labor Day 2023, his LinkedIn consultant Alec told him B2B content would "absolutely crush." He wrote hot takes aimed at SaaS revenue leaders, including that BDR teams don't work anymore.
  - A typical post got 30-50 likes. The first of these got 3,300 likes in 24 hours.
  - He then dropped three more. They got about 2M impressions and his follower slope changed for good.
- **What those posts had:**
  - He talked about his own failures to peers.
  - He said what leaders only say to their spouse.
  - It was "my first feel of what a hot take was."
- **His rules:**
  - Be as transparent and human as possible.
  - Talk about how go-to-market is changing and how to build SaaS in 2024.
  - Be "a dealer of hope" and show you can bootstrap to $10M ARR.
  - Don't be boring or neutral.
- **Lesson:** he says he is in the "edutainment business" with a SaaS at the end of the funnel. If you do organic content well and large competitors can't, you have about 100x more efficient awareness generation.
- **Thought leadership ads:** boost a post that already crushed organically. Clicks are cheap because LinkedIn already rewarded the post.
- **Haters:** LinkedIn is politer than other platforms. The hardest hate came from outbound agencies when he said "outbound's dead." The Facebook-ad era, where he insulted commenters from the brand account, was bad for his mental state.
- **Book:** he recommends Russell Brunson's *Expert Secrets* for why organic social works.

## RB2B: audience first, then a free product

- **What it does:** a script on your site matches anonymous visitors to LinkedIn profiles and pushes them into Slack with a business email, free.
- **Pricing:**
  - Up to 200 leads is free, and 85% of signups stay on it.
  - Paid is $99 a month, or about $750 a month for a mid-market SaaS.
  - The idea was "take pricing off the table." It is zero-touch PLG.
- **How he got there:**
  - He deliberately chose the opposite of cold email, which he sees getting worse.
  - It is founder-led content plus a free, punchy value proposition. He did about 10 podcast spots a week.
  - He thinks enterprise software growth may now depend on this kind of distribution.
- **Launch prep:**
  - He and COO Santosh held about 300 discovery calls between October and March 1.
  - Thousands of people were on a waiting list.
  - Beta testers gave feedback on a rough version.
  - The audience was warmed up for about six months beforehand.
- **Order of operations:** audience first, product second. He names Greg Isenberg as an example of building this way. He compares it to DTC brands that start with influencers and then distribute product to the audience.
- **Free users:** he says free users may be more important than paid ones. He criticizes a competitor for dismissing them.

## Principles and quotes

- **Sell before you build:** he now advocates validating with buyers first, because he failed by building without it.
- **Channel fit:** "product and distribution and what is available at the time determines what grows."
- **Product-market fit:** businesses can go in and out of it. A company's size can be set for a market that no longer exists.
- **Layoffs:** he used the EOS framework to focus people on what moves the needle. He found he could do the same with half the team.
- **Best advice received:** "Do not take institutional capital."
- **Trait of successful founders:** focus.
- **Productivity:** a checklist.
