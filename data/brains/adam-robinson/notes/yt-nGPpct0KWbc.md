## How Adam started on LinkedIn and why it became his only acquisition channel

- In January 2022 he began posting on LinkedIn. His first company, Retention.com, was at $13M ARR with six people, and big Shopify stores looked like a dream customer.
- He picked LinkedIn over Twitter because of its creator-to-user dynamic. Many users are on the feed all day for work, and only about 2% post actively.
- The plan was to build 10-20k followers on LinkedIn, then figure out Twitter.
- For about the first year it didn't produce demos. It did make him known in the e-commerce ecosystem, so people approached him at trade shows. It also helped partnerships, credibility and recruiting.
- He says that year he did not know why he was writing what he wrote. He quotes Russell Brunson: posting is "not about engagement and deals and dollars. It's about being one day closer to finding your voice. And then once you have found your voice, you got the keys to the kingdom."
- Today LinkedIn is the only thing he does for RB2B customer acquisition. He says RB2B hit $5M ARR in 13 months with a team of four, "100% a result of this".
- Dave Gerhardt's *Founder Brand* was one seed. Its argument is that most businesses started for a reason, usually a good story, so tell it where your audience lives.

## Content-market fit: why founder content works only when it lines up

- He says "content market fit" is as real and as hard as product-market fit. You know you lack it when you post into nothingness.
- Three things have to line up: the author, the platform, and an audience that also buys the product. His fit works because LinkedIn's audience are B2B buyers. He says a brilliant TikTok creator wouldn't get the same result.
- He claims personal brand "works a lot faster with a lot less capital" than a company brand. He bought the retention.com domain for $800,000 and says it earns trust, but the personal brand did the awareness work.
- Reasoning: people go on social media to connect with people, so the best brand for a company is a public figure built around a person.
- He expects this to work better each year as more B2B time moves onto LinkedIn.

## Content pillars: build a public figure by repeating one story

- Posting consistently on LinkedIn means creating a public figure, as a PR agency would have 50 years ago, by telling the same story many different ways.
- A content pillar is just saying the same thing over and over in different ways. Brand marketing is people seeing the same thing repeatedly.
- His pillars: bootstrapped SaaS founder, future of go-to-market, bootstrapping vs VC, and working in public. Ask anyone who he is and they'd say he bootstrapped to $25M ARR, works in public, and talks go-to-market. Hardly anyone knows he has two kids, because he chose not to share it.
- Pick two or three pillars that are authentic, resonate with the market, and are not mainstream.
- Authenticity matters because breakout posts are polarizing. Maybe 10-20% will hate you for what the other 80% love, and you must be able to defend it.
- Status-game noise is what everyone does, so being genuinely human separates you.
- He learned repetition from watching Chris Walker repeat his pillars on a 16-hour drive. The skill is saying it so people don't notice you're repeating.

## Posting tactics: storytelling, listicles, hooks and credibility

- Reporting what you've done ("do cool stuff and talk about it") is a good start, especially if you're a future version of your audience.
- Deeper storytelling beats reporting. His monthly recap does decently, but a story gets about 100x the engagement. The example: a close friend emailed him that he wasn't ambitious enough after he refused to join a VC-backed startup, and that friend's company wound down the same month his made $1M profit.
- Opinion-only posts underperform posts with a story behind the point.
- Listicles are an easy template: five or six supporting reasons plus a takeaway. They keep people reading.
- Pro tip: find posts of his (or others') with over 1,000 likes and rewrite them with your own facts.
- The hook matters most. Journalists once spent 50% of their time on the headline. Of a mediocre post with a great hook and a great post with a weak hook, the first wins. With his consultant Alec, they won't write a post until the hook exists.
- Hook rules:
  - Use as many numbers as possible: "I've recorded 270 episodes, 10,000 downloads, 17 guests in two years, here are seven things I learned" beats "I recorded a season and here's what I learned".
  - Money, profit and pipeline numbers draw people.
  - Maximize credibility in every hook. Example: "I've bootstrapped 3 million ARR three times. I've gone twice as fast with half the resources each time."
  - If you lack big wins, broker information: "I spoke to 10 early-stage founders about cold outbound. Here's what's not working and the one thing that is."
- Treat each post as if no one has heard of you. A Peter Confalone analysis found only about 15% audience overlap between his posts, and LinkedIn isn't sequential, so a reader may see a post from six months ago.
- Brainstorm hooks with people (him, Alec, Pete) rather than AI.

## Audience vs. crowd, the gimmicks, and AI

- His top posts were screenshots of others' tweets with "Founders, do you agree?", from a ghostwriter early on (late 2022 to early 2023), plus engagement circles he no longer uses.
- He now optimizes for trust within his ICP, not follower count. To maximize followers he'd post about work-life balance, HR and posting on LinkedIn.
- The current gimmick is "comment YES for the slides", which draws second- and third-degree commenters. He has heard indirectly that LinkedIn favors those over first-degree comments.
- He doesn't use ChatGPT to write posts. He cites Rick Rubin: what's interesting about art is the artist's perspective, and AI has none. He thinks AI is better at amplifying a person's draft than writing it.
- He says AI SDRs haven't replaced one SDR, though spam filters have made SDRs' work harder.
- He posts about 36 times a month. Replying to comments was an unscalable relationship tactic he still likes but cut back after having a kid in September, partly because of AI comment generators.
- Starter advice: leave meaningful comments on posts by 10 influential people in your ecosystem over several months.

## Video, voice, and the weekly live show

- LinkedIn is a text platform, so the post's strength determines the video's strength. For a real banger of a text post, they often skip the video.
- Voice is a deeper connection than text, and video deeper again. People came up to him at trade shows without introducing themselves, as if they'd talked the day before.
- He ignores LinkedIn's TikTok-style video feed because it doesn't appear targeted to his ICP.
- Video came unnaturally to him and took a long time. You don't need to be attractive, but you must get comfortable on camera.
- He runs a weekly live webinar rather than a recorded podcast, aiming to be different. He says people want "edutainment", equal parts education and entertainment.
- Segments include the "pitch slap" and "unfuck my startup", where a guest like Sam Parr critiques a viewer's landing page live. He says live media has spontaneous audience interaction that recorded podcasts lack.
