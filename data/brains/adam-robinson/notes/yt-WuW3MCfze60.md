## Why Adam argues bootstrapping beats the VC treadmill

- Adam says he knows many founders who raised VC, few who exited, fewer who took large secondary rounds, and a handful who took small ones. Those small-secondary founders "have great businesses" but have pulled out only a few million dollars.
- His view: "if you have a great business, you should make millions of dollars every year. In every other market in the world, you do."
- He describes a middle path between the tiny lifestyle business (uninspiring) and the IPO dream (statistically hard outside killer enterprise SaaS): a lean company that stalls at a ceiling but throws off cash.
- The idea came in 2017, when his company was stuck at $3M. He heard about a sole founder in the email newsletter space who grew to $30M ARR, stalled, and was "banking $20 million every year." To Adam that seemed easier than building something that grows indefinitely and IPOs.
- He says none of his businesses have had net revenue expansion. Natural churn stalls them at a certain size, so he sizes the goal around that ceiling.
- Cash flow means no pressure to sell. He pays off his mortgage and sets his family up financially without selling anything, and "it's better if I don't."
- Even on the venture track, he says, the path "is probably taking you a decade anyway and it's probably not going to work out for you."
- His pitch to founders at $3M ARR facing an $8M offer: taking it likely ends any future as a profitable dividend-paying company. If you hold out a couple of years, the outcome he is living may be available instead.
- When people say he isn't a "real" bootstrapper, he answers: "I have 30 million AR company that has 40 people. I'm a bootstrapper."

## The word-of-mouth rule and how Adam gets air cover on top of it

- Core belief: "without word of mouth, nothing else that you do will work. With word of mouth, there's a million things you can do to speed it up."
- Both Retention.com (formerly Get Emails) and RB2B launched with a novel product. In the Retention.com market, nobody had heard you could get an email from a website visitor who didn't fill out a form.
- RB2B's pricing was novel too: the product was free at launch.
- He treats marketing as "air cover" that accelerates word of mouth:
  - For Retention.com it was funny ~60-second Facebook ads with Adam and his wife. They answered the sales objection, and viewers looked forward to the next one. "If they were looking forward to your ads, you won the game."
  - For RB2B it is his LinkedIn founder brand.
- He says he will try anything, and "the crazier it is the better." He stops when something stops working, and says he doesn't know if the Facebook approach would work again.

## The Facebook ad experiment for Retention.com

- Facebook B2B normally brings bad customers: would-be entrepreneurs with about 20% monthly churn.
- Adam's product-led setup let customers click their way to a $5,000/month plan. Roughly once every week or two, a "whale" bought a $5,000 plan without talking to sales, and that paid for the prior weeks of ads.
- It worked for about 6 months. As he scaled spend it got less effective.
- Eight weeks passed, about $40K of spend, and no big customers, only the high-churn small ones. He stopped.
- Even so, the ads made his cold email work better. Signups would say "I saw the ads" whether or not they clicked, and without them growth would have been much slower.

## Why founder-led LinkedIn is brand marketing that works at zero revenue

- Adam says the only brand marketing that works for a zero-revenue startup is the thought-leadership type he does on LinkedIn.
- Conventional brand marketing, like trade show spend, is expensive and reinforces a brand already in people's minds. He says a $1M trade show spend a year ago would not have left anyone remembering RB2B.
- The same applies to DTC: category thought leadership from an influencer-style individual works there too.
- Trust built this way is much deeper than paid ads: "if you've read like 50 of my posts and you're just like man this guy's got it figured out."
- It can't be tied to metrics short term. He can't say whether December's program beat November's or trace it to trial signups, but over 12 months "it's been epic."
- Audience came before product at RB2B. After a big July B2B post about downsizing his sales force, he asked his LinkedIn consultant Alec whether he should spend a month writing about the BDR problem and other sales issues SaaS companies had in 2023.
  - He wrote three posts on a Friday and they got about 100x the engagement of his Shopify-store content.
  - Only then did he decide to build a product for those readers.
- He steers who reads a post by topic: ABM draws VPs of marketing, zero-to-one and bootstrapping draws early-stage founders, and he knows what draws salespeople.
- He predicts more B2B startups will use thought leadership in 5 years, rising at the same rate as email sends.
- Product-led growth plus an influencer profile may not reach $100M ARR, but "it is a super efficient way to create a very nice business." At the time RB2B was nearly $5M ARR without five people working on it.
- Top creators don't take attention from each other. The best "push the average people down" and authentic voices can't be copied: "you couldn't copy my stuff. It wouldn't work for you."

## Choosing an ICP and writing for it: write to your earlier self

- Easiest starting point: write to yourself from five years ago. He was stuck for 3 years before Retention.com, "I just know the pain," and says that version of him "would have killed for" content like his, since few people at his stage were writing it.
- He tried writing for Shopify store owners for about 12 months and never resolved why they'd read him over someone who builds landing pages for ecom brands daily. When he switched to B2B go-to-market, it "just exploded."
- Reason: he is a go-to-market leader who runs marketing daily and "got kicked in the nuts like everyone did in 2023," so he speaks from lived experience. He didn't feel that with ecom owners complaining about Meta flagging unsubstantiated claims.
- Lessons he cites from that period:
  - Hiring more BDRs when growth is slow doesn't fix it.
  - "You got to grow the brand first, then the marketing machine, then you can start adding BDRs" to capture the demand.
- If you are your own ICP, start by writing to an earlier version of yourself. That also helps with imposter syndrome.
- He thinks a follower copying his build-in-public approach is still worth doing, since a body of work over a long journey becomes valuable, but at 12 months it is less magnetic because the journey is shorter. His own count: about 50,000 followers a year earlier, 120,000 now.
- Storytelling beats detail: "If I stopped entertaining, zero. If I stopped teaching, zero. It's like 50/50."

## How Adam writes a LinkedIn post

- A writer's goal: write a sentence so the reader feels exactly what the writer felt writing it.
- Hook: written so the reader has to click "see more."
- Structure: a mini hero's journey. Articulate the pain, agitate it, then give a takeaway that resolves it.
- Ending: a "koi little play on words" or something pithy for a dopamine hit. Example from a post about Clay: "unicorns are built one call at a time."
- On AI: he's open to testing it, but says his lived perspective is what makes the content valuable and AI can't replicate it. It helps the best builders move faster, but the average engineer just ships crap faster.
- On a book: he won't write one until he can commit the energy, citing how much work Alex Hormozi puts into his.

## Why outbound and the "predictable revenue" model stopped working

- He calls it the predictable revenue model: put university grads in seats typing keys and clicking a mouse to create demos. "When there were 10 and 100 companies doing that, it worked beautifully. When there was 100,000 companies doing that same thing, that's just not the arbitrage anymore."
- Supply and demand flipped. More email will be sent in 2026 than in 2025, and AI SDR tools like 11x and Artisan are "just spam cannons." "The world doesn't want a robot to spam."
- The usual response to lower effectiveness is higher volume, which makes the problem worse.
- Deliverability: five years ago he used the primary domain with five people in the Philippines doing manual lead gen half the day and manual sends from five inboxes the other half. "You can't do that now... your domain will get torched." Getting cold email delivered is 100 steps now and may be a thousand next year. Those who master it still win.
- SDR/BDR headcount grew exponentially over the last decade and is now shrinking.
- What he is constructive on: owned events and real-world experiences.
  - Retention.com's only outreach is a target list of about 1,000 brands. They find people who know those contacts and build an experience good enough that the targets attend.
  - Example: a box at a Taylor Swift concert, so good that the Foot Locker CMO didn't believe the invite was real until Diana messaged her from LinkedIn.
  - Trade shows still thrive for the same reason.
- If your motion is a million-dollar deal after a sports game, it works. But the question is how you reach that person, and his answer is usually through someone you both know.

## The lean-team philosophy: spend only when it's clearly working

- He attributes running a $30M-ARR company with 40 people to a bootstrapper's attitude about spending: scale something up only once you're sure it works, versus the venture approach of "run 10 large experiments this quarter."
- Example: he and a Clay founder both use an SEO agency. Clay's founder isn't sure it's working but, after raising $90M, paying $20K a month "is totally fine." Adam paid $10K a month and says he needs to know it's working in a real way.
- Exception he makes for LinkedIn brand: he accepts it's only visible as working over long periods, and still funds it.
- Leverage framework: Clay might get 90% of growth from one high-leverage channel and layer 15 others for the last 10%, which can be worth it for them. A bootstrapper only does things where 80/20 is in their favor, "where we have not seen the diminishing returns yet," and would "rather sacrifice growth than see diminishing returns."
- Culture: his team all think and act this way, and are proud of it.
- Benchmark he showed at a team offsite in Mexico: companies at $1M ARR with as many people as his, then companies at $30M ARR, where the smallest team was about 140 people. Hardly anyone keeps $750K to $1M of revenue per head as they scale, though they're doing it the venture way, so it's not a fair comparison.
- He says few bootstrapped companies reach $25M-$30M because investors get in early and put founders on a track many shouldn't be on.

## RB2B funnel: product-led to sales-assisted, and the plan for outbound

- Year one (RB2B turned a year old in 11 days from the interview): customers signed up and used it on their own. The workflow they pushed, "put a human being in front of this noisy Slack channel," wasn't great.
- They also told people to use the leads to book demos, which he no longer believes is the value of the signal.
- Current hypothesis: run the signal through the CRM and alert reps via CRM workflows:
  - add it to lead score;
  - send it to a Slack channel for a target account;
  - start automated outreach from the CRM.
- That fits customers with reps, a CRM and a target account list, so it needs human onboarding. He has one salesperson now and will probably add another, calling it sales-assisted PLG.
- Next step: outbound off his LinkedIn profile to sell a 30K-50K a year improved suite to a defined buyer persona, starting with frequent content engagers. Those lists can be pulled with tools.
- He thinks the same signal motion works for a sales-led business as for PLG.

## Courses, community and education as a funnel

- Last year he got excited by Russell Brunson, who bundled education on growing an internet business with the ClickFunnels platform fee. He tried a webinar-style sale.
- The problem was ICP mismatch: his paying software customers are marketers at roughly $10M ARR and above, while his content audience is zero-to-three-stage SaaS founders. Russell's overlap was about 100%; his is partial.
- Marketers either like or hate his content; founders mostly see him as who they want to be in five years.
- Belief from that experience: startup content makes founders do things before product-market fit that they shouldn't (cold email, ads). Unless you get word of mouth, "the only way to get to word of mouth is to get in a proper customer discovery loop and it's super manual."
- So he is starting a community and premium funnel around the message that you're wasting time and money on anything other than talking to prospects and customers until you have word of mouth, plus a network for lonely founders.
- It's a separate product line, a "sideshow" not directly feeding the SaaS, and he put his head of growth, Pete, in charge.
- Closing line: "edutainment is the secret weapon for growing a B2B company. There's no doubt."
