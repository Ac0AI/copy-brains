## Adam's content schedule and time allocation for founder-led LinkedIn

- Adam is CEO of two tech companies (retention.com and RB2B), and still spends most of his working time on content. He calls a calendar with a lot of content slots a "light week".
- He aims for 3-4 LinkedIn posts a week. Hooks for those posts are meant to be nailed in a weekly 1-hour idea session with his LinkedIn coach (Alec). They review last week's posts, what worked and what didn't, and what's happening in his work life.
- Monday is internal meetings for the other company, plus the coaching session. Tuesday is his content day. He fills open slots with other people's podcasts and webinars.
- He keeps Wednesday mornings free until 11:00 for his two-year-old daughter.
- He hands off everything he can. The retention.com business (50 people) is run by his co-founder, so he can spend most of his time on content and on RB2B marketing and product.
- His rule for gaps: "whenever I don't have a meeting I'm trying to crank out a LinkedIn post."
- Content ideas come from repeated questions. He heard "isn't inbound-led outbound just marketing?" about five times in a week and turned it into a post, with the hook "says every Revenue leader in SaaS, here's why they're wrong".
- He runs a weekly inbound-led-outbound workshop with his co-founder Santos. It's 20-30 minutes of talk, then Q&A. The first one drew about 1,300 on LinkedIn Live, and later ones draw 100-300 on Zoom. Topics included "how I bootstrapped $22M ARR" and how he grew his LinkedIn following.
- He uses the workshop and weekly calls to gather material for the next season of his docuseries. He invited Aaron Ross (author of Predictable Revenue) to debate it, because Adam says "predictable revenue is dead".

## Why Adam believes organic founder content beats paid ads in B2B

- He argues organic content is now worth "1,000 times more" than paid ads for B2B SaaS.
- He says that if he had $100 million but could not post on his own profile, he could not have built the awareness and trust he has. He began writing for B2B in September, and says the awareness is higher than any ad campaign or sponsorship would have produced.
- Reach compounds through collaboration. He uses other people's podcasts, webinars and co-created content to get in front of their audiences.
- He calls the efficiency of spreading awareness this way "dumbfounding".
- Scale result: RB2B launched its website about 13 weeks before this recording, and he says "basically everybody in B2B knows what RB2B is".
- He credits Chris Walker's playbook for the video approach.

## Overcoming the "I don't know what to write about" objection

- Adam says the muscle is hard to build. Ideas and writing are difficult at first, and it takes a long time to find what resonates and where you can offer real insight. "Everything is working against you in the beginning", like any startup.
- His recommendation: start with a ghostwriter and watch them write for a few months. They can show you what you might write about.
- He later switched from a ghostwriter to a LinkedIn consultant, Alec, who insisted he write himself. Alec's view: it only works if you actually write it, it will suck at first, then it becomes natural. Adam says he pays Alec more than he paid the ghostwriter.
- Sanity check first: is your audience on LinkedIn? Adam sells to salespeople, marketers and founders, who spend lots of time there. If you sell to hospitals, you probably can't systematically book demos through a LinkedIn profile, so find another way to use organic social.
- Even where LinkedIn doesn't drive demos, a CEO profile helps recruiting, partner and agency relationships, and anyone vetting the company. It is less likely to drive business outcomes.
- If you get "the content bug" (it's doing something even if you can't say what), then work on making it your own.

## What good LinkedIn content looks like and how to study it

- Adam rejects the view that LinkedIn content is dumb or all selfies. He says your feed is not indicative of great content. LinkedIn is where the deepest thought leadership goes because the audience is professional and the platform is still long-form and text-based.
- Seek out the best creators in your space or adjacent spaces, not the accounts with the most followers. Big-follower creators often aggregate audiences that let them sell ghostwriting, courses or coaching. He says that audience is not valuable to a business.
- He studies Chris Walker's feed to work out why one post got 600 likes and another 150: the hook, the topic, or the template.
- Walker "only talks about like five things" and has for seven years, but makes each post feel new. Recurring topics include multi-touch attribution, the dark funnel and "why paid ads don't work".
- Walker's loop: the topics hit, super-fans form around those ideas, he brings them to a weekly event where they ask deeper questions about the same ideas, and that reinforces the loop.
- Adam's own content is more diverse and more ambitious, because he uses a consultant.
- For LinkedIn, he says to treat every post as brand new because the platform is not sequential. The hook has to be a strong credibility-building statement that makes people read more.

## Measurement, authenticity and "make them feel like they know me"

- Adam admits he doesn't really measure content. He keeps a loose scorecard of signups and impressions, and notes that the week of a particularly well-fitted post had far more impressions and signups than a later week with similar impressions but worse audience fit.
- His core principle: make people feel like they know him as a friend. He engineers content to do this by telling stories, bringing people along on the journey and showing his face.
- His reasoning: software is getting commoditized. He compares it to the Kardashians selling lipstick: hundreds of millions of people feel like they're friends with them.
- He says that if he optimized only for what can be measured, his content would drift toward Justin Welsh-style content, he'd avoid video because it lowers reach, and he wouldn't make a docuseries or start a YouTube channel.
- He is wary of running everything on metrics: "the most important things cannot be measured", and measurable things crowd them out. At an early stage, running largely on gut is not crazy; he says he'd be criticized for it at a $100M ARR company.
- He cites Elon Musk as an example of valuing unmeasurable things (energizing executives with big-picture talks, unfiltered authenticity on Twitter) and says Jeff Bezos doesn't get this.

## Prioritizing content as a founder with limited time

- Adam's position: product-market fit is the thing that matters first. Once you have it, audience-building skill amplifies it dramatically. He had a product with fit and was already good at content when RB2B launched, so he commits fully.
- For an early-stage founder, he advises documenting the journey and writing about the problems you're solving. If the problems are real and you write well, you can build an audience on some network (LinkedIn, Twitter or elsewhere). Level up once the product has traction.
- Example: Triple Whale reached about $20-25M ARR in under two years on founder-led social, in coordination with other Twitter accounts. Their founders had around 23,000 followers, "the perfect followers", highly influential people in the Shopify ecosystem.
- Make yourself a presence in whatever ecosystem you operate in.

## A starter routine and engagement tactics

- If starting with limited time, he would write on two days a week (e.g. Monday and Thursday) and spend the other days commenting.
- Make a list of about 10 influential people in your ecosystem and thoughtfully comment on their posts, not with AI-generated comments. Commenting can lead them to comment on yours, and you build karma in the ecosystem.
- He dislikes AI-generated comments (for example, summaries of his post followed by a generic question).
- He tries to reply to every comment on his own posts, with a reply about as thoughtful as the comment.
- His connection-request and inbox handling is semi-automated by his coach's team, depending on the goal at the time (for example, booking discovery calls with VP-level marketers at Series B startups).

## Getting the team to post and the shared-brand approach

- Adam says more people posting widens the "attack surface": every employee's profile links to the website, which is RB2B's only traffic source.
- His co-founder Santos is heavily courted by recruiters but stays because he finds the work interesting. Adam is not worried about poaching.
- He encourages new hires to build an audience. His pitch: joining him means that in two or three years they're likely to get a much better job, because content is "a forever skill".
- Building a personal profile is about 10 times easier than building a company profile, because people go to social networks to connect with people, not companies.
- Posting is a strong recommendation rather than a requirement. If a role truly required it, he wouldn't hire someone who refused. Otherwise it's their loss.
- He also credits the co-founder pairing: they support and shout out each other on posts.

## Turning website visitors into outbound, and where outbound is heading

- RB2B puts a pixel on your site and resolves anonymous visitors (who didn't fill out a form) to a LinkedIn profile and business email, pushed to Slack. It is free and unlimited for the first month, then capped: about 85-90% of users stay free and the top ~10% pay.
- Follow-up timing: reach out within single-digit hours. Immediately, they'll connect it to your site; after two hours they may forget; after a day they may have forgotten the brand; after a week they have never heard of you.
- Use the same playbook as for outbound demos (phone, LinkedIn connect and message, cold email sequence). He says RB2B won't help if you can't book outbound demos; it gives you the best leads and makes your traffic channels more efficient.
- He had sold similar tech to the ecommerce space for five years and says being contacted after visiting a site is not infuriating in 2024, since people are used to retargeting.
- GDPR: RB2B doesn't identify EU traffic and only has US data subjects on US soil. He argues a European company using it for US traffic is not out of GDPR compliance, because the EU isn't seeking to regulate US tech on US soil. Compare CAN-SPAM, which is opt-out, not opt-in, a similar widespread misunderstanding.
- Outlook: he believes outbound response rates trend toward zero because of automation volume and blocking. Some of it goes back to field sales (target account lists, events, meetups through mutual contacts). His inbound-led outbound thesis survives this: people who engage with a founder's content will still answer a message from him.
- A message from his profile to book a call with a teammate (Pete) has a 100% book rate and 100% show rate in his experience, so the profile scales beyond his own hours.
