## Why Adam chose LinkedIn and video as his distribution engine

- Adam started posting on social about a year and a half before this 2024 conversation, while selling retention.com (a roughly $22M ARR bootstrapped company) to big Shopify stores.
- He wanted the fastest way to spread awareness of a product he believed worked for everyone with no real competitor.
- He read Gerhart's "Founder Brand" and heard "just tweet every day" advice, but refused to tweet into the void without knowing how the game works. He compares himself to someone who is "LeBron James" at a few things and "just lost" on social media.
- Instead he sought out a vendor selling to the same people that had reached about $10M ARR using only social. He offered to teach them his cold email motion if they taught him social.
- Their head of social said Twitter had worked for them, but LinkedIn was the ripe opportunity now. That led to LinkedIn, with an agency writing for him at first.
- He added video on purpose. His reasoning comes from his experience of watching ten courses by a business-coach founder over about two weeks. After about 100 hours he felt the guy was his best friend, while the guy treated him like a stranger. Adam wanted to turn that one-sided affinity into an advantage.
- Video builds affinity "in a way that nothing else can" because people observe your mannerisms, phrasing and voice.
- The result: strangers now walk up and ask "are you Adam Robinson?" A founder told him he put the B2B product on his site and his BDR booked five demos in a day.

## What made LinkedIn click after 9 to 12 months

- LinkedIn didn't work for roughly 9 to 12 months. Something clicked when he replaced a ghostwriter with a consultant, because he wanted to understand the machine himself.
- He also changed the subject. He pivoted from talking to Shopify founders to talking to SaaS revenue leaders, about the unpleasant experience of trying to scale his own company: sales, marketing and people problems.
- He started addressing SaaS revenue leaders around Labor Day 2023 and launched the B2B product on March 1 (2024). By launch he had about six to seven months of audience-building behind him.
- He notes that the distribution informed the go-to-market decision, not the other way around. The LinkedIn response was so strong that he decided to launch with a massive free offer, a Mailchimp-style go-to-market, because the free thing "is going to work way better for me with this megaphone than anybody else who didn't have it."

## Reading business context before copying a playbook: his churn lesson

- He says context matters: what he's doing may be wrong for others. He is bootstrapped, profitable, expecting high single-digit millions of free cash flow, has no investors and no time horizon.
- Retention.com economics: a paid-trial close is about an 18-month LTV at roughly $1,200 a month, so a mid-$20Ks customer. It is mostly inbound with little spend and generates about $7M of free cash this year, but churn is so high that growth is hard (he hopes to reach 30% in a couple of years).
- Why: in martech the lowest-churn products are systems of record (CRM, email platforms). Mission-critical workflows come next. The worst category is selling leads and intent data, and the buyer was an SMB e-commerce store, a high-churn persona.
- Comparison: Klaviyo has roughly 120% net revenue expansion, while his gross churn is about 60%. With Klaviyo-like SaaS metrics he says he'd be a unicorn.
- His early read (huge TAM, fast closes, churn would fade) was wrong because they were overselling, and the TAM was smaller and deals slower than expected.
- Lesson on funding: raising money on a plan to grow from $15M to $50M would have made his life miserable, because the actual problem was revenue contraction. He enjoys being able to run without "walking the plank."
- Fixes he names: point sales above the SMB buyer persona, improve operational efficiency (such as detecting when competitors put scripts on a site), and tighten positioning.

## The free-offer strategy for the B2B product

- The product: anonymous website visitors are identified at person level and the leads are pushed to Slack for free. He compares the model to Mailchimp, which was about $2M ARR when Constant Contact went public, then launched a huge free tier and bootstrapped to a roughly $12B exit.
- 22 days after launch: about 2,500 signups. 75% self-served installing the JavaScript, and activation doubled in the following week and a half. About 100 new signups show up on a normal day, with 70 of them installing the script.
- His thesis: be incredibly patient on revenue, on a scale of years. Give away what others charge $50K to $100K a year for, and become synonymous with person-level website visitor identity "synonymous with my face," so no competitor can match it. His data cost is low enough that a new entrant would have to pay unit costs he doesn't.
- The free offer spreads on its own. An agency owner told him he was "literally selling your tech" repackaged for a $5,000 setup fee and $3 per ICP lead while paying Adam nothing. Adam's reaction was "great, I hope you make a lot of money." Another user said he can't stop talking about it because it feels like getting away with something.
- On pricing, his stance is: keep adding value to the paid plan until conversion is decent, rather than taking value away from the free one. He is building goodwill with the audience by keeping it free.
- His planned paid hooks (about $495 a month): an ICP filter (once volume passes roughly 50 to 75 leads a day, traffic is mostly noise and the higher the traffic the more noise), plus a two-way HubSpot and Salesforce integration. Free users don't get repeat-visitor info or page-view history. He says today's paid features are not yet enough for 5% conversion; a $99 plan with the same features would not convert at 5% either.
- He gives full-featured 30-day trials, and wants the paid workflow to be a lower-churn use case than the lead data itself.

## Turning free users into value: reviews and the chatbot question

- A friend, Stuart, runs Shopify apps (e.g. email newsletter capture) and holds that "no user is a free user": you get something from everyone. Segment early, and send users who won't pay down a funnel that earns something else, such as reviews.
- Stuart's review machine: the onboarding creates any excuse to start a live chat. He hires about 40 "highly charismatic women in their 20s" with one bubbly profile, who "nice them to death" through onboarding. About 30% of those conversations convert to a review, and he gets a hundred times more reviews than competitors. Reviews often praise the person, not the product.
- Adam's contrast: he has nine G2 reviews after 22 days and a self-serve, no-human app, like Mailchimp's free tier, where you couldn't reach a human if you tried.
- Open questions he plans to test this quarter: can he and a colleague personally reach free users to get reviews at Stuart's rate? Can that be scripted for a team? And the "holy grail": can an LLM chatbot create the interpersonal obligation? He thinks that is probably a couple of years away.
- He doesn't favor gaming reviews.

## Staying engaged in public and building community

- Reply to comments on every post. He answers comments more than DMs on purpose, since public answers build community. He frames free users and commenters as "giving something back."
- He builds in public with real numbers and failures (including a mean-tweets style video of reading his Glassdoor reviews for the first time after a layoff), not vulnerability for show.
- He mentions that a coworker told him to say "we" more than "I." His LinkedIn headline approach doesn't work with "we."

## Why small teams, AI and underpriced SaaS are an opening

- He's excited by AI letting a very small group do what a large one used to. Example: one support person processing 2,000 tickets a week.
- Combining AI with an "influencer-type founder" distribution can create a playbook for bootstrapped startups at scale, with highly skilled staff using AI tools.
- On Clearbit (told to him by its former CEO): they reached $8M ARR in about 18 months with two reps and three other people, underpriced relative to value, which built great brand and goodwill. After raising venture money, boards pushed for fair-value pricing (e.g. "you charge GitHub $40K, how do we make it $500K?"), and that hurt the brand over eight years. Clearbit also could not compete with a free data company.
- His broader read on SaaS: it is bloated with bundles. Zoom Info and Apollo each have one product everyone wants (contact data) and several others that about 90% of users don't use. A self-serve point solution with a massive free offer and a tiny team could undercut them, and the incumbents' only lever is raising prices.
- His light, self-serve identity app is partly a stepping stone toward a contact-data app with a similar UI.
