## Work in public as the foundation for a LinkedIn audience

- Adam started writing on LinkedIn about a year and a half before this recording. Tommy from Triple Whale offered to ghostwrite for him through a side agency.
- He started when the business was at $12 million ARR with six employees. He knew that combination would shock people, because most founders have less revenue and more staff.
- His point: working in public only works if the numbers are impressive. A founder under $1M saying "I'm working in public" is not interesting. A founder with $12M and six people sharing everything makes people listen.
- The first plan was 60% email content and 40% work in public. He hated the email content ("I don't care about email marketing, I've never sent a DTC email") and it felt inauthentic, so it moved to all work in public.
- Even though it did not generate hard DTC leads, the LinkedIn presence raised the brand's profile among DTC agencies and vendor employees, and made hiring easier.

## Finding the format: twelve months of bad posts before a hit

- His early videos (about September of the prior year) were rambling. He says they looked like a person who did not know what he was doing.
- A video producer pointed him to a YouTube format: hook, story, three lessons, soft CTA. He bought whiteboards and tried to post one video a day, writing and recording them all in one session.
- Posts got about 22 likes. People at trade shows said "I watch every one of your videos," but he knew the audience was tiny.
- He bounced between topics (work-life balance, spirituality, firing people, hiring A players) with no focus.
- He then worked with Alec Paul, a coach and consultant who is not a ghostwriter. Alec is strategic and acts as an editor: he sharpens headlines, knows how the LinkedIn machine works, and shows templates that hit. They talk about what happened in Adam's life that week and decide what to write.
- Adam's average post got about 30 likes; a great one got about 100.

## The B2B pivot that made posts hit

- Adam felt it was inauthentic to write about ecommerce because he had never run a store. Alec told him: "If you actually sell something to that audience... we have a machine that systematically generates inbound leads. It will work, I promise."
- Around mid-August he started writing to salespeople, marketers and founders. The breakout post was about speaking to 30 BDR leaders and the eight pains they share: a pipeline crisis, being told not to buy, outbound emails that no longer work.
- He then sat down one night, wrote three posts, and decided that if they hit, he would know what "great" is. They hit. His reasoning: once you know what great looks like, you can keep the machine going indefinitely.
- It took about 12 months to reach that point, and he says people could probably get there faster by being more direct about audience.
- Over the next three months the audience grew so quickly that it shaped the product decision (below).

## LinkedIn audience shaped the product and go-to-market

- Adam says the LinkedIn presence was "informing the product" and the whole go-to-market.
- Original plan for the B2B product: skip small customers (they churn) and go straight to mid-market at 30 to 50K ACV.
- Because the posts hit so hard, he switched to a big free product with an upsell, gating integrations. He reasoned a big premium offer helps disproportionately someone who already has a megaphone.
- Pricing logic: sell the idea "if you want to save time with integrations, pay $500; if you don't, knock yourself out with the free app forever." Anyone who wants to save time has already found value in the free product, and $500 a month is "a drop in the bucket."
- The free product sends LinkedIn profiles of site visitors to Slack. His goal is 250,000 people using it.
- Ambition: move upmarket later, once the privacy question for larger buyers (who have a CISO) becomes relevant.

## Brand and community as the only moats

- Adam says that in SaaS without network effects, "brand and community are the only moats." Retention.com has no network effects, so his moat is his brand.
- The community is built around problems and ideas tangential to the technology, not the product itself. He calls it extremely difficult and very long game.
- His advice on where to start community: begin on a social network where your audience is. For his audience that was Twitter or LinkedIn.
- He chose LinkedIn because only 1 to 2% of LinkedIn users create content versus a much more crowded Twitter, so there is more reach for the effort.

## Premium media: the docuseries and what it cost

- A producer, Christie, made a reality-style series about Adam. A cameraman, John, traveled with him to about five or six work trips over a year, plus a weekly one-hour interview. Episodes were pieced together out of sequence.
- It cost over $10K per episode (he had budgeted $5K) across 18 episodes, around $250K total. His view: it makes the viewer feel connected to everything he does, and people say "I've watched every single episode."
- Distribution caveat: the premium content did not perform on YouTube or LinkedIn. A polished eight-minute episode got 5,000 to 6,000 view minutes at best, while a hot-take video of him talking to a laptop got about 40,000. LinkedIn rewards talking-head takes over Netflix-style production. He did nothing to promote the series.
- He hired Christie full time. Current shows: "Events That Don't Suck" (partner Kyle building an event motion people want), and "The Art of Excellence" on the DTC side, profiling revered founders like Curtis from Portland Leather Goods and Ben from True Classic. These are brand stories, not case studies, and do not mention the product.

## Building in public weekly and getting the team to create content

- LinkedIn lacks a timeline, so episodic series do not work there; it serves old and new posts together. To give people a more immersive view, Adam records his hour-long Monday B2B strategy and tactics call, posts the Notion board with key metrics and a TL;DR, and plans to do it every week for a year.
- His reasoning: he would have "died to know" week by week what 0-to-1 and 1-to-10 companies focused on.
- Getting the team to create content: Alec's advice is that when others see you doing it regularly, especially if it works, they follow. Santos and Kyle have joined; Diana is harder due to her workload.
- Stunt example: he plans to make ChatGPT the entire support org for the B2B business and hire a person to be a chatbot influencer. He says he would sacrifice sales and support quality because the "no people using OpenAI" story should create more virality than it costs.

## UGC and influencer-led PLG plan for B2B

- Adam believes hiring 20 SDRs will not work; one talented growth marketer can do more. The best motion looks like what Jolie Skin did: a tiny team plus a huge UGC machine.
- Idea: a premium PLG product where users post, in detail, how they booked their first demo, like 20 LinkedIn posts a day of "this is how I booked a demo with Retention.com." He says he has not seen anyone try this in B2B.
- He is working with Morgan Ingram on a six-month deal: 10 sales influencers (not micro, not big), flown to Atlanta two per month, filmed using the product. Each produces three to five pieces of content, and they agree to put "Retention.com" in their LinkedIn title.
- The content is then amplified with inexpensive thought leadership ads. Goal: create the perception that Retention.com is everywhere.

## Early marketing, churn, and lessons from Robly

- Early Retention (Get Emails) marketing in late 2019 to early 2020: about six months of Facebook ads, one 160-second selfie-style video ad per week answering sales questions with his wife. COVID made ad inventory cheap, so ads paid back in a day.
- Lesson: "If you want to go fast you care about time to payback; if you want to grow big you need low churn." Facebook-acquired customers churned around 20% per month, so he stopped.
- Churn: he expected a lower end-state churn than reality. Systems of record are the lowest-churn category; selling leads or intent data to small businesses is inherently high churn. Mid-market customers are stickier than SMB by orders of magnitude.
- Approach: sell upmarket, ensure correct onboarding, and make sure the customer understands what the product does. The product works better for bigger stores because it amplifies throughput. Pricing is not linear, so bigger stores get more value.
- Robly (email marketing) reached about $3M ARR then stalled. Mailchimp's free offer beat them: nothing Robly could say that someone who knew Mailchimp would not choose Mailchimp. Mailchimp went freemium at $2M ARR while Constant Contact at $100M ARR could not follow because it could not take the revenue hit.
- Lesson: a premium offer or a free offer can be the thing that beats an incumbent. Get Emails started as a Robly feature that people used without the rest of the app, so he spun it out. His model: run positive cash flow businesses that fund experiments on other ideas.

## Privacy and role

- Privacy view: after hundreds of hours with privacy attorneys he believes laws are tightening but will not hurt this business. Outcomes will mostly be more cookie banners and disclosure. CCPA does not apply to businesses under $25M in revenue. There is no private right of action, so enforcement would target large companies first, and he argues the fear is greater than the reality.
- B2B vs DTC: business contact data is mostly untouched except by CPR, and B2B companies care about privacy only once they hire a CISO.
- His role: he handed the Shopify business to co-founder Diana Ross and spends about one hour in an executive meeting plus one-on-ones on it. He is now founder-CEO of a zero-to-one, very public experiment on the B2B side, acting as the entire marketing engine and much of big-picture product. He is building the brand from zero, before the product existed.
