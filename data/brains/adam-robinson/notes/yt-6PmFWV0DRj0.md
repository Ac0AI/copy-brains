## Why LinkedIn works for Adam, and when it doesn't

- Adam's LinkedIn growth took about 12 months to find "content market fit" (a term he credits to Devin Reed of Gong). He says it is as hard as product-market fit and needs the same perseverance and curiosity.
- He says he is in a near-perfect situation: his ICP (revenue leaders and SaaS CEOs) lives on LinkedIn, so the profile is literally his marketing channel.
- He would not recommend going all-in to be the best creator on LinkedIn if you sell to, say, hospitals. It still helps with hiring and ecosystem credibility, but won't generate leads.
- His audience: roughly 75% ICP, and about 85% unique engagers post to post (measured by Peter Conforti from his downloaded LinkedIn data). Typical posts get about 50-60k impressions and 200-500 engagements.
- How he picked the platform: he was targeting large Shopify stores, who are more active on Twitter, but Twitter was very competitive. He started on LinkedIn because the ecosystem (vendors, agencies, employees, partners) is there.
- Nine months of posting with a first ghostwriter (Tommy) built ecosystem-level credibility. It helped him reach the brands he wanted because people took him seriously.
- The big shift came when the company decided to build a B2B product. Writing aimed at B2B CEOs, with a different writer (Alec), is when LinkedIn became the main channel.
- Litmus test for any founder: is your audience actually active on the platform? Look at who engages with your posts. He only learned the Shopify founders weren't on LinkedIn by trying.

## Authenticity, vulnerability and the AI "B minus" problem

- Adam shared an office with the Jasper AI team for two years, before Jasper. Watching it left him with a deep belief that B-minus AI content would multiply roughly a million times, so the most authentic, human, vulnerable content would be amplified even more.
- He calls it "the LIE of AI" to think it can offer perspective. People want a point of view, not generic AI posts.
- What makes his content unique: he describes in "excruciating detail" the pain a CEO of a $20M ARR SaaS company experiences, with transparency and vulnerability. One early team member told him it was the kind of thing you'd tell your wife or best friend.
- He uses video because he believes ChatGPT can't yet replicate him in that form.
- Example: his monthly update said revenue was down 2.3% for the fourth month in a row, the competitive environment was terrible, and "we are deeply in the woods." Other founders said they couldn't send that to their own executive team, let alone post it.
- He says he has no investors, and they probably wouldn't love it. He admits it isn't easy to write: his ego is tied to ARR, as a SaaS founder's is.
- His limits on sharing: he wouldn't name a specific underperforming individual or say he's about to let a customer success person go.
- Payoff: early-stage SaaS founders tell him they read every post and share them with their teams, because he is "actually in the trenches" and reports every detail.
- Controversy rule: he only stirs the pot "when it's real." Trolling for its own sake gets old quickly on LinkedIn.

## Thought leadership as a hiring and team-alignment tool

- The seed: at a trade show, the president of Kajabi was asked what he'd tell himself to do differently when he joined at $6M ARR. His answer was to start posting on LinkedIn every day. Adam had six employees and about $6M ARR at the time.
- The reasoning Adam took from that conversation: a steady stream of content about why you do things and how you think lets employees opt in rather than being pushed in reluctantly. The Yellowstone example: John Dutton has no mission-values poster, but you know what it's like to work on that ranch from the show.
- Result: the right employees arrive having already read everything. He has "people hurling themselves" at him to work there, even though his Glassdoor rating is a 2 after a layoff (61 to 55 employees, last year).
- He refuses to manipulate review platforms and takes a "this is the authentic me, take it or leave it" stance. He read his negative Glassdoor reviews for the first time on camera in a "Mean Tweets" style post.
- He recommends Dave Gerhardt's book *Founder Brand* for the case for founder-led thought leadership, even if you're not in his ideal situation.
- Even when selling to hospitals, a founder's thought leadership benefits the enterprise.

## Objections, ghostwriters and his current content workflow

- Two sets of objections: "I don't want to be famous" and "I don't have the time or experience to compete with professionals."
- On fame: you won't become truly famous, just known in a small part of the world, which helps you and your company. Building a parasocial relationship with tens of thousands of people is very good for business.
- A ghostwriter solves the second set: they know the game (hook, credibility, keeping people on the post, a dopamine hit at the end so they react).
- A competent ghostwriter should ask: who are you, what stage is the company, who are you hiring, who do you sell to, are they on this platform, and what pain can you articulate for them.
- Best post structure per Adam: a mini hero's journey. Agitate a pain, share a transformational moment, then give relief and a takeaway. Articulating pain is the most relatable and cuts through.
- Even with a ghostwriter, he believes you'll feel "I don't know what this is doing for me but it's doing something and I like it."
- His current setup: he no longer uses a ghostwriter and writes everything himself. His consultant Alec helps with idea generation, tightens hooks, and edits. Alec also understands the algorithm and what a post should have done versus what it did.
- Idea capture: he jots ideas on a notepad, and sometimes gets post ideas from questions asked on podcasts. He says it's like having another version of himself over his shoulder asking whether this is good content.
- Weekly cadence: a one-hour ideas meeting with Alec on Mondays, and his whole Tuesday goes to content in some form. He says he runs two startups at the same time.
- Advice to founders who say they have no time: if you hire a ghostwriter and get the feeling it works, you'll figure out how to delegate other things.

## Known well vs. well known: two audience-building strategies

- Strategy 1 (well known): you don't care who engages. You want a big number and use tactics like memes and viral formats. Content is optimized for virality over meaning, and then you funnel interested people to a newsletter or podcast. Chase Diamond is an example (LinkedIn as awareness, newsletter and podcast as trust), and Dan Murray's approach is similar (memes pulled to his newsletter).
- Chase advised Adam to work on distribution: he spends as much time sending his posts to chat rooms of other big accounts (so they engage) as he does creating the post. Adam's own time goes almost entirely into making the content as good as it can be.
- Strategy 2 (known well): use LinkedIn as both distribution and trust platform, writing for a specific audience you know you'll attract. This makes readers feel they know you and are on a journey with you, a deeper trust than viral content gives.
- Adam prefers strategy 2 and calls it more efficient and elegant because you aren't transferring people across platforms.
- Newsletter growth loop he heard about and is trying: post on LinkedIn with the newsletter as the call to action. Even your best post reaches a small share of LinkedIn, so when you send the same content in a newsletter a week later, it hits a largely different audience and drives people back to comment.
- He says LinkedIn lacks a place for people to go deeper, so he started a newsletter and a weekly webinar (a 10-30 minute monologue followed by Q&A) pointing to his Rb2b landing page.

## The 6sense cease-and-desist post as a distribution play

- Context: his company bought 6sense for about $18K in November, mainly to compare against Rb2b (he notes this technically violates the terms, but "everybody does that"). His salespeople wouldn't use it because it was harder to get value from than Rb2b.
- 6sense emailed that the price was going up 3x, then offered a flat renewal if he renewed six months early. He says that in the Shopify world, a 5% price increase would get him "burnt at the stake."
- He wrote an inflammatory post (no swearing in the hook), giving theories for why they were scared: their data is bad, person-level beats company-level, and they fear the viral expansion of his tool. He framed it as fun but not as fact.
- Four days later at 6pm he got a cease and desist. His reasoning as a marketer: what could be better than an industry leader (6sense raised at $5.2B) acknowledging a small company, and even looking afraid of it?
- Instead of the demands (take the post down, apologize, and tag the 463 commenters), he uploaded the cease and desist as a PDF and wrote that 6sense tried to sue him over a LinkedIn post. He listed what they demanded, said what he did instead, and restated his theories with a David vs. Goliath framing.
- Results: about 1,600 engagements on that post, which was well above his average. Follow-ups also beat average (one about 800).
- Follow-up: the one credible claim was that you can't buy their tech to compare it with yours, so he checked Rb2b and found they had apparently signed up for an Rb2b account too.
- Takeaway he draws: ask what you can say that others can't or won't, without torching the company. He also notes that the cease and desist wasn't the reason they sent it, but it let him credibly say they were afraid of him.

## How he uses LinkedIn for pipeline: the B2B motion and Rb2b

- The motion he describes from Alec (who works with B2B CEOs): put out great content, get inbound connection requests, accept only ICP requests, ask what caught their attention, wait about 60 days (by then they've seen your posts and see you as a celebrity), then ask if they want to talk about a problem. He says accept rates are very high.
- He hasn't even needed this yet because the content alone worked better than expected. He now has a free offer used to book discovery calls or agency conversations (for example, talking to 100 HubSpot agencies).
- On free offers: he could charge but would plow the revenue back into marketing anyway, so he makes it free.
- Product-led growth: when a high-value lead signs up for the product and doesn't install the script within four days, his LinkedIn team member reaches out to them directly.
- The product he promotes, Rb2b, does person-level website visitor identification rather than company-level reverse IP lookup (the Clearbit, 6sense, ZoomInfo style). Adam says company-level is useful but limited.
- His personal history: his first startup was stuck at $3M for four years. He says he was dying for someone ahead of him to explain what they were doing and why, and that motivates how openly he shares today.
- Where to find him: rb2b.com, LinkedIn, and retention.com (aimed at e-commerce stores).
