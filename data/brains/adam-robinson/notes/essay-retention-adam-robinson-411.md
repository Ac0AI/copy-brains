## How Adam finds uncontested channels and gets to $1M ARR faster each time

- Adam bootstrapped three SaaS companies to $1M+ ARR with no outside funding: Robly in 17 months, Retention.com (originally Get Emails) in 27 weeks, and RB2B in 16 weeks.
- His core belief: "what makes acquiring customers expensive is competition." If you have a channel with no competitors and a good offer, acquisition is cheap.
- Each company got its speed from a different uncontested channel: a scraped customer list with a call center (Robly), provocative Facebook ads plus targeted cold email (Retention.com), and a pre-built LinkedIn audience (RB2B).
- He says his advice for the best business decision he's received is "Do not take institutional capital." He argues VCs show up once you have traction with "a lie about how if you sign this piece of paper you're going to be worth $30 million." He wants to show people you can bootstrap to $10M ARR.
- You still need a small pool of starting capital, roughly $50-100k, from savings, an agency, an info product, or friends and family. Adam lived off savings from 10 years as a trader at Lehman Brothers.

## Robly: a scraped list and a call center to $1M ARR in 17 months

- Robly was an email marketing product competing with Constant Contact and Mailchimp. It launched January 1, 2014.
- Adam's team built a crawler to find websites with Constant Contact signup widgets. Only paying Constant Contact customers would have one, so the list was a targeted list of paying customers of a public company.
- A team in Pakistan manually enriched about 250,000 domains by Google search to find phone numbers.
- A call center (run "out of my apartment") cold-called with the pitch "We can give you 50% more opens for half the price." They then upsold to three quarters of the price and migrated the customer over. Plans were roughly $15-50 a month.
- The economics worked because nobody else called that list, so human sales was viable even at $15-50 a month.
- About 5,000 people switched from Constant Contact this way.
- Onboarding was manual because the "magic moment" was getting a customer to hit send on their first campaign. Customers who did stuck 100% of the time, but busy small-business owners (a coffee shop with one employee) would not do it alone. They started with three customer success people building templates one-to-one. Then they moved to one-to-many onboarding calls, which had a better show rate and felt like a community. A $25 off incentive got people to show up.
- Why it stalled: once the list was burned through, the product wasn't competitive. Mailchimp had a better product and a free offer, and Robly wasn't even cheaper than Mailchimp. Growth stalled in the low single-digit millions of ARR and then shrank, though it stayed cash-flow positive. The company later sold to private equity in an eight-figure exit in 2021 with no outside funding.
- Adam says those lists still work, but now everyone has access via BuiltWith and similar tools. His own verdict: with what he knows now, he "wouldn't start it."
- The cash from Robly funded the experiments that led to the next company, since the idea came from a feature built inside Robly.

## Failed attempts before Retention.com, and the idea that worked

- Before Get Emails, Adam spent about two years and hundreds of thousands of dollars on things that didn't work.
- Attempt one: a Constant Contact "authorized local expert" style boots-on-the-ground distribution model (Constant Contact had spent about $50M building it). It was a complete failure, wasted a ton of money, and ended with a lawsuit from Constant Contact over the non-compete of the person involved.
- Attempt two: building a product aimed just above Mailchimp's buyer. His co-founder Diana had seen her old company succeed with it, and they hired engineers and spent a year building. At launch, no one cared because 10 other companies were already doing it. Adam calls this the opposite of how he advocates building now: sell before you build.
- The breakthrough came at a conference (the Traffic and Conversion conference in 2018). The stunt got attention, but conversations glazed over until he asked, "What if I could get you the email address of people on your website who don't fill out a form?" Every person perked up and wanted to buy it.
- He didn't know how to build it and spent about a year and a half digging. It first shipped as a feature in Robly's email app. Users signed up for the email app just to use the identity feature, download the file, and use it elsewhere, so he spun it out into its own product in November 2019.

## Retention.com: Facebook ads and cold email, $1M ARR in 27 weeks

- Why it worked: there were data companies with similar products, but they had a "pathetic go to market and user experience" and weren't SaaS people. Adam saw that as the opening.
- The ads: Adam and his wife made weekly one-minute Facebook videos that were deliberately inflammatory to privacy-minded people ("how is that legal?") while answering sales questions. The goal was to get people anticipating the ad. The most searched term for Get Emails became "Helen from Get Emails."
- The ads paid back fast because of occasional whales: a click-through PLG plan let someone buy a $5,000-a-month plan by clicking, which happened about once every two weeks and paid back the prior two weeks of ads.
- He also replied to people who called him a scumbag in the comments, using the fights to make better ads. He says that headspace was bad for him personally.
- Targeting was broad interest audiences: Shopify, entrepreneur, Klaviyo, and names like e-commerce influencer Ezra Firestone.
- The problem: the ads brought "atrocious" customers, roughly 15% monthly churn "wannabes." After about eight weeks without a whale, and after spending around $100k on ads, he stopped the ads entirely and focused on high-value users and targeted cold email.
- Cold email worked better. His co-founder Diana prospected to Shopify and Klaviyo user lists, and those customers had substantially lower churn and higher value. MRR grew from about $200k to $600k+ over the next year.
- Adam credits the whole zero-to-one stage to the awareness from the video ads.

## Churn, plateaus and shrinking TAM at Retention.com

- Retention.com reached about $3M ARR quickly, then sat flat for nine months. Adam was burned out and thought "maybe I'm just a 3 million ARR guy."
- In a high-churn product category with a high-churn buyer persona, you are "always trying to do things to outpace churn."
- The Shopify audience topped out ARR because of churn. Getting to the next level means larger deals with lower-churn customers such as omnichannel retailers. The company is at roughly $21.8M ARR.
- Product-market fit can fade: he once estimated 50,000 Shopify stores could succeed with the product; he now thinks it's about 1,400, and there are more competitors.
- Cold email only worked then because of a scrappy manual setup: five people in the Philippines spent half the day building lists and half sending emails by hand from Gmail inboxes, and Diana's sales assistant replied and booked demos. He says "it literally just would not work today" because more people send more email and spam filters keep tightening.
- His own 2023 attempt to scale a team of BDRs convinced him that having people click buttons and write emails and LinkedIn connections "just isn't working anymore."

## Layoffs and right-sizing the team

- Adam has done layoffs three times (the third was the day after the recording).
- Robly: they scaled the sales org around a second list of Constant Contact customers that looked promising, but nobody picked up the phone. They dropped from 38 to 6 people. "It was my fault."
- Retention.com 2023: they scaled outbound sales on false signals about TAM and deal size, and customers churned and contracted. The VP of sales quit and instead of replacing him they cut the sales org from 20 to 4. He says it cleaned up the culture and the team started moving faster.
- Latest round: the company was still sized for the market of 18 months earlier. Throughput was low and he realized they could do the work with half the team. He cites running the company with EOS as helping him see what actually moves the needle.
- His view: no one regrets that move, and the day you realize you can do it with half the people you have to make it. It is "the most liberating feeling" for the founder but awful for employees, and the worst part of the job.

## Building a LinkedIn audience from zero to 92,000 followers

- He started from zero in September 2022 and had about 92,000 followers when recorded.
- The first 12 months didn't work. He posted a daily video with no theme, aimed at Shopify store owners to sell Retention.com. He struggled to explain why an e-commerce store owner would listen to him instead of an e-commerce agency guy. Posts were good but didn't book demos.
- He kept going anyway. Lesson: you find your voice by producing, and the time spent was "learning what not to do."
- The turning point came in 2023. Their scale-up attempt failed, the VP of sales quit, and the sales team shrank by about 75%. He wrote about it and it exploded (3,300 likes in 24 hours, versus 30-50 on a typical post).
- His LinkedIn consultant, Alec, told him to pivot the content to B2B because all his other clients were B2B and none were willing to be this open.
- He then wrote three more hot takes, such as that BDR teams no longer work, and got about 2 million impressions across the three posts. His follower growth rate permanently steepened.
- The formula: work in public, share financials and failures, say what people only say to their spouse behind closed doors, and be as transparent and human as possible. Talk about how go-to-market is changing and how to build SaaS. He tries to be "a dealer of hope" that you can bootstrap to $10M ARR.
- On haters: LinkedIn is more polite than other platforms. The strongest pushback comes from outbound agencies when he says outbound is dead, though most comments agree.
- He says content can't be boring: you need a real point of view.

## Edutainment and audience-first distribution

- Adam says he is "in the edutainment business and I happen to have a SaaS at the end of the funnel." His job is making entertaining, educational content about building a SaaS and how you can too.
- Organic social is the most efficient way to build trust and sell. Demand for content about startups is higher than for almost anything else.
- LinkedIn Thought Leadership ads let you put spend behind a post that already performed organically. Clicks are cheap because LinkedIn already wants to show the post, and you know it works. He says that if you can create great organic social content and your large competitors can't, "you have 100 times more efficient awareness generation potential than they do."
- The playbook is less clear than cold email's, but he says it is much more powerful.
- He believes B2B may follow the DTC pattern of influencers first, then distributing product into their audience. He points to Greg Eisenberg as building a portfolio of companies audience-first. Field sales (taking a buyer to a Lakers game for a million-dollar deal) won't change, but for point-solution SaaS, distribution is the hard part.
- He recommends Russell Brunson's Expert Secrets for why organic social content is so powerful.

## RB2B: $1M ARR in 16 weeks from a pre-built audience

- RB2B matches anonymous website visitors to LinkedIn profiles and pushes them into Slack with a business email. It is aimed at SaaS companies with sales teams.
- Pricing: the core product is free for up to 200 leads a month, which is about 85% of signups. Paid plans start around $99 a month, and a mid-market SaaS pays about $750 a month. The idea was to take price off the table: zero-touch, PLG, a "hundred million dollar value proposition that just punches you in the face" that is free and spreads on its own.
- It was deliberately designed in reaction to the death of outbound: Adam asked what the complete opposite of a go-to-market that no longer works would be, and answered with founder-content-led distribution plus a free product.
- Launch prep: between October (when they decided to build) and the March 1 launch, they held about 300 discovery calls and had a beta version with testers giving feedback. By launch day thousands of people were on a waitlist.
- Results: $1M ARR in 16 weeks after the March 2023 launch (he describes 1 to 2 million ARR in six weeks), and it was past $2M ARR a few months later. Adam also does about 10 podcast appearances a week as part of the go-to-market.
- He says it works "because of the year of blood, sweat and tears I put into LinkedIn audience building," which warmed up the market six months before launch, and it is now the whole vehicle behind growth.
- He credits the same lesson he took from earlier failures: sell before you build, and design the product around the distribution channel you have.
