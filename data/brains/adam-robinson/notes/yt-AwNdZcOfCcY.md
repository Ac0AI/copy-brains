## Why RB2B's price undercuts competitors' acquisition cost

- Adam launched RB2B (person-level website visitor identification) with the explicit goal of building "the leanest system possible" where AI could amplify human labor "by a thousand X."
- That goal forced a freemium, PLG, SMB-funnel model. He accepted a cap on revenue in exchange for an operation that eventually runs with almost no human input.
- The risk of that model is a direct competitor starting a feature war, which makes the product complicated again.
- Competitors copied RB2B after a supplier publicized how much money it was making. Two advantages let Adam price below what a rival spends to acquire a customer:
  - **Near-zero acquisition cost:** his LinkedIn thought leadership gave him very cheap awareness.
  - **Near-zero fulfillment cost:** RB2B was already a data company with two other product lines and data scientists. It layered "a few hundred thousand dollars of B2B data" and other signal onto an existing data asset, so it had "no marginal cost of fulfilling a contact."
- Warmly, by contrast, was paying a data vendor (he names Apollo.io) about 7 cents per email. Its unit economics of fulfilling a contact were "literally higher" than RB2B's subscription price, so it could not match the price.
- He also argues RB2B's mechanism for identifying people was better than rivals', though it took the market a while to notice.

## Competitors forced upmarket, leaving the cheap simple product alone

- Because rivals could not compete on channel cost or price, they built extra features and moved to roughly $30K per year contracts. Their investors also wanted guaranteed new recurring subscription revenue.
- Adam's read of the last vendor to do this: the competitor had "the perfect product," then built everything else. The remaining position was "put the pixel on your site and we'll resolve the contact and push it somewhere else."
- His framing of the offer: if you want anything else, go pay someone else $20,000; if you want to get started in 60 seconds for $79, RB2B is "the only game in town."
- Result: with no direct competitors left at that price point, he chose to leave the product "frozen in time."

## The lean-operation outcome

- Approaching $9M ARR ("going to hit it any day now") with a very small time cost:
  - His developer (Tate) has not touched the code in 6 weeks.
  - The support lead (Rob) spends about 15 minutes a day on ticket escalations.
  - Adam posts about three times a week on LinkedIn, and not about RB2B.
- The wider company has about 30 full-time employees and a 5-person executive team. Two executives run the other two product lines, and one handles finance, legal and HR.
- He notes the lightning-in-a-bottle element: "so much of business is… an incredible multivariate equation," and parts of this he could not have planned.
- He says a business at this scale needing 15 minutes a day is "the dream" and that he would like "a hundred of those."

## Sub-VC-scale businesses as an advantage

- He values a business that is too small for venture capital, because VC-backed rivals cannot afford to chase it. He frames the opportunity as having no heavily funded competitors going after your market share.
- He accepts that RB2B's churn is bad and that it will top out. Even so, a portfolio of lean $10M ARR businesses, some growing and some shrinking slightly, is an appealing goal to him.

## When to raise venture capital (and when not to)

- Capital is necessary for some businesses, such as OpenAI's data centers, space travel, or lending businesses that need money to lend out.
- For software, he argues money does not buy product-market pull. Spending before you have pull is "a big waste." Create the pull first, then spend on channels to speed it up.
- Seed money can come from outside the VC industry, for example friends of your parents who think you are smart, as with a restaurant.
- Raising can make sense when the metrics are exceptional. He cites Fixer, a UK email company with 155% net revenue retention, a working Meta funnel, strong CAC/LTV and a very large TAM.
- The danger he describes: venture investors hold a portfolio and care about it, not about you. They expect you to "grow forever" until someone acquires you at roughly 3x or more.
- Many founders who raise do not have Fixer-like metrics. He says a hyper-efficient company without that capital has a better quality of life and a higher chance of success, with a harder start and a much less stressful later stage.
- His Klaviyo example (from a conversation with its founder): the success depended on factors the founders could not have foreseen. They tied themselves to Shopify when it was a $50M valuation (now $150B), picked the SMB space with real net revenue retention (e-commerce email priced on list size), and benefited from a Mailchimp opt-out controversy. He says most peers who raised at that time failed.

## Keeping the team lean

- He advertises clearly that the company is "highly profitable, super capital efficient." That attracts scrappy, lean people.
- Some founders are wired the opposite way. He mentions his mentor Brett Hurt, who would scale an ice cream stand globally. He notes that approach worked for Hurt but that Hurt began his career when what Adam does was not possible.

## Pricing and positioning for his new product (Moltsets)

- The product is a contact database positioned for autonomous agents and Claude Code users, who might otherwise use Clay only to build lists and enrich emails and phone numbers.
- Planned pricing: fully unlimited tiers at $19, $199 and $999, with API rate limits as the only difference. He hopes to get "5,000 people pay me 200 bucks a month."
- The offer is meant to be disruptive on price because his marketing leverage keeps customer acquisition cost low. As with RB2B, his database is already paid for, so there is "no incremental cost of fulfilling" a subscription.
- Go-to-market plan:
  - UGC and influencer content with creators in the same ecosystem.
  - A weekly workshop called "Claude did what? B2B experts share Claude skills that you can deploy today," which does not need to involve his product. The aim is to aggregate attention around the Claude Code for go-to-market mechanism and become the "make my face equivalent to Claude skills for go-to-market."
  - His recent Claude Code show drew about 900 live attendees, which he reads as strong demand.
- He admits low conviction on this one ("the least conviction I've had since I knew what I was doing"), but sees idea-to-launch in about 5 weeks and extra upside: LinkedIn content helps all his products at once, and success would also help RB2B.

## Content as the distribution engine

- It took about a year to find his voice. He tried Shopify-store content with a ghostwriter, then worked with a LinkedIn consultant (Alec) and switched to writing exclusively about B2B SaaS, with unusual transparency.
- Routine: Tuesdays are for content, about 4 hours a week on LinkedIn (about a day including other channels). An hour-long strategy call produces five post ideas with hooks and main points, then he writes them in a block of roughly 90 minutes later in the week.
- Channel roles: LinkedIn and Twitter for awareness, newsletter for trust. He estimates about 90% of the value comes from LinkedIn. He admits he is weak at capturing audience into the newsletter.
