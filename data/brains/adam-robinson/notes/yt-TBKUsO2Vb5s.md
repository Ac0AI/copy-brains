## Retention.com: from a stuck newsletter app to 13M ARR with six people

- Adam's first startup, Robbley Email Marketing, stalled at $3M ARR in a space Mailchimp dominated.
- He cites the "22 Immutable Laws of Marketing" idea that a player with over 60% share must be outspent about 3 to 1. His read: if 7 of 10 people in a room use a product, you use it too.
- He then spent about two years on catastrophic bets:
  - Rebuilding an entirely new product in a market with 10 vendors, without proper customer discovery. It cost a year and $1M, and he deleted the code.
  - Taking over an "authorized local expert" program from Constant Contact. The unit economics didn't work for a small startup, he shut it off, and Constant Contact sued him.
- The pivot came from a feature. He learned it was possible to identify an anonymous website visitor and tie them to a deliverable email, and white-labeled it inside the newsletter app.
- Customers loved the identity feature but wouldn't use the newsletter app. They downloaded the file and loaded it into Klaviyo. That was his product-market-fit signal.
- He spun the feature out as Get Emails (now Retention.com). On Nov 9, 2019 he launched with a Word Art video made on Upwork using DigitalMarketer's video-sales-letter template, recorded on an iPhone. He put $5,000 of ad spend behind it and reached $10K MRR in three weeks.
- Facebook ads gave "air cover": cold email replies said "I've seen the Facebook ads, I'll do a demo." The ads stopped working after about 6 months.
- Retention.com launched with 6 people and had the same 6 at $13M ARR:
  - Adam on marketing, plus a product and support pod with Tate (CTO) and a support/product teammate.
  - Diana on sales, with a sales assistant on every call and an onboarding person.

## The ICP, churn and defensibility mistakes before RB2B

- Get Emails was a high-churn PLG product. Adam didn't find an ICP until about 3.5 years in, and didn't realize ecommerce was the best fit until about 2 years in.
- The signal: big Shopify merchants kept sending friends, never asked to be paid, and never cancelled. So the team gave demos only to big Shopify merchants.
- His plan was to add features, get Klaviyo-like stickiness and expand revenue.
- Context: he had watched Jasper go from stuck to unicorn while renting a desk in their office, and thought he could do the same.
- He hired heavily, and every week his expectations rose.
- He misjudged the market. In Dec 2022 he thought there were 50,000 stores to sell a $50K deal in 7 days at an 80% close rate. In reality there were about 1,500 stores for a $20K deal in 30 days, at a still-high 60 to 70% close rate.
- Churn never got solved.
- Pricing mistake: he put customers paying $10K to $20K per month on $5K, planning to raise it to $10K the next year. By then six competitors were offering $2K deals, even though customer ROI was around 50x.
- Defensibility lesson: there was no network effect, and he oversold those customers.

## How LinkedIn content turned into the RB2B opportunity

- In July 2023 the VP of Sales quit. Adam cut the team from 20 reps to 4 and didn't replace the VP, believing outbound no longer worked and the brand was what pulled in demand.
- He wrote a post about it that got about 30x his normal engagement. People then asked "how's it going?" and he said it was "moving faster."
- A follow-up post on BDRs got hundreds of times more engagement, with thousands of likes.
- He took this to his co-founder and pitched pivoting his content brand from Shopify merchants to B2B SaaS, splitting 50/50. He kept tweeting for Shopify and wrote for LinkedIn on B2B.
- Test of the hypothesis: he wrote three more posts for B2B revenue leaders on three different topics. Each got 2,500 to 3,000 likes within 24 hours. Had they flopped, he would have concluded he didn't understand LinkedIn.
- Santosh, a B2B veteran (Apollo, ZoomInfo), had pushed him for a long time to build a B2B product. The first attempt in 2022 had failed. It was personal emails for business use, and Adam removed it from the site.
- Adam started posting about account-based marketing (ABM), 6sense and demand-based marketing, with a footer line along the lines of "DM me if you want to close the loop on ABM and get website identity at the person level." Hundreds of people wrote in.
- Between Oct 2023 and Mar 2024 (launch), he and Santosh ran about 300 discovery calls. They built a 2,000 to 3,000 person waitlist.
- He calls this "the most unfair marketing advantage you could possibly have." The reach was bigger than the unicorns in the space, and it was exactly the people they wanted to sell to, before there was a product.

## Discovery and validating RB2B before and while building

- Discovery was aimed at ABM practitioners, mostly senior marketers. The questions were about the existing workflow:
  - How are you using website identity right now?
  - How does that information get to your reps?
  - What do they complain about?
- Every time, people said they wanted to know who the person was. He treats the complaints about the current process as the validation, not "do you like this product."
- Early test: they mailed spreadsheets of data and tried to charge $495 a month. Nobody bought it, though a couple of people said they'd pay $99.
- They then pushed the data into Slack. Adding an actual headshot and LinkedIn URL created a "magic moment." Adam would pull out his phone at parties and say "these people are on my website right now."
- He had planned to charge for integrations. His CTO Tate, normally the one who wants to charge for everything, argued the Slack integration should be free if he wanted it to go viral, because that was the magic moment.
- Takeaway: free doesn't mean giving away everything. Give away the part that makes people feel the value.
- V1 was a Slack integration, a PLG signup flow that worked end to end, and a basic dashboard. He knew it was differentiated because it was first to market.

## RB2B pricing: what failed and what worked

- Inspiration: Built With, which he calls the coolest product pricing (about one person to roughly $20M ARR). His first idea was free unlimited, then $495 with integrations.
- It confused buyers (why pay $495 if $295 works?), and the jump was too big. People could also back-door the contacts through Zapier or just parse the Slack block, so they didn't need the paid integrations.
- Adam posted about his pricing struggles publicly. That made buyers wait for the price to drop. It still dragged on for about 6 weeks.
- What worked: a $99 entry point, credit-based. The final shape:
  - Free tier with the Slack integration.
  - Contacts capped at 150 before paying.
- Free-to-paid conversion has been about 10 to 12% ever since.
- He dislikes credit-based pricing but doesn't know how to do value-based pricing here. The product is most valuable for companies with high-value deals, who generally have low traffic. A company selling a $19 product with huge traffic pays a lot and gets less value. Someone with 100 uniques a month selling $10M deals pays little and gets enormous value.
- Lesson: if the jump from free to paid is too big, few people jump. If there's no usage limit or key feature that makes them want to, it's hard to pull off.

## Channels, transparency and moats

- Channels at RB2B: LinkedIn organic (his estimate is about 95% of the result), cold email ("pirate" style, multiple domains), LinkedIn ads promoting thought-leadership posts, and a UGC program.
- His view on the extras: he couldn't tell whether cold email mattered, and the ads were not clearly better than organic. He cut them at the end of 2024.
- UGC program:
  - Pete cultivated a community of Clay agency people who posted regularly.
  - They benefited from the exposure.
  - Adam would feature one of their posts in his own feed.
- He added his co-founder Santosh as a character in the content, and planned to add Diana (sales co-founder, the only rep on two $0 to $10M journeys) for the sales-facing V2.
- Pause on growth: he stopped growth spending at RB2B until the product is fixed. He says he sees deep flaws, such as an expected 10-month LTV, and doesn't want to burn leads. He says he can afford that clarity because the company is profitable. A venture-backed company would be told to 10x and ignore churn.
- Why he builds in public:
  - The product category triggers red flags (how is that legal?), so total transparency builds trust.
  - He would have wanted this information himself when stuck at $3M.
  - Money details spread on social media.
- RB2B metrics are public at rb2b.bearmetrics.com.
- Downside: competitors. One person copied his content playbook and added AI, aggregating identity tools into an AI SDR. Adam says it won't work. He thinks competitors will exist regardless, and a copycat only takes a small share from the trusted, best-known brand.
- Moats he lists:
  - Founder brand.
  - Distribution.
  - Data. The plan is a premium product that sits on top of a salesperson's inbox, with hundreds of thousands of users opting into a data co-op. Examples: "did this contact sit in a sales demo in the last 7 days" (5 out of 5), "responds to email at this address" (3 out of 5), unknown (1 out of 5).
- Honest take: if the next version has no network effects, RB2B might add roughly another $5M ARR. If it's stickier, maybe $10M to $15M.
- If RB2B reaches $20M ARR with his lean approach, he calls that doing great.

## Staying small and removing sales friction

- Adam's rule: don't hire until the company is above 10 people. He wants to reach $10M ARR on the current team (RB2B reached $5M ARR with five people).
- Small teams force focus. People wear many hats, and there's no one inventing goals.
- Owner-operator reality: he says it isn't realistic to be the owner who lets operators run everything. He enjoys doing a lot himself, like rewriting all the website copy in a day rather than coordinating five team members.
- Sales and onboarding friction:
  - RB2B: free trial, no credit card, no payment. The trial started as free forever, then 30 days, then 7 days. People get the full value first, and calls happen only when they have questions. He is still unsure where sales fits.
  - Get Emails: 25 emails for free. Higher-traffic accounts were locked until they took a roughly 5-minute call with Diana, who answered the same questions every customer asked. She then put them on a 60-day paid trial at a smaller volume than they needed, and they either cancelled or upgraded. She could do about 15 of these a day.
- Reference point he mentions: UserFlow with three people sold for $60M, so a small team doesn't hurt exit potential.
