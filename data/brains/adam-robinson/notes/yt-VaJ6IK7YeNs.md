## The "elite founder gap" and why order matters

- Adam says the average founder has under a 10% chance of success, while elite founders succeed over 50% of the time.
- He calls the difference "the elite founder gap". Elite founders take the same steps as average founders but in a different order.
- Average founder: idea, build too much product, then hunt for buyers with one-to-many growth hacking.
- Elite founder: find buyers before building, build almost nothing, skip growth hacking, and do gritty one-to-one work.
- Growth hacking before product market fit (PMF) either fails or "works just enough" to give false hope.
- His own proof: Robly was built for 18 months with zero customer conversations and modeled 800 paying customers at $15/month in month one. It got 10. It survived only because of a lucky growth hack (crawling the web for competitors' signup widgets, then offering "50% more opens for half the price"). It topped out at $3M ARR and shrank about 0.5% per month for years.
- Later, Robly spent over $1M and a year building a Mailchimp-upmarket app without talking to anyone. At a trade show "people's eyes glazed over" because about 10 vendors already filled the gap, and the code was deleted.

## Sell before you build: the Barkbox lesson and RB2B/Retention.com origin

- Adam learned the approach from Henrik, co-founder of Barkbox. Henrik showed a photoshopped mockup on his iPhone in Washington Square Park and sold about 50 $30 monthly subscriptions via Square, with no website, product, employees or office. Barkbox later had 100,000+ monthly subscribers at $30, versus Robly's 2,500 after three years.
- Three reasons not to build first:
  - Human intuition is a poor indicator of market viability.
  - People are polite and won't be honest. "The single best way to figure out if someone actually likes your idea is get them to hand you money." Even $1 is a strong signal.
  - Software is slow and expensive to build.
- Retention.com origin: after the failed trade show, he told 100 booth visitors "What if I could get you email addresses of people on your website who didn't even fill out a form." All 100 perked up. He took business cards and, for six months, still didn't know how to build the tech.
- 12 months later the product existed. Beta testers came from those cards and Robly's customer base. It reached $1M ARR in 27 weeks and about $22M ARR bootstrapped four years later.

## The four-step 0 to $1M framework and MVP rules

1. Attention grab and pre-sale.
2. MVP and customer feedback loop.
3. Do things that don't scale.
4. Grow the right way, funded by customers rather than investors.

Early adopter flow:
- Use a sizzling one-liner to get a 15-minute call, then a 5-minute quick pitch, then an offer to get them to pay something.
- If they won't pay, get them to commit to regular feedback in exchange for free access.
- Get 10 to 20 committed users before building.
- Alina Vanderberg (Chili Piper) went to every B2B meetup in NYC, found the most connected people, and showed a notepad drawing asking if they'd buy it and what would have to change.
- Questions to ask:
  - Do you understand the problem?
  - Do you have the problem?
  - How do you solve it today?
  - Would you pay for it as described if I delivered tomorrow?
  - What else would it need to do?
  - Would you pay $100/month?

MVP rules:
- Build as little as possible. Do things manually. RB2B onboarded beta testers by hand for five months before having a website: he emailed a pixel and a daily spreadsheet. The team ran 300+ discovery calls between October 2023 and the March 2024 launch.
- Pick the smallest niche. ConvertKit (about $40M ARR bootstrapped, per Adam) launched for food bloggers, won the top ones, then expanded to all creators.
- Run every onboarding call yourself. Then automate a feedback email sequence: use AI to write 4 to 6 variants of "just checking in, how's it going, are you seeing X value?" and schedule them over 8 to 12 weeks via Boomerang, GMass or a mail merge from Gmail.
- Prioritize features in a Google Sheet every two weeks, with columns for value of the requesting users, request frequency, and estimated build time.
- Watch for "magic moments": overjoyed reactions, people trying to pay for something unfinished, desperation when it goes down. RB2B users said "This feels like I'm cheating."
- Test: "If I took my product away from my users, how upset would they get?"
- Target 25 to 50 paying users in this phase. Get them by referrals (best signal), posting in public, podcasts (matchmaker.fm), commenting on influencers' posts without pitching, LinkedIn advice requests, events, and intros from friends and investors.

## Product market fit rules

- Do things that don't scale until you have PMF. Before PMF, no ads, SEO, influencers, UGC, partnerships, affiliates, Product Hunt or sponsored newsletters. "Nothing will work if you don't have product market fit. Everything will work if you do."
- Signs of PMF:
  - Strong word of mouth and high retention (exponential organic growth with no effort).
  - Customers who love it despite bad UX (Retention.com began as an identity feature inside Robly; users exported it to Clay and said they couldn't live without it).
  - People prepaying for something that doesn't exist.
  - The market is pulling the product out of you.
  - Customers would be livid if you removed it, and cash is piling up.
- Become ramen profitable so you control your own destiny and don't need investors.
- Be different in a way that matters. "Same but cheaper" gets only 3 to 5% of someone else's business. You need to be 10x better or 10x cheaper, ideally better, cheaper and really different. "Same but free" works only with a premium tier that funds it (his Mailchimp example: a $12B acquisition with no outside funding). Features for a niche that a big incumbent can't copy also work.
- If you already have $250K to $500K ARR but no PMF, restart as if you had zero customers: let prospects, not intuition, define what's valuable, and reintroduce yourself to customers as someone focused on their biggest problems.

## Sizzling one-liner and quick pitch templates

One-liner: use DigitalMarketer's before-and-after worksheet. Answer what they have, what they feel, what their day looks like, and whether their status changes (e.g. Google certified agency, Inc. 5000, G2 high performer).

- RB2B before: company-level visitor ID ("someone from Microsoft in Atlanta"), reps guessing and cold emailing. After: person-level identification. One-liner: "RB2B: person-level website visitor identity." It opened 300+ discovery calls and is still the site headline.
- Make it clear, concise and differentiated. Never "like competitor X but cheaper."

Quick pitch (four slides, under five minutes):
1. Problem statement, via "It sucks that ___" (e.g. B2B companies can't identify visitors at the person level).
2. Introduction, borrowing the one-liner.
3. The story, one paragraph, memorized, with an image or two instead of words.
4. Solution and offer. Anyone paying before it exists is a strong signal. Offer a huge discount, e.g. RB2B's pitch: pricing starts at $5,000/year, but the first 10 beta testers get unlimited access for $500 for 12 months. Fallback: free for 90 days in exchange for regular, extensive feedback.

More calls make the pitch better and show whether to pursue the idea.

## Finding your first 10 conversations

Define a tight ICP: "Anybody is the worst answer. Men or women is the second worst. Developers who live in Seattle is a great answer." Outreach works at least 10x better with an intro or an existing relationship (a reason to build a personal brand).

- LinkedIn DMs: ask your network for 15 minutes of feedback on "[one-liner]" built for "[job/industry]". Ask mutual connections for intros. For strangers (director level and below), offer an interview for a paid advisor role. He found his co-founder Diana Ross with that template.
- Cold email (Andy Mewborn's "gift card play"): inbox rotation via Instantly, two emails. Email one asks for feedback and credibility with numbers, with a PS offering a $100 gift card. Email two has a blank subject and says "if you aren't interested, you can tell me to go kick rocks." Reported result: 44 booked demos in 30 days.
- Industry events (the Alina approach), expert networks (Clarity, MentorPass, MentorLoop, around $500+ per call; ask experts for referrals), and professional communities (Facebook, WhatsApp, Slack). Seek feedback, don't sell.

## Growing from $0 to $1M with customer funding and no hires

- Hot take: hire nobody before $1M ARR. Co-founders only: one builds, one does sales, marketing and success. Examples: Klaviyo's two co-founders hired no one until past $1M ARR (they IPO'd in 2023 at a $9B+ valuation per Adam); Jolie reached a $50M run rate with three employees.
- Recover customer acquisition cost (CAC) fast. Russell Brunson spent $3,000 on webinar traffic and sold six-month ClickFunnels subscriptions bundled with training, reaping about $50,000 upfront per week. Adam ran Facebook ads for Retention.com in 2020: poor long-term customers, but a $5,000/month whale every week or two paid back spend, and the awareness helped cold email and word of mouth.
- Run cheap experiments to find 2 to 3 channels for $1M to $10M, favoring zero or low upfront cash. Double down on the leader.
- Inbound-led outbound: drive traffic, identify visitors with RB2B, filter by ICP, then LinkedIn connect or warm email.
- Talk to every churned customer; require a conversation before cancelling, while making clear cancellation is easy. Adam would not offer an incentive.
- Give early adopters excellent support. If they have large relevant audiences, give them the product free.
- After $1M, hire 1 to 2 people (support, or a sales rep if demos eat your time). Goal: $10M ARR with no more than 10 employees.
- Seven founder mistakes:
  - Overspending on offices, VAs, interns, equipment or a CRM (his team used Google Sheets until $10M ARR).
  - Hiring before $1M.
  - Copying vendors but cheaper.
  - Being an "ideas guy" waiting for an operator.
  - Not doing unscalable things.
  - Not posting daily on organic social (it's to find your voice, not get reach at first).
  - Thinking it's a quick exit.

## Why bootstrap to $10M ARR with six people

- Adam's thesis: bootstrapping to $10M ARR is the one goal, because afterward you can sell, hire a CEO, or keep paying yourself millions.
- Retention.com went 1 to $10M ARR in 27 months with six people; he says it hit about $12M ARR with six employees (about $2M revenue per employee).
- The mistake: in October 2022 he hired about 50 people in under 60 days, thinking he had a unicorn. Revenue per employee collapsed, the outsourced go-to-market playbook failed, the VP of Sales quit after about six months, 16 of 20 salespeople were let go, and he became "the most hated CEO on Glassdoor." His own conclusion: "Adding more people... does not speed you up."
- He disagrees with Jason Lemkin's benchmark of about 50 employees at $10M ARR; he says 10 max, ideally 6.
- Roger Bannister analogy: once someone proves it's possible, others follow. Let "$10M ARR with six people" be the four-minute mile.
- Founder time: in a six-person team, the week is customers, selling, prioritizing with the CTO, demand creation and some support, all spent improving product and go-to-market (he and Diana went home at 5 p.m. and skipped weekends). At 25 people, a founder loses about 60% of the week to internal meetings, interviews and investors.
- Metrics he tracks: ARR per employee, monthly growth rate, CAC payback (as low as possible, even immediate), churn, profitability.
- Why $10M bootstrapped beats VC:
  - Two co-founders can each clear $1M+ per year.
  - Sale at roughly 4 to 5x revenue ($40 to $50M) to many buyers.
  - Option to hire a CEO.
  - Option to keep working.
  - Option to raise later with more leverage and less dilution.
  - Option to reach $25M ARR with 25 people and pay yourself $10 to $15M a year.
- His case against VC: "imaginary wealth effect" valuations, go-to-market "help" that burns cash, and "VCs are not your friend." He argues growth is worth 3 to 10x profitability only if you reach an exit, so discount that multiple by 80 to 90%. His advice: talk to no investors until $10M ARR.

## Inbound-led outbound: replacing SDR-led pipeline

- Outbound is "dead" because buyer demand for subscription software is cyclically low, more sellers chase the same prospects, and AI-volume outbound is countered by spam filters.
- At Retention.com, going from 1 to 5 SDRs still booked one meeting a week. His lesson: "These SDRs didn't create demand. They were only capturing demand created elsewhere."
- Four steps:
  1. Drive traffic through LinkedIn and social, podcasts, partnerships, events, SEO, or wherever buyers are.
  2. Identify website visitors at the person level with RB2B.
  3. Retarget first-time and repeat visitors by email, LinkedIn and phone.
  4. Focus sales (about 90% fewer resources) on bottom-of-funnel buyers.
- Claimed results: ultra-low CAC, 50% higher close rates, two-thirds shorter sales cycles, and more deals closed by 10 to 20% of reps.
- Key principle: scale unmeasurable original demand sources (podcast guest spots, events, organic social posts, others posting about you) even though you can't attribute them. Don't scale only what you can measure, as he did with BDRs.
- Demand-creation tactics: organic LinkedIn posting/connecting/commenting; thought-leader ads behind best posts; matchmaker.fm podcast spots; incentivized UGC; influencer campaigns coming to B2B; newsletters and joint-venture webinars; weekly live events (he reports 600 to 1,000 signups and 200 to 350 attendees, with 66% repeat visitors); partnerships; and a LinkedIn Live event titled with the buyer's pain with $10/day ads, then Phantom Buster to pull attendee contacts.
- Pick 2 to 3 channels and go all-in.

## Getting unstuck between $1M and $10M ARR

- Stuck means a PMF problem. Fix: stay or become very lean, iterate fast, and stay close to customers. Don't raise money to get unstuck. If you're already too big, cut the team to the necessary functions.
- Strategy 1, go all-in on your lowest-churn segment: segment customers by persona, find the lowest-churn group, define it as the new ICP, and stop giving demos to anyone else. Turn down off-ICP revenue and product requests. This "took retention.com from 12 million to 23 million ARR"; the segment was Shopify Plus stores.
- Strategy 2, solve broad churn (when top-of-funnel and close rates are fine but churn is bad):
  - Talk to every churned customer personally. Alina Vanderberg (Chili Piper) emails each: "I would really, really, really love to know what you hate about our product and why you churned."
  - Questions: why did you churn, how did you use it, did you replace it or just stop, would cheaper keep you, which features would keep you, how often did you use it.
  - Explore adjacent features. Clearbit told him a three-feature customer churned 75% less than a one-feature customer.
  - Move upmarket to larger deals, which churn less.
- Strategy 3, run bold, low-long-term-cost experiments on pricing, features or new segments (e.g. the rebrand to Retention.com and adding features for Shopify Plus that upmarket vendors already had).

## Hiring principles for a team under 10

- Co-founder split: the business co-founder owns product plus sales or marketing until $10M; the technical co-founder builds and talks to customers. If you lack a technical co-founder, hire one engineer.
- Principles:
  1. Hire people, not teams. A management layer cuts velocity by up to 75%.
  2. Hire experienced individual contributors (5+ years) in areas to offload. Look overseas (Eastern Europe, Brazil, Colombia, Argentina; Canada is about 30% cheaper). Wellfound is a source.
  3. Hire with LinkedIn job ads, not expensive recruiters. Post about hiring in organic content and put a hiring link in the email footer.
  4. Use contractors and agencies for non-core work.
  5. "Hire unreasonably slow, fire unreasonably fast." Hire only when it hurts; fire once you're sure. Be definitive, take the blame for the mishire, be kind, and try to find them another role.
- Support: hire a support or success rep to answer tickets and improve self-serve docs. A salesperson with a sales assistant for admin and follow-up is more productive.
- Retention.com's $12M ARR org: Adam, Diana Ross (sales) with a sales assistant, Clint (onboarding), Tate (built everything), and Anne (support and product direction input).
