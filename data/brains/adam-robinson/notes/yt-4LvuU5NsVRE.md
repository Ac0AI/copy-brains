## Why Adam says founder-led LinkedIn content is no longer optional

- He argues outbound as it was a few years ago is dying: response rates are collapsing, spam filters keep tightening, and personalization only works if a human actually reads the message.
- Predictable-revenue style sales (target lists, Lakers-game dinners) will still exist, but it is not efficient. It is not what the last decade's startups grew on.
- His stance: "I don't think it's an option anymore." You either build a founder brand around yourself (his preferred route) or harness organic social the way Clay does, by aligning incentives with a tight group of users who post about the product.
- People are on social media to connect with people, not companies. You can build affinity around a person in about 1/100th of the time and money it takes to build it around a brand (his example: what Drift spent in 2017 to make a word and a robot matter).
- He would pick "the ability to be great at creating organic social content over any other marketing strategy by far," and for B2B that means LinkedIn.
- He expects products to change to fit the distribution available: founder-led, free, a point solution you can understand in one second, which spreads on its own as outreach gets harder.

## How he sells to mid-market and enterprise with it, and how long it takes

- His motion, learned from Alec Paul (who runs this for his clients' CEOs):
  1. Early on: outbound connection campaign to your ICP, they accept, you post great content, wait about 60 days, then ask for a demo. He claims about 80% conversion on those demos because prospects see you as a celebrity.
  2. Once big enough: inbound connection requests, accept ICP visitors, ask what caught their attention, post for 60 days, go in for a demo.
- Connecting with about 10 ICP people a day for a year to a year and a half gets you connected to most of the people you want. You do not need 100,000 followers; a slow, steady inbound flow compounds.
- Timing: it took him about a year. The click came around Labor Day 2023, when he understood hot takes, sharing weakness, and naming things people fear to say (for example, "it's okay to admit that outbound is dead, it's not your fault").
- Niche fit: he says two non-martech CEOs on a Dave Gerhardt panel (a parental-leave SaaS and Bridge, an online/offline data company) got about 80% of lead flow from founder-brand LinkedIn content. He concedes it likely works better today for sales/marketing tech, but more industries are arriving on the platform.
- Even if it does not book demos at first, he says committing to posting until you find your voice "will open up doors," because you are building a character people care about, not hitting content pillars nobody cares about.
- Why VC-backed companies struggle: it is not repeatable on a timeline, so investors would say hire a VP of Sales instead. He sees that as the opportunity for bootstrapped founders willing to commit.
- Founders need founder-market fit.

## Building the audience: connections, followers, and the early stage

- Follower-boosting tricks (e.g. "comment interested and I'll send the top 15,000 ads"): helpful early because follower count signals credibility, less important later when you care about who the ICP is.
- Over time, prune off-ICP connections (for example engineers if you now sell to sales), but not at the very start. Better: run an outbound connection campaign to the ICP and trim the lowest-quality connections as people accept.
- He used LinkedHelper for outbound connection campaigns once; now Alec Paul and Kyle Williams handle triage of inbound, and they no longer run outbound.
- Engagement pods: he used one at the start (he had under 1,000 followers in September 2022) and thinks it helped a bit, but the durable answer is "write a great hook, write a great body, write a great close, and drop it."
- First fans: connect with and comment on influencers in your space, 10 connections a day, write thoughtful comments, and answer every comment on your own posts. He dislikes AI-generated comments that just summarize his post and ask a question.
- "The best time to plant a tree was 20 years ago, the second best time is today."

## Format and cadence choices

- Format: he is a video guy. It has less reach but a deeper connection.
- Cadence: once a day, five days a week is right when starting. He had dropped to three a week and is trying to post more to lift organic impressions. He notes Andy Mowat posts twice a day, but Adam still favors quality over quantity.
- Timing: Alec posts his content around 3-4 pm Eastern, weekday or weekend; Adam does not challenge it.
- Channel choice: if he had to pick one channel for life it would be LinkedIn for B2B, because it builds an audience and community around him. Email can't create that kind of connection.
- UGC (the Clay model) is one layer more complex than founder content because you are wrangling people you do not control, and it only works if the product fits their workflow. He says it works for RB2B for similar reasons.
- Repurposing one idea into text, image and carousel versions can work, but he thinks the real driver is consistently reinforcing one identity in the market. He points to Chris Walker, who has talked about attribution for five years in ways that never read as repetitive.
- Switching audiences means starting over. He cites Devin Reed moving from writing for sales to writing for marketing.

## How he writes hooks and chooses what to say

- His best-performing generic business posts (10,000+ likes, for example on not micromanaging employees) did nothing for RB2B. He compares followers earned that way to "being rich with Monopoly money." He chose niche value over reach, citing Sam Jacobs's feel as what he wanted his brand to be.
- Hooks are the most important part. In his weekly 45-minute Monday idea session with Alec, they discuss post ideas and hooks first; ideas with weak hooks do not get written.
- Hook rules he gives:
  - Treat the reader as if they have never heard of you and build as much credibility as possible in the hook (for example "I bootstrapped...").
  - Cite conversations with important people, even anonymous ones ("I spoke to a CTO who has to cut his dev staff by 80%..."). Anonymous can feel more mysterious than naming someone, unless they are famous.
  - Voice what people say privately but are afraid to say publicly.
  - Stack numbers (time period, dollar amount, customer count, days) in one hook.
  - Be definitive: "the golden era of SaaS is over," or "99% of startups think they should focus on reducing churn, they're wrong."
  - He reads Chris Walker's posts before writing his own, such as "X in 60 seconds" style hooks.
- Weekly planning covers: what worked or didn't last week, conversations he's had, work-in-public updates, and which of his three audiences (marketers, sales, founders) he has not hit lately.
- Polarizing views work only if you actually believe them. He holds that falling response rates mean software floors rise (for example from 25k to 50k ACV and beyond), so outbound-heavy models break. He says LinkedIn comments are mostly respectful, so backlash is manageable.

## Work in public and AI

- Until RB2B, his work-in-public was about one monthly update; the rest was market takes and conversations. A report he was shown said founders care about work-in-public, while sales and marketing audiences prefer predictable-revenue style content.
- He writes about milestones the day they happen: the idea, the launch, $1M ARR at 16 weeks, and $2M. He calls it "good journalism." Typical work-in-public posts do worse than a strong bootstrap vs VC or "predictable revenue is dead" post.
- He is adding SEO, a UGC motion, ads, and a Russell Brunson-style one-to-many "how to bootstrap to $10M, plus RB2B" motion, which gives him more to write about.
- On AI: he writes all his own posts. "Art is interesting because of the perspective of the artist," and ChatGPT has no perspective. He wants typos, emotion, and original experience, and thinks AI is better for repetitive mechanical jobs like customer support.

## LinkedIn thought leadership ads: spend logic and numbers

- Approach: post organically, see what crushes, then put money only behind posts the platform already likes. He suspects better ad fit lowers bid costs.
- Test: spent $10,000 in the first month.
- Numbers he gave: click-through to free signup costs about $70, with a 10% conversion rate, so roughly $700 per paying customer. Average subscription is $275 and conservative LTV is about $2,700 (customers stay about 10 months). He notes this metric is the worst one, since the program isn't optimized for it.
- He argues the ads also boost brand impressions and trust beyond signups, amplifying a program already working.
- Plan: double spend each month until the signup cost metric "shits the bed" (he'd back off if it rose to around $150 per signup) rather than fixating on a signup target. LinkedIn ad contacts told him $10K is low.
