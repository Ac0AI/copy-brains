## Discovery before building: 50 calls and real prepayment

- Adam says most founders fail because they "fall in love with their idea instead of their customers' problems." They build for 12 to 18 months before talking to anyone and confuse building something with building something people want.
- He cites that 90% of startups never reach $1M ARR.
- Before writing code, he commits to at least 50 discovery calls. The goal is to find people who will pay for something not yet built. He means actual prepayment, not "Yeah, that sounds interesting."
- His favorite example is BarkBox. Matt Meeker owned a Great Dane in NYC, where stores sold tiny toys for tiny dogs (2010, pre-Amazon Prime). He took a Photoshop mockup of a monthly delivery of five large toys for $29 to Washington Square Park and swiped cards on a Square reader on his phone. He got 50 purchases on day one, before he had a website.

## Getting the first discovery calls

- Start with your personal network on LinkedIn and other social platforms, targeting people who match your ideal customer profile (ICP).
- He shares Andy Mowat's outreach message for Distribute: "I was at Outreach.io and now I'm building a HubSpot and Seismic competitor, three times cheaper and 10 times more user-friendly, and we currently have around 4,000 active users that love us. Would you be open to giving honest feedback about the product I'm building?" Andy offers a $100 gift card for their time. Adam says this still works.
- Another tactic he has used: offer paid advisory roles to people who don't make much money (director level and below) but are still in the ICP.
- The "Alina Vanderberg approach" (Chili Piper, now around $40M ARR): go to industry events with a notepad sketch of the product vision and ask influential people, "Would you buy something like this? And if not, what would we have to change for you to buy it?"
- To spot the most influential person in a room, watch who people walk toward to hug. The recipient of the hug is who Alina talks to.

## What to say on discovery calls

- Lead with a "sizzling one-liner" that makes eyes light up. For RB2B it was "person level website visitor identification," because before that only company-level identification existed.
- Questions he asks:
  1. Do you understand the problem I'm trying to solve?
  2. Do you have the problem?
  3. How are you solving it today?
  4. Would you pay for the solution I'm describing if I could get it to you tomorrow?
  5. What else would the solution need for you to pay for it?
  6. I'm thinking of charging X per month, would you pay that?
- The goal is to understand their pain so deeply that you can solve it better than anyone.

## Build the minimum product and stay manual

- Build only what is needed to deliver the core value proposition. If it takes more than 4 to 6 weeks, "you're probably building too much."
- Anything that could be done manually should be done manually first. At RB2B, they sent spreadsheets over email manually for five full months before putting up a website.
- Why manual is an "unfair advantage":
  - You stay close to customers and learn things automation would hide.
  - You iterate about 10 times faster than competitors trying to automate everything.
  - You reach the market months earlier and get real feedback.

## Product-market fit signals and inbound velocity

- Three signals of product-market fit, and he says you need all three:
  1. At least one referral per week from paying customers.
  2. Customers would be genuinely upset if you took the product away.
  3. Organic growth without any marketing.
- Until you have these, do nothing except pursue product-market fit, "because nothing you do will work." He equates PMF with strong word of mouth: people sharing and bragging about the product unprompted, prospects saying "I heard about you from X," and customers creating content that drives signups.
- Inbound velocity (a steady daily flow of prospects) is not required to move forward but makes everything easier. Start by posting on LinkedIn from day one, sharing the wins, failures and pivots in real time.
- Signs it is working: website visits without outreach, inbound connection requests from your ICP, and prospects DMing about the product.
- Example: he built in public on LinkedIn for months before launching RB2B. At launch they had a 1,600-person waitlist and 300 discovery meetings booked with high-value customers, and hit $1M ARR in 6 weeks post-launch.

## The "magic triangle" and founder-led LinkedIn content

- Phase two is the magic triangle: content, outreach and retargeting. Each feeds the other two. Content drives awareness and traffic, outreach converts warm visitors, and retargeting brings back people who weren't ready.
- Only activate it after product-market fit (at least one referral per week). Doing it earlier wastes money scaling something that doesn't work.
- Why all three are needed:
  - Content alone is too slow ("you can't wait 2 years to build an audience," but start building today).
  - Outreach alone converts terribly; "cold email alone is essentially dead."
  - Retargeting alone generates no new pipeline.
- LinkedIn is the foundation. Personal profiles grow about 10 times faster than company pages, and the human connection works like free ads.
- Founder content strategy:
  - Post at least three times a week.
  - Pick three pillars. His are building in public with real numbers, the future of his industry (a polarizing stance on go-to-market), and contrarian takes (bootstrapping vs VC, where he advocates taking as little money as possible).
  - Follow the "three C's": be clear about who you are, consistent, and keep reinforcing the story.
  - Share failures as well as wins. Authenticity is the edge because big companies have to sound like corporate brands.

## Outreach: email, LinkedIn and website visitors

- Do two types of outreach: cold outreach only to ICP people, and follow-up with people who visited your website. For the cold list, they use Apollo to build a list of founders and marketers at SaaS companies with 5 to 200 employees, reaching out to three people per company. For website visitors, he built RB2B to identify them at person level (name, job title, LinkedIn, business email).
- Email infrastructure:
  - Use Instantly or Smartlead (they switch between them depending on what works).
  - Rotate hundreds of domains (e.g. tryyourproduct.com, getyourproduct.com).
  - Two inboxes per domain, sending 2 to 10 emails per day per inbox, to get past Gmail and Outlook spam filters.
  - Validate every address with NeverBounce and LeadMagic.
  - Copy should be clear and concise, state the problem you solve, and end with a clear call to action.
- LinkedIn connection requests:
  - Use the same ICP list plus RB2B visitors. Start at 10 to 20 per day, working up to at most 50.
  - Send requests with no message. Acceptance is high, and near 100% for website visitors. It rises as your audience grows.
  - Be careful with automation: he has seen warnings, and you can get permanently banned. He names HeyReach and Lemlist-style tools (he says "Log Growth Machine," i.e. La Growth Machine) for automation.
- LinkedIn follow-up:
  - Wait for them to like or comment on your content, then DM: "Hey, [first name]. Thanks for the like or comment. How are things going at [company]?" Response rate is usually north of 60%.
  - If there is no engagement after 60 days, send a soft touch such as "What caught your attention?" followed by "How are things going at [company]?"
  - Don't pitch in the first message. "You're not actually selling. You're building rapport so that you can get someone on a call to sell." Never sell on LinkedIn.
- Combining email and LinkedIn hits the same prospects on two channels, and some people respond better on one than the other.

## Retargeting as the multiplier

- Install pixels from LinkedIn, Meta, Google and Twitter on the website.
- Build custom audiences from website visitors, lookalike audiences from paying customers and prospect lists, and matched-audience ads that go straight to prospect lists.
- Ad creative uses Russell Brunson's who/what/why/how framework: who are you, what do you have, why do they need it, how can they get it. Keep it authentic. Most of the time it is a selfie video from his phone, and phone-recorded content performs best.
