## RB2B's growth model: one-to-many "edutainment" instead of a sales team

- Adam says he sells through a one-to-many awareness engine: 80% of his time goes to LinkedIn posting (about four days a week) plus roughly 10 podcast guest slots a week.
- He says he'll do any show that isn't a first episode, because it is "the most effective awareness you could get today."
- He calls this the future of B2B selling: "very long sort of like influencer, thought leadership that just slowly brings people down the funnel" and builds an audience you can do a lot with.
- RB2B went from $1MM to $2MM ARR in six weeks. He says it is the first time that has happened to him.
- He believes CEOs are about 10 years away from accepting this is how they should spend their time. His line: "business is doing what no one else is doing."
- He says there is "a tremendous arbitrage" in organic social, because it delivers an unbelievable amount of one-to-many selling.
- Organic social is already proven in direct-to-consumer (he cites the Kardashians) but hasn't reached software yet. B2B has less margin, so it has to be more efficient.
- His view of the platforms: they are built for people to absorb entertainment, so you are a media brand and must choose which one.
- He cannot measure his own performance in this job and says the individual pieces are not measurable. He spends on expensive consultants and a docu series without knowing what the series does, and treats it as the way the future plays out.

## How Adam found his voice: pivoting content from Shopify owners to SaaS founders

- His first company stalled at $3MM ARR. Retention.com started as a feature of it, because people used the feature and ignored the rest of the email platform.
- Retention.com bootstrapped to roughly $22MM ARR with six people, but with high churn.
- After narrowing to one ideal customer profile, he treated low penetration as an awareness problem. He chose a personal LinkedIn profile as the cheapest route to awareness, even though it overlapped imperfectly with Shopify store owners. LinkedIn had a better competitive dynamic for a content creator than Twitter, which he called harder.
- After nine months the content stopped winning e-commerce deals.
- Over Labor Day weekend (2023) he switched to writing for people like himself: SaaS founders feeling the outbound pain everyone had that year.
- His typical post got about 50 likes. The BDR posts, about getting rid of BDRs, VP Sales quitting and saying what nobody would say in public, got about 3,000 likes each.
- He then built a product (RB2B) for revenue teams, since the audience mechanism was so strong.
- Lesson he draws: write to people who are basically you and feel your pain at the same time you do.

## Content strategy: edutainment, vulnerability and saying what others won't

- He says he is in the "edutainment business" and happens to sell SaaS at the bottom of the funnel. He competes with rivals on edutainment, not features.
- Competitors copy "working in public" and fail. He says they tell stories poorly and aren't as credible as someone who bootstrapped to $22MM ARR in four years, then launched a new company and put a live metrics dashboard in public.
- Great content, in his view, is saying what people feel but won't admit to themselves.
- He posts about laying off 40% of the staff of one business. His chief of staff said it would make the managers' jobs impossible. His position is that it's an opportunity: with no product-market fit, "you have to get as small as possible and find a new one."
- He argues the team should know the true market characteristics (low deal sizes), so they stop asking for features and spend appropriately.
- He expects pushback on this from clients' CEOs and says almost none will do it.
- Being honest about errors builds a tribe, and he says people will love you more when things work again.
- He says it is hard to teach: you will be "bruised and beaten for a very long time," and it feels irrational to commit that time.
- Most people quit after seven engagements per post. He compares finding content-market fit to finding product-market fit; it takes a long time.

## Product as marketing: freemium and the "perception of free"

- He says every big product is both a killer product and killer marketing.
- Last year's outbound pain led him to ask: "what if response rates were zero, how would you sell software?"
- His answer was an influencer edutainment channel, plus a big freemium offer so getting started has zero friction.
- The aim is to create a market perception that the product is free, so it distributes on its own and doesn't need to be sold.
- Admitted weaknesses: it is a point solution in a high-churn category with a high-churn buyer persona. He says it could top out around $15MM ARR but would be very lean.
- RB2B has a PLG motion, so the website captures demand and no people are needed for that.

## Why Adam thinks the sales-team model is breaking

- He contrasts the venture model (hire a bunch of people, build features, charge more, churn) with a lean alternative he thinks works now.
- He argues "the VC way has become a whole lot less effective." Exits are low probability and founders end up as "underpaid salaried" workers.
- Many SaaS companies have fallen out of product-market fit. They can't switch to efficiency mode because their valuations require growth.
- On outbound: scaling reps and messages without scaling "the warming machine above it" fails. He cites a Rippling BDR leader (125 BDRs) who agrees with that view.
- His example: one outbound rep booking five demos a week doesn't mean five reps book 25. The rep is only capturing the warmth that content created above.
- He says you can't hire your way into this: "it's impossible to give a bad business good advice." If people don't want the product, no lead source fixes it.
- His mission is to evangelize bootstrapping. His plan: run profitable businesses, aim at $2-3MM ARR first, then aim a little higher with the next one, and hold on to roughly $10MM ARR. He compares it to the four-minute mile.

## Using RB2B for lead generation: his advice to customers

- He says you first need to be able to list your ideal customers, and have a sales motion that converts qualified leads. Otherwise that is the problem, not lead volume.
- Treat your go-to-market as a "tumbler" (a term he credits to someone else): email, LinkedIn connects and DMs, ads, content, thought leadership. You can't measure each piece alone, but the whole works.
- His instruction: filter identified visitors to your ICP, throw away the rest, and drop them into the existing cadences.
- Enrich to a phone number and call them if you still use phones; he calls them "super high intent."
- Expect higher response rates and better funnel conversion than from an Apollo-style list.
- He prefers not to mention you saw them on the website. He says there's no need to tell them, and that it sidesteps the "creep factor."
- Run channels together as fast as possible after intent: a message a day across two channels, two to five touches.
- GDPR question: he says the tool is US only, so no GDPR governance applies.
- Roadmap (from Oct 2023 to March 2024 discovery with demand-gen marketers): a traffic-insights product by ICP, department, industry, company size and UTM. It would show which campaigns and SEO drive ICP traffic, which Google Analytics can't. He hoped to ship by the end of the next quarter.
- Agencies are a big channel, and churn drops when customers actually use the product, which agencies help with. An agency page was being redone.

## Using LinkedIn for networking and comments

- He says LinkedIn is the most powerful networking tool in the world. His account has about 100,000 followers, but he says people with far smaller, more targeted followings use the tool better than he does.
- For referral- or partnership-led businesses: connect with everyone you want as partners, then post good content so they feel they know you over time. Then reach out.
- Trust builds passively, even when posts get only about 10 likes. He says knowing people is "a secret to business."
- Sequencing: design the go-to-market after your content is already performing. Until then, use LinkedIn as a networking tool, and content makes networking work better.
- Comments: he answers almost all of the 50-100 comments on each post, and thanks people who mention RB2B.
- He comments heavily on posts mentioning RB2B because people borrow his reputation for reach, and he wants to train the algorithm to see that as good.
- When starting out, comment in other people's feeds; he says comments are how you enter their feeds. That is harder than replying to your own, since you must read the post and write something meaningful.
- Pick a few people you want to get close to and engage with all of their content. Block half an hour a day for it.
- He avoids AI-generated comments and says he can usually tell.
