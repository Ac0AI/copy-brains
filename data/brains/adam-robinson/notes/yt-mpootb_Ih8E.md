## Getting unstuck: why Adam says "everything works when your product is good"

- Adam describes his whole entrepreneurial journey as "getting stuck and getting unstuck again."
- His first SaaS (a newsletter app, $40/month subscriptions to very small businesses) sat at about $3M ARR for four years, and he sold it for $10M.
- retention.com (formerly get emails) hit two sticking points: below $3M ARR, and again around $6M.
- When he was stuck, he went to conferences and grabbed any information about what worked for others. His takeaway now: "all of this stuff that people are telling you is working, they just have a better product than you."
- His rule: "When your product is not good nothing works." Getting unstuck means making everything you do better so it clicks into product-market fit.
- He frames his talks around himself at $3M ARR, three years in, and asks what that person wants to know.
- His favorite startup-building book is "Four Steps to the Epiphany." He says following it close to page by page raises your odds of success far above following your gut, even though there are emotional reasons not to.

## Why bootstrapping can beat "bootstrap vs. VC" thinking

- Adam says most small bootstrapped companies ("the 37signals thing": few employees, $1.5M cash, few problems) are slowly dying. He sold his for $10M.
- His approach is to get a company to that level, put someone else in charge, then tinker on the next thing. Each new product is better and more efficient because of the product-market fit, marketing and product skills from the last one.
- He claims there is a point (maybe $10M, $15M or $20M ARR, if run properly) where a bootstrapped company becomes so profitable that stacking businesses gets "absolutely incredible."
- His numbers at retention.com: $21.7M ARR, fat founder salaries, and another $6M to $8M of profit expected that year, which they would pay out as dividends.
- He pictures a roughly $20M free cash flow business run with friends.
- A bootstrapped company that big is stronger than much larger venture-backed ones: he says he could afford better lawyers than a $5.2B company while taking on a larger competitor, and it gives freedom and flexibility.
- His motivation is the game itself. If given $1B, he would use it to try to turn RB2B into a huge company, because he doesn't yet know whether he can build a $100M ARR company.
- He says he currently runs four efforts: retention.com (55 employees; he is CEO in name but not running it), RB2B, and an enterprise data sales business his COO Santos is assembling.

## What went wrong trying to grow a bootstrapped company like a VC-backed one

- The inspiration was Jasper AI. Adam shared an office with its founders when they were stuck at $2.5M ARR (he was at $3M with get emails, which was at $3M with six employees and about $1.5M cash). Jasper went from zero to $50M ARR in 12 months, and Adam says seeing that made something bigger feel real.
- Jasper spent about $2M a month on Facebook ads. His reasoning: if he spent $2M a month on sales and marketing, his business would be bigger too.
- By then get emails was at about $8M ARR with six people. Adam and his COO Santos decided to go for it, funded by free cash flow with no fundraising.
- The plan rested on three assumptions:
  - Demo only to large Shopify Plus stores, which he believed would solve churn.
  - Cut the price roughly in half while doubling the value, then double prices on everyone within a year for big revenue expansion.
  - Good sales velocity plus product-market fit in that niche would make it a "unicorn twice over."
- He also made a YouTube show (about 18 episodes, 10 minutes each, over two seasons) and started posting on LinkedIn to maximize awareness.
- Results: he missed the revenue target by over 50% and the unicorn goal by far more (he guessed 80% or 90%).
- What he got wrong:
  - He assumed it would behave like Klaviyo. Systems of record (CRM, data storage) have very low churn because you must put your data somewhere else if you leave. retention.com sells leads and intent data, so turning it off just means the revenue it produced is gone.
  - His loud success brought about five competitors, who used BuiltWith to find his customers and offered more for less at renewal. Revenue contracted at renewal instead of expanding.
  - He lost financial discipline: spending "like a sailor," hiring very expensive people, and not caring what they were paid.
- Operations: they hired 50 people in about 60 days with no processes, and the new hires brought their own ways of working. He later brought in an executive coach to roll out the Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS).
- He says the euphoric period ran from late September 2022 to roughly April or May 2023, followed by a cleanup. The business now looks like an EBITDA business, which is fine.
- His chief of staff and de facto CFO, Kelly, was more conservative and pushed back the whole time.
- He has had to lay off many people at once twice, which he says were his hardest moments.

## LinkedIn as a CEO-led marketing channel

- Adam says he spent about 12 months before it clicked and he understood why it worked. Before that it was "okay and really expensive."
- It works best when the buyer lives on LinkedIn. For a product like RB2B (a product that didn't exist two months earlier), he is selling to people who consume his content there. Otherwise it may help only with investors, hiring or partnerships.
- Practice: he posts three times a week, aiming for four, and also publishes video.
- What he writes about: real numbers and difficulties, such as retention.com being down 2.3% for a fourth straight month. His reasoning is to ask why anyone would read your content when AI produces "B minus crap."
- Jasper can't write his real financials, and it can't (yet) produce an authentic talking-head video, so he leans on both.
- His models were people building in public, such as Nathan Barry of ConvertKit. Five years earlier he would have read anything they wrote "like a rabid dog."
- His LinkedIn collaborator, Alec Paul, told him that if he wrote for B2B people, it would work given the effort he was willing to put in. Four posts in a row got about 2.5 million impressions, which showed him what LinkedIn rewards.
- The key benefit: people feel they know him like a friend, so when they need a product that substitutes for something of his, they buy from him.
- Example: at three events in one night during South by Southwest in Austin, strangers approached him. One $1M ARR SaaS founder said he sent every post to his whole team.
- He sees this fandom and affinity as unmatched by paid ads or cold email, and as the most capital-efficient way to spread awareness if you can nail it.
- His strategy for RB2B: invest zero in traditional marketing (one person does cold email, which he says contributes little) and put time, energy and money into this kind of brand, which is unmeasurable. He says no VC would let you do that.
- On employees: they are not the audience, and they knew he'd do this. His chief of staff pushed back, but he says it does more for the company than anything else, and he thinks employees prefer hearing the truth.

## Free as a product strategy

- RB2B is a free (freemium) product, and Adam says that after this experience he plans to start every product with free.
- His reasoning: "the power of free is so beyond what I think anyone understands." He cites Mailchimp.
- He compares it to burning money like a venture-backed startup: you could charge and then pay for distribution, or make it free so that "literally everybody's going to try it."
- Free also gives a large amount of information about how people use the product and how it might be monetized later.
- He also points to Jotform as an example of a free-product company (bootstrapped about 18 years, he said North of $100M).

## Founder life and personal context

- Adam spent about 10 years as a credit default swap trader at Lehman Brothers, from age 21 to about 30. He made a lot of money in that boom and bust.
- He decided to try entrepreneurship using his savings. He says it took five years from deciding to paying himself a dollar, and that a startup consumes whatever money you give it, especially for a first-time founder.
- He lived in a New York apartment with the Vimeo founders while they built the site, which changed his view of what entrepreneurship offered.
- He is 43, has a 2-year-old daughter and a second child on the way. His routine: home at 6:00 every day, works during nap time on weekends, and takes Wednesday mornings off until 11:00 for his daughter's music class. He travels mainly for keynotes, sometimes flying in for a day.
- He says his work pace is "11 out of 10," but he constrains it so there is room for family. He is much less social than he used to be.
