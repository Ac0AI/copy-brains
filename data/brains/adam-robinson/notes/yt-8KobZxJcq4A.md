## Why Adam says he pivoted from e-commerce to B2B

- Adam's co-founder Santosh, who built Apollo and ZoomInfo, kept telling him to sell the product (resolving anonymous site visitors to deliverable emails) to B2B teams. His argument was that the leads and person-level data are worth more to B2B sellers than to stores selling "a $100 pair of shoes".
- Adam's LinkedIn content aimed at e-commerce stores gave him visibility, partnerships, agency credibility, recruiting help and investor interest. It did not book demos with e-com store owners.
- His LinkedIn coach Alec told him that if he wrote about B2B he could "book demos with literally anyone". Adam made the content pivot in September, before the B2B product existed.
- The first B2B post got about 100 times the engagement of anything else he had written. He wrote three more and all of them did well.
- He puts the fit down to "author to platform to audience fit": he is his own ICP (a SaaS founder and revenue leader) and that ICP lives on LinkedIn.
- He had about 15,000 followers in the e-com space the previous July. He says he would not be where he is without the B2B shift.

## Co-creating the product with his LinkedIn audience

- Adam recommends *Four Steps to the Epiphany* (Steve Blank) and the Lean Startup method to every startup founder. His reading of them: your intuition is a poor indicator of market viability, so build the buyers at the same time as the product.
- Doing that de-risks the launch two ways. Initial buyers are already waiting when you launch. If nobody wants it, you can stop building and start over.
- While Tate built the product, Adam and Santosh took 5 to 7 calls a day with perfect-ICP people who responded to his posts.
- His posts argued things like "outbound is dead" and "third-party intent data sucks, here's what I learned from talking to 6sense customers".
- He used very soft CTAs. DM responders above a certain threshold got a phone call (Calendly link). Those below went onto a waiting list.
- The waiting list had 2,000 people before the product started.
- The content built enough trust that he planned to sell mid-market and run a free offer instead of a sales-led motion. He argues a free offer helps someone with a big megaphone far more than someone without one.

## Finding content-market fit on LinkedIn

- Adam took 12 months to find content-market fit (a Devin Reed term). He says others with it also took 12 months or a little longer.
- The unlock was writing about his own hard B2B journey: expanding a sales force too hard, then contracting it, and being down four months in a row. "No one else is sitting there saying that they're down 4 months in a row", though many people are.
- He says to articulate a very specific pain "incredibly clearly", something you can only do if you are one of them.
- Alec's view, which Adam now agrees with, is that you work out your content pillars as you go. A Justin Welsh-style content-pillar spreadsheet doesn't tell you in advance.
- Alec's formula: write the best hook you can, the best body you can, and a "killer close" that gives a dopamine hit and provokes engagement.
- Adam does not use engagement circles or ask big accounts to comment, though he did when he started.
- He studies people winning the game, such as Chris Walker, and asks why they win. He likes Nigel Thomas's list-format templates.
- The weekly "killer close" he showed was a simple soft CTA reused across several related posts.

## Why founder brand beats a good offer alone

- A listener said the offer (free visitor identification pushed to Slack) was good enough that he would have said yes to a cold email. Adam agreed it is a great offer, but said fewer people would say yes if they didn't know who he was.
- He cites Dave Cancel (Drift): HubSpot had a business-model innovation (a free CRM), but by the time Drift started there were 600 chatbot apps. Drift treated brand as the opportunity.
- Adam calls his LinkedIn effort "today's Drift". Competitors can copy the product, but the trust built through a founder-led LinkedIn presence is hard to emulate.
- The advantage is partly that he and Santosh have the experience and willingness to share, and partly that a cash-flowing other business funds the time. In effect he can give the product away and build "a bulletproof megaphone".
- He says the biggest determinant of a cold email's open rate is who sends it, more than the subject line.
- He does not measure the brand work by demos booked. "It is how we are going to win in the long run", and it is non-linear and makes everything else work better.
- Founder-profile calls still convert when someone else takes them. In the PLG funnel, if a valuable signup doesn't install the script, they get routed to Pete, Sean (enterprise) or Santosh. People show up equally for calls booked through Adam's profile whether or not he takes them.
- Caveat: it works for him because his audience (B2B revenue people) is on LinkedIn and he is that ICP. Not everyone will get the same result.

## Zero to $1M ARR: Facebook ads and an offer that wowed

- Adam's rule: with decent product-market fit and strong word of mouth, there are many ways to accelerate it. Without word of mouth, nothing you do will work.
- Zero to $1M in 28 weeks came from a Facebook ad campaign. He spent $5,000 on ads the first month and got $10K MRR. It was very high-churn MRR, but a strong early signal of product-market fit.
- Format: 60-second ads, inspired by the arrival of Snap and Instagram Stories, with a different background each time. They answered the sales questions and were designed to be anticipated.
- Tone: deliberately inflammatory toward privacy-minded people. He insulted commenters from the brand account to draw out more long replies, and the negative energy made the ads perform better.
- His takeaways: the offer matters most, and you can do aggressive, outrageous things in the first months because there is "no brand to destroy".
- The ads also made cold email work better through awareness, even when people didn't click.
- He suggests Facebook or Twitter ads as a way to get early awareness if starting from zero, and says to run tight, cheap experiments with defined time limits.
- Before $1M, he says the race is for feedback: "not a race to a million, it's a race to as much feedback as humanly possible".

## One to $10M ARR with six people

- The team was six people: one salesperson with a sales assistant, and three people in the Philippines. Those three did lead gen half the day and sent outbound email from individual Gmail inboxes the other half.
- The motion was inbound, a 15-minute demo and a one-call close, on a simple product, plus word of mouth. It reached about $12M ARR with six people.
- Adam's point is that the right product and one or two channels plus word of mouth go further than you'd expect with far fewer people, if you have the discipline. He says to get as far as humanly possible with as few people as you can.
- The cost: you need unusually productive individual contributors, and when it is time to grow nobody knows how to manage or has processes.
- The Philippines team: his co-founder Diana used one trusted contact (Jim) who could follow instructions, and Jim's cousins joined. People found alternate emails not in Apollo or ZoomInfo, and sent manually from Gmail rather than with Outreach or Salesloft.
- He treats this work as very mechanical, with no room for independent interpretation. With hindsight he would look for people who add more value than ticking off checkboxes.
- A friend, Stuart Chaney (Shopify app), advised building the playbook in the US first, then finding one person who fits the profile to work alongside you, then slowly building the team. Stuart finds people on onlinejobs.ph.

## Mistakes in the 2023 expansion and churn benchmarks

- Adam publicly forecast going from $13M to $50M ARR in 2023 (he made a docu-series) and missed by over 50%.
- Reasons he gives:
  - He believed narrowing to the ICP who loved the product would fix churn. It helped only a little.
  - He now thinks there are high-churn product categories and high-churn buyer personas, and some categories will never be low churn.
  - The market got competitive as others began solving the same problem.
  - Processes were missing. He now has an executive coach working with eight executives and is rolling out the Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS).
  - Senior hires brought their own ways of doing things on top of no company way.
  - Biggest mistake: he lost his bootstrapper's discipline with money, started behaving like a venture-backed CEO, and a highly profitable company stopped being profitable.
- Revenue stalled around $3M ARR for four years at Robly. At retention.com, quarterly revenue was flat to down for nine months (Q2 2020 to Q1 2021), and it shrank four months in a row (about 2.3% in the fourth). He still throws off roughly $400-500K a month.
- Churn benchmarks he gave:
  - Klaviyo's gross revenue retention is about 88% (usually about 70 for SMB), with about 120 net revenue expansion.
  - Retention.com's SMB lead product churns about 5-6% a month with the paid trial included and about 4% without it.
  - Enterprise logo churn is about 2% a year, with 130-140 net revenue expansion (great companies about 180). Mid-market sits halfway.
- Why leads-as-SaaS churns: many reasons to stop have nothing to do with the vendor, such as a new employee who doesn't know the tool or a lack of ROI.

## Staying small, bootstrapping and reviews

- Adam says bootstrapping caps the downside. A venture-backed company in the same bind would be in a "huge pickle", while a profitable bootstrapped one can only get so bad.
- His lesson from Jasper's founders, who were stuck at $2.5M ARR, out of money and ready to sell, is "keep building new baskets to put your eggs in because you just never know". Watching peers like that made a big outcome feel possible for him.
- On raising money or venture debt: "I would rather shrink my team than take someone else's money." Fundraising sells expectations rosier than reality, and he calls venture debt terms "a deal with the devil". His instinct is to get smaller and more efficient.
- On G2 reviews: he sees them as a legitimacy threshold (a good-not-great website, reviews across the internet), not a source of leads, and says leads he bought from G2 were terrible.
- Santosh's addition: reviews get gamed, and content like Adam's builds more trust than chasing reviews.
