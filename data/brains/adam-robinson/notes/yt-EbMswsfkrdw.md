## Positioning and category creation for a bootstrapped SaaS

- Adam says a founder must be able to fill in a statement of value card: "Company X helps group Y do Z." He learned this from Ryan Deiss's DigitalMarketer course around 2015-16.
- In his first email product he could not explain why customers should pay $45 a month instead of using MailChimp, which was free. That started a multi-year search for what MailChimp wasn't doing.
- He says it helps to coin a category even if it isn't fully real. For RB2B he coined "inbound-led outbound", meaning you bring visitors back to your site and use retargeting, with RB2B as the novel retargeting method.
- His first workshop was titled "What is inbound-led outbound", inspired by Chris Walker.
- He thinks the right move is to join a new workflow people aren't yet doing rather than compete in an existing one.

## Why Adam gave RB2B away free

- Adam credits MailChimp's free offer, and the 18,000 sign-ups a day it produced, as his inspiration. A dominant brand that is free for 90% of people makes "more free than MailChimp" impossible to beat.
- He considered a $30-50K ACV mid-market sales motion but rejected it. His reasoning: a mid-market sales operation doesn't benefit from scale advantages, while a free offer benefits a lot from a huge megaphone.
- The goal is to turn free users into the distribution engine. He wants free users to create UGC, so more free users produce more UGC, which becomes the moat. He cites Ryan Bzibzi's Jolie showerheads, which reached a $50M run rate in 18 months with a co-founder and one full-time employee. They got roughly 20,000 pieces of UGC from medium-sized influencers.
- He accepts earning less revenue than a future acquirer could: "How can I run this in the way I want to run it."
- RB2B is positioned as person-level website visitor identity, free, pushed to Slack with LinkedIn profiles of people on your website.

## What failed: the first free/paid split

- Adam's hypothesis was that people would see leads in Slack and upgrade for $450 a month. About 6,000 sign-ups produced zero self-serve upgrades.
- The "Magic Moment" is seeing leads in Slack, so he gave it away free. The planned paid hook was charging for integrations, but anyone can parse the Slack block with Zapier or no-code tools.
- On discovery calls, willingness to pay was very high before people saw the free version. Afterwards they said they'd stick with the free plan.
- The fix he arrived at: give the free tier to about 85-90% of people but gate the contact and account data. He expects high churn but accepts it because there is zero CAC.
- He also found some Enterprise leads come inbound (roughly two or three a week, from companies of around 5,000 employees such as Rippling and MongoDB), though he says closing them is hard given the nature of the product.

## Using LinkedIn as a real-time product feedback loop

- Adam posts detailed monthly updates, including monthly recurring revenue and P&L, and publicly shares hypotheses, including ones that don't work out. Hundreds of commenters give deep feedback and frameworks. He says this told him exactly what to change about the free/paid split.
- He used the content and inbound connection requests to run about 500 discovery calls while building the app, with one person building and the other on the phone with VP Marketing types about ABM and outbound.
- He cites the book The Four Steps to the Epiphany and the Lean Startup idea: build your buyers while building the product, because software is hard to get right. If you find out early that what you're building sucks, you quit while stakes are low.
- He says LinkedIn lets him do that at a scale the book's example (10 cold calls a day for a week yielding 5 conversations) couldn't match: about 10 conversations a day for three months, with ideal people who came to him after a soft CTA to DM.
- He values the quick feedback loop on posts. A bad post is forgotten within about three weeks.

## Weekly webinar as a distribution and market-research engine

- Chris Walker advised Adam to create a recurring Zoom meeting scheduled through the end of the year so sign-ups land in people's calendars. Walker reportedly gets 50-200 attendees each week, does a 10-30 minute monologue, then takes questions. Adam says it helps with narrative, market temperature and finding where the pain is.
- Adam promoted his first event a couple of times and in one LinkedIn post. He had about 120 registrations and expected around 30 attendees (using a 20% show rate rule of thumb).
- About 1,300 people attended. Roughly 200 were on Zoom and about 1,100 came through LinkedIn Live, streamed via Restream, with around 200 comments.
- He credits the personal brand (his face on the promotion) rather than the company brand. A G2 VP Marketing told him their best event ever drew 200 people.
- His view: a personal brand may be 10x on any individual action, and because the effects compound, "in a year it's 10,000x."

## Founder-led sales and the path to distribution

- Adam started as a credit default swap trader (about 10 years), then founded an email product that poached Constant Contact customers using a "BuiltWith"-style technology lookup.
- His first sales lesson: direct mail converted around 0.2% on a finite list, while cold calling a list nobody else was calling converted about 5-7%. That was roughly 40x better than direct mail, so he built a phone-sales engine.
- Once he burned through the list, he realized he needed marketing, not just sales.
- He made two big pivots that failed in consecutive years before landing on the product that became RB2B (originally called "get emails"). It began as a feature in the newsletter app. People downloaded the file and used it in Clay or MailChimp, so he spun it out and connected it to everything else.
- He says advisers split into two camps: grow the $3M ARR business that throws off about $1.5M to two co-founders, or start something new because the product isn't good enough. He ultimately thinks the second is right but landed in between.
- His rule on word of mouth: "If you don't have Word of Mouth, nothing works." Everything else amplifies it. If you have it, everything works.

## Outbound is declining and what to do about it

- Adam scaled outbound from two BDRs to about eight and booked the same number of demos. He says that worked when money was free. After shrinking the sales squad, the culture improved and things sped up.
- His core argument: response rates are getting crushed because the world will send more email each year. He'd bet "everything I have" that 2025 sends more emails than 2024. Even the best personalization fails if the channel is annihilated.
- He argues the economics: outbound only works at higher ACV as response rates fall. If rates halve, $20K ACV needs to be about $50K, then $100K, then $200K, "and then no one's got a job."
- He uses a mall analogy: a friend (a former Goldman Sachs investor) bought a mall and mall equities around 2017-18 on the thesis that Amazon fears were overdone. They went bankrupt because the mega-trend was still worsening.
- He expects the SDR/BDR role to wind down. He thinks a rep's best hedge is to build an audience organically on social media and become "a trusted source of knowledge," which gets you pushed higher as more people enter the space.
- He expects the future workflow to run on intent signals: create desire so people come to your site, capture that signal, and then move them down the funnel any way possible, with a human on the last mile.

## When to pick fights and how he handled the Six Sense post

- Adam wrote a satirical post about Six Sense after an account exec there allegedly tried to triple his pricing, and said he wondered if they had created an RB2B account themselves.
- He based it on what he'd learned about their financials: roughly, that if they stop growing they may have to lay off a large share of staff. The draft was more inflammatory, and some friends advised him to stay with facts and drop the humor. He did, and says that removed the bite.
- About 850 comments came in, roughly 80% positive. About 20% called him "thirsty," which he agrees with, especially since he also posted his P&L alongside it.
- His conclusion: "I'll never do anything like that again." He says people don't want that from him.
- His wife also told him he was "above that now" and asked what example he sets for their daughter. His earlier controversial Facebook ads for "get emails" worked because privacy-minded commenters wrote hate that made the ads perform better; he then made new videos from the comments.
- He says about 95% of what he posts is positive and inspiring. He sees removing the filter, and being open about how hard things have been, as what makes people reach out and say they thought they were the only ones struggling.
