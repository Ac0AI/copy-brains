## RB2B origin: from Retention.com feature to a B2B visitor-ID product

- Adam's first startup, Robbley (a newsletter app), had a feature that became Retention.com. Nobody wanted Robbley, everybody wanted the feature, so he spun it out. Retention.com reached about $20M ARR five years later.
- RB2B began the same way. His B2B data partner Santosh argued there was no person-level website visitor identity for B2B. Adam first resisted ("focus, focus, focus").
- He was posting founder-brand content to promote Retention.com to Shopify stores. About 25% of it was SaaS content on his own journey, and that content drew a different audience with roughly 10x the engagement.
- He followed that signal and built the B2B product. RB2B hit about $6.7M ARR in under two years, bootstrapped, with 3 full-time staff (4 once a designer joins).
- His view of the "1,000 true fans" playbook (build the audience, then the product): it rarely happens that way. More often one thing works okay, and you stumble onto a better thing and go all in on it.
- Retention.com resolves visitors to a deliverable personal email (ecom). RB2B resolves to a LinkedIn profile plus B2B enrichment, so it has a separate site, go-to-market and brand.

## Validating with a crude product: the Slack feed was the unlock

- His rule: ship something crude and see if people pay. If they won't use or pay for it, "you have the wrong product" and need to address a bigger pain.
- Version 1 was a daily spreadsheet of visitors' personal emails. Enthusiasm was about 2 out of 10.
- Version 2 was a spreadsheet of business emails. Slightly better, but a spreadsheet is a headache to read.
- Version 3: his CTO Tate built a fake Slack channel showing visitors with headshot and LinkedIn profile. Adam showed it on his phone to people. Enthusiasm jumped to about 11 out of 10, with reactions like "is that out yet? What's the URL?"
- The data was identical. The presentation changed. The headshot and LinkedIn URL made visitors feel like real people ("a line of data in a spreadsheet is kind of just like, well, you could be making that up").
- The Slack idea came partly from a conversation with Brendan Short, who wanted lead gen to feel like a dating app: people are fed to you and you say yes or no.
- Adam likes premium (free tier plus paid) models, modeled on how Mailchimp took on Constant Contact. The team considered keeping the in-app view free and charging for Slack.

## Why LinkedIn worked: leverage, timing and transparency

- He had about 20,000 followers, then grew to 40,000 in 30 days and 80,000 in the next 90 days after posting about B2B visitor identity. That showed him he had unusual leverage: "if this is even an okay product, it should be a pretty good business." It proved true.
- Launch benefit: the audience gave him a big waitlist and a large amount of Zoom discovery calls. Reaction split into a privacy camp (it's wrong) and an aggressive-marketer camp (it's transformational).
- Why it broke through, in his view:
  - First mover on radical transparency while building in public at a growth-stage SaaS (over $20M ARR; almost no one else was doing it).
  - He shared unique numbers, including "we just laid off 40% of our staff."
  - He argued that putting a 20-year-old in front of Outreach.io to click through sequences no longer works.
  - Bootstrapped vs VC became a core pillar. VC suits outliers (his examples: Elon Musk sending people to Mars, OpenAI), but the YC/VC pipeline pulls in many businesses, by his estimate about 80%, that would have been better off bootstrapped.
- He says a year of obsessive effort preceded the breakout, and that "if you can't get very obsessive about things, you should probably find another job."
- He expects imitators to dilute it. The Gamma CEO now builds in public at about $100M ARR, which he welcomes.
- Because AI-written posts now copy his style, he is looking for a style that is the opposite of the noise.

## Content playbook borrowed from Chris Walker

- Adam says he "completely ripped off his playbook." What he took from Walker:
  - Video: he believes the parasocial relationship from video goes beyond what engagement metrics show, and he used a lot of it.
  - A funnel from big LinkedIn post to "listen to the pod."
  - Strong, hard-held beliefs stated with authority.
- His key insight, from listening to Walker's podcast for hours on a 15.5-hour drive home from Aspen: Walker says the same four or five things over and over from different angles. You don't notice until you're deep in. "You say the same couple things over and over again in a thousand different ways on every single possible channel, and then you're the guy that is that."
- He built a spreadsheet of Walker's content: main point, sub-points, and enemies. Then he wrote his own version: his points, his enemies, mapped to his own positioning.
- His positioning is a bootstrapped founder who builds in public and is wildly transparent, a few other small themes rotate on and off. Anyone who reads him would say that, which was intentional.
- Post architecture: go through your own and others' real bangers (over 1,000 likes, not "comment X for Y" posts). Line them up and study the structure. Putting your story on a proven structure has far better odds than starting from scratch. LinkedIn "likes the bones of that post."
- Learning it is like learning French: it takes sustained commitment. Storytelling improves as you absorb great content and emulate it.
- Organic social is "the cheapest awareness you could ever get," because people come to you and sit through the content.

## Channel concentration risk: the LinkedIn shadowban

- His account appears to have been shadowbanned since August 27 after an automation flag, though he says he doesn't use automation. A human was sending DMs for a "comment this for that" campaign. Reach has dropped by about 80%.
- Evidence it's the platform, not the content: his 2026 predictions post, which peers (his LinkedIn consultant Alec, Brendan Short, the SaaS Club chat) called his best in six months, got about 13,000 impressions, versus roughly 150,000 to 250,000 normally.
- Lesson he draws: he is "totally beholden to this machine" and needs other ways to market besides the founder brand. Heavy dependence on one channel is a risk.
- Diversification moves: YouTube, a live show, a Twitter/X specialist, LinkedIn ads, SEO and AI search (done with an agency). He weekly spends about one Tuesday on content: one YouTube video, one live show, three or four LinkedIn posts.
- He had always been bothered that only LinkedIn worked for him. YouTube videos got about 100 views against hundreds of thousands of LinkedIn impressions for similar content. He thinks each channel needs someone familiar with it to adapt content.

## Churn ceiling and the plan beyond RB2B

- RB2B's buyer is a US-based marketer at a 5-20 person SaaS, but his founder brand reaches a wider set (very early-stage, international founders). A narrower paying audience caps the top of funnel.
- Churn problem: visitor ID doesn't magically book demos. Buyers who haven't done outbound before ask why it doesn't work. His answer is that they need to know what to say to whom and combine it with other signals. He sees this as roughly a 25% ceiling for the business as it is.
- Fix: widen the top of funnel and build more into the product so it gets stickier. He has talked to ex-founders of Clearbit and Leadfeeder: you can beg people not to cancel and win maybe 10%, but building other features makes it stickier.
- Idea he is exploring: a simple, premium all-in-one outbound platform (contact database plus visitor ID, e.g. RB2B plus a database) using other vendors' APIs, run with AI. Likely buyers are early-stage founders who wouldn't pay for RB2B alone.
- Market read: tools like Instantly and Smartlead are in a feature arms race with products far beyond what's needed for cold email. Flodesk reached about $30M ARR bootstrapped in four years in the newsletter space with a narrow, cheap, dead-simple product and inbound funnel.
- Growth logic: "if you're not growing you're dying." He expects to hit $10M just continuing as-is within about 18 months, then slide without expansion. He projects roughly a $29M run rate across RB2B and Retention.com by year end. He calls $10M ARR "FU money."
- He is not yet convinced of the final feature set. He wants to build with AI and test whether a year-long project can ship in a quarter.

## Reflections

- He doesn't think much about doing things differently. Whatever he did at launch, the five competitors who later entered visitor ID would have positioned against RB2B anyway, so a different offer would have drawn a different response.
- He believes every lesson took the time it needed, and the AI support system he refined is now something he can copy to other products.
- He says the drive to keep growing is a net positive if it's driven by interest in the problems, not by a goalpost that never arrives. He is moving full-time to Aspen, Colorado, as an extreme version of the lifestyle-business idea.
