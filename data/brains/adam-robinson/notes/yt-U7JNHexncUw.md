## Starting with a niche email tool, then finding a product-led wedge

Adam's first startup was Robly, an email newsletter app he began around 2012 after leaving a credit-default-swap trading career. He wanted a small lifestyle business, inspired by Tim Ferriss and 37signals.

- **Go-to-market came before the product.** He scraped small business websites to find Constant Contact customers (flower shops, churches, 1-3 person businesses), then called them pretending to be Constant Contact to validate they were customers. He pitched "50% more opens for half the price" at about $50/month and got roughly 5,000 of their 300,000 customers to switch.
- **That channel was exclusive.** The product was uncompetitive everywhere else (MailChimp had freemium and a better product), but nobody else was calling Constant Contact's list. When the list ran out, growth stalled.
- **Robly hit about $3M ARR and stayed there for roughly three years.** It was profitable with about six people.
- **What he would change:** He wouldn't have started in the small-business email newsletter market, where "it's so hard to pay salaries on a $30 a month SaaS" and churn is highest. He would also have niched down much harder, as Nathan Barry did with ConvertKit by targeting food bloggers first.
- **He recommends "The Four Steps to the Epiphany."** Develop customers in parallel with the product. If people pay for something that doesn't exist yet and the TAM is big enough, "you will almost surely succeed to some extent."

## Spinning out a feature that outperformed the main product

Adam's breakout came from a feature inside Robly, not from Robly itself.

- **The feature:** It identified anonymous website visitors and added them to email lists. He positioned it as "everyone's trying to grow their list. What if you could get emails of people that didn't fill out a form but hit your website."
- **The signal:** Customers downloaded the file into Klaviyo and gave it a 10 out of 10 NPS without using the rest of Robly. He realized Robly's ESP was too far behind to compete with Klaviyo.
- **The spin-out (Nov 2019):** He built a standalone product with basic integrations to three or four ESPs. He wrote a sales letter from the DigitalMarketer course and paid a freelancer on Upwork to make a 2.5-minute animated video. The first $5,000 of Facebook ads produced $10K MRR with a self-serve PLG flow. "If I spent $5,000 on Facebook for Robly I wouldn't have gotten $50 of MRR."
- **The wide price range:** Warby Parker signed in the first month at $20,000/month, while the plan started at $9. He says they didn't land a customer like that again for about four years.
- **Exit:** He sold Robly for about $10M (he recalls roughly 7x EBIT) to a customer that was rolling up small ESPs.

## Choosing ICP and why churn followed

The product grew to about $25M ARR (the transcript has him giving "25 million" as the peak figure) as Adam narrowed to Shopify ecommerce brands.

- **Early stall:** It went 0 to $3M quickly, then sat at $3M for 9 months. At month 18 he wondered if he was "just a 3 million ARR guy." Diana started prospecting ecommerce more heavily at month 18, and the company went ecommerce-only at month 36 (roughly $8-9M ARR).
- **Why ecommerce worked:** Big Shopify stores told friends they loved it, rarely churned, and had lists large enough to layer in these emails.
- **Second product:** Abandoned-cart and abandoned-product flows for the shoppers Klaviyo can't reach because they aren't logged in.
- **Team size:** About 6 people at $10M ARR, with growth from cold email and inbound. He says "product actually enables go to market": outbound only converts if prospects are also hearing about you elsewhere.
- **What he got wrong:** He assumed churn would look like Klaviyo's. It didn't, and the categories differ:
  - Email is a system of record, and list growth outpaces cancellations.
  - Leads and intent data are high-churn: when results disappoint, the only thing that changes is losing the leads.
  - Because it's a pixel, builtwith.com shows every customer, so five competitors pitched "better and cheaper" within 18 months.
  - His $5,000/month unlimited plan for large brands, meant to expand later, ended in contraction when competitors offered $2,000.
- **Average customer life** is about 18-24 months, so the book has to be replaced every 18-24 months.
- **Current strategy:** Move upmarket to mid-market and enterprise for stickier revenue, add cost-per-lead deals, and explore publishers. His stated 2025 goal is revenue quality over growth. He also says that getting multiple departments on the product reduced churn at Clearbit.

## Buying a premium domain and an affiliate-driven sales motion

- **The domain:** He bought retention.com for $800,000 (a broker negotiated; the seller had held it about 29 years and had earlier backed out of a similar deal). He treats it as a "$300K tax" over what patient negotiation might have cost, justified by the $20K/month lease-equivalent math and having the cash. When he bought it the company had eight people.
- **Why it paid off:** The name signals a big business ("you just assume it's a big business"). He compares it to cart.com spending $5M on its domain. He cautions against buying a huge domain at the start because it can give false positive signals, and says a weaker name early forces the product to be great.
- **Affiliate motion:** He paid 20% of cash collected for introductions to ecommerce brands. Deals were roughly $30K at the time, closing around 80% of people who showed up in 7 days, and the sellers were young DTC operators motivated by about $5K per intro. Affiliates only work if the incentive is large enough that they "drop everything" for you, and the deal size and velocity must match who the connectors are. Billionaire VCs won't care about 20%.
- **Today:** Sales is largely in-person and enterprise-style, such as getting a Foot Locker CMO to a Taylor Swift box and then running a nine-month process.

## Layoffs and running lean

- **Overbuilt for a unicorn that wasn't coming.** At the end of 2022 he assumed the company would reach $100M+ ARR, and he hired accordingly, including a COO (Santos), eight VPs in one day in Feb 2023, and a 20-person sales team.
- **Team size follows throughput.** He says headcount should track the number of deals you sell and service, not revenue. Deal volume fell to about a fifth of early 2023 levels even as revenue doubled.
- **The cut:** In 2024 he cut from about 50 to 30 people, mostly in go-to-market and customer success, keeping engineers. The business had about $8M annual free cash flow. His rule: "the smallest possible version of what you can be at that time."
- **Cuts mean less work, not more.** He told staff the remaining team should do less activity, only the activity that matters.
- **Customer success was oversized.** At $1,500/month, customers see the product as self-serve and want fast, thorough answers when something breaks, not quarterly business reviews. A main churn reason is something going wrong in implementation.
- **Outcome:** Profit rose after the layoff. He says profit is now about $1M/month and he owns a little under half the company. Revenue run rate is about $12M, and he targets about $30M across product lines with no added headcount.
- **EOS:** He adopted the Entrepreneurial Operating System with an executive coach for planning, focus and accountability, calling it "not the answer" but a useful shared framework.
- **Santos's value** was in the 10-to-100 stage; he transitioned out as processes matured.
- **His lean-company thesis:** Getting to $10M ARR with two co-founders and very productive individual contributors is "the promised land," since you can then raise, sell, or take distributions. The risk is accepting $8M of investor money at $4M ARR that you don't yet know how to spend. "If you don't take money you can only spend what you have."

## Building RB2B and its early go-to-market

- **Origin:** His COO Santos pushed for person-level B2B identification. A half-hearted attempt in late 2022 failed.
- **The trigger (Sept 2023):** He posted about shrinking the sales team and why BDR headcount doesn't scale linearly. It drew about 3,000 likes in 24 hours. His point was that a BDR now mostly captures demand from other marketing rather than creating it, and that hiring 10 BDRs requires 10x'ing the surrounding marketing. A consultant (Alec Paul) told him a LinkedIn-led motion would "crush" for a B2B product. He decided to go all-in on B2B content.
- **Method:** He batch-wrote posts, found the ones that resonated, and kept writing. Offers at the bottom of posts led to calls with him and Santos for qualified people, with others placed on a waitlist, which generated lots of customer discovery before launch.
- **Result:** Launched in March 2024, reached $4M ARR in about 9 months, mostly from his and Santos's posts. Cold email was a supplement. Impressions were about 500K/week at peak and about 150K/week when recorded.
- **Content cost:** He did 9-10 podcasts a week plus 3-4 LinkedIn posts during launch and says he wouldn't repeat that. He now plans content a month ahead and holds a weekly one-hour idea call, with a goal of writing a month of posts in one day.

## RB2B churn and the shift from "leads" to signals

- **The problem:** Churn hit 11-12% per month in Oct-Dec. Adam says his Clay-driven user-generated content (UGC) pushed an inaccurate use case, "LinkedIn profiles into Slack, put a human in front of a Slack channel," which fails for ordinary B2B companies with noisy traffic. Customers also get discouraged when the visitor list shows low ICP traffic.
- **New thesis:** A website visitor is "not a lead, it is a signal." He contrasts a signal-led use (feeding a lead score, for sophisticated PLG teams) with a TAM-first use: filter signal through the company's target accounts.
- **Implementation:** Send signal into HubSpot or Salesforce. If the visitor is a named account, alert the rep or start a sequence, and ignore the rest. He wants every rep to get "a win a day," meaning an insight, not necessarily a booked demo. This cut churn by about two-thirds while sign-ups slowed.
- **Roadmap:** Salesforce integration, reverse-IP company-level lookup (to replace tools like 6sense or Clearbit), and later extra signals such as job changes. He doubts customers want another outbound "platform."
- **Also:** He stopped cold email and ads for now and is monetizing his founder community with courses and help, around $1,000-2,000, focused on customer discovery and clear messaging.

## Building in public, SaaS lean teams and AI

- **Why he builds in public:** He started in 2022 as the best way to build awareness among Shopify brands. He says he wouldn't have reached $4M ARR on RB2B without it. His angle is credibility: "a guy who's at 25 million ARR who's working in public. There's no one else." He shares what's going wrong too, including the layoffs and a pricing change that didn't work, and was writing for who he was five years ago when stuck at $3M.
- **Channel reasoning:** LinkedIn's creator-to-consumer dynamic is more favorable than Twitter's, so the plan was 10-20K followers there first.
- **Founder-brand caveat:** Brand marketing is hard to tie to dollars for a bootstrapped company, so benefits are mostly unquantifiable. He says copying Jason Fried's style would not work for him because each founder has to find their own angle.
- **AI view:** He is a late adopter. AI helps a tiny team, and his customer support (Intercom) handles many tickets with two people. But he thinks it increases competition, since more people can build SaaS quickly, and sees enterprise sales as the hard part to defend. His advice: ask "what is hard in a world where building is easy?" He doubts that agents will replace SaaS soon, guessing five years or more.
- **Related example:** He notes the builtwith founder ran to the high-teens millions ARR before hiring anyone.
