## Why personal profiles beat company pages on LinkedIn

Adam argues that "people do business with people, not companies," and that LinkedIn lets a founder scale human-to-human connection in a way a company's ad cannot.

- He grew from 4,000 to 140,000 followers in 24 months. He says he reached 100,000 in 18 months.
- He took RB2B from 0 to $3M ARR in about 6 months, with five people and no funding, which he credits to LinkedIn.
- Organic content works as free ads. Large companies have to pay for the impressions.
- He says personal profiles grow about 10x faster than company pages and get far more engagement.
- His comparison: a post from competitor and market leader 6sense versus one of his own. His own post won on engagement.
- Don't copy big-company branding. He says not to build a slick, sterile brand or a logo in the same colors as everyone else, and he mocks 6sense's line that it "reimagines the way revenue teams create, manage, and convert pipeline."
- Your humanity, flaws, adversity and beliefs are the advantage a big company can't match.

## The three C's: clarity, consistency, constant reinforcement

Adam's framework for building a personal brand has three parts.

- Be incredibly clear about who you are.
- Be consistent about who that person is.
- Constantly reinforce that story.
- The more vulnerable, flawed and relatable the message, the more it cuts through. He says very few creators on LinkedIn will talk about real problems, and doing so puts you in the top 1%.
- Example: he posted about a layoff and admitted the company had fallen out of product-market fit and failed to fix churn for 18 months. His executive team begged him to soften it. He refused.
- His test: "If you can say things to LinkedIn that you would say to your spouse behind closed doors, it will do better than anything any large company could ever post." He notes that investors or co-founders may limit how far you can go.
- A tip he credits to Devin Reid: people behave on LinkedIn like they behave at work, so being a little inappropriate stands out.
- Sharing financial numbers makes building in public far more interesting.
- He quotes Alex Hormozi: "Do, then talk about it."

## Content pillars and being polarizing

Adam recommends one or two pillars, plus building in public.

- Pick topics tangential to the problem you solve, where your prospects share those problems. Choose things you do daily and think about more than most people.
- Example for a CRM aimed at car-dealership sales teams: building in public, how to attract, train and retain salespeople, and the future of dealership sales with AI.
- His own pillars: build in public, the future of BDR/SDR and go-to-market, and bootstrapping versus VC.
- Bland, down-the-middle content gets lost in the noise. He uses Russell Brunson's "polarity" idea: between mainstream and crazy, there is a sweet spot where people on your side love you and the other side hates you.
- His example: around Labor Day 2024 he posted that he got rid of his BDR team and pipeline generation sped up. He argued that the predictable revenue model "was born in 2012 and died in 2023," and that demand is now captured by SDRs, not created by them.
- His alternative was to drive traffic to your site and retarget visitors. He calls it more efficient but less predictable.
- Early on, the goal is a trust-building landing page, not a lead engine. His friend Curtis Matsko (Portland Leather Goods, a $100M/year handbag business) spends 30 minutes a week recording videos. His assistant posts them daily. Engagement is under 10% of Adam's, and he has never tied a purse sale to LinkedIn. But his last three executive hires came from people who watched the videos, and an investment banker now does free finance work for him.

## Profile setup checklist

Adam says his profile alone drives hundreds of inbound RB2B leads a month. Each fix is small, and together they compound.

- Headshot: professional, looking at the camera, solid background, ideally smiling.
- Banner: branded, with a clear value proposition and a call to action. Use Canva (free tier) or hire someone on Fiverr or Upwork.
- Headline: clear, credible and accurate.
- Link or custom button at the top, pointed at your site or a cold-traffic page with a front-end offer.
- About section: very detailed and specific, with numbers. Put the credibility in the first line, above the fold. Cover what you have done, not who you aspire to be.
- Featured section: direct-response calls to action.
- Roles: detailed descriptions of past and present roles, plus investments, advisory roles and professional networks.
- Education, including advanced degrees and certifications.
- At least 3-4 recommendations, swapping with others if needed.
- With LinkedIn Premium you can see who viewed your profile. He treats those people as warm leads for opening conversations.

## Writing posts that work

Adam says to write for the audience, not the algorithm, because the algorithm exists to serve the audience. His fix for any algorithm change is to make better content.

**Finding ideas ("idea hunting")**, using cold email as the example niche:
- Study the top creators in the niche. Spend hours on their last 90 days of posts, log the topics in a spreadsheet, and pick the ones where you can take a polarizing view. Don't copy their ideas whole.
- Ask Gemini, which draws on Google Search and YouTube, "What are the most common questions people have about cold email?"
- Mine Reddit threads for the questions people ask.
- He says these three sources give enough ideas for the first 30 days. After that, revisit creators' top posts from a year ago and add your own spin.
- The best posts answer questions people have about your niche.

**Swipe file:** spend two hours collecting the best posts from creators in your space (hooks, templates, closes, calls to action). He looks to Chris Walker for hooks and Nigel Thomas for templates.

**Hooks:** the hook is everything before "see more," and it is the most important part of a post. Write as if no one has heard of you, build credibility fast, and use numbers because "LinkedIn loves numbers." Examples:
- "I spoke to 20 CEOs over the past 30 days."
- "I bootstrapped $20M ARR in three and a half years."
- "I just audited a $50M ARR SaaS with monthly ad spend of X" (a Chris Walker hook).

**Template:** he often uses a list plus two sentences, or bullets. Each post should agitate a specific pain point, offer a solution, and tie it together with a takeaway, like a mini hero's journey. Once you've mastered writing you can drop templates.

**Close:** end with a clever closing line that gives readers a "dopamine hit" and prompts engagement. He says not to write "let's continue the conversation in the comments" or tack on a stupid question.

## Connect campaigns and conversation opening

Adam's outbound system on LinkedIn:

1. List your 100-250 best prospects.
2. Send 10-20 connection requests per day with no message. Accept rates are high. You can work up to 50 per day, but automation is against LinkedIn terms and risks a ban.
3. When a connected prospect likes or comments on your content, DM them: "Hey [name], thanks for the like/comment. How are things going at [company]?" He says response rates are usually north of 60%.
4. As inbound requests grow, stop outbound and accept only ICP connections. Don't accept people who want to sell to you.
5. After accepting, message "what caught your attention?" and then ask how things are going at their company.
6. Wait 60 days before pitching. Most people wait 48 hours or 5 days. By day 60 you are "famous to them" from your content.

He says to start conversations with anyone who viewed your profile or company page, engaged with a post, or replied to your comment, without pitch-slapping them. His friend Megan Bowen connected with 20 ICP people a day manually for four years, reached thousands of prospects, and now has 30,000 followers and an agency pipeline of leads, partnerships and investment opportunities.

## Community through comments

- Adam answers every comment on his posts, typically 100 or more per post. He meets many people at in-person events who already know him from the comments.
- Commenters helped fix RB2B's pricing. The company had 3,000 free signups but only 13 paying customers. He posted about it repeatedly, and the feedback led to a pricing change that reached a 10% free-to-paid conversion rate and helped growth from $0 to $3M ARR in 6 months with five people.
- Early on, make a list of influential people in your space and leave at least 100 thoughtful comments a week on their posts. Don't use AI to restate the post and add a question at the end.
- Spend as much time or more commenting each week as you spend writing.
- The audience built around your pillars can eventually move to another platform.

## Video and the 30-day commitment

**Video:** Adam says getting people to watch and listen to you speak intelligently raises your status in their minds. He copies Chris Walker's format: more polished than a phone in a bedroom, but not heavily produced, with a polarizing view.
- His process: write the post first, then record a free-flowing monologue off the post's bullet points. The video and post end up slightly different.
- Tools: Riverside for recording, and an Upwork editor at about $250 per video.
- Video isn't required. He suggests waiting until you've found your voice and have strong engagement. He also flags LinkedIn's mobile video feed test as something to watch.

**30-day commitment:** he asks people to follow his guidelines for 30 days, about 16 posts and 20 outbound connection requests per day. The expected results are:
- A pull-based recruiting mechanism, because candidates read your posts and are pre-sold before interviewing.
- Inbound partnership approaches from your ecosystem.
- Possibly investor interest, which he says to avoid because he thinks "VC is the enemy of the founder."
- Possibly inbound leads.
- His main reason to stick with it is that you get 30 days closer to finding your voice.
