## Why audience-first beats product-first for startup go-to-market

- Adam argues you can now build demand first and the product second. Building an audience of buyers while building the product is, in his words, "four steps to the Epiphany" turbocharged.
- He had not decided to build RB2B until he saw how effective his LinkedIn content was. The opposite of "build a product, then search for demand."
- During the roughly five months of developing RB2B he did 300 discovery calls. Many came from people commenting on his posts, whom he could reply to with "by the way, we're thinking about building this thing, would you talk to us about it?" Every one said yes.
- His rule: if you have access to a voice that can create organic social content for a group of target prospects who truly want it, create a product for that audience. He compares it to why the Kardashians or billionaires sell so efficiently in B2C.
- He frames it as "a brand new path" to bootstrapping. What used to need a 25-person sales and marketing team (and about $5M raised) is done by his LinkedIn brand plus two contractors.
- Product still matters equally. He says RB2B was disruptive in its pricing, and "magic lightning in a bottle happens when those two interact with each other."

## Why LinkedIn content worked for Adam: skill, fit and radical transparency

- He wrote about this topic for a year before it really worked, and stuck with it. He started writing in September of the prior year. A glimpse of what might work came in July, then a Labor Day experiment produced four absolute bangers in a row.
- The shift: he stopped aggregating an audience of Shopify stores and wrote directly at SaaS revenue leaders about a problem they all shared but nobody acknowledged. Big BDR teams might not work anymore; "you could probably reduce the head count by 90% and get the same amount of book demos," because power shifted back to marketing.
- He identifies a "perfect author to content to platform fit."
- Positioning rule: be different from everyone else, but in a way that is "not quite crazy but definitely not mainstream," and related to what you do. Down the middle doesn't work.
- Being bootstrapped lets him do things VC-backed founders can't. He shows financials and monthly recurring numbers, and he publishes a live SaaS metrics dashboard for RB2B in his newsletter every week, including bad churn.
- Why he shares it: when he was stuck at $3M ARR for four years, he reached for anything written by someone further along. He knows how novel that transparency is.
- Advice: don't copy someone's tactics; understand the thought process behind why they made a decision.

## Turning controversy into free PR: the cease-and-desist post

- He attacked a larger incumbent and got a cease and desist. He believed there was a good chance he would be sued, but posted the letter on LinkedIn and rewrote what they had sent him.
- He calls it a pivotal moment in his career. It moved him from being vaguely known as "a LinkedIn guy" to being understood by all of that incumbent's customers, who he expects will become RB2B customers as it drifts upmarket.
- He describes it as "millions of dollars of free PR" and says it could not have happened any other way.
- He says it was not malicious: it was a plain account of what was happening, which is where the authenticity comes from.

## Using organic social and podcasts as demand creation (not just lead gen)

- He considers himself "in the edutainment business" and happens to have a SaaS at the end of the funnel.
- Cold email and LinkedIn outreach to launch a startup (the predictable-revenue playbook) was "a hack" and "shouldn't have been that easy." He argues it was really a demand-creation mechanism, and that those days are gone or on the way out; founders should plan for a future where it's impossible.
- His current approach: cross-pollinate audiences with people who have audiences made up of your prospects. Once your audience is bigger than the host's, they want you on their show and distribute it for you.
- He does about 10 guest podcast slots a week related to go-to-market. A year and a half earlier he paid Lemon Pie to book them; now it is inbound. He has told the market he'll come on any show that invites him.
- Why he likes it: it's a "prospect dinner" he didn't have to attend. Talking to one person about what he's doing is easy for him, and he doesn't drink.
- Even if only 10 people listen and half check out RB2B, that counts.
- Downside: it can't be measured. When signups dip by 100 in a week he has no idea why, and he can't attribute signups to specific activities.
- He argues a VC-backed founder couldn't easily pitch investors on "spend four days a week creating founder brand content and I'm not sure when it will start working."
- The compounding value: that audience also helps in recruiting, partnerships and investor relations, even where customers aren't on LinkedIn (his example of agriculture tech or insurance).

## Building the habit, ghostwriters and getting through the zero-follower stage

- Posting is work for him. He treats it as a muscle, like going to the gym, and says every habit he has built got easier with time.
- He ran a ghostwriter for nine months, and recommends starting with a ghostwriter for about 90 days to learn how writing on LinkedIn works. You can see the benefits (inbound from investors, recruiting) without committing heavily.
- He later switched to a more expensive coach (Alec) who doesn't write for him. His view: you writing it yourself is the more valuable skill, and "there's no way a ghostwriter could write as profoundly as you."
- His own barrier was that he wouldn't post without understanding the game, since he is now "playing in the pros" at product and marketing and wasn't going to "start tweeting against LeBron James." Learning why things work solved that.
- The start is "brutal": a first post can get about four engagements while he gets 1,600. "At some point everyone had zero subscribers, zero followers, zero revenue." He cites "the best time to plant a tree was 20 years ago, the second best time is today," and says it is only just beginning.
- Content as beacons: every piece is a little beacon, and if it's true to you, interesting people and problems gather around it. Old posts keep pulling people in years later.
- He treats being a founder as finding the point of maximum leverage and going as hard as possible until you max out. For him, LinkedIn is "100 times more leverage than anything else" in either company.

## The long game: attention arbitrage and a voice that outlives the company

- His thesis: if you build an organic social voice and know what good content is, you can carry it to any niche. He thinks he could make good content for another audience, such as agencies instead of SaaS, though less prolifically.
- Quoting Russell Brunson as his source: once people will pay you for advice, "you are one of the most powerful people in the world."
- Benefits beyond revenue: he can message anyone who spends time on LinkedIn and get a 30-minute chat, including CEOs. He says this is a bigger superpower than the money he has made.
- Founders resist this because they are entirely focused on the current business. He says he used to think "I don't care about that," and now definitely does.

## Finding product-market fit through a one-liner: the Get Emails / Retention.com story

- His earlier email marketing platform (a V2 aimed at a segment he thought a competitor was leaving) took six engineers and a year. At a trade show people's eyes glazed over; they already had the problem solved. He describes that night as rock bottom: "no one cares."
- Having heard it was possible to resolve an anonymous site visitor to a deliverable email address, he started asking on day two of the show: "What if I could give you email addresses of people who were on your site but didn't fill out a form?" The reaction was immediate: "where do I buy that?"
- He had no idea how to build it technically. The signal was strong enough to send him digging until he found the few people who understood that part of the internet. They white-labeled a provider and then made it their own; that became Retention.com.
- The product launched as Get Emails. In the first month he spent $5,000 on Facebook ads and reached $10K MRR. It had very high churn, but a high-ticket PLG dynamic: "we get 100 bad customers and some whale would come in and pay five grand and pay the ads back."
- His takeaway: it started with a searingly clear one-liner and watching people's faces react, before he even had the product.
- RB2B followed a similar pattern. When he described person-level website visitor identity, people first said they already had it (meaning company-level tools like Clearbit), then asked where to buy the person-level version.
- He is betting against sweeping regulation of tracking and retargeting, calling it a fundamental underpinning of the internet's economic engine. He notes that third-party cookie removal was predicted "in 12 months" every year for about five years and then did not happen. He says the product works only in the US.

## Views on venture capital and what actually accelerates growth

- Widely held belief he rejects: that venture capital under $10M ARR will accelerate your growth. He says what accelerates growth is making your customers happier.
- He thinks VC often slows you down early by adding people and management layers when you don't yet know what you're doing. "When we were seven people and not 22... we were iterating so fast."
- He says capital is never the answer to hitting a wall. He believes it can help speed things up later, but not before roughly $10M ARR.
- He argues for efficient, bootstrapped paths. Funds like Sent invest only in founder-owned, profitable, growing businesses past $15-20M ARR, and he believes that model is better for founders than the VC odds.
- Current working tactic outside LinkedIn: a UGC motion where lead-gen agencies post on LinkedIn to grow their own businesses, using RB2B and the content Adam gives them. He calls it working "incredibly well" and a green field for B2B companies.
