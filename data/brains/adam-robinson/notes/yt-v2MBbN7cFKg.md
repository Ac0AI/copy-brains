## Why Adam built a personal brand on LinkedIn, and how a viral post set the strategy

- Adam began posting on LinkedIn because he was selling a Shopify app with strong product-market fit and wanted the fastest way to spread awareness. His reasoning: "people follow people, not companies", so he built a public persona.
- He planned to write about his journey as a SaaS founder, since few "build in public" voices were at his stage.
- Over a year he saw that honest posts about the 2023 SaaS downturn (outbound stopped working, layoffs, business-model resets) got roughly 100x the engagement of posts aimed at ecommerce stores.
- Over Labor Day weekend 2023, a post said that BDR (business development rep) outbound wasn't working, that he laid everyone off, and that growth accelerated afterward. It got thousands of likes and many DMs.
- He tested the result by writing three more posts before dinner. His reasoning: if all three went viral, he would know what the algorithm wanted from him. They did.
- He then chose to write for the B2B software audience and to build a B2B version of his product, launching it with that awareness already in place.

## Designing the business around AI from day one

- The product is person-level website visitor identification. It already existed for ecommerce but not in B2B.
- He took inspiration from Intercom's Fin, which moved from per-seat pricing to charging per resolved question.
- He designed the company around what AI could augment. He said starting from zero gave an advantage over retrofitting an old business.
- His view is that AI support works best when:
  - the product is simple;
  - there is no complex sales cycle;
  - it is product-led (people buy without talking to anyone);
  - pre-purchase questions are the same for everyone;
  - the homepage explains the product instantly;
  - there is a free tier and an easy upgrade path.
- He refused to add complexity so the documentation could be good enough for Fin to resolve nearly everything by pointing to docs and looking at the customer's account.
- RB2B launched this in 2024, when Fin resolved about 25% of tickets. For RB2B it now resolves 98.6%.
- His point is that a simple product plus good docs is the main lever, not a sophisticated multi-agent setup.

## Simple beats complex: "nobody's touched it in a month"

- Adam says his CTO hasn't touched code in over a month. He posts three times a week on LinkedIn, and not even about RB2B. A teammate built the AI support system. RB2B is at $8.6M ARR and still growing.
- He did not build a sprawling marketing machine and replace people with many task-level agents. He kept the business simple and used AI where it fit.
- He contrasts himself with Amos (Swan AI), who builds the "autonomous business" with many agents. Adam notes Amos spends his days on eight calls and his team works around the clock. In his view RB2B is already the autonomous business, and it was simpler to build than people assume.
- He is skeptical of the Coinbase-style approach (15% layoffs, one-person teams with agent fleets). He called it the complexity-heavy path.
- He expects a billion-dollar one-person company is possible and mentioned a reported two-person company in the compounded GLP-1 space that scaled direct-to-consumer sales with many AI agents.

## Why RB2B survived copycats: awareness, fixed-cost data, and a low price

- Competitors entered and tried to copy the product. Adam says each hit the same wall that Amos did.
- He is candid about the product's limits:
  - Web traffic is noisy.
  - Persistent ad-tech IDs are inherently inaccurate.
  - Few of an ideal customer profile's site visitors are actually prospects yet.
  - The result is a signal that must be combined with other signals before anyone acts on it.
- Competitors compensated by building many extra features and charging about $20-30K a year, so they fight over the same later-stage startups. They also had venture investors.
- RB2B starts at $79 and $149 a month, with an average subscription around $200. He is self-funded.
- Customers watch their visitors until they no longer want to, then cancel. Churn is "terrible", he says, but he expects it to level off and it is still strong.
- Awareness: no one else could create the same reach, which gave RB2B a very cheap way to acquire customers.
- Data cost: it is effectively a data company with three business lines (RB2B, Retention, and a data arm that sells flat datasets). A shared data team runs one data asset. He puts the cost at about $1M a year across salaries and annual vendor contracts.
- With that fixed cost, resolving a contact, enriching it and sending it to Slack costs no extra money. Competitors pay about five cents per email.
- That combination lets him price below what it costs most B2B competitors to acquire a subscriber.

## Why his thought leadership is hard to copy

- The writer, the platform and the readers line up:
  - The audience is B2B software people on LinkedIn.
  - Many of them are aspirational followers, either early-stage founders or employees at bigger companies.
  - These readers are also the buyers of what he sells.
  - His credibility as a SaaS founder carries weight with them.
- He says nobody at his level publishes similar content.
- On vibe coding, his view is that if anyone can copy your product, the defensible advantages are:
  - breaking through the noise in marketing;
  - owning unique data that AI agents can act on;
  - network effects (a two-sided market with 10M users on each side cannot be vibe-coded).
- On Greg Isenberg's take that audience is the hard part, with services first and technology later: Adam says that is easier said than done, and he hasn't seen big wins from that content brand yet. His own path was a version of it: he built the audience before the product.
- He would keep posting on LinkedIn even without RB2B and believes in compounding a personal brand.

## The next bet: a contact-data brand for AI agents

- He is exploring a contact-data business (emails and phone numbers) positioned for Claude and AI agents. It could be API-first or agent-first with no traditional UI.
- The idea: sell data he already owns, at a very competitive price, behind the awareness funnel he has already built. A brand could mean something like "contact data for Claude".
- The market is a competitive commodity: two big players, a few mid-sized ones, and many faceless small ones. His question is whether a disruptive, brand-led approach can win.
- He says he may launch soon, since shipping is cheap now, and pull the product if it doesn't work.
- He is hiring one person to maintain part of RB2B while the three who built it work on the new project.

## Team size, scale and strategy

- At an earlier company he launched with six people and stayed at six until $13M ARR, which took three and a half to four years. That ran much heavier on human work than RB2B does now:
  - sales demos for everyone;
  - an onboarding person;
  - a support person;
  - a sales assistant;
  - Adam handling product marketing;
  - a CTO.
- He says everyone was swamped. RB2B, designed AI-first from scratch, needs far less human effort.
- He doesn't see RB2B reaching $100M ARR. Lead and intent data is not a system of record the way a CRM is, and there are "a million reasons" customers drop it.
- His goal is cash flow, not scale. He wants to pursue opportunities too small for venture capital. His plan is a portfolio of businesses that each have an interesting competitive advantage, and he thinks that portfolio could reach the 50-100 range.
- On incumbents such as 6sense: many companies from that era went further than they should have, because cheap money and portfolio companies buying each other's products made it hard to tell whether intent data was useful. That matters more now that money is tighter.
- He says each business is different, and 6sense's product may have lost relevance for reasons beyond AI.
- Intercom is a rare case of a legacy software company pivoting. It had an AI research group for three years before ChatGPT and bet fully on it.
- His view is that changing a group of people is hard and starting from zero with an AI-first approach is easier.
