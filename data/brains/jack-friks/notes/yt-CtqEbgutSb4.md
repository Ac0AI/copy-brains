## Jack's method for finding organic TikTok/Instagram formats: scroll, save, copy

- Jack found the format for his Curiosity Quench app (a stop-scrolling app) "by consistently scrolling every morning." He argues you can't figure out a platform "just posting and not being on the platform. You have to be a user."
- Early routine: about an hour a day on marketing, which meant scrolling his branded page or a page in his niche to see what goes viral, then making one different video. He did this for roughly 100-200 days with little result before one format hit.
- Current routine:
  - Set a 15-minute timer and scroll TikTok and Instagram.
  - Use the save feature to bookmark formats.
  - Later, set aside 30 minutes to an hour to make a video from the saved material.
- He says he finds a "gem" nearly every session, though sometimes it turns out to be 8 months old and stale. He can still learn from videos in unrelated niches by borrowing the structure (a listicle, a 6-second background clip with a caption overlay).
- His principle: "If you can try to get some of what's working for an existing viral video rather than starting over from scratch, I think that's the best way to go." Remaking a proven format also takes far less effort than inventing a new one.
- He credits a daily habit with training his brain to see content ideas everywhere: set time aside for the goal and "now my brain is going to be thinking about this even when I'm not in that set aside time."
- He says some days he posts a bad video anyway, and sometimes those do well.

## Curiosity Quench: 400 copies of one slideshow format, $15K peak month

- Curiosity Quench gives people action plans to pursue hobbies instead of doom scrolling. It took about 8 months to make real money, with 50-60 downloads a day before the viral format. Jack says the lesson was "downloads don't equal money."
- Peak: $15,000 in one month. September 2024 was the second-highest month and October 2024 the next.
- Format: he saw an Instagram video with about 1M views showing four pictures and a funny caption ("most attractive hobbies"), unrelated to any app. He swapped in hobbies and pictures from his app (widgets) and changed the caption.
- He made that video about 400 times. After the first ~100 he saw it getting a lot of views and kept going.
- Call to action: he put the download link in a comment and pinned it early. People commented angrily on the hobby lists, which drew attention to his pinned comment.
- Roughly every 20 videos he put the app in one of the pictures. Some of those reached millions of views and brought thousands of downloads.
- Results: a day with 2M views and about 1,000 downloads, versus his earlier 50-60 a day. The format ran about a year and slowed around 3 months after the peak. By the interview it was "milk dry": "golden geese don't last forever."
- Trade-off he describes: a video can get 10,000 views and 1,000 downloads, or 3M views and 100 downloads. He keeps posting anything that consistently brings in some downloads.
- Limits: attribution is nearly impossible. He had no idea how many downloads a given 1.8M-view video produced.
- He runs the same format across several accounts and reposts to TikTok, Shorts and Instagram.

## Paywalls and monetization lessons from his first app

- The first monetization was confusing "quench credits" and a shop where users had to find premium access themselves. Few people bought.
- He added a paywall with RevenueCat first, but it was clunky and he couldn't customize it. Then he migrated to Superwall.
- Revenue went from about $500-$1,000 a month (over roughly 5 months) to $2,000-$4,000 a month after 3-4 months of testing on Superwall.
- What changed:
  - Realizing paywalls and free trials are the standard. Before, he didn't know.
  - Testing headers and copy.
  - Gating certain features.
- He used Superwall's templates and only added his logo and colors. He says "templates are really good, so I mostly just use those." A paywall takes about 5 minutes on a new app. Looking legit increases the chance someone buys.
- Doof (a food diary app) used a template paywall too, without much thought.
- On Lovely, he switched from a hard paywall to freemium after bad reviews and because he wanted friends to use it.

## Lovely: Twitter launch, a viral grocery list and 100K downloads in about 10 weeks

- Lovely is a couples app. A doodle or love note appears on a partner's home screen widget. He made it for himself and his fiancée, and a friend suggested it.
- Launch tweet: he said he'd work on the app for the rest of the year and take it from $0 to $5,000 a month in about 10 weeks. He quote-tweeted it about six times with updates and context, and each one got more views.
  - Two hooks: the build-in-public story of making $5,000 a month, and the novelty of love notes on a home screen.
- A user replaced a love note with a grocery list. That tweet got 16M views and 33,000 likes. Jack added context in a quote tweet reading roughly "I made this app, someone's now using it as a grocery list," which got 300,000 likes and about 40,000 downloads overnight.
- Russian news picked it up, which brought about 10,000 downloads from Russia the next day. Another tweet later reached about 20M views.
- Twitter plus Russian news added about $2,000-$3,000 MRR before he posted on TikTok.
- Totals: about 102,000 users, about 10,000 paying, and roughly $4,200 MRR, against his $5,000 goal.
- He kept adding context in follow-up posts, which created "a neverending cycle of interesting things that are happening and they're all about this app."

## Lovely video formats: from 150K views and ~100 downloads to a format converting about 20x better

- TikTok started about a week after the tweets. He posted once a day from a burner phone with a VPN and tried new formats found by scrolling his niche.
- First attempt: a relationship-themed TikTok that hit 150K views but brought only about 100-200 downloads. He calls it "silver or copper," with lots of views and little conversion.
- Winning format:
  - He saw a competitor couples app posting a shared-whiteboard-style video that was doing well on TikTok.
  - He built it as a presentation mode of his existing shared whiteboard feature in a day and posted videos the next day.
  - The fifth video reached 490,000 views. He scaled from 1 a day to 2 and then 3 a day.
  - On Instagram the first one got 300,000 views and several reached millions within two weeks.
- Format: an 8-10 second screen recording of him using the app, ending by showing the app. It converted "like 20 times better" than the two-by-two slideshows.
- He did not analyze why it worked: "I knew if it's going viral for them, I can make it go viral for me." He says TikTok and Instagram rarely correlate, so you test on both.
- Principle: videos that look organic but show the app are "gold," versus the "silver" of unrelated meme formats.
- Doof used the old slideshow format and got one 5M-view video that drove a bunch of downloads, which he says shows "all it really takes is one good video." He still thinks going mega-viral without mentioning the app is worth trying, but should not be the only bet.

## Building apps: copy proven niches, build for yourself, bet on frames

- Idea rule: everything he's built "has a successful counterpart." He builds a personal version of something that already works and takes marketing cues from existing apps in that niche.
- He builds what he wants to exist (Curiosity Quench, Doof, Lovely). Doof was a 30-day challenge to go from zero to $1,000, which he hit in the first month using Instagram, TikTok and Twitter.
- He thinks "the idea is the biggest part" and any app can be made interesting by how it's framed.
- Buyer insight on couples apps: if a girlfriend asks her boyfriend to buy the app, he'll probably pay, and two users enjoying it together are more likely to talk about it.
- He keeps his marketing from looking like ads: people won't watch ads, so consumer app content should look organic.
- On early failures: before coding, he spent about 3 years on print-on-demand, Amazon KDP, crypto and newsletters. The lesson was to make things for himself and then work out how to bring people to them, and that a business has to solve a problem.
- Advice: "Get it out there and see what happens. Start making videos... you're going to be 10 times ahead of the person who's been sitting on their hands."

## PostBridge, the $1M offer he turned down, and staying small

- He built PostBridge, a simple social scheduling tool (upload a video, post to all channels), because posting by hand took about an hour a day. Batching content for a week or two cut it to about 10 minutes of work.
- It took off immediately after launch and displaced the mobile app he was working on.
- In April (of the prior year), Sahil from Gumroad tweeted an offer of $1 million for it. At the time PostBridge was making about $12,000 a month and growing 30-40% per month. Jack declined because he was having fun and it was at its high. It was still making about $20K MRR at the time of the interview.
- He weighs fun heavily: "if I'm going to be working for myself and building, I want to make sure it's enjoyable." He accepts some apps may make less (for example, not using hard paywalls).
- Stance: he wants to stay solo and very small, so "profitability is the number one metric to be able to keep going." His mindset hasn't changed since earning money, only less stress.
- Scaling Lovely: the app is premium so paid ads don't make sense (he hasn't used them). He reinvested about $1,000 to hire people found through Twitter and word of mouth to post the winning format across 20-40 accounts, aiming for hundreds of videos a day. He says it's still in the testing phase, with Instagram the best bet.
