## Why Jack builds for himself and keeps Post Bridge simple

- Jack built Post Bridge in October 2024 for himself because every other social scheduler was "too expensive" and had "too much features."
- He made it "as simple as possible and as useful as possible for me." It turned out to be useful for others, which he did not expect in a red ocean market with many similar tools.
- He admits the approach does not solve everything: "you could make something that, you know, maybe only three other people want."
- His angle is value at a fair price. He does not argue "this is better, that's better." He points people to the pricing page and the features, because the tool does similar things and works.
- Because he uses the tool daily, he is incentivized to improve it for one specific customer. Right now that customer is mobile app founders.
- He does not compete on small things. He gives something of value, and people share it with friends. He says word of mouth has never gone as far for him as it has here.

## How he uses Post Bridge to market a mobile app

- His app Curiosity Quench had about 60,000 downloads, many from organic videos.
- He makes a roughly six-second video and posts it to Instagram, TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Twitter threads, Bluesky and more. Posting manually everywhere means uploading the same video each time. Post Bridge saves him ten to twenty minutes per video per day.
- After finding a video format that worked well, he automated it. In Post Bridge he fills in a simple template and it outputs the video. He does this daily and uploads to all channels from the app, running it close to autopilot ("not exactly autopilot").
- He also uses it for text posts. A tweet goes to Twitter, Threads, Facebook, LinkedIn and Bluesky, even though he only opens Twitter himself.
- His pinned tweet is a free guide to how he grew his mobile app with organic content. It is one of his most popular tweets because it is genuinely useful, and readers then go use the tool.

## Attention as well as time: why he built a scheduler

- Curiosity Quench's mission was to help people stop scrolling. To promote it, he had to scroll while manually posting videos, and "you get sucked into the loop every time you manually post."
- So Post Bridge saves attention as well as time. He can post videos without opening Instagram and TikTok, which he calls "dreadful suck you in apps." He does not open them for months at a time.
- On the objection that he adds to the noise, he says he does not see a way to avoid that short of "not existing on the Internet."
- He does not moderate content. He leans on the official platform APIs, which moderate content themselves.
- He says he does not prevent abuse. He expects algorithms to push content people enjoy, and he hopes people make their own decisions about what to watch and post.

## Keeping the product lean as a solo founder

- Jack mostly builds for what he uses day to day: "How can this make my day simpler and easier?"
- He adds a requested feature only if it is "very reasonable" and does not add a "super complex" workflow.
- For each change he asks whether it will bloat the software and make it less valuable to a normal first-time user.
- His rule: "if it still can remain simple, I'll add the feature." He wants to center the product on being as simple as possible.
- The next focus is content generation. He frames it as its own product that does not complicate the base tool; it only adds optionality.
- He does not use AI inside Post Bridge, which keeps it "bare bones as possible."

## Guided content generation instead of done-for-you AI

- For visual content (video and images), his plan is aimed at businesses driving organic growth.
- The vision has several parts:
  - Templates that have worked and produced results.
  - Instructions on how to use them.
  - Guidance on evoking a reaction or emotion from the target audience.
  - Ideas for each niche, since niches differ.
- Goal: guide someone to a valuable result, such as getting users to their product.
- He sizes the scope carefully: "if I go too narrow or go too wide, no one knows where to start." People do not know where to start, so he wants to provide a starting point. It is a "guided approach," not done for you completely.
- On shelf life, formats vary by platform, so he is always iterating. A format may work for five months or a year. He says people usually switch it out before results stop, because it feels repetitive. His rule: "if you have something that works, you keep using it," and when it stops working, iterate until you find something that works again.
- The content-generation aim is to help people find what is current. He admits that is hard because things change quickly, and says he will try rather than promise perfection.

## Social media as a public journal: doing first, posting second

- Jack uses Twitter "like a personal public journal." He posts raw, unfiltered thoughts, often triggered by something he sees while building.
- His expectation: half the time no one sees a post, and about a tenth of the time a normal thought turns out controversial or big.
- He argues that because so much content is not human, honest posts with visible flaws will stand out and build something valuable "in a place where there's a lot of junk."
- He never aimed to grow Twitter. His goal was to make a valuable business and document it. The loop is "I post, I do something, I post, I do something," and it grows the business. Followers are a byproduct.
- He says the common opposite approach (grow followers to grow a business) does not work well, because people can tell when someone is really doing something versus "tweeting aphorisms."
- He admits to sometimes doing things so he can post about them, and works to play the game in a way that also serves his mental health, since constant posting drains you.
- He uses no AI for tweets: "I just post what's on my mind."

## Staying kind and not polarizing

- He says being kind is partly conscious and partly personality ("people pleaser"). He accepts that people will be mad whatever he says, but there is a nicer way to say things.
- He tries to keep his opinions objective and non-alienating.
- He wants to attract the right people. After about a year, his replies are mostly very nice.
- He has blocked maybe three people, mostly mutes, and has never blocked a topic or word. He says his feed is curated by focusing on what he wants to see more of.
- He cites a kind, positive founder who inspired him.

## Engagement and notifications

- He checks Twitter daily and replies to people there. Other platforms he checks roughly once a week or once a month, if he remembers.
- He does not automate replies, because it would be robotic. He argues there is good in not managing everything: "people realize you're a human."
- He keeps social media notifications off for everything. His only push notifications are texts from a few people.
- His view is that you cannot get to everything on the Internet. He thinks the original point of the internet was doing things when you want, and he is "unflipping it."

## Learning to code with AI and security

- He uses Cursor to code. Less than two years before this interview (June 2025) he did not know how to code beyond basic HTML and CSS.
- He calls AI "an amazing tool for just rapid pace learning and understanding because no one's judging you."
- Outside coding, he does not use AI in his workflow, including customer service and blog posts. His reasoning: "If it's not a thousand percent beneficial, I don't bother." He likes the human aspect and the mistakes, and says he is "skeptical still."
- He does not object to others using AI to help write.
- On security: early on he understood that security mattered and checked things. He says many skip checks and get away with it until they have users and get hacked.
- He was attacked twice in a few months, partly because he was a proponent of vibe coding. No user data was harmed, and the main cost was a bigger bill from his provider.
- He learned the basics of securing sites quickly. He says it is a cat and mouse game and nobody is 100% secure.
- His advice: with customer information, "you can prompt your way to making something decently secure," but you should consider security. A hack or leak can end a business overnight. You should be careful, not scared.
