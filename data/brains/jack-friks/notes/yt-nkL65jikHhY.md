## Validate with media before building the product

- Jack's core idea: the best way to validate a product is to build the media first and see if people want it, before building the product.
- Anyone can build an app now, so the hard question is whether people want it and whether it can be distributed.
- He validated his first mobile app this way. Day one, he posted a video mocking up a solution to "I'm stuck on my phone all day". It got about 2,000 views and 10 comments.
- He kept posting. About 10 videos later, an AI-generated mockup video got about 30,000 views.
- He then made a sign-up page and got about 1,000 users to the web app in the first month and a half, even though "it was so bad".
- His view: if a content format with your problem as the hook can't go viral organically, the pain probably isn't big enough to build a business on. The bigger the pain, the more organic potential.
- He suggests organic social can validate the idea with just a Figma screenshot or mockup.

## The "media as vehicle, caption as call to action" model

- Jack's rule: don't make the content an ad. Organic ads get roughly 100-200 views every time.
- The less call to action in the video, the better the views. A few viral pieces lift the whole account and boost its other videos.
- His top format is a 6-second, four-image post with no call to action in the video. The app promotion lives only in the caption and the pinned comment.
- That one template drove about 50,000 downloads. Videos hit 1 to 5 million views, and some days brought thousands of downloads.
- He says he was often too lazy to even comment. The caption alone did the work.
- Content must still be related to the app. Putting the app in one of the four images has also reached a million views, but it is less likely to go "way viral", because people dislike ads on their for-you page.
- Mixing in a little salesy content works once the account has real reach and isn't stuck at 200-view walls.

## Finding viral formats

- His process is not very tactical. He scrolls an Instagram account that follows only his target niche, then likes and comments on videos.
- He looks at what drove the comments, why people watched to the end, and whether it can be replicated as a format.
- If it can, he saves it to a folder and recreates it in CapCut or another short-form editor. If it works, he turns it into a template.
- He started by testing about 50+ formats before landing on one that hit a million views.
- Engagement angles that worked: relatable ("that's me") posts and deliberately outrage-bait ones, such as misspelling "astrology" and "astronomy" so people argue in the comments. He has cooled on the outrage angle.
- If a niche has no established format, he says you can still find an angle. Post two a day for about two months and test hundreds of videos.
- Misses are part of it. Many people quit because the numbers game is discouraging.

## Scaling volume across accounts and platforms

- Jack's limit on scale is how many videos you can make and how many warmed-up accounts you can post to.
- Accounts must be warmed up. They should have been used like a human, so they aren't suppressed at 200 views a video.
- His rough rule at the time was about 3 videos per account, spaced 3-4 hours apart, divided across the number of accounts.
- He posts the same media to all channels but optimizes for one. He targets Instagram because he scrolls it and finds formats there.
- Nearly 99% of his views for the four-image format came from Instagram. TikTok gave only some extra views.
- He advises one channel at a time. In 2025 it is free to cross-post to the others, and he has not opened TikTok in about three months while it still brought thousands of downloads.
- Pick the channel where your buyer actually spends time.

## Funnel and product fit

- The funnel worked well because it pointed at a mobile app, which is easy to reach from mobile video. Jack estimates a web app would get about 10% of the conversions.
- He still thinks the approach works for web apps, just less efficiently.

## Turning an internal tool into Postbridge

- Jack built his own scheduler, Postbridge, because competitors charged about $50 a month for the number of accounts he managed, and he "just hates spending money".
- He posted a tweet teaser with a UI screenshot saying he was working on something cool. It got about 1,000 likes and 200,000 impressions, and someone said he should sell it.
- He added a creative section to make videos and templates inside the tool. At the time it had only three templates, and finding viral formats was the part he was focusing on.
- Results at the time: about 4,000 videos created in a month and a half, with roughly 300 customers.
- The mobile app's growth funded the idea, and Postbridge now makes more than the mobile app that it scaled.

## Building and what to build

- His build advice: start with any AI helper (Cursor, GitHub Copilot, ChatGPT), get a prototype out, and don't overthink it.
- He thinks mobile apps are not much harder than web apps. The costs are the lag before results, store review, and no live users right away.
- He believes web and B2B is easier for a solo founder to make substantially more money on.
- He builds what he needs for himself. Both of his main ideas worked out, so the hit rate from "build for myself" has been high.
- His plan is to polish Postbridge, then use it to promote other mobile apps or scale one very far.
- He is skeptical of paid ads personally and has leaned organic because it is free to start. He says that is a personal preference and acknowledges paid ads work.
