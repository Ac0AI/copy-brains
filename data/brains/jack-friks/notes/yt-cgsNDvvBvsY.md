## Warming up a new TikTok/Instagram account before posting

- Jack's rule for a brand-new account: no posting for the first two days. He scrolls his niche for 15 to 50 minutes a day, and likes, comments on and follows accounts in that niche.
- The goal is for the algorithm to learn the niche, so the For You page feeds that niche back.
- For his food diary app account, he saved many videos from this scrolling to recreate later.
- If an account has already posted and gets under 100 views, he says to stop posting for a few days, scroll the niche, and then post once a day.
- If posting several times a day, space posts a few hours apart at minimum.
- If the For You page still isn't showing the niche, he suggests making a new account.
- On shadow banning: "Shadow Banning is kind of a thing but it's more or less if you're not acting like a human you're going to get less reach so act like a human."
- He says to use the platform for the first week so it treats you as a genuine user, then start posting.
- He says views can also depend on location. He is based in the US or Canada, and posting from elsewhere might change results.

## Finding formats to recreate instead of inventing content

- Jack's process: find a format that has already done well, recreate it, post every day, and try different formats until one beats the others. Then reuse that format and repeat.
- He says to "try new formats", not "the same video over and over again" while hoping one goes viral.
- Source of ideas: videos he saved while warming up the account.
- Examples he reviewed in his niche:
  - A video with AI food pictures, which turned out to be an ad for a TikTok Shop.
  - A single grocery cart photo of healthy items with 655,000 likes, which he calls very easy to copy.
  - A video with the text "Society's normalized junk food so much that when someone eats real food it's considered dieting", over about 5 seconds of quick shots.
- He picked the third one: its message (junk food is normal, eating healthy gets called dieting) fits his app's theme of quitting junk food and eating healthy.
- He skipped a "no carbs" video because most of his content should be about quitting junk food and eating healthy.
- Expectations: "a lot of the time it takes like a week to get something that works." He expects a few weeks to land a good piece of content and work out the niche.
- He says a million views overnight can happen, but it's unlikely. Making more videos makes it more likely.

## Recreating a viral video in CapCut (live walkthrough)

1. Get the original sound. Copy the video link, paste it into a TikTok downloader (he used Snaptik) and download it. If one downloader fails, try another. Watching an ad gets the HD version.
2. Drag the video into CapCut, put it on the track, and detach the audio.
3. Replace the visuals with his own images: put the original's text on top, then swap in his own pictures for the reference clips.
4. Count the images in the reference, since the pacing matters. His version was about 6 seconds with roughly 14 to 17 cuts, which he said is a good length per image.
5. Use the reference clip as a timing guide, then trim to match its length and delete the rest.
6. Raise the sound a bit and export.
7. Send the file to his phone and post natively.

- He says there are "a million ways" to do this, but this is how he does it and it works.
- Once he finds a format that works, he makes a CapCut template. He also mentions making them with code or with Postbridge.
- He sees the discovery stage as testing formats, not perfecting a template.
- Text: he tried several fonts and settled on Arial Bold for the default look.
- He tried a small shadow on the text, disliked it and reverted it.
- He argues the text being slightly hard to read can help, since it looks more natural. At about 6 seconds, people have to watch it two or three times before skipping, "which is exactly what we want."
- He checks the video on his phone before posting, since small text may not read on a desktop preview. It read fine on the phone.
- He adds small funny touches, such as a comment-style overlay. He says these can help a video spread without taking away from the message.

## Making free AI and stock images for videos (ImageFX)

- Jack uses ImageFX, Google's free image tool, to generate the food photos.
- Prompts he used: "a plate of food top down", "healthy meal POV", "peeled mango", "healthy grocery cart POV", "healthy breakfast fruits", "healthy snack", "sweet potato mash dinner plate".
- He says POV prompts seem to work well. Portrait orientation (9:16) suits TikTok better.
- He sometimes grabs real photos from Google instead of AI.
- Quality rules:
  - Use varied foods. His first batch was the same thing over and over, so he swapped in different meals, snacks, a grocery shop and veggies.
  - Pick images that look like natural, real photos.
  - Reorder or replace weak images, and zoom in where an image needs it.
- On curation: "you do have to curate you can't just put out slop... doesn't get views for a good reason."
- He keeps a library of previously generated images in CapCut to reuse.

## Posting and call-to-action setup

- Jack posts the same video to both TikTok and Instagram.
- He posts it natively from his phone with the original sound. He says you don't have to pick the official TikTok sound, as it usually recognizes it anyway.
- Caption:
  - He reuses the original's hashtags (food, diet, healthy, relatable, for you page).
  - He added a call to action with the app's search term, written like "quit junk for good using our app (food diary)", so people can search for it.
  - He also tried "find food that fuels you best", and says the call to action doesn't have to be perfect.
- When the video itself has no call to action, he makes the top comment one telling people to download the app. He copies the caption into a comment, adds a couple of emoji, and pins it on Instagram.
- He says if a video gets 1K views but no downloads, keep posting, keep trying new things, and make sure there's a call to action in the video, caption or comments. He also recommends a branded account.
- He didn't add a link in this post.
- After posting, he scrolls the home feed for about 5 seconds and interacts with similar videos. He watches them through, saves some, and comments. He says this isn't required.
- Targeting: his own followers (such as stream viewers) inflate views but aren't the target audience. An earlier video got 800 views mostly for that reason.
- If an account is in good standing, he keeps posting manually for now.

## Why content is the main driver of app downloads

- Jack says getting people to download the app is "the biggest part of anything".
- On whether an app needs an Instagram or social account: "you really do need it... who's going to download your app if you don't talk about it". He says other approaches exist, but he thinks it's needed.
- He says "execution really is everything" now that apps are easier to build.
