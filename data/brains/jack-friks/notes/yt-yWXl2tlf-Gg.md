## Why Jack built PostBridge: scratching his own itch in a red ocean

- Jack built PostBridge in October (about 7 months before the interview) because existing social schedulers were "too expensive" and had "too many features."
- He made it as simple and useful as possible for himself, and it turned out to be useful for others too. He says he didn't expect it to do as well as it did.
- Arvid describes it as a business doing roughly $13-18k a month and helping thousands of creators and founders. Jack didn't confirm a specific figure himself.
- The problem it solved: posting the same short video to Instagram, TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Twitter, Bluesky and more meant manual uploads on each. PostBridge saves him 10-20 minutes per video per day.
- He acknowledges that building for yourself "doesn't solve everything" because you could build something only three other people want. In his case the market was bigger.

## Competing in a crowded market with simplicity, fair pricing and a point of view

- The space is a red ocean with many similar tools. Jack doesn't compete on small feature-by-feature claims ("this is better, that's better").
- His angle: "something of value for a very fair price." He points people to the pricing page and features, because the tool does similar things and works.
- He uses his own tool daily, so he is incentivized to improve it for a specific persona. The current persona is mobile app founders.
- His pinned tweet explains exactly how he grew a mobile app with organic content using PostBridge. It is one of his most popular tweets because it is genuinely useful, and people then go and use the tool.
- Word of mouth has gone further than with anything he has built before: "people appreciate that a lot and they share with their friends."

## Keeping a product lean as a solo founder

- Main filter: build for what he uses day to day and ask "how can this make my day simpler and easier?"
- He adds a feature if it is reasonable and won't add a complex workflow.
- With every change he asks whether it will bloat the software and make it less valuable to a normal first-time user.
- Core positioning is "as simple as possible." If a feature keeps the product simple, he adds it.
- Next focus is content generation, which he treats as almost its own product. It is an input stream into the tool and adds optionality without making the base tool more complex.
- He has no AI tooling inside PostBridge, which keeps it "bare bones."

## Marketing mobile apps with daily organic video

- His earlier app, Curiosity Quench (an anti-scrolling app), reached about 60,000 downloads, largely from organic short videos.
- Workflow: make a roughly 6-second video, then post it to Instagram, TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Twitter and Bluesky at the same time.
- After finding a video format that worked well, he automated it. In PostBridge you upload the basic parts of a template and it outputs the video.
- He now makes the template-based video every day, uploads it to all channels for his app and runs it close to autopilot.
- The irony that started it: promoting an app meant to reduce scrolling required him to scroll while manually posting. PostBridge let him post without opening the "suck you in" apps. He doesn't open Instagram or TikTok for months at a time.
- He treats time saved as the highest-impact kind of product value.

## Planned content generation: guided templates, not done-for-you AI

- Vision: templates that have produced results, instructions on how to use them, and guidance on evoking an emotional reaction from the target audience, with ideas per niche.
- Goal: help businesses drive organic growth and get users to their product.
- Reasoning: if the scope is too narrow or too wide, "no one knows where to start." He wants to provide a starting point, "not done for you completely, but like a guided approach."
- Formats go stale at different rates by platform, so he says you should always be iterating.
- Observation on iteration: a video format may work for 5 months or a year, but most people drop it because it feels repetitive, even while it still works. His rule: if it works, keep using it; when it stops, iterate until you find something that works again.
- The product aims to help people find what is current. He says he won't do it perfectly but will try.

## Building in public: do things, then talk about them

- He says follower growth was never his goal. His goal was a valuable business, and he documents it on Twitter, which has about 40k followers.
- The sequence: do something, post about it, which grows the business, with followers as a byproduct. He contrasts this with people who try to grow followers to grow a business: "you can tell if somebody's really doing something or they're just tweeting aphorisms."
- He uses Twitter as a "personal public journal" and posts unfiltered stray thoughts. About half of them get no attention, and roughly a tenth are controversial or hit.
- He uses no AI for text posts.
- He is kind by choice. He tries to make his points objective and nice, believing people will be mad regardless but there is a nicer way to say things. As a result his replies are mostly friendly.
- His view on where social media is heading: because so much content is not human, honest, flawed, raw posting will stand out and build something valuable amid junk.
- Pitfall he names: the "monkey brain" urge to do things just so he can post about them. He tries to play the game in a way that also protects his mental health.

## Engagement, notifications and moderation

- He engages mainly on Twitter and checks other platforms about once a week or month. Replying everywhere would be robotic and unsustainable, and "people realize you're a human" when you can't get to everything.
- All social notifications are off. His only push notifications are texts from a few people.
- He doesn't moderate content in PostBridge. Because it connects through official platform APIs, the platforms moderate themselves, and he trusts algorithms and people's own choices.

## Using AI to learn to code, and security lessons

- Less than two years before the interview he didn't know how to code beyond basic HTML and CSS. He uses Cursor and calls AI a non-judgmental teacher for fast learning.
- Outside coding he mostly avoids AI, including for customer service and content, unless it is clearly very beneficial. He isn't against others using it.
- He was attacked twice in a few months, partly because he was a vocal proponent of vibe coding. No user data was harmed. The main cost was a bigger bill from his provider.
- He learned the security basics quickly and says you can "prompt your way to making something decently secure."
- His advice: if you handle customer information, take security seriously, because a breach can end the business overnight. Don't be paralyzed by fear, but know someone could attack you.
