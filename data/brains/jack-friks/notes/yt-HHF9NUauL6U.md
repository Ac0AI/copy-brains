## Warming up a new account before posting (Jack's B2C app playbook)

Jack's plan for getting 1,000 downloads to a B2C app for $0 is organic Instagram and TikTok content. He says about 95% of his results came from Instagram. His earlier app, Curiosity Quench, reached 100,000 downloads across three accounts that used the same format.

- **Use a brand new account.** Don't reuse an old one. An old account carries its past interests, and the algorithm keeps pushing content to that old audience, which is "very hard" to change. Zero followers is fine. A single video with 200k views will bring followers.
- **Don't post for the first 2-3 days.** Jack prefers 3 days or more. He says: "if your account is not warm it doesn't matter how good your content is it will not get reach." Skipping this step is why most people get under 300 views per video.
- **Use the account like a human for 15 minutes a day.** Jack sets an actual timer. Do this per account and per platform.
- **Engage only with videos in your niche.** Skip everything else. Watch videos to the end, comment, and follow niche accounts. Don't mass-like or mass-comment, because that looks like a bot.
- **Search for your niche to shape the feed.** The For You page won't match your app at first. Jack searched "healthy foods" for his food-diary app and followed niche accounts. After about 10-15 interactions the feed fills with relevant content.
- **Warm up on your phone.** Jack believes TikTok in particular trusts mobile devices more, because a real device is harder to spoof than web.
- **Save ideas while you scroll.** After 2-3 days you should have roughly 10-30 videos to recreate for your app.
- **Use one account at a time per phone.** Jack tried warming two at once and got no reach. If he ran six accounts, he'd use a VPN or separate phones.

## Posting cadence and manual posting for new accounts

Jack treats the first week of posting as a period for looking human to the algorithm.

- **Post the first ~7 videos manually, from your phone.** Scheduling tools post through an API, and "bots post things through the API, humans don't." Move the file to your phone and upload it in the app.
- **Post one video per day for the first week.** Two on some days is acceptable. His maximum ever was about 3 a day to one account, on Instagram.
- **If you post more than once a day, space posts 3-4 hours apart.** Don't post "seven reels within 1 hour" or one every 10 minutes. His view of TikTok is that it "hates when you spam videos like every 10 minutes."
- **Reply to comments.** This builds presence on a new account.
- **Start slow and raise volume gradually.** Spamming usually leaves a new account with fewer than 200 views per video. If an account's videos already get 5,000+ views each, more frequent posting (still 2-4 hours apart) may be fine.
- **Switch to a scheduler after the first week.** Jack plans to schedule through Post Bridge, the tool he built. Afterward he only opens the apps to reply to comments.
- **Add a second account only once the first is working.** His rule is to wait until the first account gets thousands of views a day. Otherwise, post more often to one warm account.
- **A bad start is normal.** If the first 10 videos get only 10-100 views, stick to the plan. Stop posting for 2 days, keep warming up, and take it slowly.
- **Expect a few thousand views from one video within about two weeks.** If none reaches that, slow down.
- **Multiple platforms:** Post the same videos to TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, Twitter/X threads and Facebook. Pinterest also needs warming up. Jack added Pinterest to Post Bridge but never warmed it up, and he gets zero views there.

## Finding viral video formats and recreating them

Jack doesn't invent formats from scratch. He finds videos that already work in his niche and recreates them for his app.

- **Source of ideas:** the warmed-up For You page. For his food-diary app Doof, he found photo carousels of healthy meals with tens of thousands of likes. One had 82,000 likes, and another had 26,000.
- **Recreate the pattern.** For Doof, that means a photo carousel of food with a POV caption and trendy audio. The last slide shows the app logging the food, how it made him feel, and notes.
- **Use any source of images.** AI-generated images, internet images, Pinterest, or your own photos all work.
- **Adapt formats across niches.** Jack's example is an informational photo post for a habit tracker, such as "how to build healthy habits that last long."
- **Make it valuable first.** The value can be a laugh, making viewers feel seen, teaching them something, or giving them recipes.
- **Don't be the only judge.** Jack says "not everyone is you," so something you find boring may do well. Post it, iterate, and improve each video a little.
- **Tools:** CapCut (free, runs on desktop or phone, about a day to learn), Post Bridge, and each platform's native editor. Jack made videos in TikTok's native editor for his first 4-5 months.
- **Hooks:** Jack has no special prompting strategy. He makes apps for himself and makes the videos he would find funny or interesting. Daily posting lets you test different hooks.

## Never make an ad: value content with a comment-section call to action

Jack's main rule for organic app marketing is that a video must not look like an ad.

- "Never make an ad. Ads do not perform, they don't go viral." Straight ads get pushed down to about 200 views. If you're making an ad, he says, "might as well just spend money on ads."
- **Sell solutions and results.** Don't put the product in front of people's faces.
- **Product demos:** A demo longer than about 5 seconds is probably an ad, and viewers skip ads about 90% of the time. Standalone product demos typically get under 1,000 views. Demos can work as a short clip at the end (about 5 seconds, "download this") over background footage of someone using the app.
- **Better approach:** Show screenshots or bits of the app's UI inside valuable content. Viewers then ask in the comments "what app is this?" and visit the app page.
- **Call to action:** Comment on the video, pin the comment, and put the download link in the caption. Jack calls this "the best way to do it."
- **First videos:** You don't need an app call to action in them. Get some traction first.
- **Why this works:** Jack says most conversions come from the comment section. A video with 3 million views converts even if only a small share of viewers download.

## Formats Jack has used and the results

- **"2x2 fade" hobby format for Curiosity Quench.** Images of hobbies, text and a catchy sound. After about 200 of these videos, several got millions of views, including one at 600k. Once four in a row hit millions of views, Jack built the format into Post Bridge. The format works because viewers "feel seen" and fill the comments. It contains no product mention, only small pieces of the UI.
- **Talking to the camera about what he built that day.** Jack did this 360 times on his personal account. He posted the same kind of video 343 days in a row. Most got about 4K views, then one hit 1.3 million. Others reached 800k and still brought in downloads.
- **Habit Garden (his friend Mark's habit app).** Jack made a new account, warmed it up for 3 days, and posted 12 videos. The first videos got a few hundred views. After the fifth video he reused the 2x2 meme format from his other app, with memes, emojis and a controversial angle. One video got 5,000 views and the next got 5 million, which brought a few thousand visitors to the app. Other accounts then copied the videos.
- **Keep what works.** If a format still produces results, "milk the crap out of" it. A brand account doesn't need new formats while the old one works. Jack still uses the same format daily on his own apps.
- **Don't rely on a single format.** The 2x2 format is limited, and higher-converting formats exist.
- **Expect many misses.** Jack posted hundreds of videos (about 800 on one account) before seeing traction. One hit shows what works, and then you repeat it.
- **Platform notes:**
  - Instagram pushes content that resonates with an audience, regardless of geography. He has seen people in Asia do well with US audiences (example: the Rise app).
  - TikTok favors the US and Canada and is hit or miss for him.
  - Trendy audio helps because people like good-sounding music. Being on the official trending sound page isn't the reason.
  - US reach on TikTok: some people use a US account with a US SIM, or a US account with a VPN and Post Bridge, which has US servers.

## Doof launch and 30-day challenge plan

- **Challenge:** Build an app in 30 days, then reach 1,000 users and $100 in revenue. This stream is day 8. The app was already live on the App Store.
- **Day-one results:** About 300 downloads and 23 trials started from a single tweet, with no other marketing.
- **What Doof is:** A food diary to quit junk food. It tracks how foods make you feel (digestion, sleep, temptation), not calories. Jack says logging is "10 to 100 times easier" than a calorie tracker.
- **Marketing split:** Jack says getting downloads is "90% of the game" of making money in the App Store. Once the app is built, he stops coding and spends his time on marketing.
- **Plan:**
  - Warm up TikTok and Instagram accounts on his phone.
  - Save 10-30 ideas.
  - Post reels first to build discovery and followers.
  - Add photo carousels later, which he says have been performing well and are easy to make.
- **Why one viral video matters:** A video with 1,000 or 100,000 downloads also boosts the app's natural App Store ranking.
- **Who it works for:** Wide-appeal B2C apps (health and wellness suit TikTok and Instagram). He says it doesn't work easily for B2B.
- **Niche apps:** For a narrow audience like a YouTube organizer, memes won't work. Jack suggests higher prices, paid ads, or face-to-camera videos that explain the tool, and says it's harder.
- **Finance and similar categories:** These may need LinkedIn or Reddit, which is a different strategy. Jack hasn't marketed an app there.

## Paywall, onboarding and tech stack for Doof

- **Paywall:** Doof has a hard paywall with no freemium. Jack says a hard paywall "is just going to make more money." He uses freemium on some other apps. If a business is chasing a revenue goal, he goes hard paywall and may free up features later.
- **Onboarding:** Extensive, at least about 10 pages, if the app blocks users behind a paywall. The onboarding has to explain why the app will help them, or they delete it.
- **Free logging before the paywall:** Users can skip the first paywall and log one food. They see how logging works, their dashboard, streak, food mood and recommended foods. The hard paywall appears when they try to log a second food.
- **Stack:** React Native with Expo, Cursor, and sometimes Supabase for the backend. He uses a boilerplate with payments and onboarding already set up, linked in his Discord.
- **Scale:** He built with scale in mind. Hosting cost a few more dollars as usage grew.
- **Learning to build:** For people new to coding, Jack suggests cloning the boilerplate into Cursor, reading the Expo docs, and pasting every error into ChatGPT.
- **Post Bridge:** A scheduler Jack built to post to Instagram, TikTok, Twitter and Facebook at once. It has no API yet, but one is in the works. He has used it for every Curiosity Quench post for four months, and the posts weren't shadow-banned because the accounts were warm.
- **Paid creators:** Jack has little experience. He prefers hiring creators to post on branded accounts, such as "app number two", instead of on the creator's own account. He has messaged creators from his personal account.
