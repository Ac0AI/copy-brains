## Starting from a 1k MRR side project: quitting the job and the first-year goal

- Damon left Cisco in May 2021, about 4-5 months after launching Testimonial. He had been a software engineer for 8 years.
- Trigger: during Covid his daughter (1.5-2 years old) stayed home 24/7 and his wife also worked from home. He was under heavy pressure juggling standups for his day job and childcare, and felt his day-job output was suffering.
- He decided family was the top priority: "it's only once for a dad to spend more time with her in her young age."
- Testimonial had just hit $1,000 MRR in April, which he took as the signal to double down and give up the engineering salary.
- He publicly tweeted a commitment to his wife: reach $100k ARR by the end of year one, or go back to a software engineering job. He notes getting hired in Silicon Valley would not be hard, so the downside felt manageable.
- Result at the time of the interview (June 2022): about $16k MRR, nearly $200k ARR, as a solo founder with no employees.
- His reasoning for staying committed: he had spent years dedicated to a single employer, so he should give his own project the same long-term consistency.

## How Testimonial grew out of Lonely.dev and early validation

- Damon's first product was Lonely.dev, a video-first community for makers and indie hackers, where members record short video updates and ask for help. He built it during Covid when he felt isolated working from home.
- Making videos for that community pushed him to talk on camera and expose himself publicly, and he then moved to Twitter for broader reach.
- Testimonial was a pivot: Lonely.dev already had video recording, sharing, hosting and streaming, so he reused much of the code. He launched the MVP in about two weeks.
- The idea came from his own pain: he built side projects with no reviews, then saw a section of video testimonials on a Shopify page and thought of productizing the video part.
- He launched with a lifetime deal at $199 and sold roughly 20 copies, about $3-4k in two weeks, which validated demand.
- Based on a Product Hunt comment suggesting a subscription model, he switched to subscriptions only and has focused on recurring revenue since.

## Build in public as an accidental flywheel

- He did not plan to build in public. He started tweeting to document his journey and the audience grew beyond what he expected. His first customers came from Twitter.
- His core strategy: "build stuff that my customers want."
- Flywheel as he describes it: early customers give feedback, he ships the requested features, happy customers recommend the product, and each shipped feature gives him more content to share publicly, which brings more customers.
- Example: the "Wall of Love" (embeddable testimonial display) was suggested by early customers. The first version only let customers collect videos, with no way to distribute them.
- As a developer new to marketing, he leaned on his existing strength (shipping product) rather than trying to become a marketing guru.

## Advice for developers who don't want to do marketing

- Building a business is not just launching a product. A product launch does not bring millions of customers automatically; marketing and sales have to be learned.
- Commit to at least one to two years, otherwise it stays a hobby that only builds technical skill.
- Start with a niche audience and a simple product for a specific need. A clear value proposition and an easy-to-use product are easier to sell.
- His warning: with a CRM with hundreds of integrations and no marketing help, "how are you going to sell?"
- Validate the market with a simple tool, then let customers determine the features instead of building what you fantasize is cool and finding months later that nobody wants it.
- Listen to people who actually have the problem and are waiting for someone to solve it.

## Finding the right customer segment and handling churn

- His initial assumption was that Testimonial would suit influencers collecting testimonials from fans. It didn't work: influencers already have tons of social proof and reputation, they are solo and price-sensitive, and at $20-30 per month they churned quickly if the product didn't justify the cost.
- He dug through who had actually subscribed and found agencies and marketing departments in sales-oriented companies. They paid and stayed longer, so he shifted his target to SMBs.
- Churn: it was historically around 2-3% monthly, and spiked to 5% the previous month.
- Collecting feedback showed customers called it too expensive, and most churners were very small businesses. Checking usage showed churners had collected few videos, while retained customers received many videos and got marketing assets from them.
- His focus: help customers collect more testimonials so they get value.
- On fixing churn, he prefers to look forward: ship a better product and more features rather than chase churned users, especially as a one-person business. Some churned users reactivate monthly after he ships features they wanted.
- He rejects paid or incentivized reviews: testimonials "should be candid."

## How to get testimonials from customers

- Ask at the moment of peak excitement. When a customer requests a feature and he ships it, he contacts them directly for feedback. If they say they love it, he asks for a testimonial then, because "the customer will be more likely to give you a testimonial when their excitement is at a big level."
- He does not wait a month after signup and cold-ask. He goes after each happy customer individually and only asks those who feel good about the product. A good product comes first.
- Automation he sees customers using: Zapier triggers from review platforms such as Trustpilot or Google. When a customer leaves a five-star text review, an email is triggered asking them for a video testimonial.
- Large companies he observes embed a testimonial link in marketing campaign emails whenever they launch a new product line, sending to millions of users, which shows up as traffic spikes on his monitoring.
- Other use case he noticed: a company running a testimonial campaign to collect video best wishes from employees for a retiring colleague in a remote team.

## Why testimonials are an ongoing asset

- Embedded Testimonial widgets carry "Powered by" branding, which gives him backlinks and social proof. A business embedding 10 videos shows visitors that others collected them with his tool, even if that customer later churns.
- Marketing teams need fresh testimonials for paid campaigns, since reusing one person repeatedly gets stale.
- Video testimonials on landing pages also build the trust that supports conversion.

## Prioritizing as a solo founder and running on gut

- Early days were chaotic: he tended to ship any request within hours and exhausted himself, then built a large backlog.
- His prioritization rules now:
  - If only one customer asks for a feature, put it on hold, note the request, tell the customer, and wait for more similar requests.
  - Prioritize by number of requests.
  - Prioritize features with revenue impact, such as paid-plan features that make people upgrade.
  - Follow his gut and vision for where Testimonial should go.
- On data vs gut: with a small customer base, he argues gut matters more. If it doesn't work out, "I'm a one-man business, I can pivot pretty fast," and it becomes a lesson.
- His days run late (bed around midnight or 1am) because of the workload.

## Mindset and influences

- Book recommendation: The Intelligent Investor by Benjamin Graham. It moved him from day trading to long-term value investing (he cites his Tesla stock gains). He applies the same idea to business: find an underserved niche, build a better solution, and be patient for several years.
- Founder inspiration: Pieter Levels, whose one-person business and "12 startups in 12 months" showed him he didn't have to climb a corporate engineering ladder.
- To regain energy in tough times, he spends time with his daughter and tries to look at problems the way she does.
