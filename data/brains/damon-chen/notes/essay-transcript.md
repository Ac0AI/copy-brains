## Damon's path from employee side projects to a bootstrapped full-time SaaS

- Damon worked at Cisco for eight years in Silicon Valley. When the pandemic hit in early 2020 and Cisco went remote, he felt lonely working from home.
- He read the Indie Hackers community but felt disconnected because it was all text. So he built a video-focused community, lonely.dev, "just for fun."
- He kept the day job while building. In late 2020 the pandemic dragged on, his daughter was about one and a half, and the family wanted to keep her out of daycare for safety. He took a six-month unpaid leave.
- The unpaid leave removed his paycheck, which created financial pressure. That pushed him to turn what he had already built into a standalone product.
- His rule: building a brand new product has big opportunity and time costs, so reuse as much existing code as possible.
- He had built several side projects, and every one shared the same problem: how to get social proof so buyers trust you. He saw video testimonials on a Shopify page and decided to build a video testimonial product.

## Building Testimonial from lonely.dev and launching without validation

- Damon reused roughly 80-90% of the lonely.dev code for Testimonial (the video recording and streaming features he had already shipped).
- He built the MVP in about a week and did not validate the idea beforehand. He says the launch results were a surprise.
- He launched on Product Hunt the next day after finishing the MVP, right before Christmas 2020.
- He sold a lifetime deal: 10 or 20 copies in the first two weeks, which brought in about $5,000 in revenue.
- The launch revenue was a strong validation that "it probably can be a thing."
- He says the Stripe notifications "energized" him during the first launch days.
- Context for the launch: his wife was sick (possible COVID) and isolated in the bedroom, so he was cooking and caring for his daughter alone. He only worked during her nap time and in the evenings after bedtime.
- His daily routine while on leave: play with his daughter, read books and play Lego during the day, and work on Testimonial during naps and after bedtime.

## Quitting in public and the risk framework

- Before quitting, Damon had a worst case in mind: losing one year of income (he put it around $200,000 in salary). His wife worked full-time, and after eight years at Cisco the family had enough savings to cover it.
- He promised his wife a goal: hit $100,000 ARR by the end of 2021, and if he could not, he would go back to a tech job. He says he made it before 2021 ended.
- He tweeted publicly that if Testimonial hit $1,000 MRR he would quit his job. He reached $1,000 MRR at the end of March 2021 and quit because he had promised the internet.
- About a month later he reached $2,000 MRR. Early signs like $1,000 and $2,000 MRR also gave his wife more confidence, since she was carrying the family's income.
- His framing: it is a balance between freedom and financial stability. Staying employed gave stability. Quitting gave freedom, meaning time with his young daughter and no daily progress updates to a boss, which he called "a mental torture" after eight years. Quitting meant it would take time to rebuild financial stability.
- He felt that the job had become boring and full of trivial tasks.

## Building in public

- Damon says his build-in-public journey started by accident. Because the first thing he launched was a community, he had to be its champion, posting videos and bringing people in from outside, so he restarted Twitter.
- He had signed up for Twitter when he moved to the US for his graduate degree but never used it. He says he had to become "an extrovert" and keep posting product updates.
- He wrote a thread summarizing what he shipped in 2020: most apps made $0, and only one made several thousand dollars within a few days. His point was "keep building and be patient."
- He shares almost everything publicly. His one exception is a private Slack group called 5k Twitter, for founders with several thousand followers on the way to 5,000.

## Why he took money from Calm Fund instead of traditional VC

- Damon took money from Calm Fund (Tyler Tringas) in May 2021, after quitting in March. Calm Fund's Gil reached out to him on Twitter when he hit about $2,000 MRR, then he spoke with Tyler.
- His awareness of VC was limited to TechCrunch and Y Combinator. He applied to Y Combinator several times and never got an interview request.
- He heard Tyler on an Indie Hackers podcast and realized there was a VC that gives you money to keep doing what you already do, without pressure to move faster.
- He says traditional VCs, including Y Combinator, often do not consider solo founders. Calm Fund does not penalize solo founders or side projects.
- His fit criteria: he has family and a young child, so he did not want to take money that added external pressure.
- His view: "the biggest pressure should not be external." The founder should have their own motivation to grow. Money cannot solve the growth problem; the founder has to.
- Community was the other reason. As a solo founder who had just quit, he wanted a community of founders a few miles ahead of him with a couple of million dollars in ARR, who could give practical advice and inspire him. He did not want a community of CEOs of NASDAQ-listed public companies.
- He treats Calm Fund as a hands-off investor: he took the money and did not need supervision, but values having a community to lean on during lows.

## Advice for side-project founders deciding whether to go full time on funding

- Figure out exactly what blocks you from quitting.
- If it is money, such as needing tens of thousands as a financial cushion, take the money. He says that makes the move nearly risk-free and opens a bigger opportunity to make an impact on your life and family.
- If it is confidence in the product, wait until you see initial product-market fit. Then, if you want faster growth, take money to fuel the business.
- He says he was well prepared before quitting: financial support and mental support from his wife, plus savings, plus a community of indie hacker founders to follow, which is why he took the funding from Calm Fund.
