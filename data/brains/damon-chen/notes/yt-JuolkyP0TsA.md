## Origin of testimonial.to: from Lonely Dev to Indie Log to video testimonials

- Damon built Lonely Dev in early 2020 to solve his own loneliness. He was a Cisco employee in the Bay Area forced to work from home, and he missed coffee chats with coworkers.
- He did not want another text-based community like Indie Hackers or a subreddit. He built a video-focused community where members post video updates and ask for help on camera.
- He never planned to monetize it. He had a paycheck, and the community was only tens to a few hundred active members. As sole moderator, 20 to 30 video updates a day was plenty to watch and reply to.
- Community members said "Lonely Dev" sounded inactive, so he renamed it Indie Log. He kept "Indie" from Indie Hackers for brand recognition and paired it with "log" for vlogging.
- He built several other products in 2020 that made no money. As an engineer looking for a shortcut, he wanted to add social proof to landing pages, but text reviews did not appeal to him.
- Seeing video reviews from Shopify developers on a Shopify developer website made him think video testimonials could plug into his video-focused work. He pivoted the product from Lonely Dev into testimonial.to.

## Why video testimonials and social proof work

- Damon's view: "people buy from people." A visitor who has never heard of a product or seen anyone vouch for it is less likely to convert.
- He sees text reviews as easy to fake and not very attractive. He describes them as short, templated lines like "great product, highly recommended" with five stars, often with stock-photo avatars.
- Fake text reviews can hurt a business. Video is a different technology: even if the person says nothing, you can tell from their face and how they talk whether they are really happy.
- He says video is the format that no other kind of review can compete with on authenticity.
- He built the product around connecting with real people, a thread that runs back to Lonely Dev.
- testimonial.to lets you collect testimonials and import reviews from third-party sites (e.g. Twitter, Amazon), and positions itself as a one-stop shop to manage social proof across the internet in one "wall of love."

## Growth: from $1k MRR to $400k ARR through building in public

- At the time of the interview (Nov 2022), testimonial.to had reached $400k ARR. Damon described it as growing almost double-digit percent month over month, and had been at about $4k MRR a year to a year and a half earlier.
- Most of his traffic comes from social media. He calls it a snowball: more exposure leads to SEO and word of mouth arriving organically without extra work.
- The product markets itself in a self-referential way, since a testimonial tool can be used to show testimonials about itself.
- He expected to reach $1M ARR around the following year. He called $1M a magic number many bootstrappers aim for.
- His team was just him plus a marketing hire he made the week before the interview.

## Quitting the job: the $100k ARR promise and what he would advise

- Damon did not quit with zero revenue. He quit his job when testimonial.to hit $1,000 MRR.
- He showed his wife his MRR chart to project growth, and promised to reach $100k ARR within nine months. If he failed, he would go back and find a job. If he made it, he would stick with it. He hit $100k ARR after nine months.
- He says he would not suggest anyone quit without validation. His advice is to be financially prepared (some revenue validation) and mentally prepared.
- His safety net was being a software engineer in the Bay Area, where he could always get a job again. Before the pandemic he had interviewed with big tech companies but found the interview prep (months of coding practice) boring. He saw that a new employer would mostly mean the same work for higher pay.
- A worst case of losing six months of paycheck seemed acceptable to him.
- The trigger was the pandemic: his daughter (born 2018) was not in daycare, his wife worked at a startup with growing demands, and one of them needed to take on care. He chose to do side hustles and build products.

## Running a business with a young child and setting expectations

- Early on, with an immature product and a lot of customer feedback, he cared for his one-and-a-half-year-old daughter during the day and worked at night, burning "midnight oil" to fix things and implement features.
- He says he told customers things like "I have to take my daughter to the playground, can I reply in an hour or two?" Customers understood, and some knew him from Twitter.
- He prioritizes family over instant support. He says he sacrificed a paycheck for precious family time, so it makes no sense to sacrifice that time for customers. Losing a few customers is recoverable; a toddler period only happens once.
- His technique is to set the expectation low and beat it: he tells customers a reply will come by end of day, then usually replies within 30 minutes to an hour, so they get responses faster than expected.
- Damon says it is not good to run a one-man business, partly because if he is with his kids the business is paused and nobody else can move the needle.
- He and his wife had a second child in June 2022, which was one reason to bring on help.

## Hiring and delegation philosophy

- Damon hired a marketing person (about a week before the interview) because he does not feel he handles marketing and sales well. He does not want to spend time on repetitive, human-effort work like cold outreach.
- Reasons it was the right time: the business was growing and profitable, the new baby, and a single-point-of-failure problem. The hire should complement his skills by handling marketing and sales.
- He has no plans to grow the engineering side. He does not want to manage engineers.
- When he does work with others, he wants to be the manager he wished he'd had. He gives people full control over a project.
- He uses Upwork freelancers and contractors for small, defined projects. He gives ideas and mockups, and wants them to deliver a working version without constant questions.
- He dislikes contractors who ask for constant feedback and charge a low rate. His example: someone at $50/hour who also costs him several hours a day effectively costs about $100/hour once his own time is counted.
- He sometimes hires more than one person on the same project to compare results. He can always check the GitHub repo but does not want questions on small details.

## Exit thinking and what "life-changing money" means in the Bay Area

- Damon had no exit plan. He noted that a founder friend (Jon Yongfook) mentioned he could sell Banner Bear at about 5x ARR, roughly $2M, but chose not to.
- For Damon, $2M is good money but not life-changing in the Bay Area. Software engineers there earn $200k to $300k per year, and housing costs millions.
- His own benchmark: if he worked at a big tech company at $300k and could then not work for 20 years, that is about $6M. He treats a figure in the several-millions range as life-changing.
- A team also argues for a larger exit so the team benefits.
- He relayed advice from an unnamed founder who sold his business (a "live training zone" business): think about exit now, shop around, and negotiate well, since a poor exit negotiation can cost several million dollars.
- That founder reportedly got a strong offer by presenting a valuation based on VC-style numbers to private equity and naming the price he would sell at.

## Funding: Calm Fund, and bootstrapper-friendly options

- testimonial.to took a small investment from Calm Fund (formerly Earnest Capital) when it was close to $2,000 MRR. Damon described the equity given as a single digit percentage.
- What he valued was the community of portfolio founders, many doing several million dollars in revenue. He says they give practical, approachable advice rather than random YouTube advice. He says he "paid equity to get a ticket into the community."
- He plans to stay bootstrapped for further funding.
- He mentioned non-dilutive options like Pipe, where you get annual revenue upfront in exchange for giving a discount to the funder, which he sees as a way to get capital without giving up equity. He also pointed to Founderpath as another such option.
- His rationale: the capital is used for the team, and these options are data-driven and tied to revenue, not to disrupting a market.
