## AI-native service firms: agents do the delivery, humans review exceptions

- Greg defines an AI-native service firm as one where agents do more of the delivery. He disagrees with people who say agents must do all of it. In his view agents can do "90% of the work" and the firm is still AI-native.
- His example is a bookkeeping firm for one type of business:
  - It holds the client records.
  - Agents prepare the books.
  - A human reviews the exceptions.
  - The firm completes the monthly books.
  - It charges for the completed job. That can be a monthly fee, or a set price each time books are completed.
- Why he likes the model:
  - Margins are high.
  - It doesn't need many clients.
  - Better models make the output much larger.
- He did a full episode on creating a creative agency this way, with "logos as a service" or "ads as a service" as examples. He calls these "super high margin" and says he is actively ideating on them.

## Offline businesses and the "food truck version" MVP

- Greg argues that people still pay to do things together in person, even in an AI age.
- His example is Othership, a neighborhood sauna club that started in Toronto:
  - It has a big communal sauna with other people and music.
  - Staff help with cold plunges, and tea is served.
  - It sells a membership.
  - A visit is "not cheap".
  - It has been scaling in New York.
- He likes offline businesses that tap trends (here wellness and community), especially with a membership model. Agents can handle much of the admin, but "the room, the experience is what people are buying".
- The hard part is cost. Offline is much more expensive to start than an AI-native service firm, especially in high-rent places like Manhattan or Toronto.
- His fix is to ask what the food truck version of the business is. For a restaurant, a food truck is a far cheaper way to prove the model.
- He says Othership's first version was in the founder's backyard. The founder invited friends and community members, and only later got a space. Greg's advice is to take it one step at a time.

## Distribution and the ACP funnel (audience, community, product)

- Greg says that with a few prompts you can now have a startup, but getting people to the product is harder than ever. He expects the price of reaching people to keep rising.
- Ad cost comparison:
  - When the Facebook ad platform launched around 2009, clicks were about 5 cents.
  - He says clicks now run roughly $1 to $2 or more, and $3, $4 or $5 depending on target and country. He doesn't have exact data.
- He concludes that owning organic audiences matters a lot. He suggests starting with distribution even before an offline business or an AI-native service firm.
- You can get paid by advertisers to build an audience around useful niche information, then build the products those advertisers and that audience ask for.
- He calls this the **ACP funnel**: Audience, then Community, then Product.
- He thinks people who own multiple meme pages, YouTube channels or Instagram audiences will do well in this era.

## Proprietary data sets

- Greg says "data is the new oil". He notes that Mercor and Micro One sell data to the big labs, at valuations in the billions and with hundreds of millions in ARR.
- The big-company version is to pay people for data the big models lack and mark it up to the labs.
- The solo-founder version is a business making a few million dollars a year. The goal is useful information people can't get from their personal agents or ChatGPT.
- He thinks a gold rush for acquiring data is coming and that "we're in the first inning". Owning data in the AI world "looks really good".

## Domain-specific harnesses are the new GPT wrapper

- Greg's definition, using a car analogy:
  - The model is the engine, and it sets how fast the car goes.
  - The harness is everything around it: the tools it can access, workflow rules, memory and checks. In the analogy that is the body, steering wheel, paint and electrical. He admits the analogy is imperfect.
- The opportunity is turning a general model into a system that understands how an industry works. His example is an operating system for a freight brokerage agent.
- His claim: "harnesses are the new GPT wrapper."
  - About two years ago people said GPT wrappers were useless, and many did go nowhere, like the 90% of startups that fail.
  - The top 10% turned into harnesses. They started as a wrapper on one model, improved as the model improved, then added industry tools, workflow rules and memory.
  - He points to legal examples such as Harvey and Spellbook.
- He sees a gold rush for harnesses alongside the one for data.
- A related category is vertical agents: giving an agent responsibility for a recurring job in a specific industry. His example is purchasing for independent restaurants, where the agent checks stock, compares supplier offers, asks for approval and places the order. He says people have built these for about 12 months and he still sees "a ton of opportunity".

## Robotics, physical AI, and physical products with a fan following

- **Robotics and physical AI**
  - Greg says people think robotics is as far off as the Jetsons, but "we are almost there".
  - His example is Osmo, which he says raised about six or seven million. It sells window cleaning as a service: building owners pay for clean windows and the company deploys and operates the equipment.
  - He cites a tweet from Brian Norard: the best way to sell software is by selling hardware.
  - You don't have to build robots. You could buy some and rent them out, or work on the sales and marketing side.
- **Physical products with a fan following**
  - His example is Fellow, which makes specialty coffee equipment. Its kettle was designed for coffee people, and it now has a large product suite. Greg owns a Fellow grinder and says he is not affiliated.
  - He says the latest AI models make it possible to design hardware and physical products that were previously too niche. He mentions prototyping with a Chinese device, then mass-producing and selling.
  - Another example is the Minimal Company, made by a friend. It makes phones for people who don't want to scroll, and he says it raised millions of dollars on Kickstarter. Apple isn't making that product.
  - His advice is that if you really understand a niche, now is a good time. He sees pent-up demand for specific products, especially ones that can go viral in the niche.
  - He wants to make an episode on using AI to create physical products.

## Scarce inputs and real assets: compute, energy, real estate, security

- **Compute and energy**
  - Every agent needs chips, electricity, cooling and somewhere to run. Owning that scarce input is "a good place to be".
  - He cites Crusoe raising $3.9 billion for its vertically integrated AI infrastructure platform. He also mentions Michael Dell's son raising billions for an energy company.
- **Real assets**
  - His example is small industrial workshops rented to local trades such as electricians, cabinet makers and plumbers. He also mentions industrial warehouses.
  - His reasoning is that the new economy still needs physical locations, so he finds real estate "still going to be a really interesting business".
- **Security**
  - He sees two kinds. One is software companies such as 1Password and Cloudflare. The other is access control for sensitive workflows, with his example being agent access control for accounting firms that lets an agent read invoices and draft entries. He suggests working through firms and their existing IT providers.
  - He expects things to get hacked and says it wouldn't surprise him if a personal-agent startup has a big breach. He says it "is going to happen".
  - He also expects growing demand for physical security, such as an Uber-style service for bodyguards and armored cars. His conclusion is that people feel less secure and will pay for security.
  - Security is what he'd most want to own.

## Health and longevity, marketplaces and social networks

- **Older adults, health and care**
  - Greg says older adults are an under-served ICP, especially because younger founders don't build for them.
  - He sees room for high-quality products and services with a high NPS. He also sees room for care service businesses, such as agencies of caregivers.
  - These are hard to run, but he says they are good businesses and "you're doing a good thing".
  - For longevity, he says there is still room to be "the gal or guy" in the space by niching down. He compares it to the way Huberman and Brian Johnson each tapped a health trend.
- **Marketplaces and social networks**
  - Greg says few people build these anymore. He compares them to the Uber, Thumbtack, Instagram and TikTok era of about 2013 to 2015, when he first came to Silicon Valley.
  - He likes them because success gives you "a moat". The hard part is liquidity, meaning having demand and supply at the same time. He calls it "more art than science".
  - He sees people returning to them. His example is Mark Louu's trustmr.com, a marketplace for buying and selling startups.
  - His argument is that personal agents will want to access marketplaces and social networks, and the data from both is interesting.

## How Greg frames the list

- He says there are "only 13 businesses" left to build in the agentic era. He rejects the idea of a "permanent underclass" and thinks the outcome won't be that dire.
- His actual claim is that in a world of agents and abundance, owning assets like these puts you in a better position.
- The 13 are:
  1. AI-native service firms
  2. Offline businesses
  3. Distribution
  4. Proprietary data sets
  5. Domain-specific harnesses
  6. Robotics and physical AI
  7. Physical products with a fan following
  8. Compute and energy
  9. Health, longevity and care
  10. Marketplaces and social networks
  11. Real assets
  12. Vertical agents
  13. Security
- He says he is most interested in AI-native service firms and security, and is actively ideating on the first.
