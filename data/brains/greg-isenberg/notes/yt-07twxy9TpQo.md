## Smart software and where the AI opportunity sits

- Greg calls pre-AI software "dumb": a CRM like Salesforce relied on a salesperson to enter and score every lead by hand. A multi-trillion dollar industry was built on that.
- "Smart software" injects human-like intelligence (an LLM) into the product. The software can scrape the web and score a lead against criteria without a person doing it.
- An agent goes one step further. It completes a whole task, such as "close 10 sales leads this month" for CEOs earning $1-10M a year in England.
- He thinks AI is still underrated even though everyone talks about it. His view is that people listening now are early and can build businesses around it.
- He warns there will be negative effects on society, but prefers to look at it "glass half full".
- His advice is to avoid jargon: "insiders like to put complicated words associated to simple things to keep people out." He says the terms don't matter, the outcome does.

## Two business models Greg says are open: cheaper SaaS clones and AI-fulfilled agencies

- **Vibe-coded SaaS.** Tools like Replit, Bolt, Lovable and Cursor let you prompt software into existence. Greg says to build cheaper or free versions of big SaaS products. His examples are "the DocuSign for Germany" and "the monday.com for Canada" at a tenth of the price.
- He notes these companies make billions a year, so even 1-2% of that market is large.
- **AI-fulfilled agencies.** Consulting, freelance and agency work employs thousands at firms like McKinsey. He asks what happens when you can "spin up a junior McKinsey analyst in a second".
- He expects to undercut agencies on price and fulfil the work with AI.
- He doesn't claim McKinsey or Adobe go to zero. People buy those brands because "no one gets fired" for hiring them. His point is that billions of dollars are available for cheaper services with similar results, and few people are pursuing them.

## What separates people who build from people who don't

- **Curiosity.** The builders are intellectually curious, try new tools as they appear, and push things into the world. Greg says he wants to "get my hands dirty" whenever he hears about a tool.
- **Willingness to do the work yourself.** He tells the story of a famous clothing-factory owner who sweeps the floors of his factories one day a year to remember how the business works.
- On "AI isn't good enough yet": if a tool is "96% there", that is exactly when to train. When it reaches 100% you are already a master at prompting it.
- Even if you never ship a product that makes $10k, $20k or $50k a month, using AI in your workflow makes you smarter.
- He says modern business school should teach three things: going viral on social (niche, formats, posting times), using AI to build products that connect with that audience, and pricing and business models. He is dismissive of MBAs, saying "don't tell me that you're learning business if you've never played with Replit."

## Why Greg dropped out of university

- In 2009-2010 the App Store had just launched, and he says it was obvious mobile would reshape everything, just as AI is now.
- He was at McGill, and his class was teaching desktop apps. His professor told him "there's no apps in the textbook."
- He realised his degree wasn't something he could fall back on when his bank account was empty. The only things you can fall back on are assets: financial assets, a following of a million people, or an in-demand skill such as AI coding.
- He quit because he wasn't building assets there.
- He had already been pushing apps to the App Store before they were polished, so he would be ready when a billion people used apps.

## Step 1: Find a painful workflow

- Greg's thesis: every export button in a SaaS product is a possible $1M-$100M a year AI business.
- People export data to analyse it, and that analysis is still done by humans. His example is exporting bank data to categorise expenses for his taxes.
- The test is to ask, "If this could be done via AI, what would that look like?" whenever you meet a tedious workflow.
- **Case study: Icon (he has no involvement).** Facebook and Instagram ads need creators, editing and copy. The best advertisers make hundreds or thousands of ads. Icon uses AI-generated creators and an "AI ad maker". Its pitch is that creative teams make 30 ads a month and Icon users make 300. It is a human-in-the-loop business, because a person still reviews and posts the ads.
- **Who finds the best ideas:** "If anyone has good ideas for AI startups, it's people who work at jobs they don't like and doing painful stuff." A marketing manager making 20 ads a month already knows how painful that is.
- His exercise is to list the two or three repetitive things you do each week and dislike. Your idea is probably there, and these insiders know the workflows better than a Stanford founder does.

## Step 2: Ship a minimum lovable product fast

- Don't spend 6-12 months building a fully featured product with a brand and deck. Build a "minimal lovable product", not just a minimum viable one, as a foundation, and iterate from there. "You don't need to build Rome in one day."
- Perfectionism is similar to not posting on social media: people fear judgement from friends and family and want to be proud of what they ship.
- His answer is that "the way to perfect is actually just consistency and iteration." More quantity raises quality.
- What you think of as the product is usually one feature, not ten. Add one feature at a time.
- **V0 demo (free account, vzero.dev).** He prompted it in plain language to build a restaurant directory for NYC. He described the style as clean and minimal, like Apple or Warby Parker, with pops of color, and referenced the Belly app.
- V0 wrote the code and produced a site called "NYC Eats" with trending lists, date-night picks and search, and it can deploy to the web.
- His estimate is about 24 hours (one business day at most) to get a site you can deploy. He says that would have cost hundreds of thousands of dollars a few years ago.
- Treat these tools like an employee you chat with. You iterate ("make a logo", "add images") to go from a 6/10 to a 9/10.
- **Manus demo.** He used this AI agent platform to research a pimple-patch product for older adults, as an opening against Starface, whose branding skews young. He found the idea through a trend tool on his Startup Ideas podcast.
- Manus researched competitors, wrote a research summary and packaging recommendations, and drafted marketing tasks. It took about 12 minutes. He says that in the past he would have asked his team to do this.
- He warns that Manus is a Chinese startup, so be careful with what data you put in.

## Step 3: Distribution is the moat, so build an audience first

- When the number of software products grows enormously because building is nearly free, many founders will launch to "crickets". Greg says "the distribution is the moat."
- Post content every day and position the product as a movement.
- Make the brand different. He recommends against copying Apple or Warby Parker, and says to make it "anti-Apple": chunky, with lots of color.
- He says there are only two ways to get customers, organic or paid, and organic is better.
- **Why social works now:** anyone can sign up for a free account and reach billions of people who have credit cards. For-you pages mean an account with 500 to 1,500 followers can get 1-3 million views if the content strikes a chord.
- Success depends on three things: content that resonates with an underserved niche, the ability to build or vibe-code a product, and getting people to pay.
- **ACP framework:** audience, then community, then product. The old way was to build the product first and the audience second. Your AI startup idea can start as a Twitter or Instagram account where you test what people like.
- **His four-step audience playbook for 2025:**
  1. Identify who you're creating for and what they aren't getting from others. If you get no engagement, go one level broader than your niche.
  2. Pick a format. Choose one format per business day, and copy formats from outside your niche.
  3. Build systems. Make a "creative faucet" routine (podcasts, blogs, social), capture ideas in a notes app, and schedule posts.
  4. Set a primary goal that isn't followers. Examples are a 25,000-person email list or $1M in sales through social.

## Step 4: Retention, the core loop and voice of customer

- Greg calls much AI revenue "curiosity revenue". Startups announce $3M ARR, but it is people trying the product, like an impulse buy, with no real core value.
- A minimal lovable product is a foundation built on one killer feature, the core loop, which drives retention. Retention just means people like the product and use it every day.
- **Two kinds of analytics to use together:**
  - Behavioral: dashboard numbers such as 30% retention or 25,000 daily active users.
  - Attitudinal or voice of customer: open-ended questions about what people liked and didn't like.
- His Walmart example is a shopper who spends 45 minutes and $200 against a $100 average cart. The numbers look great. In reality the shopper couldn't find what they wanted and had a $500 budget, so a sale was missed.
- His rule is "data informs, not drives." Fully data-driven companies make tasteless, undifferentiated products.
- **Finding the core loop:** it is similar to finding product-market fit. People "will be screaming it at you." The hard part afterwards is staying focused.
- Calendly is his example. For a long time it focused on one feature, sending a link so people can book time. It bootstrapped for a long time and became a multi-billion dollar company. He still sees more Calendly links than Google Calendar ones.

## Step 5: Partner with creators, and how to cold-reach people

- Building an audience takes time, so consider giving creators 25%, 40% or 50% of revenue to promote your product. Frame it as a win-win. It need not be forever, and you keep building your own audience to reduce dependence on creators over two to four years.
- He calls "I don't know any creators" a limiting belief. When he had under 2,000 followers, he found about 50% of accounts on Twitter had open DMs.
- He reads all of his own DMs and responds to roughly a quarter of them. He says he has hired people, partnered, invested and acquired companies through DMs. He says one in every hundred DMs leads to "the most interesting conversation of your life", and that you must be okay with rejection.
- **Islands press story (a Discord competitor, around 2016, later sold to WeWork):** he got constant press without an agency and with almost no followers.
  - He made a list of 175 journalists.
  - He DMed each one on Twitter with a 30-second selfie video: who he was, what he was building, and why it might interest them.
  - The ask at the end wasn't "cover my story". It was "can we meet?", and in the chat he gave "value, value, value".
  - He says the success rate was "staggering".
- **Advice for reaching busy people:** put yourself in their shoes, since they get bombarded. Use video (a Loom with screen share works), add value, and be different.
- He once had a 600-follower stranger DM him a video he thought Greg would like. Greg invited the person onto the Startup Ideas podcast, which he says has millions of listeners a month. His summary: "Shoot your shot, be different, use video, add value."
