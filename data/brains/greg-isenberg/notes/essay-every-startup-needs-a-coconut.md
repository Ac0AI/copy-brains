## The "coconut": one tiny, obsessive detail that makes a product memorable

Greg argues that startups win word-of-mouth and "soul" through small, possibly crazy product details, not just the grand vision or hockey-stick growth. He calls this finding your "coconut" and asks: "What small, possibly crazy detail can you obsess over that will make your users fall in love with your product?"

- The details are what separate a product in a category where "every app looks the same, sounds the same, feels the same."
- Obsessing over them can make a product the "anti-Amazon, the anti-Google, the anti-whomever."
- His instruction: "Find it. Pursue it. Perfect it." The wild things founders do for product/market fit are sometimes worth it.

## The original coconut story: sound as a differentiator at Islands (2017)

In 2017 Greg was building Islands, a Discord competitor that was getting traction. He worried about how to stand out in a sea of chat apps.

- His insight was sound: every chat app was silent, mute and boring, so sound was a chance to be memorable.
- He hired an indie musician in Montreal and told him to "go wild" on the notification sound.
- The next day the musician texted: "Bought, opened and emptied three coconuts for recording last night lol." He had bought real coconuts at a grocery store and smashed them together to make the notification sound.
- Users "went nuts for it." Greg says it got people talking about the app because of coconuts.
- Takeaway: a cheap, quirky choice in an overlooked sensory channel (sound) can create a distinct identity where competitors ignore it.

## The "1mm Rule": small details and timing decide escape velocity

Greg cites Nikita Bier's framing, which he endorses: "After building social apps for a decade, my biggest learning has been that when you're launching a rocketship and you're off by 1mm, you'll miss the moon & land in space. Details and timing matters."

- Greg's reading: the smallest details can be the difference between hitting escape velocity and burning up on re-entry.
- It is easy for founders to get lost in the big picture and the grand vision. Greg pushes the opposite: sweat the tiny things.

## Examples of "coconuts" in well-known products

Greg lists tiny details that he says gave products personality, reduced friction or created word-of-mouth:

- **Nomad List (Peter Levels):** a real-time chat showing where nomads are logging in from, creating a sense of global community and FOMO.
- **Gumroad (Sahil Lavingia):** selling with just a link, with no website or complex setup, opening e-commerce to creators.
- **Baremetrics (Josh Pigford):** a "Panic Button" that quickly hides the dashboard when someone walks by, addressing a real founder anxiety.
- **Carrd (AJ):** the "Publish" button that turns green and wiggles when you make changes, giving instant, satisfying feedback.
- **Indie Hackers (Courtland Allen):** product milestones on profiles, which gamified progress and kept founders motivated.
- **Notion:** emojis in page titles, making organization fun and visually scannable.
- **Superhuman:** "Undo Send" with a custom duration, giving users control and reducing email anxiety.
- **Loom:** the bubble video of the speaker in the corner, making videos feel more personal and engaging.
- **Calendly:** time zone auto-detection, removing a major scheduling friction point.
- **Todoist:** karma points for completing tasks, a gamification element that kept users coming back.

## Patterns across the examples (Greg's framing)

- Many coconuts are small features that remove an anxiety or friction (Panic Button, Undo Send, time zone detection, sell-with-a-link).
- Others add delight or feedback (wiggling Publish button, emoji titles, bubble video, Islands' coconut sound).
- Others use gamification or social proof to drive retention (Karma points, milestones, live nomad locations).
- He frames these as "injecting soul and word-of-mouth" through little features, as distribution that comes from the product itself.

## The challenge Greg poses to founders

- Ask yourself: "What's your coconut?"
- Pick one small, possibly crazy detail, obsess over it, and perfect it, aiming to make users fall in love with the product.
- Greg shares this kind of idea weekly in his newsletter, "Greg's letter," which he says has over 100,000 subscribers.
