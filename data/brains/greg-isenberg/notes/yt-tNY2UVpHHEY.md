## The ACP framework: audience, community, product

Greg built his career and all his businesses around the ACP framework: "A stands for audience", people in a niche who are passionate about a topic and underserved in some way. C is community, the hardcore people from that audience. P is product, what he sells to those people.

- Order matters: start with the audience, then the community, then the product.
- He argues a product-first approach only works for the heavily funded who "have years to burn" and can figure out marketing later. For a bootstrapped business, audience first is the only sensible route.
- Attention is the real bottleneck. You get it free (organic) or paid. Organic is better because going through the process puts you "at one with your audience and community", which makes a better product and a more indispensable business. It also makes your later ads better, since "the best ads start by being organic first". He is not anti-ads.
- He finds it very hard to make the case in 2025 for not being on social. He calls an audience "your unfair advantage".

## Step 1: Pick who you're creating for and stand out

- Identify exactly who you're creating for. Greg posts and consumes content almost exclusively from his own ACP world: people interested in startup ideas and building them.
- Understand what that audience isn't getting from other creators, then be different. Greg says not enough people actually audit what other creators make.
- His own differentiator is "sauce": giving away executable detail instead of holding back. Example: he tweeted a playbook for vibe coders to band together and build free versions of legacy SaaS products like DocuSign, Figma and the Adobe stack.
- He accepts the downside that a tiny fraction of readers might execute and compete with him.
- If you aren't getting engagement, start one level broader than your niche. You need reach before you can specialize, and a niche that is too small may never reach the For You page.

## Step 2: Find your format

Greg says nobody talks about how building an audience is mostly formats. Pick one format per business day and test until one crushes.

- A format is how you package content. Naval does one-line philosophical bangers. Greg writes very long-form posts because he can't "give the sauce" in one line.
- Look for formats outside your niche. Copying what is saturated in your space makes it harder to stand out; he says you'll lose trying to out-Naval Naval with zero followers.
- Example: "philosophical memes" might be a format to import into a different niche.
- Consider what is working platform-wide. Short-form video is working in 2025, and he thinks Naval might do it if he started today.
- Pick what feels natural and enjoyable, so you stay consistent. If you hate making video, you'll churn. Greg has created on X for almost 20 years because he found what was fun.
- Test for 90 days and let the platform analytics tell you what's working.
- Formats have life cycles. They used to last about two years; now sometimes "almost two weeks". Keep watching what others do so you can reinvent.

## Step 3: Build systems so you actually do it

- Build a "creative faucet" routine: find what gets your ideas flowing. That could be following 50 people in your niche on a fresh account, going to in-person events, or walking while listening to podcasts.
- Use a simple idea capture system. Greg uses Apple Notes because it's everywhere and simple, not a fancy notes app.
- Set a weekly time to turn ideas into content. On Fridays he turns ideas into long-form tweets and newsletter pieces. Some are bad, some good. He treats tweets, podcasts and newsletters as "minimum viable products".
- Use a scheduling tool (he mentions Post Bridge) so you don't have to think about publishing.
- Be strategic about timing: match the platform to audience behavior (don't post on LinkedIn at 3am on a Saturday night). Post when the topic is relevant, since the "why now" matters. His example: posting about DeepSeek in March is too late because it worked a month earlier.
- Join trending conversations (newsjacking) and publish when success is most likely.

## Step 4: Pick one goal and measure it

- Pick one primary goal, such as 100,000 followers in 12 months, a 25,000-person email list, or $1M in sales through social. "A lot of people pick multiple things. No, just pick one thing."
- Measure each format against that goal every 30, 60 or 90 days. He considers 90 days enough, but you must track or you'll get lost.
- Treat attention as scarce and content as a product, with the rigor of a job. He suspects Naval's tweets come from a system, not the toilet.
- Optimize for your goal, not vanity metrics. For his podcast he cares more about positive comments than subscriber count.
- Start without analysis paralysis and be patient. Greg started on Twitter almost 20 years ago and reached 10,000 followers only in 2020. He says it took 15 years to come up with the playbook and five years to execute it.

## Where to start: X, replies, 90 days

- Greg's advice for a first MVP is X, because you can experiment with formats and it is the best place to be a "reply guy". Do replies for about 90 days, then tweak.
- Replies give a confidence and dopamine boost and are less daunting than posting on your own profile.
- Replies also surface which formats work, which feeds your own format choice.
- He thinks X is better than a personal Instagram for niche business topics, where friends see you as a different person.
- You can do this alongside a full-time job or while building a startup. Four hours a week is his example budget.

## Why build an audience: the insurance policy

Greg says the number one reason to build an audience is "to have something to fall back on, period". He calls it insurance and a retirement account you can tap.

- Clarity: you learn what your niche wants, their motivations and incentives.
- Tap-in value: when you need customers or beta users for a startup, they're there.
- Jobs: in a recession you can message people who've followed you for years instead of applying randomly on LinkedIn.
- It lets you switch revenue on. With an audience, a new idea can bump the business whenever you want. He also says having an audience frees your creativity, because you aren't struggling to get anyone to see what you make.
- Sponsorships are another option once an audience is large.
- It compounds, like index funds.
