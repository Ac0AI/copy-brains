## Pay-per-task as the next software business model

- Greg argues the internet is going through a business-model shift, and says the founders who notice it early "will be the leaders of the next software wave."
- He names the new model **pay-per-task**: customers pay for work completed, not monthly seats. His framing: "The future internet sells work, not monthly seats."
- He says software got addicted to monthly charging over about 15 years. SaaS became the default because VCs, public markets and founders all liked recurring revenue. Monthly pricing makes sense for some software, but not every software business needs it.
- He says people have become quietly tired of spending thousands a month on software.
- His claim on timing: this was already in the air for a couple of years, and AI is what accelerated it.

## Why AI pushes pricing toward work done

- Greg's reasoning: AI gives everyone an intern for basic tasks. Software used to give databases and tools; now it gives digital assistants working 24/7. "An AI agent is basically an intern at this point."
- If the product does the work itself, he argues, the natural unit to price is the task, not the seat or the logged-in human.
- He frames this as a market opportunity. The SaaS world is a $592B+ market "going through an earthquake," and he says that is when startup ground is fertile.
- His advice to founders: challenge the business model and run interesting experiments with pay-per-task and pay-per-usage pricing.

## The seat-pricing pain: Figma example

- Greg's team paid about $40k/year for Figma licenses.
- He says the product is valuable, but some teammates use it heavily, some lightly and some not at all.
- He says he cringes at the bill, knowing not everyone is spending time in the tool.
- The takeaway he draws: flat per-seat pricing charges for access, not for value received, and buyers notice.

## Real examples of the shift

- **Intercom Fin** (Intercom is a $1B+ company): a customer service bot that answers customer questions with no humans, only AI.
  - It does not charge by employee count or support team size.
  - It charges $0.99 per resolved question.
  - Greg says this "aligns customer and company incentives beautifully": the vendor earns only when the problem is actually solved.
- **Zapier** (about $5B valuation): if you go over your monthly plan, it moves to a pay-per-task model. Greg would not be surprised if Zapier moves fully to pay-per-task once this experimentation phase is done.

## Software should operate like a developer tool

- Greg says the future of software "looks a lot like a developer tool."
- He quotes B2B SaaS investor Brianne Kimmel, and says he "could not agree more." Her points:
  - Usage-based pricing is better than seat-based subscriptions.
  - Some percentage of the product should be open-sourced to allow community contributions and continuous improvement.
  - Live events are not purely marketing and sales; they are critical for product, customer support and community growth.

## Pay once as a parallel counter-trend

- Greg notes that 37signals founders Jason Fried and DHH believe software is moving back to "pay once."
- He says he could see that happening "a bit too."
- The example he cites is Once.com, a new 37signals software company built around that model.
- His position: the monthly-subscription default is breaking apart, and usage-based, per-task and one-time pricing are all live alternatives.

## Lesson from the freemium shift

- Greg draws on personal history: in summer 2004, a classmate showed him Skype. He expected to pay, since "good software cost dollars," but the core was free and only long-distance calls cost money.
- His description of freemium: an experiment in economics, "make the product free for 90%, charge 10%." Free for most, paid for die-hards.
- Outcome he cites: Skype sold to Microsoft for $8.5B, seven years after he signed up.
- His rule of thumb: when you are early in a new business model for a validated product in a big enough market, you get explosive growth.
- He says earlier model shifts produced billion-dollar businesses "from Dropbox to Salesforce," and he expects pay-per-task to do the same, naming Drew Houston (Dropbox) and Dharmesh Shah (HubSpot) as the kind of founders to emulate.
- He adds that freemium fell out of fashion in enterprise software (replaced by SaaS) but still thrives in consumer.
