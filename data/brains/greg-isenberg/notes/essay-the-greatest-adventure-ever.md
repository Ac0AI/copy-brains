## Founder lessons change with age: naivety is an asset, but distribution and timing matter most

Greg met a 22-year-old founder who reminded him of himself 15 years ago: "Full of fire, convinced his technical innovation would change the world, and completely blind to the landmines." He argues that some lessons can't be passed on by warning; they only land once you've felt them.

- At 20, "you're dangerous because you don't know what's impossible." While others build "AI for X," a young founder asks why Visa holds money for 3 days or why Instagram no longer feels like a social app for friends.
- Naivety lets you question norms veterans treat as immutable laws.
- Being broke gives a hunger that well-funded founders can't manufacture.

## The expensive lessons founders learn in their 20s

Greg lists the painful patterns reality teaches:

- A technically "worse" product with better distribution can win.
- An MVP gets traction but can't scale when real users arrive.
- Users love the product but refuse to pay what it costs to deliver.
- The feature everyone asked for in customer interviews goes unused once shipped.
- A "dumb" high-school buddy sold his company for $15M because he knew the director of product at Google, while the technically superior product couldn't get a meeting.
- The biggest competitor is inertia: "Users hate their current solution but hate changing more."
- Customers who say "yes" enthusiastically in meetings never write checks.
- A scalable tech stack is irrelevant if you can't find users.
- A product that never answered "why now" isn't forgiven by the market.

## Patterns visible by age 30

Greg's list of what the game looks like by 30:

- Paper valuation is "kind of BS," and optimizing for it is a trap.
- The best companies start by solving a small, boring problem perfectly.
- Consumer social is the hardest game in town, but endlessly alluring.
- "X" is a bubble (crypto, AI, no-code, etc.).
- Failing marketplaces become SaaS companies; growing SaaS companies build marketplaces.
- "Every big company is a distribution company wearing product company clothes."
- "Your 'clean' architecture matters less than your first 100 customers."
- Most startup ideas are timing mistakes, not product mistakes.
- Many "overnight successes" have powerful networks, family money, or industry connections; the playing field isn't level.

## Lessons at 35: profitability, distribution, and boring markets

Greg's later-stage principles:

- Profitability gives ultimate leverage to do what you want in life.
- "Bad UI with distribution beats great UI without it every single time."
- Consumer indifference kills more startups than competition.
- The best products look like toys to incumbents.
- If you plan to sell, know the people who make those decisions.
- What you can't measure is usually what matters most.
- Market timing beats execution every time.
- Edge cases reveal your true moat.
- The best products solve problems so fundamental they look obvious in retrospect.
- "Cash-flow positive" starts sounding better than "Series A."
- Drift toward boring industries with real problems rather than crowded spaces with manufactured ones.
- "Fundraising isn't validation - paying customers are."

## The ideal founder: 20-year-old naivety plus 35-year-old wisdom

Greg argues the most dangerous founder questions everything but knows where the landmines are buried.

- Brings fresh eyes to old problems while understanding that distribution trumps innovation and timing beats brilliance.
- Knows the most elegant solution is worthless if users don't care enough to change.
- To get the 35-year-old lessons at 21: find the right mentors, study failures more than successes, and question the startup gospel everyone recites without thinking.
- His framing: "we all have to earn our scars. The question isn't whether you'll learn these lessons, it's how expensive they'll be when you do."
- Despite the unfair game, building something from nothing "remains the greatest adventure available."
