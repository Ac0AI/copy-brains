## Community-first startup method: audience, then community, then product (ACP funnel)

- Greg's thesis is that "community-based products outperform non-community based products." Every business starts as a Twitter account or a Discord community, which he calls the minimum viable product. It is essentially free to start.
- He uses the ACP funnel: audience, community, product. He never starts with a community. He starts with an audience.
- He separates the two terms. An Instagram or TikTok page is an audience, not a community.
- Step 1, build the audience: "how do I create a category of one" and own a word in a niche. Example: nobody talked about boring marketing before they created boringmarketing.com, although "boring businesses" was a rising trend.
- Step 2, convert the audience into an enclosed community once it reaches about 10,000 followers in a niche. This protects against algorithm changes and lets you capture phone numbers or emails. The goal is to turn people into raving fans.
- Step 3, launch a product or agency on top of the community.
- Examples:
  - "You Need a Robot" began as a Twitter account about AI productivity tips about a year before the interview. It became a Discord with 20,000 people, then an agency that implements automation and AI for companies.
  - Boring Marketer began as a Twitter account about AI-assisted SEO. It became a community, then an agency.
  - His Discord reached 20,000 members from a Twitter audience of about 35,000 that he converted into a free community.
- Communities can be monetized at any size. He says a dozen people can generate $12 million a year, and 120,000 people can generate $120,000 a year.

## Keeping a community alive: rituals, gamification and peer-to-peer

- Greg says about 95% of digital communities are "zombies." Getting people in is not the finish line: "the work actually starts."
- Rituals are the biggest factor. He uses the religious term on purpose and tries to make the experience like a religious one. His comparison: religion is a good operating system for community, with a text, short-term and long-term goals, and a weekly gathering. Ask what the equivalent of "church on Sunday" is for your community.
- Example ritual: the "punch you in the face" meeting in his Community Empire membership. It comes from a Monday 7:00 a.m. accountability meeting he used to hold with a friend, where each person says what they did last week. It is a goal-setting meeting for a community of internet builders.
- Communities fail when they are all about the leader and not enough about peer-to-peer connection. He points to the forum (the group of about 10 peers) as the main value in YPO and EO.
- Communities also need a potent mission. He cites YC's "make something people want." People spend a year building a community with a mission that is not strong enough.
- Gamification: he likes School because content, one-on-one calls and events unlock as members get more active. He wants to turn a community into more of a game. He has been building many School communities for about 18 months.
- Community Empire engagement shown on screen: 334 members, 99% active in the past 30 days, and about 60%+ logging in weekly. He says "people go to what they care about."
- He says "memberships is the new internet community."
- His roots in community are Counter-Strike clans and IRC, where each clan had a brand, a website and a sense of belonging.

## Hiring community managers and CEO/GMs

**Community managers**
- Hire from inside the community. Find the very active members who are organically doing a good job and ask them to take the role, even if they are a dentist or a marketing manager elsewhere. They understand the mission.
- Pay at least what you would pay a marketing or social media manager, plus 30-50%. A lead community person can expect six figures.
- A good community manager is "worth his weight in gold" and is partly the product. His head of community at Community Empire does an onboarding call with every member and thinks about them unprompted.
- Recruiting hack: build a community of the best people in your field and hire from it. He says about 90% of Late Checkout's staff come from their communities or followers.

**CEOs / GMs / partners**
- A CEO or GM is a partner, so he wants to "break bread" in person. Cold DMs alone are not enough. Hiring is like marrying someone, and he does not want it to end early.
- He finds candidates by cold-DMing people on the internet whose content he finds interesting.
- He looks for someone he can trust with a bank account, someone he never has to hover over, who is resourceful at finding answers, and who has "something to prove" or a chip on their shoulder. They are often not Stanford or Harvard types.
- About half of his CEOs/GMs have "been there, done that." His lesson going forward is to "pay up" for people who have done it before.
- He gives a caution: lots of people claim they did something when they were only around people who did it. Being at Facebook in 2006 does not mean you grew it to a billion users.
- He prefers sharing experience over giving advice, because advice is case-dependent.

## Late Checkout holding company: structure, equity and profit share

- Late Checkout started in June 2020 as a studio that incubates community-based ideas. It began with an innovation agency, then added a fund, and the group also acquires businesses.
- Size at the time of the interview: an eight-figure holding company, with mid-seven figures in profit expected, and almost 100 people. It has never raised venture capital and focuses on profitable companies.
- Agency deal size: the innovation agency's average deal is seven figures, and it works with Fortune 500 clients.
- Boring Marketing came from an acquisition. They bought 50% of an existing seven-figure business, and that business incubated Boring Marketing.
- Philosophy: "build skyscrapers, not strip malls." He builds on top of what works instead of launching unrelated companies. For example, the AI tech stack built at Boring Marketing for content and SEO could be spun out as a SaaS. He calls this "multipreneurship": multiple products inside one ecosystem, going deep rather than wide. He compares it to Berkshire Hathaway and Constellation Software.
- Equity at Boring Marketing: the Boring Marketing team (the CEO and employees) owns 50% and Late Checkout owns 50%. The CEO decides how it is split. He says it could have been 30%, but the team being incentivized is part of why it is winning.
- Profit share at Late Checkout: employees get no equity in the holding company. They get a profit share every six months, based on seniority and contribution. Profits from portfolio companies flow up to Late Checkout. Employees on specific companies can also receive equity in those companies, so they "double dip."
- Compensation philosophy: "if I'm eating, everyone should eat, just as much if not more." If a business makes a few million a year, he wants the operator to make $500K to $1M. If it makes $200K to $500K, breaking even for a while is fine.
- He contrasts profit share with venture-backed equity, which can mean waiting 12 years. He sold his last company to WeWork and calls WeWork a great example of venture capital going wrong.
- He argues the future of VC looks more like "venture capital meets private equity meets a studio." Typical VC value is about 90% the check. Late Checkout works for equity and helps with community, ads, SEO and design.
- Operating style: he does not hover over portfolio companies, since people reach out if they need him. He focuses on new bets, acquisitions, business development (including flying founders to Miami for dinner) and product sketching in Figma.

## Why Greg favors teams over solopreneurship

- Solopreneurs are a single point of failure: bank accounts, taxes and downtime are all on you.
- He sees three types of solopreneurs: people whose projects can't replace their job, people who cap around $100K a year with high stress, and the top 0.1% (for example an AI app doing $7M a year with one person), which he still calls high stress.
- "There's no middle class for solopreneurs": you are either at the Justin Welsh level or essentially a freelancer.
- A team shares responsibility, allows long vacations and is more fun. He compares it to preferring multiplayer to single player.
- A multi-company structure with a CEO/GM for each business is lower stress.
- His future outlook: "micropreneurship," meaning you plus a couple of people and AI. He thinks a $5M/yr Justin Welsh-style business could be 5x in about five years with AI.
- If you started today, he would teach yourself design, marketing and engineering and get good at all three. That makes you "deadly."

## Networking and DMs: shoot your shot, throw dinners, record a video

- As a teenager and early 20s, he bootstrapped his network by throwing dinners in cities he was visiting (New York, LA, Paris). Paying for dinner is "the best money you could probably spend" and removes friction, though he cautions against going into debt. He gives the example of $100 a head for 12 people, which is $1,200.
- Be a good host: the right food, music and number of people, and think from the guest's perspective. Don't expect anything back, and stay long-term focused.
- Dinners in SF with tech marketing leaders eventually led to deals with Lyft and Looker, with no expectation attached.
- At 17 he went to the Le Web conference in Paris and walked up to people to introduce himself. He ended up at a hotel bar where each cocktail was about €65 and he paid his share.
- At 22 he flew to San Francisco on a few hours' notice for a party at a PayPal co-founder's house ("buy the ticket, take the ride"). He showed his video app to a stranger, who introduced him to StumbleUpon's CEO. Four days later he had an offer to buy his company. Mobile was the big question mark at the time. He acknowledges luck.
- Say yes to opportunities. Expect rejections and have thick skin.
- DM people you admire. DMs are free. Use the two-minute rule from David Allen.
- The art of a good DM: record a 30-60 second video with a story (Loom works) rather than text, because everyone else sends text. At 24-25 he spent 20-30 minutes making each video. One video led to a deal paying 30-40K a month.
- He met two heroes in 2023 by DMing them on Twitter. One was Rivers Cuomo of Weezer. Cuomo liked his tweet, Greg DMed immediately, and he got backstage in Tampa.
- Stay inspired from multiple sources, such as group chats (he calls them "the new social network"), mentors and old high-school friends. Pulling inspiration from one place only leads to groupthink.
- He deliberately travels to LA, NY and SF to throw events, even though he prefers staying in Miami.

## Creator and product ideas, AI and design taste, and Greg's content process

**AI, software and taste**
- He expects the software market to be 10x to 100x larger because AI makes software easy to create. That means a lot more competition.
- Ways to stand out: brand, design that feels good and community.
- The last 10 years were the year of the engineer, and he thinks the next 10 will be the year of the designer. Taste matters.
- Learn to think like a designer: build a mood board, then distill it into product design. His advice is "push pixels, don't push pages." He says to learn by doing, not by reading design books.
- He doesn't think learning to code is useless. Learn to think like a programmer. He took a few computer science classes, which gave him credibility with engineers.
- He cites Sora: taste is what separates good AI videos from bad ones.

**Startup ideas he mentioned**
- A "Coffeezilla for technology" (or for other niches): an investigative channel that exposes scams. Coffeezilla has two to three million YouTube subscribers. The downside is that many people will hate you.
- Partner with a creator to build software. His example is James Clear's habit app, Atoms, built with Tiny (Andrew Wilkinson's holding company), reportedly on a 60/40 split with James Clear holding 60. It was charting around #13 in the App Store. He sees 100+ opportunities like it. Another example he cites is MrBeast's ViewStats. He wouldn't build a keyboard company because of the supply chain.

**Content process**
- He keeps an "ideas" note on his phone. On Fridays he spends half a day turning those into tweets and then expands the best ones into his weekly newsletter.
- He posts when he has something good rather than twice a day, and wants posts to feel like a "drop." About 5 hours a week covers tweets plus newsletter.
- He treats social as a game and a slot machine. Best practices help, but the algorithm is a black box, so you have to keep pulling the lever.
- He stopped spending about $7,000 a month on short-form video clips of his "Where It Happens" podcast, since it didn't get traction. The podcast is about startup ideas, which don't produce viral short-form moments. He asks whether a clip is a "babe, wake up" moment. He now spends a few thousand a month on podcast editing, written in his own voice.
- On authenticity: "I don't want to be a robot on the internet." He thinks honest opinions win, and he has more freedom than a CEO to share them.
