## The ACP funnel: audience, community, product

- Greg builds every business with the same funnel in mind, which he calls ACP: Audience, then Community, then Product.
- He always starts with the audience: go to a rented platform (Twitter, Instagram, LinkedIn) and create niche content that gets shared.
- His target is "10,000 followers" before moving on.
- Next he asks how to pull the most hardcore of those 10,000 (maybe 50 to 100 people) into a community.
- He says "once you see it you will see it everywhere" and that it changes how you launch social, community and products.
- An audience helps with the cold start. A large following makes launching a community much easier.

## Audience vs community: the TRIBE framework

- Greg says an Instagram page with followers is an audience, not a community. If you stop posting for 2 to 5 months, engagement disappears.
- He defines a true community with the TRIBE framework, and every ingredient must exist:
  - T: Togetherness
  - R: Rituals
  - I: Member identity
  - B: Belonging
  - E: Engagement
- A community can be free or paid. He uses TRIBE to judge whether something is a community when he builds, buys or invests.
- A great community needs a transition: members must get from point A to point B, so he defines that "pain-to-gain" path clearly.

## Launching a community with scarcity

- Greg launches communities as waitlist-only and private. They could be free.
- He does this because "scarcity drives human behavior".
- He quotes Andy Warhol: "Studio 54 is a dictatorship at the door but a democracy on the dance floor."
- He pictures a velvet rope and a bouncer. He says he hates being exclusionary but it works, and once members are inside it is a democracy.
- Example: an "AI for marketers" community. Build the Twitter or Instagram account to 10,000 followers, then open an invite-only waitlist.
- Start with about 50 people. Their input tells you what to build. Greg says clarity from 50 active members, even in B2B, is "priceless".
- To grow from 50 to 500, lean on those first 50 members for what to create.

## Community vs membership: optimize for access, not engagement

- Greg says community is not the "bees knees". The bigger opportunity is a membership.
- A membership is a niche bundle of value (community, directory, events, software, content) that people pay for access to.
- Optimizing community for engagement is a losing game. As members are added, engagement eventually falls, and communities tend to turn into places for self-promotion and spam.
- The membership KPI is access, not daily engagement. He calls it "yearly access".
- Analogy: a beach club in Miami. He doesn't go often, but access reminds him he lives five minutes from the beach.
- He thinks membership is the future of community because it is far more sustainable than an internet community.
- Onboarding matters. In his paid community (Community Empire), a new member has a call with the community manager to learn their goals.
- Setting the "this is access, not engagement" expectation at onboarding means newer members will behave differently from earlier ones.

## Choosing a niche and pricing

- Niche matters because of attraction. Using the beach example, people within 5 miles want access far more than people 10 to 20 miles away.
- He cites David Spinks' book *The Business of Belonging* and its concentric circles of community (core to casual).
- A narrower niche (for example AI B2B marketing) would make the job about "3x as easy" than broad B2B marketing.
- Check the niche's cycle. Ask where it will be in 12, 18 and 24 months. Many people now try to sell AI communities and newsletters to exit.
- He recommends a low-ticket plus high-ticket structure for memberships:
  - Low ticket: a legitimate "no-brainer".
  - High ticket: some communities charge a couple thousand dollars a month.
- His analogy is coach versus first class: both reach the destination, but first class gets a flat bed.
- Exercise: pick the price first (for example $200 a month), then decide what access makes that price "a stupid no-brainer".
- He is wary of free communities: "free scares me to death".
- He suggests charging, since a paid MRR line gets execs to see a community as a revenue driver and keeps salespeople at bay. A Fortune 500 CEO called it "SaaS-like MRR without building the SaaS".
- In B2B, charging works because members can expense it on a learning and development budget.

## Keeping a company community healthy

- Keep community members as far from salespeople as possible, for as long as possible.
- He has seen communities worth millions a year lose their magic once they were promoted heavily. A few such events in a short time can destroy a community.
- Set expectations for what the community is, why it exists and what success looks like.
- Charging for it is one way to fight sales and executive encroachment.

## Twitter and content tactics

- Greg started on Twitter in 2008 and had about 10,000 fans from 2009 to 2020.
- In March 2020, interest in internet communities, memberships and audience products surged, and his following grew. He now has about 376,000 followers.
- His content didn't change much. He says the world moved him from "100 miles from Miami Beach" to "on Miami Beach".
- He advises you to "pick a lane", even an unpopular one, and keep writing through years of 1 to 3 likes. You don't need to be right that many times in a career.
- Elon's Twitter/X is more TikTok-like. Reach is less consistent: one tweet got about 30 million impressions and about 50,000 likes, while others get about 150 likes.
- He says "follow me" or "subscribe to my newsletter" replies after a viral tweet don't work: "no one is subscribing".
- He prefers long posts (over 240 characters, with "show more").
- He seeds a teaser midway through a long post (for example "next week I'm going to tell the story of how Adam Neumann didn't show up to the first meeting and then I went on his private jet"), which earns followers without a CTA at the end.
- His lesson: swim with the algorithm, and if everyone does "follow me", do something different that is scroll-stopping.
- For his newsletter, he posted: "how the hell do I get people onto my newsletter" with "this is a kind of uncomfortable ask but I'll do it anyways". He says it got attention because it was different.
- He signs off posts with a bonus hook in brackets, such as "(come see my new website)".
- He uses handwritten graphics to stand out against AI-generated images.
