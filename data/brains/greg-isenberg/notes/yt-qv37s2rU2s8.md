## Starting from trend data, not inspiration

Greg says he used to get startup ideas on a beach walk with a glass of wine, then fall in love with them. He now prefers ideas built on data rather than a feeling at 6:32 pm. His preferred method for the highest-probability startup in the AI era is to study trend data: what people tweet, which posts are trending, search volume, what people type into chat apps, and how they show up in online marketplaces.

He describes two ways to do it:
- **Manual:** build a content diet (specific people on Instagram, specific subreddits, specific accounts) and triangulate from what they say.
- **Automated:** connect an MCP (an API or link to a data store) to Claude Code or Codex. For example, use the X MCP to pull trending tweets in chosen niches each day. The LLM then refines, ranks, finds weaknesses, proposes new ideas and sends a daily summary. Greg built his own startup-idea MCP, Idea Browser.

Example prompt to an agent: "there's an opportunity to build software for dentists in South Florida, maybe shaped like this with these features."

## Why most people get bad results from AI

Greg says AI is only as good as the context you feed it. People who say AI-made landing pages, startup ideas or posts came out bad usually made two mistakes:
- They didn't give the right context or connect the right platform. They never said "this is how I write" or "this is my framework for coming up with startup ideas."
- They didn't define what good work looks like. He compares it to onboarding a junior social media manager: you'd show them an excellent tweet and a good reply.

The technical term for defining good is "evals": "this is good work, and before you give me the output, check or grade it against what I consider good." Don't hand back anything that fails it.

## Routine work versus creative work

Greg splits work into two kinds.
- **Binary/routine work** (e.g. SEO: writing and optimizing posts where you either rise in Google, drop, or stay flat). The effective parts of this are about 12 to 18 months from being fully autonomous.
- **Creative work** (tweets, video, brand, marketing copy). Even at 1% creative, a human stays involved. Humans are rebellious by nature and that is hard to program, so he expects creators to stay.

He says he consults for some of the biggest brands in the world on making their businesses AI-native, so he is not dismissing the automation.

To get a team or company started, he asks: "What would you do if you got 5, 10 or 15 hours of your week back?" and "What if you could automate 60% of your tasks?" People light up because there are things they always wanted to do and never had time for.

His view is that jobs will shift rather than vanish: AI takes the binary tasks and people do more creative work. Example: Boris, creator of Claude Code, says he hasn't written a line of code in six months. Instead he reviews code, designs systems and talks about engineering on podcasts. In an age of abundance, creativity becomes the competitive advantage, and people who move into creative work will like their day-to-day more, since a lot of office work is button-pushing.

## The multi-preneur era and simple ideas

Greg calls this the golden age of entrepreneurship. Before the industrial revolution many people were effectively entrepreneurs, and factory employment is a relatively recent idea. He expects millions of people to come back to entrepreneurship, some pushed by job loss, some by an idea they can't stop thinking about.

He coins the "multi-preneur": someone with several businesses and multiple income streams, like creators. A creator claiming $75k a month may be summing brand deals, affiliate, revenue share and more. His view is that a multi-preneur will usually have one main product, service or business unit producing most of the revenue, plus several others. If agents can run the others, and you build a high-margin business with a system around it, that works well.

He prefers simple ideas because he dislikes heavy competition. He means ideas that make $1M, $5M or $25M a year, not Palo Alto-scale ventures. Example math: an AI-enabled business making $20M a year, with a spinoff making $5M that sells for $20M (four times revenue). Competition in simple ideas has risen because people like him talk about them, but opportunity stays in your own geography.

## Localization as an opening

Greg tells a Quebec story: it is one of the highest Pepsi-consuming regions in the world. When Coca-Cola entered Canada in the 1960s it ran the same ads everywhere and simply translated them into French for Quebec. Pepsi hired a local agency and made something specific to Quebec, and about 65 years later people still drink Pepsi there.

His takeaway: businesses have been boring about local marketing, and AI opens a large opportunity. Localization was one of the first problems AI solved. His startup spent hundreds of thousands of dollars on localization tools before modern AI, then rebuilt them in three weeks with all text translated in real time. You can also adapt ideas that work in San Francisco or New York to your own region and language.

## Builder plus distributor

Greg says anyone can build now, in any language. The way to compete is to understand distribution. Skills he names: creating Instagram posts and videos that capture attention, and using Kling (a video model) to make interesting ads, wiring them into Meta ads and running them.

His view: "this blend of builders and distributors is probably the most dangerous type of person in the AI era." They know how to get attention and convert it to revenue, and the product still has to be good, because the bar for digital products keeps rising. You must collect feedback, improve and ship features.

## Loop engineering with customer feedback

Greg describes "loop engineering," a term he attributes to Andrej Karpathy and Peter Steinberger of OpenClaw (he is unsure of the name): telling an LLM to keep building on a loop. He finds it more interesting for business than for engineering.

His example setup:
1. Talk to about ten customers a day.
2. Sync meeting notes to a folder in Google Docs or Drive.
3. Run a daily loop: read the notes folder, work out what people like and dislike, pick the single most interesting problem, build a feature to review.

His own workflow for his startup: he ran an agent (Hermes) with Grok 4.5 as a "co-founder." He gave it an idea and told it to build features, improve them based on analytics, and run SEO overnight. He says he pays roughly $1 to $2 an hour for this, versus millions in venture capital and a team in the past. He expects fully autonomous companies in 2 to 5 years, where you set a goal such as revenue and the system picks a feature daily from customer conversations. A side benefit is lower costs for customers: his cost structure is a tenth of what it was.

## Venture capital is one model, not the only one

Greg says you used to need Stanford, Harvard and Silicon Valley, and the right investor at the right time. He calls investors the gatekeepers of ideas and says AI democratizes the process. He is not anti-VC: friends are VCs and he has run venture-backed companies. His point is that VC works for under 1% of companies, so most businesses shouldn't be built on that model.

Example: in one company he raised money because he had six engineers, $300k in recurring revenue and roughly $100k cost per engineer ($600k). "It was a golden anchor that would sink our entire ship." Today, with two or three people building with AI, he thinks it could have been bootstrapped.

Self-funded companies grow by earning money from customers who keep paying, tell friends and build momentum.

## Greg's 90-day plan to start from zero

If fired and needing income within 90 days, he would:
1. **Pick a niche** he loves where he feels he has an edge. Everyone has some edge (a neighborhood, a school, a network, a skill).
2. **Follow his ACP framework: Audience, Community, Product.** Every business starts as an account on a platform (Instagram, TikTok). Build an audience first, since in the AI era you can build anything and distribution is often the differentiator. Spend the first 90 days finding a content formula that attracts the target audience. Use a clear bio, e.g. "Follow Greg Isenberg for startup ideas in the AI era."
3. **Turn a small slice into a community** (digital or local): a WhatsApp group, a local meetup.
4. **Then build the product or service.** Building takes about a day. Choose a model that relieves real pain, not a nice-to-have, and that he believes he can sell in a relatively short time.

He ties idea generation back to asking customers what they want, plus trend-data MCPs like the X MCP and Idea Browser MCP, and trying many products to see what works.

## Learning AI tools and hiring

Greg says becoming AI-native takes about a thousand hours, joking about "Isenberg's thousand-hour rule." Chat is instantly rewarding, but orchestrating agents ("a world where I run 100 agents") is tedious to tune.

He compares it to learning guitar: it sounds terrible at first, then clicks. He encourages building even just to learn, not only for money.

Hiring examples:
- He asks candidates which AI tools they use. He is shocked when engineers say "none."
- When an engineer asked for a big raise and the title "AI engineer," Greg asked to see their cloud credit usage. The engineer started using AI more that day and the team shipped more.

He cites a Dubai founder friend who laid off his whole engineering team after running out of money, learned to code with AI, rebuilt everything solo, and was being acquired for millions of dollars.

Stripe's new-company numbers and the App Store boom are, for him, evidence of the multi-preneur era.
