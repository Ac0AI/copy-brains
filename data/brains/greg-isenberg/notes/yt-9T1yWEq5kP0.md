## The 30-step "future of SaaS" playbook: start narrow, own the workflow, sell outcomes

Greg's thesis: "This is the greatest time ever to build SaaS." Building software, building an audience around it, and reaching buyers has never been cheaper. He argues SaaS isn't dead, it's evolving. This section covers the opening frame, which is aimed at people who want a cash-flowing startup ($100,000 a month to $1M a month) rather than a venture-scale one. He says venture can come later.

- Start with a subniche inside a big market. Example: the FIRE (financial independence, retire early) movement for Gen Z inside finance.
- Don't build for a huge market, because "that's where the venture boys are playing."
- He names ideabrowser.com as a tool for finding niches.
- He says he will give the framework, not "the one idea that you should do."

## Steps 2-5: map the workflow, find where money moves, quantify the time saved

Greg's method for finding a software wedge inside a niche, using a local roofing company as the example.

- **Step 2, map the niche's workflow end to end.** Roofing owner's day:
  - Check new leads (website form, Google Ads, Facebook messages).
  - Respond to the homeowner and qualify the job.
  - Schedule a site visit and drive to the house.
  - Take photos and estimate materials.
  - Write and send a quote, follow up, negotiate.
  - The exact steps don't matter. Mapping them end to end does.
- Three ways to map it: call owners and ask about their day, be the owner (or work for one) so the knowledge is already in your head, or have AI research it (he names Manus, Claude Code, OpenClaw, ChatGPT).
- **Step 3, mark where money changes hands.** Examples: negotiate scope or price, collect a deposit, order materials from suppliers. That is where you can wedge in and "take a piece of that pie." Any tool works for mapping (FigJam, Excalidraw, Google Docs, pen and paper).
- **Step 4, spot repetitive mechanical steps.** These are the candidates for an agent or automation. Example: checking new leads across form, ads, Facebook and phone takes 5 to 10 minutes a day.
- **Step 5, quantify the cost of those steps.** If the owner earns $250,000, $500,000 or $1M a year, saved minutes become thousands of dollars. Pitch it as "I'm going to save you 50, 100, 150 hours this year. What's that worth?" If the owner's time is worth $400 an hour, the value is easy to state.

## Steps 6-10: build media alongside the product (content, organic-to-paid, email)

Greg says not to just create a product: "You're going to want to create media."

- **Step 6, pick one channel** (Instagram, TikTok or X) and build an audience around the workflow. He admits "easier said than done."
- Use AI as an "AI CMO":
  - Ask Manus, Claude Code or ChatGPT how people go viral in the niche, and for content ideas and a calendar.
  - Automate the repetitive parts: idea generation, research, scripts, even the content itself. He says some AI video is now hard to tell from real video. Faceless content or recording yourself also work.
  - Push it for non-obvious ideas, give it consistent context, and run it as scheduled daily tasks.
- **Step 7, study posts that get saves, replies and DMs.** He has posted on Instagram for roughly the last 6 to 8 months and uses the insights view to build intuition. Example: his latest video had 2,700 likes and 5,000 bookmarks.
- **Step 8, double down on the organic angles that convert.**
- **Step 9, run paid ads on proven organic winners.** If something works organically, there's a good chance it works as an ad with good ROI. "Viral" means viral within your niche, which could be 60, 80 or 120 likes depending on niche size.
- **Step 10, capture emails from day one.** The list is the foundation. When sales are down you can email a discount or event, because you can't rely on social or ads working.
- While building the product, publish a minimum of one piece of content every day, try ads on what works, and build "the foundation of a media company at the core of this."

## Steps 11-15: do the work manually first, then convert it into agent workflows

Greg says many future SaaS businesses will "start off as service businesses with human beings at the core of it," meaning you fulfill the task yourself first.

- **Step 11, manually perform the workflow.** It isn't sexy, but you need to be dialed in on how to deliver the outcome. Prior experience helps (e.g., having been the roofing owner).
- **Step 12, document every step precisely.**
- **Step 13, separate judgment from mechanical tasks.** AI is "incredible" at mechanical tasks and "sometimes good and sometimes bad" at judgment.
- **Step 14, turn the mechanical tasks into agent workflows.** He says this is where much of the value will be.
- **Step 15, design agents to complete the full tasks**, not just fragments.

## Steps 16-19: tools, orchestration, memory and high-touch onboarding as the moat

- **Step 16, connect agents to real tools.** Use MCP so agents can reach email, Slack, a CRM, Stripe, Facebook and so on. In the roofing example: email, Facebook and Stripe. Do this manually at first, then give agents the access.
- **Step 17, add orchestration, retries and verification.**
  - He cites a tweet from his friend Scott Belsky (formerly product at Adobe, seed investor): "the orchestration layer is the new interface layer," where you coordinate agent workflows model-agnostically, validate outputs with a human in the loop, and resolve issues. Greg says the ultimate layer to own is where coordination takes place.
  - He calls this a new paradigm: you orchestrate agents, and outcomes and resolutions are the end goal.
- **Step 18, store user preferences and long-term memory.** These become part of the moat.
- **Step 19, launch narrow with high-touch onboarding.**
  - Don't default to a short, sweet signup flow.
  - Collect as much useful data as you can without asking "a thousand questions," balancing a good experience against a moat.
  - Missing data upfront makes the experience worse and lets competitors knock you off.

## Steps 20-23: publish proof, then move pricing from per seat to per task to outcomes

- **Step 20, publish measurable proof.** Convert time saved into revenue and hours saved for the owner.
- **Step 21, move pricing from per seat to per task.**
  - He links this to why many public SaaS companies are down 30 to 50% from all-time highs. Investors fear both vibe-coded competitors and the loss of per-seat appeal.
  - Customers want "do this thing for me." Example framing: a completed workflow "is worth $200 every time you do it."
  - You don't have to start per task. You can start per seat if it's easier, but aim to get to per task.
- **Step 22, move to outcome pricing.** He calls this "the goal here."
- **Step 23, raise prices as value compounds.** More workflows, a better product and more brand trust all support higher prices.
- Why a small player can beat incumbents, per Greg:
  - A narrower, more bespoke niche.
  - Media at the core, giving an audience, an email list and better paid ads.
  - Agents doing the fulfillment, with a lower cost structure and no need to raise millions or hire big teams.
  - AI hallucination is manageable if you keep scope small, avoid overloading context and add checks.

## Steps 24-30: expand into adjacent workflows, lock in switching costs, become the default execution layer

- **Step 24, add adjacent workflows** once the first one maxes out LTV. Decide whether to buy another company or build it yourself. He notes that with AI, building is often easy enough that buying isn't needed.
- **Step 25, orchestrate multiple agents across the lifecycle.**
- **Step 26, build switching costs through data and memory**, layering on more of both as you add workflows.
- **Step 27, turn power users into public case studies.** Aim for people well known in the subniche. Don't stop at articles or tweets: "send a camera crew or go yourself and film these people," then put paid spend behind it.
- **Step 28, hire operators from inside the niche** once you're profitable.
- **Step 29, reinvest profits into distribution and product depth.** Go deeper, move into adjacent workflows, and spend far more on content and paid ads.
- **Step 30, become "the default execution layer for that subniche."**
