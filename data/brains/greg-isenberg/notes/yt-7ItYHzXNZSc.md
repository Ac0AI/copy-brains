## Community vs. audience: the TRIBE framework

Greg tests whether something is really a community with his "TRIBE" framework:
- **T**: togetherness
- **R**: rituals
- **I**: member identity
- **B**: belonging
- **E**: engagement

He says a community-based product is a mix of design, brand, product and social "in a way that makes people want to go on the rooftop and scream about your product." If those pieces are missing, it is not a community.

Audience vs. community, with an example:
- A meme Instagram account has about 3 million followers and makes roughly $2.5M profit a year. Its owner calls it "my community."
- Greg says it does not fit TRIBE. It is an audience: if the owner stops posting, engagement "will go basically to zero."
- A real community keeps going without its founder posting.

Greg warns that "community-led" is often marketing speak. When a community-based product is real, he calls it "magical."

## Where to start: a space, and in-person events

To create togetherness and belonging, Greg says you need a space. It can be digital or in person.

- Founders default to digital spaces because they are easy.
- He thinks there is "a huge amount of alpha right now post-COVID" in simply running events that bring people together.
- His reasoning: humans are social creatures, and broadcast social media is "more artificial than it is natural."
- He predicts the brands that turn customers into "raving fans" will be the ones that master TRIBE.

## Define the community by a milestone, not just a group

Greg's advice for starting a community business today:
- Don't start with "I'm building something for founders."
- Start with a milestone for a specific group. His example: founders who have gotten through YC, to help them raise Series A, B and C.
- The community must be for these people and for an outcome, because "human beings are selfish and we want to get something out of the products that we buy."

His own paid community follows this logic. It is for people who want to build community-based products. A free Skool membership is bundled in.

## Tying membership to product revenue: the Rapha example

Greg's favorite community-based example is Rapha, a high-end cycling apparel company.

- It sells a yearly membership in cities like London and New York that matches you with other local cyclists.
- Members show up in Rapha gear. Wearing Nike would mark you as not part of the club.
- Revenue comes from two places: recurring membership fees, plus one-time gear purchases that rise as members get deeper into cycling.
- He credits David Spinks' "community commitment curve": members get more committed as they buy more product. He calls it "a beautiful flywheel."
- The brand benefits too. Members become an army wearing the brand around.
- Greg's lesson: position your product as the entrance fee to the club, so the cost of not owning it is social.

## Scarcity, friction and exclusivity

- Greg says marketing has two plays: make it free or make it scarce. Free communities "aren't interesting."
- He cites Andy Warhol's line that Studio 54 was "a dictatorship at the door but a democracy on the dance floor." Without a gatekeeping door, joining becomes much less interesting.
- Soho House example:
  - It is reportedly closing membership in New York, Miami and LA because demand is far above capacity.
  - Greg says the problem is that letting everyone in at about $5,000 a year makes the experience worse.
  - He calls this a negative network effect: past a point, more people does not make the community better.
  - Soho House Malibu requires owning a home there, which shows the extreme version of a gated community.
- He says the best communities seed the right initial members, because "a community-based product is culture."
- The goal is that a new member leaves their first event thinking "that was for me." Mean people or a mismatch of expert vs. beginner can drive them away.

## Grow community businesses slowly, and keep growth tied to quality

Greg says venture-style hypergrowth can hurt a community business:
- He has raised millions and was part of the WeWork story ("we raised billions"), so he knows the pressure to grow at all costs.
- His metaphor: you "sprinkle gasoline, not throw it." A community is "a plant that you water daily," not a fire.
- Hockey-stick growth "will likely kill a community" because quality drops as it grows too fast.
- It is closer to growing a marketplace, where you balance quality against new members, like a seesaw.
- Founders have a bias for action and want numbers to go up, but the best community founders "grow linearly for a while."
- This does not mean community is a small opportunity. He notes there are billion-dollar community-core businesses.
- Hockey-stick growth can happen in adjacent products. A Rapha-style brand can spend heavily on a Super Bowl ad or SEO to sell gear, as long as the core membership experience is not diluted.
- Some parts need linear growth, like the cycling group or Soho House tea time.

## Staying ahead of copycats: rituals and a culture of experimentation

Greg predicts AI will flood the market with similar-looking content and software. He gives this example: he saw a video of someone drawing Twitter's interface on paper and using AI to build it.

- He expects brands to start feeling like McDonald's (generic) while community-based brands stand out.
- He cites Seth Godin's "purple cow." You may look odd wearing Rapha, but to someone else who is into Rapha, you are cool.
- To be the one copied rather than the copier, Greg says to constantly try "really crazy ideas" that might resonate and "build a culture of experimentation" so you ship new ideas faster than others.
- The alternative is "a commodities game" where you just try to keep your head above water.

Rituals are a key differentiator. His example is the "punch you in the face" meeting:
- It started years ago with his friend Julian Smith, a New York Times bestselling author. They lived in Montreal and met at 7 a.m. every Monday.
- Each held the other accountable for what they committed to the week before. Walking through two feet of snow to a coffee shop was part of the deal.
- He says a lot of founders fail for lack of accountability.
- The ritual is now part of his paid membership. He says "these are the ideas that not only create a moat but also create a culture."

## Paid memberships as SaaS-like revenue without building SaaS

- Greg expects many more paid memberships. He describes them as "SaaS-like revenue without building any SaaS."
- Steps: decide who it is for, define the milestone, then bring people in to get them from point A to point B.
- His own paid community for people building community-based products:
  - Price: $99 a month, which equals the price of a Skool (community platform) membership, so he says it is "literally free."
  - Revenue: about $20,000 a month.
  - Marketing: he did little marketing for it.
  - Valuation: about $20,000 a month is roughly "quarter of a million dollars" a year. Valued like a SaaS company at about eight times, he says it created "seven figures of enterprise value" out of thin air.
- Differentiators: unique rituals like the punch meeting, credible leaders and members, and good seed members.

## B2B: why and when to add community

For B2B founders and CMOs, Greg says we are entering an era where a B2B business that ignores TRIBE-style thinking will find it harder to:
- find new customers
- keep current customers
- get customers talking about it to other customers

He tells founders to ask how they can create an identity around what they do and to build a roadmap toward community. It does not need to happen tomorrow, but it should be planned ASAP, because "the downside of not doing it is pretty high."
