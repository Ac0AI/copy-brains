## The audience-first path to a profitable SaaS

Greg's blueprint for a profitable SaaS (written July 2024) starts with an audience, because the audience is the future customer base.

- He offers two routes: a faceless brand (AI tools like Claude for captions, posts and scripts, RunwayML for video) or a personal brand where you share the journey "warts and all". He notes authenticity still matters either way: "Your audience isn't stupid."
- He says to pick one platform and dominate it (Twitter, TikTok, LinkedIn). Each has hundreds of millions of users, so which one you start on "doesn't matter all that much".
- Then pick a content format (memes, startup war stories) and experiment until something clicks.
- He tweeted for 2 years before anyone paid attention. He prefers linear growth over exponential, overnight virality, because it is more sustainable.

## Lead magnets and "community magnets"

- At roughly 10,000 followers, Greg says to convert casual followers into die-hard fans with "value bombs": templates, e-books, exclusive content.
- He trades these for email addresses and first-party data, for two reasons: build affinity, and keep a way to reach people if algorithms change.
- His favorite lead magnet is a free community, which he calls a "community magnet". He has seen WhatsApp groups and Skool turn into hotbeds of engagement and loyalty.
- He claims 90% of communities are "zombie communities". The ones that work let people level up, which builds raving fans.

## Unbundle an incumbent with one killer feature

- Greg's reading of the market: big players try to be everything to everyone and fail, users want personalized niche solutions, and AI makes targeted products easier to build.
- The tactic: look at the giants, find the one feature they are missing or doing poorly, and make it your entire business.
- Example: Calendly pulled scheduling out of bigger CRMs and productivity suites, perfected it, and became a billion-dollar company.
- His phrasing: "Find the squeaky part and replace it with a smoother, shinier one."

## Pricing: lifetime deals and usage-based

- Greg argues subscription fatigue is real (he finds forgotten $9.99 charges on his card statement), and that this opens a window for alternatives.
- Lifetime deals and pay-per-usage reduce friction and make a product feel like a no-brainer.
- Examples he cites: AppSumo built an empire on lifetime deals. Snowflake uses usage-based pricing and has 130% net dollar retention and billions in revenue.
- The positioning: be "the refreshing alternative in a sea of subscriptions."

## Affiliates and revenue share

- Greg says many of the biggest SaaS companies are "essentially glorified affiliate marketing machines". He cites Adobe's affiliate program paying over $100 million in commissions in a year.
- Recruiting tactic: reach out to potential affiliates with personalized Loom videos (or use AI to do it at scale) showing exactly how much they could earn.
- Early on, be generous: 40-50% revenue share for top affiliates, and 20%-50% as the general range. He calls it "rocket fuel for growth".
- Later, dial it back. New affiliates get 25-30%, while original promoters stay on higher rates as evangelists, because the customer base now refers other customers.
- His view: pay evangelists rather than "Mark Zuckerberg" (ad platforms).

## Skip VC, sell for 5x+ ARR

- Greg describes being offered "$2M on a $15M pre" and finding it intoxicating.
- His counterpoint: a $2M ARR SaaS business can easily sell for $10M+ if built right. That is life-changing money without giving up equity or starting a 10-year VC treadmill.
- His advice: VC money will always be there if you are growing, so ask whether you really need it before signing a term sheet.

## Retire or repeat

- With a cash-flowing SaaS, Greg sees two options: sell and retire (a $10M+ exit on $2M ARR), or repeat by buying another SaaS app or starting from scratch with the knowledge and capital you now have. The second time is easier.
- He says he has seen founders build entire portfolios of profitable SaaS businesses with this playbook.
- His stated goal: not to be the next unicorn, but to build a business that gives "freedom, financial security, and the satisfaction of solving real problems for real people."
