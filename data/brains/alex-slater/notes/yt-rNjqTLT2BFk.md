## Alex's origin story and the "AI app" opportunity

- Alex Slater (19, from the UK) dropped out of school at 17 and flew to New York alone to chase startups. His first idea was a failed attempt to raise $100 million pre-seed to build "the WeChat of America".
- At 18 he was nearly broke (about $500 left) and traveling the US. In Miami he approached a group at a hotel pool and ended up meeting Zach Yadegari. Zach was then building Cal AI with Blake Anderson.
- Alex used Cal AI's growth (50K, then 100K, then 200K, then 400K a month, roughly doubling monthly) as proof he could do it too. He says "I was with Zach at the beginning... I saw this go from nothing to something and I was like, I can do this too."
- He launched Quittr, an app that helps guys quit porn. About 12 months in it has about 1 million downloads, roughly 250K paying users and about $500K a month in revenue.
- His view of the opportunity: AI let small teams build products that weren't possible a few years ago, and social media made distribution easier. Combining the two is the macro bet.
- On being in an "AI bubble": he separates the foundation model layer from the application layer, where App Mafia sits. He compares it to the 2000 internet "bubble" that still produced durable companies.

## Building Quittr: a community-based habit app

- Alex got into the idea from the self-improvement rabbit hole, where "stop jerking off" is a recurring piece of advice and a hard thing to follow through on. He says the topic is taboo, and people won't talk about it with friends.
- Core product mechanism: a community where users post streaks and how they feel. Members earn "orbs" for time clean, for example a "super rainbow orb" at 90 days, shown publicly next to every post. Accounts can be anonymous.
- His diagnosis of why it works: a community of people with the same struggle creates accountability. The sense of "we're going to quit together" is the main driver of community-based apps.
- Proof points he cites: about 30,000 five-star reviews, near-daily emails from users, and in-person thank-yous.
- He argues the real reach is the whole problem space (billions of people with the habit), and that many users are very young, which is part of why the app matters.
- He says the greatest inhibitors of success are vices, and that stopping heavy weed use (3+ times a day for 5+ years) was what let him find success.

## The App Mafia playbook: picking problems and building

- Problem identification: find problems you or the people close to you experience. Do market research, identify the key pain points, then study what other apps in adjacent industries do and build a solution for the specific problem.
- Alex says the team cares about self-improvement, which shaped which problems they chose.
- Skills needed are lower than people think. He says he has IQ tested people he has worked with, and found even people around 100 IQ built very successful apps. His view: some baseline intellect is needed, but beyond that "it's really a problem of agency."
- Agency means finding the right information, assessing the core problem, looking at what worked in the past, using other apps as case studies, and rebuilding for a novel solution.
- Marketing in a sentence: identify the right creators, identify the right hooks for UGC, create content, put it out, iterate, double down on what works, drive people to the app, and provide value so you get paid.
- Everything in consumer apps is open source: download a competitor's app and you see its exact funnel. He says Cal AI's funnel and how it reached its revenue is visible to anyone who installs it, and revenue is verifiable on public databases like Sensor Tower.
- On learning to code: use AI as a tutor, not a copy-paste machine. Ask it to explain concepts (for example MVVM in SwiftUI, backend security, API calls, state management), implement, iterate and rewrite yourself. Copy-paste without understanding teaches nothing.
- On other copycats: tens if not a hundred-plus other apps make over $100K a month by rebuilding the experience for other domains. His examples are Quittr-style apps for sugar and alcohol, Cal AI-style apps for scanning supplements, and UMAX-style apps for tailored skincare.

## Fundraising vs. bootstrapping

- Alex says all the App Mafia apps are bootstrapped. Investors reach out "all the time".
- Reasoning: the VC model fits companies chasing multi-billion outcomes. In their space the bottleneck "is not cash", it is insight, knowledge and agency. The difference between having $100K and $5K to start is "very low", mainly speed.
- He expects the group will likely raise for future, much bigger companies, but not for these.
- Why not raise: starting from zero teaches more than starting with a $250K check. Starting with cash lets you hire people to do everything and never learn the skill yourself, which in this space he thinks is a mistake: "you're actually kind of shooting yourself in the foot if you don't learn how to do the thing yourself."
- He says some funded apps raised millions and he questions what they spent it on.
- Group scale he cites: across App Mafia's portfolio of founder-built apps, they do over $70 million a year, not counting Bags (which he thinks could push it toward $100M+).

## Selling the App Mafia course and building a personal brand

- The App Mafia course teaches how to scale apps. Structure: ideation, design, development and distribution, with over 10 hours of content, plus case studies updated roughly monthly.
- Price: $2,500 for access. Alex says there are no guarantees. Launch was set for Saturday, October 23 on appmafia.com (the domain cost about $10K).
- Why make it: they got DMs constantly from people trying to learn this, but never had the economic incentive to produce a structured course. They concluded the knowledge is a black box and they could push the industry forward by structuring it.
- Alex says they don't need the money. The main reason is leverage on personal brands: they plan to put all proceeds into ops, videography, the house, events and marketing for App Mafia to grow their personal brands, then use that distribution for hiring and future businesses.
- The group lives together in a house in Pinecrest, Miami (about $40,000 a month rent, described as a roughly $10M house). They plan to vlog about an hour every other week.
- He frames content as a skill to train like building apps: "we have all the recipes for success... it's a skill, the same way that building an app is a skill."
- Giveaway mechanic: one free course for a listener who DMs @appmafia_ on X and explains who they are and why they want it (and why they can't pay). He says they will give more free access to people who can't afford it.
- Alex's own proof point is that he and Connor saw what Zach and Blake did with Cal AI and ran the same playbook themselves.

## Views on AI and self-improvement products

- Alex agrees AI is among the greatest technological developments in history and that it commoditizes intelligence (personal tutors, doctors, coaches).
- On whether AI dependence hurts social skills: he argues AI can act as a coach. Feedback on RizzGPT-style products showed people learned better flirtatious but kind responses themselves by watching how the AI recommended replying.
- Design principle on insecurity: the goal is "inspire action without creating insecurity". For UMAX face ratings, they set a floor of 55 out of 99, most users score 70+, and a potential score always sits 5 to 10 points above the overall one. Recommendations are basics like going to the gym, eating less calorie-dense food and washing your face. They avoid mewing, bone smashing and hormones.
- He rejects building judgments about competitors: "I like capitalism... the free market is a good thing", and says the best answer to bad information is better information (build Quittr) rather than policing others.
- Product philosophy: more people building software is good because competition makes products cheaper for consumers, and the new apps are tools for human improvement.
