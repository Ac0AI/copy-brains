## Quitter and the consumer-app opportunity

- Alex frames consumer apps as the new version of SMMA, crypto, trading and dropshipping. All of those were attention arbitrage, and apps are "a digital product" pushed through influencers.
- His case against ecom: physical products carry low margins. Software "has zero cost for distribution", so "the only cost is marketing" and it is almost pure profit.
- He says an app can start "from your bedroom", especially with AI and vibe coding, and that "if you prompt it well, you can create a multi-million dollar app."
- Two things changed since he started: building is far easier, and the zero-to-100k playbook is now widely known. When he began, "you figure it out yourself"; now founders share strategies openly online.
- Benchmarks he cites: Quitter did $30k in its first month. Kai did $250k. Cal AI went from $50k in month two to $100k in month three and then to half a million. Flo does about $10 million a month. Before these, he says, nobody had shown what was possible in consumer apps.
- His own failures came first. At 17 he tried to raise $100 million pre-seed for an Instagram/Facebook/Twitter-style social platform and "failed brutally". He then built Rich AI, which also failed, before Quitter.

## How he built the belief that a million a month was possible

- His vision did not start as "two or three million a month". It came from a friend (Zach Yadegari of Cal AI) proving the path first.
- He snuck into a hotel in Fontainebleau, Miami while broke and met Zach, who was making about $1k a month from consumer apps. Months later Zach said Cal AI would hit 50k in a month, and it did.
- Alex started building his own app once peers were around $200k a month.
- His rule: "Whatever Zach achieved, I know I could do." Seeing a peer reach a number makes it a template rather than a fantasy.
- Seeing a peer win without you also drives him. He describes imagining that heartbreak ("I could have done it myself but I didn't") as the strongest motivation, and says something in the app world recently gave him that feeling "in a positive way".

## Keep the big picture, but work from the first sale

- Alex says to know how big the idea can get. A product that's perfect for one person won't necessarily get millions to use it, so start with "a big idea, high pain point".
- He warns against fixating on "millions a month" while you have $10 in revenue, because it kills motivation.
- His scaling sequence: get the first sale, then 10, then 100, then 1,000. "If you make money in one channel, if you did 100x the output, could you make 100x more?"
- On his own goals, he treats milestones as stepping stones: $500k a month is revenue, not profit, and not personal income, so he doesn't put achievements "on a pedestal".

## Scaling paid ads: speed

- Quitter has run around $500k a month on Twitter ads since roughly April or May. As of January 2026 he calls that too slow ("I've been way too slow stabbing for Twitter") and is ramping up.
- The plan: increase daily ad spend by 20% a day. He expected about $1 million a month within a week and about $2 million a month the week after.
- He says he wants "a whole new focus on speed and scaling" and admits the company missed that for a while.
- He says one day in Miami helped him close roughly $1.1 million of investment and "completely flip the trajectory" of Quitter, and that the pace of the Miami environment was a big part of it.

## Drive over curiosity

- Alex and Zach concluded they succeeded because they're interested in the world: "The more you learn, the more you understand, the more you know what to do next."
- Alex then says drive matters more than curiosity. You can sit in his Discord, learn how to scale an app from zero to a million and recite it, but "if you don't have the drive to actually figure it out and do it yourself, you're not going to make it."
- Drive creates interest: even if the interest wasn't there originally, drive gave him the interest to figure things out.
- He credits massive self-belief: "I don't understand why I would not believe to the max and put all the work in and figure it out."
- Tools such as vibe coding make building easier, but he believes the determined people "will figure it out regardless of circumstances". His blunt view is that "you're either a killer or you're not."
- If he hadn't succeeded with Quitter, he says he'd "have done something else" and would never have stopped until he made it.

## Reacting to other people's success

- Asked about people discouraged by seeing flexing on social media, Alex says he finds that reaction unnatural and offers a reframe: treat the money flowing through the ecosystem as proof, and ask "how can I do the same thing?"
- He describes seeing a multimillion-dollar plane overhead and thinking there's a whole other world of business to learn, instead of being ignorant of it.
- He notes that the app space lets founders show revenue that's publicly verifiable, which builds trust.

## Exit plan and next moves

- His stated plan is to sell Quitter for around $100 million within roughly four to twelve months. He calls it "totally realistic".
- After that he plans to go into music next, citing Drake, Kanye and Rihanna: the power of attention and image is valuable, and another tech company would make him "too much of a nerd tech guy".
- Long term he wants to enter UK politics, because he believes UK leaders are not real business people and he wants to be a billionaire with that power.
- He says business skills don't decay, so he could return to real business later.

## Environment matters: Miami vs London

- Alex got a $10 million Miami house for YouTube content, was evicted a few months later for throwing too many parties, and moved back to London to be near friends and family.
- After two months in London he returned to Miami, closed the roughly $1.1 million investment on day one, and found he wanted to stay.
- His reasons: Miami moves faster, has "billions of dollars of people" around, and has far more entrepreneurial peers. London has few entrepreneurial friends and bad weather.
- His rule for his 20s: surround yourself with people smarter and more successful rather than being "the best one in the city", and don't commit a year to a place that won't help you grow the most. He previously moved to the US on an O-1 visa for this reason.
