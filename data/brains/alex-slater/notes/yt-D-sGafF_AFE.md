## Soar's guerrilla launch: stunts, flight credits and "mind share" in San Francisco

Alex's team (Soar, a flight-booking platform) set a goal of getting 100,000 San Franciscans to use the product. Alex describes the setup as "a million dollars to spend on marketing", with investors adding more money.

- The tactics are loud and physical: a Cybertruck, a Lamborghini, a cheap 1980 Volvo, a TV truck driving around the city, planes, billboards, hackathon sponsorships, paper planes thrown from a roof, and a treasure hunt with a million-dollar prize.
- Everyone in SF "works all day". The team wants to "bring some life to the city" and get everyone taking photos and knowing what Soar is.
- The deliberately meme-y "vault of investor money" branding is meant to make some people mad. The logic: if people mock it and it works, "we're right".
- Reported traction: the team says it is "nearing a million dollars in flights booked" about 2 months after launch, and hit a record day of roughly 28K in booking volume.
- Booking counts rose daily: 41, then 42, then 47 bookings in a day, including 8 bookings in one minute.

## Mind share first, then convert with a good product

Alex lays out the core logic: do stunts so everyone in the city knows the brand, and some will try it when they need a flight.

- "Anyone needs to book a flight might try us. They try us and they like us, they'll stay." He frames the goal as positive ROI while having fun.
- He argues that "you get enough eyes on the brand, you have a good product, like that's all that really matters." Location (San Francisco vs Miami) doesn't matter.
- The market is big: Alex pitches flight booking as roughly "a trillion a year", with incumbents (Skyscanner, Google Flights, booking.com) that are "old companies" and no "life" in the category.
- Product proof points used in pitches: booking in about 13 seconds, saved details, auto check-in, agent booking for airlines without a booking API, and iMessage/text booking. The team also pitches "book flights faster than this car" as an exaggerated hook.
- Refund protection is sold as a $17 add-on that makes a flight fully refundable for any reason until departure. Soar offers it itself, not through the airline.
- Early traction came from Twitter: Alex says the first week got about 2 million views.

## QR-code flight credits and gamified giveaways

Offline distribution is built on cheap, trackable incentives that get strangers to try the product.

- The team handed out roughly 200-300 QR codes outside YC Startup School, each giving a $25 discount. Alex estimates the stack is "easily like 3K worth of flight credits" for the month.
- A blackjack game is played with passers-by: win and get $25 in credit, lose and "you lose nothing. There's no risk here." It turns a flyer handoff into a conversation and a demo.
- In conversations, the team demos booking on the spot and pitches against pain points people mention (for example, a customer who lost most of a flight's cost despite travel insurance).
- A tweet offering $250 in flight credit to anyone who brings a girl to the party was funny enough that a news outlet (Business Insider Australia) wanted to cover it. Alex's plan is to post the coverage on Twitter and get people to repost or quote-tweet it.
- His summary of the approach: "we just got to keep doing stuff like this, literally."

## Branded car and wearable assets for events

The 1980 Volvo and merch are used as rolling billboards.

- The Volvo cost about $2,000, is unregistered and was bought for fun and visibility ("You going to see why I like shitboxes?"). Alex says plates from another car are used to drive it.
- New designs on the cars and apparel (a faded gradient matching the app's UI "clouds") mean "we can now go into hackathons or events and they'll see the logo and be like, 'Oh [expletive] you're the Soar guys.'"
- He considers chalk spray paint as a cheap further creative touch.
- The team keeps a rule of picking up stunt ideas from other founders. A person who runs crazy stunts like "Hollyweed" approached them to get involved.

## Content as a distribution channel

The whole campaign is filmed as a vlog series, and the content itself is part of the marketing.

- Alex's reasoning: someone interested in startups who watches the vlogs will see the brand, try it, follow along, and tell friends.
- He sells the series on two things: entertainment ("sit back, chill with your boys") and learning ("you'll learn a lot if you stay with us").

## Lessons from the Airbnb party incident

The team's public party invite got them removed from their rental, which shows the risk of public stunts.

- A tweet linking an open party at their Airbnb was spotted by Airbnb, which told the host the group could not stay (policy against parties). Alex says they had AI-generated the first listing image, and the team had not actually thrown the party at that house.
- They were asked to leave within a day and replaced the rental with a house in Danville with a hot tub, booked at what Alex called "8,000 euros".
- Alex's take: record bookings kept coming ("we've been breaking our record every day") even while housing was in chaos, so the stunt cost was acceptable. Public links can create real operational problems, though.

## Hiring and team signals

The team picks up growth talent by watching who shows up.

- A candidate for head of growth said he scaled a previous company to a couple hundred thousand and pitched himself on hunger: he flew in and showed up when "nothing was guaranteed".
- Alex jokes that they "did just fire our head of growth. Wasn't good enough."
- The team found the candidate's location using an AI image search of a tweet photo, and found phone numbers through paid data tools ($5 to $60), which they treated as a sign of resourcefulness.
- Soar's team has one developer on the back end, building the agent booking and auto check-in.
