## Pricing and the paywall: low entry price, volume over per-user revenue

- Quitter's app (a porn-quitting app) moved off its original price of $12.99 a month and $45 a year, where most revenue came from the yearly plan. The new price is $6.99 a week and $29.99 a year.
- The point of the change was to lower the barrier to join. Alex says (credited to Blake Anderson) "it's not about how much you can make per paying user, it's about the volume of users you can get to pay for a smaller amount", because that produces more total revenue.
- Payments run through Superwall, which integrates with Apple Pay.
- Quitter has no free trial, except an offer shown to people who are uninstalling the app. Alex's reasons:
  - The business is predictable: a given number of downloads converts to a known number of sales and revenue, which is reinvested into marketing. Free trials add uncertainty that would disrupt that, especially in November, their biggest month.
  - "When something's free people act like it's free." Retention and activity go up when people pay, because they have high intent to use the app and join the community.
  - They would rather lose some users and have the rest pay than give the app away and try to convert later.
- About 85% of users do not convert on the initial paywall. Alex is collecting emails so that discounts can be pushed to that 85%, with the hope that 10-20% of them convert. He calls it a numbers game and a way to "squeeze as much juice" out of every download.
- In the onboarding flow the app asks for a review. Only about 1% do it, but at 20,000 downloads that is about 200 reviews. It also asks again while people are using the app.

## Influencer marketing: how Alex structures deals and pays

- Standard deal: a retainer of about $1,000 a month for one video a week (four a month), plus a minimum view count and video count per month. Alex says "the key is volume".
- Alex was paying about $1 per thousand views early on and calls that a very good deal. It has since moved to roughly $2-3 per thousand, and in general it comes out between $1 and $5. They are about to test paying $1-2 per thousand views directly.
- Because deals have minimum videos and views, he can estimate ROI: if 100,000 views bring in X in revenue, then multiplying by four months shows what the retainer is worth. Contracts can be structured to test a creator first.
- Not every thousand views is worth the same:
  - A video with 20-40k views earned about $2,000 (he also cites a roughly 40,000-view video earning about $2,000 and a 20-30k one making "two free grand" in the speech).
  - A 1 million view video earned about $200.
- Takeaway: find out who watches the creator and whether they are your target market. If so, pay for engagement even at 20,000 views a video, and he would pay more for a highly engaged creator than for a viral one with little engagement.
- How he evaluates creators: he looks for consistent views (5-10k, 10-20k, 30-50k per video with some hits near 100k) rather than follower count. A creator with a million followers who gets 20,000 views will still ask for a lot of money. He then reads the comments to see whether people ask about solutions to the problem the app solves, and he says he "eyeballs" engagement rather than using a fixed metric.
- Negotiation: be honest about budget ("my budget is only $2,000, can we do a few trial videos"). He learned from Ridge Wallet that when someone asks for a figure like $6,000 it is usually what they want. Talk to creators on a call, and expect to DM about 100 influencers to get one response.
- Spread the budget: do not put all eggs in one basket. With a couple of thousand dollars, deploy about $500 a month across four creators for three to four videos each, and use different creators with different creatives.
- Creative control: give creators a structure but let them stay in their own style. Pushing something inorganic to their audience does not work, and Alex says they made that mistake before. Make sure the app is pushed in the call to action, subtly.
- Tracking: they give creators codes and also line up post times against when sales arrive. Codes only capture some sales, so they use them mostly to see where traffic comes from, and pay creators a flat $3 per code use in some arrangements so that not every user is paid for.
- A creator's audience matters more than the CTA. Alex says a Horsey-style creator's video converted well with a subtle CTA because of that creator's engaged, dark-humor audience, not because of the call to action.
- Where Alex is moving next: shifting from "draining" a creator's existing audience toward viral content that reaches a wider audience, with deals that incentivize creators to post viral material.
- Outreach tips: go through TikTok, Instagram, emails, even LinkedIn if needed. Be respectful but follow up, since many people are busy and a bump often gets a reply.

## First users, validation and finding an idea

- Alex got his first users from Reddit and Twitter organic posting. He made $2,000 from Reddit before being banned, and says the workaround is making other accounts. This is for the early stage without money.
- Timeline: 20,000 downloads in a month, with paying customers within about two weeks.
- How they chose the idea: both founders arrived separately at porn addiction. Alex says the top app in the category made about $100K a month, with only about 10,000 downloads, and "there's millions of men struggling with this". Knowing a competitor made that revenue gave him confidence it was a business.
- Tool: Sensor Tower (sensortower.com) to see competitor revenue and market size. Alex notes it is an estimate and that it can run about half of the real figure, but it is a good reference.
- Alex's advice for choosing an idea: use Sensor Tower to find categories where apps make millions, copy the winners (he mentions sleep apps), and win on distribution. Copying a proven idea "significantly reduces your risk".
- A complementary view from his co-founder: validate by seeing competitors making money, then out-distribute them.
- Pre-launch demand: you can run ads on several ideas to see which gets the most sign-ups, but Alex would rather trust his gut and build, because you learn more by building. A friend used ManyChat so people DM'd for access and got on a waitlist, and Alex says that is fine if it makes the founder feel more secure.

## Building the product: speed, quality bar and tech

- Alex builds in SwiftUI. He skips Android because it makes around 20% of what Apple's platform does in revenue.
- He leans on AI heavily: "you can build any app of your dreams in two, three days". Most of the Quitter app, including the hard parts like communities, was built with AI. Method: tell it what you want, paste the code, run it, and iterate with prompts until it matches what you picture. He also expects Xcode to add full AI app creation.
- Backend: Firebase for databases and Heroku for hosting, with payment security handled by Apple and platform providers rather than custom work.
- Quality bar: "don't ship something that you wouldn't use yourself." If you would not pay for it, it is a bad product. He is a perfectionist and says that trait stopped him from shipping. His advice is to spend an extra week making it a bit better, then push, and that someone will pay for anything you put out.
- Co-founder Connor adds: "great distribution with an average app beats a great app with average distribution," as long as the app solves the problem. Quitter went three months without its core content-blocking feature working and still reached almost $100,000 in revenue.
- Why mobile over web: everyone has a phone and consumers spend more on apps because payment is one tap.
- Content blocking plan: use Apple's Screen Time adult-content block setting, which the app can override, plus a desktop version later.
- Development vs marketing: Alex says development is harder to learn, but he thinks you need both, since marketing does not work with weak product or pricing.

## Community as a moat and retention driver

- The Telegram community existed from the start. Alex did nothing special to make it engaging, people simply asked questions and talked, and it grew slowly. That showed how valuable it was and led to a forum in the app, then moderators.
- He considers community "the best thing for retention": subscribers lean on it when they are struggling, so it is worth paying for.
- It is also a moat. Alex says you cannot "just rip off Quitter and make money because we have a community."
- Gamification plus community: once people build momentum they usually keep it.

## Scaling beyond influencers

- Alex and Connor are building systems to automate what is currently manual (DM the creator, sign a contract, watch results) into a repeatable formula that reaches many more creators.
- Plan by stage, from a document Alex shared: $0-10K a month is basic ASO plus organic posts; at $1M-$5M-$10M a month it is mostly paid ads, because ads let you allocate budget and force content at scale without the DM bottleneck. Master one stage before moving to the next.
- Paid ads: Meta ads launching that day (the ad was approved as he spoke). Targeting is men 20-28, US-based, health and fitness, with a broad pixel finding the audience, and creatives including street interviews, app demos and a 28-day challenge. Tracking uses the Facebook SDK in the app.
- Why Meta even if worse than influencers: influencers might return 10x on some deals, but Meta gives a consistent 2-3x that can be scaled by adding budget. He mentions that a competitor reportedly scales to $2,500 a day, which suggests it works.
- Tests coming: clipping content (for example from self-help and podcast figures talking about why porn is bad) to push volume; he also likes the idea of a dedicated Twitter-style page and notes viral videos can sometimes be reposted on a new account for similar results, which he hasn't validated himself.
- The ASO effect: more organic and external traffic raises app store rank. Searching "quit porn" puts Quitter around third and they are aiming for first.

## Co-founders, equity and personal brand

- Finding a co-founder: Y Combinator co-founder matching, or Twitter (messaging people in Blake Anderson's comments). Alex says you must be able to code, or you are "that guy that can't code". Co-founder Connor brought money to fund the first influencers, had a knack for sales and negotiation, and complements Alex's skills.
- Influencers and equity: Alex warns about giving equity to influencers. A friend, Zack, gave an influencer equity, later tried to terminate the contract, and is now being taken to court after the company grew. Alex's view is to pay a small fee and test, not give equity. Connor adds that if you do give equity, use a vesting cliff tied to conditions such as staying a year and delivering specific work.
- Personal brand: Alex thinks now is a "gold rush" and that those who build a personal brand and share information before the space saturates will last. He values it more long term than making money from a single app.
- Selling to wealthy parents (advice given to a student building tools for high school competitions): sell the outcome, such as getting into an Ivy League, rather than a higher score, and learn how existing tutors acquire clients. Alex's blunt view is that if the goal is money, copy proven categories rather than invent a niche.
