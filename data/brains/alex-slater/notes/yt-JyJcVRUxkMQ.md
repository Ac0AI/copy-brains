## Alex's path into apps and the Riz GPT launch

- Alex's first app, Peer Pressure, failed. He then saw consumer utility apps working and got serious about the space after a friend sent him a podcast featuring Blake Anderson and Omar.
- He was at buildspace season 5 in San Francisco. He had met Zach-side collaborator Connor eight months earlier through YC co-founder matching, when Connor was looking for a developer. Connor pitched an app to help men quit porn. Alex had already built it the week before, and Connor took on marketing. That became Quitter.
- Alex's reasoning for Riz GPT: people should be packaging ChatGPT into apps, and more and more people consume on phones rather than laptops. His plan was to build a product and market it on social media.
- He picked the most obvious idea: an AI dating assistant for guys who don't know how to talk to girls. The user uploads a screenshot and the app tells them what to say.
- He expected $10-20K a month, "enough for me to live" while he built other businesses. It did 80,000 MRR within 3 days of launch.
- The launch channel was influencer marketing with "underground niche, underpriced creators" who had a "super cult-like following." This was the very start of the AI app wave, when mobile apps were mostly social tools (NGL, TBH, Gas, Instagram wrapped).

## Why consumer apps took off now: three macro trends

Alex lays out three shifts, and says every new player in this space uses at least two, usually all three:

1. **New technology.** From the launch of the App Store until the release of ChatGPT in 2022-2023, there were no big technology shifts. Most big players were founded in the early 2010s (My Fitness Pal, Headspace, Calm) and kept winning. LLMs let you embed real functionality into a single-player product.
2. **Building is easier.** You used to need to hire developers, raise capital, or be highly skilled. Now you can code with LLMs, and database providers like Supabase are far easier to use.
3. **Dropshipping-style marketing.** It is very easy to get a product in front of people through influencers.

## Why influencer marketing works for apps

- The group sees influencer marketing as still working for apps, where some ecommerce founders say it is dead for physical products.
- Alex's explanation is novelty: shorts or gym clothes will "never be as novel as some like crazy new AI tech." A post about a new app can go more viral than the creator's average post.
- Emotional pain points also help. With Quitter, quitting porn is a strong emotional pain for men, and a community that helps them is what they need. The influencer or meme is only the vehicle.
- On saturation: Alex says the low-hanging AI app ideas have been captured, but novel ideas still have room. He notes that most influencer promos are for physical products, not apps, so apps are not what is crowding influencers.
- Deal structure is what makes it profitable. The group's contracts are an in-between of flat fee and incentive-based, with terms built to ensure a certain number of views. He also points out that an ecommerce brand switched to the Cali-style strategy of paying influencers with big followings instead of micro accounts where "one out of 10 videos would blow up."

## Low-effort, high-margin apps

- At the time of recording, Riz GPT does about $4M a year and UMAX about $4M a year, with Cali just under $40M (about $45M across the three). With Quitter at about $6M, the group is just over $50M in annual revenue.
- Alex's point: "if you build a good product, you don't have to maintain it. The code continues to provide the service on its own, and if you have the marketing system set up correctly, it'll just continue printing."
- Those apps run at over 50% profit margins. Each of UMAX and Riz GPT has only one full-time employee, a social media manager, plus a VA.
- Revenue on these apps is flat rather than churning, and the products are still being sold mostly to new users through ongoing downloads. Plans are weekly and other tiers.

## Monetizing the community without selling out the business

- App Mafia runs a free 14-hour course for people new to the space, and a paid "Insiders" community with an introductory call, weekly office hours, coaches and a network of successful founders.
- The reasoning: they get 100+ inbound requests a day for advice, and the paid community is how they decide who gets time. One member went from about 10K MRR to over 80K MRR.
- All the cash goes back into the community or operations (videographers, editors, clippers), not out as personal income.
- Strategic upside: learning what founders need and who succeeds helps if they later build a fund. Their personal brands grow, which they can use for hiring and for future businesses.
- Alex frames the content chapter as a personal-brand phase, not his whole life, running alongside his apps.

## Personal brand: when it helps and when it doesn't

- Alex argues against a one-size-fits-all rule. A personal brand has upsides (attention, DMs, options, hiring, influencing views) and costs (privacy, time).
- If you are building deep tech or B2B SaaS where distribution does not depend on social media, "posting little short-form skits isn't going to do anything for your business," and effort should be spent carefully.
- He still sees "a lot of opportunity and alpha" in building one, because eyes on your work give you options and let you "nudge the world."
- Long-term ambition: be the "Mr. Beast of entrepreneurship," giving people business entertainment instead of mindless entertainment.

## Advice for starting from zero

- Alex's own view, which he returns to as the closing advice: study and reverse-engineer successful products. He says the internet is "open sourced": you can see a competitor's App Store screenshots, logo, marketing and product and realise "I could probably do that too."
- Revenue as proof: "it only takes $1 revenue to give you the self-belief to keep pushing higher." He promised himself that after the first $10 sale he could reach 100, then 1,000. The first month hit 30K.
- Persistence: a founder should keep launching until one works.
