## Positioning: attacking a commoditized, confusing flight-booking market

- Alex's pitch for Saw (spelled "Sore" in the transcript) is "the best way to book flights": "[ __ ] Google Flights. [ __ ] Sky Scanner. Join the future. Book in one click."
- Extras he lists: auto check-in and iMessage flight updates.
- He is 20 and the founder. He describes his own role as co-founder: hiring, coming up with new ideas, and "doing the growth."
- His diagnosis of the category:
  - "No one's innovated. Everyone's too scared to because the margins on flights, they're very low. They're commoditized."
  - Airline companies "make money by confusing you with the upsells, the multiple pages." Flights themselves "will generate nothing."
- His bet is to win on experience even at lower margin: "even if we make less money, we can still be the best. And like that that is like very powerful."

## Business model idea: simple product first, monetize bags, seats and later hotels

- Alex's planned revenue is a small fee on add-ons: "if we charge like a small fee on bags and seats and then maybe we get into hotels, I think we'll be fine."
- The product promise is one tap: find the flight, book, enter code, done. He contrasts it with the old internet's "multiple pages of [ __ ]."
- He compares Saw to Tesla's direct online car sales: Tesla cut the clicks needed to buy a car online and removed the upsell-heavy flow, and he says "what Tesla did to the auto industry is kind of like what we're doing."
- The ambition he states: "I want 100 million people using it." He says he "really see[s] that being a reality."

## Attention-grabbing branding: spending investment money on a car

- Alex owns a branded Saw car, bought partly with raised money: "we raised some money. We spent some investment money on it. And hey, look, it gets the attention. So, job well done."
- He treats it as an attention play and judges it by the attention it gets, with no ROI figure given.
- His stated personal goal is "just be the best. Be the biggest."

## Fraud problem: stolen cards and VOIP numbers booking expensive flights

- Alex describes a fraud wave that first hit in June and came back. It comes from people in Accra, Ghana, who he thinks are using stolen credit cards.
- The signals he sees in the orders:
  - Fake VOIP phone numbers, so the customer has no iMessage.
  - High-ticket flights, such as $1.2K and $3.3K.
  - A stream of disputes in Stripe that Saw is losing: "lost, lost, lost, lost, lost."
- Why a simple country ban won't work: "someone's actually going to have a real flight from there and that's not good."
- His candidate fix is to compare the name on the card with the name on the flight and ban on a mismatch.
- The flaw he sees in that rule is personal assistants who legitimately book flights for other people, so banning that whole group isn't sustainable. He leaves it unresolved ("I need to figure this out").
- Volume check: he counted six flights in one hour and extrapolated to about 120 flights a day.
- He also wants a notification every time a Saw user is in the air.
