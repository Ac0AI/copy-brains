## Building a community to retain users after press spikes

- Pieter's problem: press coverage (e.g. Nomad List's launch) creates traffic spikes, then users leave and the tail slopes downwards.
- His fix is a community around the product's audience, which keeps bringing people back.
- A community "sticks a lot longer than press coverage". Once engaged, it can be turned into users for whatever product he pushes at that moment.
- He argues that "the hardest part of startups these days is not building the product or app. It's getting people to actually use what you built."

## A community doesn't need to be a forum or tied to your brand

- A community can be informal. It need not be a forum, discussion board, comments section, social network, or even on your own site.
- Example he cites: Ryan Hoover building Product Hunt by personally engaging users on Twitter and inviting product makers to comment when their products appeared on the site.
- A following can form organically. Press focused disproportionately on Pieter working remotely. He knew he needed a narrative for press, embraced it, and it gave him a following of people into remote working.
- Platforms can be Twitter, Slack, in-app, or in real life through meetups.
- For Nomad List he kept branding subtle: only the logo, and the name #nomads instead of Nomad List. The community targets the site's audience (digital nomads and aspiring ones), not only current users, and is only loosely tied to the brand.

## Launching the #nomads Slack: seed, test, roll out, then announce

Step by step, as Pieter did it in 2014:
1. Identify an audience that already exists but isn't connected. People tweeted and emailed him daily, but didn't know each other.
2. Set up a Slack chat with subtle branding.
3. Seed top-down: first invite authorities in the remote community who knew him and Nomad List (Amit Gupta of Photojojo, John O'Nolan of Ghost, James Seymour-Lock of Simple As Milk).
4. Test for a few weeks, then invite more people each week.
5. About a month later, launch publicly with a tweet and a nice picture ("open to new members until midnight EST", 27 October 2014).
6. Since Slack needs manual invites, link a Typeform sign-up form to Slack to auto-invite.

His view on quality: "quantity != quality", but seeding the right type of people from the start helps keep the base high quality.

## Results and a second launch on Product Hunt and Hacker News

- Within 24 hours: 300+ members and 5,000+ messages. He couldn't keep up with sending invites.
- Conversations organized themselves around cities (#chiang-mai, #bali) plus the main #nomads channel. Derivatives appeared on other platforms (a Sqwiggle room, an impromptu video chat).
- A competing nomad chat (NomadNet) pointed its members to #nomads because it was growing faster.
- A few days later he built a quick landing page at hashtagnomads.com and submitted it to Product Hunt and Hacker News. It passed Microsoft's Band there and pushed the community to 500+ members, adding tech people interested in nomadism.
- By December 2014 the community was almost 1,500 users.

## Keeping spam out and the community inclusive

- With spammers and predatory HR recruiters arriving, he added a $15 to $25 price for new sign-ups (depending on how popular the chat is) as an artificial limit on growth.
- He deliberately seeded with people aware of sexism, aiming for a gender-positive community. Past nomad communities were often male-dominated ("bromads").
- His reasoning: he wants everybody to become a nomad, so the community must be "highly inclusive, non-threatening and inviting to everybody."
- He appointed a diverse group of moderators and admins.

## Why a community pays off: user research, launches, and IRL meetups

- Free user focus group: he sees what the audience talks about, what their problems are, can ask what they want built, and gets feedback while building.
- Launch channel: new products or features can be launched instantly to the community for users and feedback.
- IRL: city channels spawned meetup talk within a week. He made them official Nomad List meetups. The first, in Berlin, was organized by David Nagy. Others were planned in Montreal, Chiang Mai, Phuket, Hong Kong and Bali, and anyone can sign up to organize one.
- Meetups are a natural form of marketing for Nomad List and future nomad products.
- His conclusion: build an engaging community from day one. It improves engagement, supports real-time user research, and the Nomad List user tail "goes up again".
