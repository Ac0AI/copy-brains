## Why Pieter started as a digital nomad: cost, tech timing, and building startups

- Pieter began traveling in 2013 after graduating in Brazil. He did not know the term "digital nomad" yet.
- Holland was too expensive to stay and build startups on his limited money and savings, so he went to Asia.
- He credits "a lot of coincidences": internet was just becoming fast enough to work from a laptop on the other side of the world (Skype did not work in Bali in 2014, only from 2017), and he was a new graduate who wanted to build startups rather than get a normal job.
- He blogged and tweeted about it, which pulled the idea into the developer and programmer world while it was still "very nascent."

## How the digital nomad market has split into subgroups

Pieter says the single backpack-and-laptop niche of 10+ years ago has fragmented. The people who were 25 are now 35, often with kids, and want to do it their own way.

- **Settled nomads:** many stopped traveling and settled in places other than where they were born (he lives in a beach town in Portugal, "very like hippie and chill, kind of like Bali"; others choose Costa Rica). Some homeschool, live in eco villages, or grow food.
- **Crypto and Bitcoin people:** have their own nomad space.
- **Network states:** such as Prospera, are popular, especially with crypto people. He notes Switzerland seems to be starting something similar.
- **Young people in their 20s:** travel and work remotely "without even knowing it" because they grew up with the internet. For example, Americans bring a laptop to Spain or Portugal and stay for months.

He says he is thinking of giving another presentation, in person, on where this goes next.

## What startups to build now: services for settling nomads and migrants

Pieter's advice on where the opportunity is:

- Look less at the nomads of 10 years ago and more at settling nomads looking for the most attractive place to live.
- The decision factors he lists: taxes, wealth, entrepreneurship and business climate, free speech, health care, safety, clean streets, how good it is to raise kids, education, and whether international schools exist.
- Build services that get these people to those places and help them move.
- He says companies helping people migrate already exist and it is "a big market. If you want to jump in there, for sure."
- Health care is an essential component: "You don't want to be in a foreign country and you can't go to the hospital and you can't pay for it."

## Why remote work is now normal, and why top talent keeps freedom

- COVID normalized remote work. Before, it was "very strange or fringy"; after, everybody had done it and knew it worked.
- Companies are now pushing people back to the office (often hybrid), but people prefer freedom.
- Top talent keeps its freedom because it can choose where to work. He points to top AI talent, who "can work with however they want to be because they are so important and so valuable."

## Pieter's view on Europe: no startups, high regulation, and slow government

- Having lived in Asia for about 10 years, then returning to Portugal during COVID, he says some things in Europe are better: clean air, more freedom and democracy, and free speech in theory.
- Much is behind: it is "not modern" or convenient, and there are hardly any startups. He says no European company is in the top 25 by market cap and there are no big European tech companies.
- European founders move to San Francisco to raise money and never come back, so "Europe is not benefiting from startup economies at all. They educate and raise."
- Rather than only complain, he started tweeting solutions and crowdsourcing ideas. People then asked him for advice, and he talked to the ex-prime minister of Italy and the ex-director of the European Central Bank, whose report included his feedback.
- He says change is very slow and he has not seen results yet.

## Why Pieter thinks network states and places like Dubai are gaining ground

- He ties his bullishness on network states to a tweet about people, especially founders from the UK, moving to Dubai.
- He says Dubai has a bad reputation in Europe, but he has been there and "things are pretty good": people seem happy and there is a lot of business and entrepreneur activity.
- In Europe he sees higher taxes, more regulation, more censorship (Germany, Britain), and slow health care with waiting lists of 9 months to 2 years for certain operations. His view: "If you're paying into this system, you're not getting rewarded anymore."
- He frames network states less as new countries and more as places like Dubai, special economic zones, and Portugal that attract entrepreneurs and foreigners with money to spend. Qatar, Dubai and Singapore run in "a more rational way": how to make the best service for citizens.
- His tension: startups move fast and governments move slowly, so they "don't go together."
- He still values democracy and free speech and prefers to live where he can "say whatever I want" without going to jail, noting that many top-performing countries are autocratic monarchies.

## What a global safety net should provide

When asked what the most important feature is, Pieter says:

- Emergency care first: if something happens, he wants to reach a local hospital wherever he lives or travels and get the best care possible.
- Due process like any other service: claims handled fast, with money paid quickly so he can cover bills wherever he is in the world.
