## Starting with 70 projects, and why most fail

- Pieter says he has launched "over 70 products projects" and only three or four worked out, such as Nomad List and Remote OK.
- He puts the odds of any single startup working at about 3 to 4 percent, even when you do well.
- His advice: "you need to try like 20 30 times." One project worked on for a year will probably fail.
- A first product will probably fail and so will the second. The failure is a reality check that your product has to be better or cheaper to matter.
- He warns against founders who say "I'm gonna build a billion dollar company" and won't share the idea. They launch and "nobody cares."

## Finding ideas at the fringe of what people secretly want

- Pieter's test for a market: be where many people are privately thinking the same thing but aren't yet saying or doing it.
- Nomad List came from watching 20 to 40 nomads already living in Chiang Mai. They were a "fringe" group of slightly weird people doing something new.
- Rebase (immigration as a service for moving to Portugal) came from his own move and from people repeatedly asking him how to do the same.
- He quotes the idea that what nerds do on the weekends, everybody else does in the week ten years later.
- He argues people self-censor ideas that feel too crazy. Being "radically honest" about what you think surfaces what others secretly think.
- Doing what everyone else is doing gives horrible results. You need to be on the edge of something new.
- He credits timing and topic over launch technique. The remote work and nomad topic had been hot since 2014. People point to his followers, but he says that wasn't true at the start.

## How Rebase started, step by step

- Origin: he registered as a Portuguese resident himself after living in Ericeira during Covid, and had immigration lawyers and tax advisers check his setup.
- People kept asking to do the same. He first made a Typeform and referred his immigration advisors, getting some paying customers.
- In November he built a landing page and launched it on Twitter with a photo of his laptop on his bed: "POV: building an immigration as a service startup." It went viral and got 500 signups in a month.
- His naive approach: take slow, email-and-invoice-and-bank-wire processes and put them on Stripe with digital forms. He says you need a naive perception that this will work.
- Current scale as he states it: 400 to 500 applicants per month, and he says that rate would be about 10 percent of Portuguese immigration, roughly 50,000 to 60,000 people moving to Portugal a year.
- He says Rebase makes "two to three million a year" with almost no expenses, though he doesn't say whether that is revenue or profit.
- His pride is in helping Venezuelan families get out. He calls it "a PHP script in Excel" saving people.

## Launch platforms are different from each other

- Pieter's biggest annoyance: people who write a tweet, then post the same thing to Instagram, TikTok and Reddit. Each platform behaves differently.
- Reddit is "completely anti-spam." The only way to use it is to be a beneficial community member, give value and ask for feedback, not announce "I launched my startup."
- Nobody cares that you launched a startup. Put yourself in the reader's shoes and ask whether you would click.
- He says people overestimate their own idea's specialness, and a real launch gives a reality check.

## Side gig first, savings and cheap living second

- He recommends starting as a side gig. He cites his own path: YouTube music channels earned him $1,000 to $2,000 a month, sometimes $8,000, during university (2008 to 2010).
- That income was his base when he left in April 2014. His first real money from nomad projects came around August 2014, over a year later.
- Warns about "golden handcuffs": the higher your salary climbs, the harder it is to leave, because earning 5k or 10k alone takes years.
- Use freedom to live somewhere cheap. In Chiang Mai he paid about $200 a month for a hotel room and a dollar or so for a meal. A small town in your own country also works.
- Cheap living can multiply your runway (he says about 5x versus an Amsterdam rent of 1,500 or more).
- He says university is a good time to work on projects because you barely need to attend.
- Don't run out of money and have to go back. He doesn't recommend burning your ships on savings alone.

## Why entrepreneurship is closer to art than corporate business

- He did a business bachelor's and an entrepreneurship master's, and says entrepreneurship is "way closer to creativity" than to corporate management, which he traces to 1950s US military management theory.
- Compares entrepreneurs to artists: you make things nobody likes for years, and it is emotionally exhausting because nothing you do works. The difference with a job is you don't get paid from day one.
- He calls it "one of the hardest things to do in life," a thing that takes sacrifice, and says the barrier to entry is why not everyone succeeds.
- Business theory like Porter's five forces is "all quite subjective" and can't be tested. He says he learned a thousand times more from eight years of doing than from university.
- If he were 18, he would skip university, go nomad and start businesses. He sees university mainly as a discipline test and a social experience.
- On stress: his late-20s breakdown peaked at 27, driven by fear of turning 30 unsuccessful. Therapy and getting the business working made him calmer and more autonomous.
- A million-dollar company means roughly 100k to 200k a year of revenue at a 5 to 10x multiple. He calls it "absolutely possible" for a solo app or website.

## Mindset: build solo, build to help, stay unattached

- He builds alone: he writes the code, designs, makes the logo and database and does the marketing, and says you can change people's lives from a laptop.
- Not every project is monetized. He built an airline safety and route-map site (Airline List) partly because it was fun, and it filters for safe airlines.
- He values autonomy over your own decisions as a growing trend, linked to decentralization (crypto, censorship resistance), though he thinks the tech isn't ready yet.
- He ties impermanence and not clinging to things to being able to take risks like moving countries.
