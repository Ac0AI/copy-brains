## Why Pieter prefers the indie hacker path over VC

- He says the main thing he hates about VC is seeing companies with no users raise $50M or $100M on hype, with no traction, and then disappear.
- He recommends validating first and building a real business before scaling, e.g. getting to 50K or 100K a month.
- Raising money locks in a "go big or bust" expectation. As a founder you can't easily stay in between: you can't make $10M a year, you "kind of need to make $100 million a year" and become a unicorn.
- He notes the odds of becoming a unicorn are "a few percentage maybe", and people spend five to seven years of their 20s on it.
- The indie alternative: a $10M a year company where you keep ownership. He calls this liberating.
- He says that in 2013-14 there was no indie hacking or bootstrapping scene. The norm was raise money, grow fast, hire a lot of people, get a big office.
- Despite being financially independent and getting VC DMs, he hasn't raised. He says playing with other people's money "sounds fun" but he isn't convinced yet.
- He has started investing as an angel (Cursor is one example) but says he feels like a "faker".

## Scale, portfolio and what the business looks like

- His businesses make more than $3 million a year in revenue, run solo.
- Figures cited in the interview: Nomad List reached $700K ARR, Remote OK about $3.4-4M in revenue, Photo AI $600K ARR.
- He has launched about 70 projects, possibly more, and loses track.
- He tries to keep most of them running because he likes keeping "internet history" alive.
- Go Fucking Do It (gofuckingdoit.com) is a commitment product: you set a goal and deadline, name a friend as referee, and enter a card via Stripe. The card is not charged up front. On the deadline the friend is emailed, and if they say you failed, you are charged and he keeps the money.
- That product still earns about $50 a month. He hasn't changed the PHP code in 10 years.
- He started making money in 2014, when Stripe arrived in Holland.
- Earlier, he monetised music on YouTube as one of the first monetised channels, making $1,000 to $8,000 a month while still at university.

## Make things to solve your own problems

- His origin story: for a decade he worked on his laptop, traveling and making small creative projects to solve his own problems.
- Most of the time nobody else needed them. "A few times it worked."
- He learned from Patrick McKenzie's Appointment Reminder, which made $60,000 a year, and from Yongfook (Singapore, nomad, a well-built SaaS).
- What inspired him was seeing "honest cool clean businesses" that were not VC-funded.
- He argues that profit comes from many shots at small projects rather than one big bet.

## Audience, brand and distribution

- A host noted he sold $50,000 of pre-orders for his book before writing it.
- He attributes this partly to his brand and partly to work: he tweeted about 125,000 times over 10 years, roughly 40 tweets a day.
- He says ChatGPT referrals have grown fast. Google traffic was about 100 against ChatGPT at about 4, i.e. 4%. A month before the interview it was 20%, a 5x jump, and he saw the same story on other sites.
- He says this helps because he hates SEO and is a solo person who just wants to "make a cool product and a cool website with a nice title".

## Automate instead of hiring

- He dislikes hiring because he doesn't like managing people and prefers creating things.
- Automation is what lets one person run multiple projects.
- Example: the Nomad List community chat (about 40,000 people) was draining to moderate by hand, with constant drama and accusations of bias.
- He now writes down the chat rules and has the GPT API moderate. It doesn't ban people; it mutes them for ten minutes or a day, like debate moderators muting mics.
- He says the AI is neutral, which a human moderator can't be.
- His rule of thumb: either hire people or use the GPT API, and he picks the API.

## Money, investing and product ideas

- He recommends index funds (S&P 500, MSCI World) and not selling in drawdowns.
- He warns against home-country bias, citing his parents holding Royal Dutch Shell and Dutch stocks instead of S&P 500.
- He says to put founder income into the market rather than spend it all, since startup earning time is limited.
- He suggested a European-focused index investing product as a business idea, since good products for Europeans are lacking. The host's counterpoint was that resale margins are thin at Vanguard-like fees (0.07%).
- Personal finance isn't taught in school and cash "disappears the moment you have it."

## Stripe feedback and working with payments

- He says the Stripe API got more complicated after COVID and felt "run by engineers, not by people who actually use Stripe".
- His example: a change to the API stopped creating a customer so everyone became a guest, a default he found illogical.
- He thinks it has improved and that "sanity has returned".
- He wants Stripe to keep its original simplicity: one line of Stripe.js gave you a payment button.
- His own usage: he goes to Stripe to see how much money he made, then goes back to coffee and code. He doesn't have time for advanced features like workflows.
- He says to avoid becoming corporate as a company grows, and to expose users slowly to what's possible.

## Europe and EU accelerationism

- He started an "EU accelerationism" push, inspired by effective accelerationism in the US, with a crowdsourced bug board where tens of thousands submitted and upvoted ideas to fix Europe.
- His top idea: remove the regulatory burden for new businesses, and kill cookie banners.
- He argues Europe needs politics that is both pro-business and pro-federal Europe, which doesn't currently exist.
- His view: "Getting rich is simply a representation of your added value to the world economy in most cases." He says Europeans should drop the idea that business and getting rich is bad.
- He says it's good it's going badly now: "Never waste a crisis."

## Digital nomad lifestyle

- He has lived in more than 40 countries and 150 cities.
- He recommends anyone try it regardless of age. His first experience was a university exchange in Korea around 2010.
- He says it is safer than people think. In 10 years nothing happened to him; the only burglary was in his hometown.
- He admits it can be very lonely, and that you lose contact with your home culture and become "untethered", which takes years to rebuild.
- He advises staying flexible: "You need to be neuroflex."
