## Pieter's case against startup scenes, conferences and mentors

- Pieter argues most startup scenes outside Silicon Valley are "all about events" and conferences, with little actually being made. He singled out the Dutch government-backed startup ecosystem organizations as spending millions on events.
- He says networking coffee meetings mostly produce "investors" who turn out to be bullshitters.
- His rule: "just make stuff and then get it over with." Creativity is sitting alone in front of the laptop and doing it, not listening to someone at a conference.
- He thinks the academic habit of needing mentors and teachers is a trap for founders. You learn from doing, and "real reality is the best school."
- His TL;DR: be original, do cool things, don't take courses, learn the code yourself, learn the basics, and stop going to conferences and network events because you won't learn anything.
- He says ads don't work well anymore because people use ad blockers and want real things. The product itself has to become the marketing, like a great beer you come back for and tell friends about.

## How the 12 startups in 12 months experiment led to Nomad List

- Pieter spent about a year building a YouTube analytics dashboard (database and API for managing multiple channels). Vice and other big media companies used it, then stopped. It made zero dollars.
- He then committed to launching one project every month and blogged it as "I'm launching 12 startups in 12 months." He finished roughly seven or eight, because Nomad List took over.
- Projects included:
  - Play My Inbox: scraped Gmail for YouTube music links into a playlist.
  - Giftbook: animated GIFs turned into flip books, sold roughly 500.
  - Goal-setting app with a charged credit card if you fail: went viral, made a lot of money, but wasn't a sustainable business.
  - Remote job board: filtered remote listings from other boards.
- Nomad List began as a Google spreadsheet of cities (cost, internet speed, climate) that he shared on Twitter. Thousands of people edited it, and he turned it into a website.
- It hit number one on Hacker News (600 points), number one on Product Hunt, and the front page of Reddit, then triggered press interviews.
- Later monetization: a paid social network for about $20-30 a month with almost 10,000 members, and a database of about 1,000 cities and 100,000 data points. Revenue went from about $5,000 a month to about $40,000 a month, "almost half a million a year."
- He built it alone from a laptop, owns 100%, had hardly any costs, no funding, and no paid marketing.
- His five stages: idea, build, launch, grow, monetize.

## Finding ideas: solve your own problems, get original, start small

- Build for problems you personally have. You are the best customer, and if a thousand others pay $10 a month, that's $10,000 a month.
- Everyone has the same ideas (friend finders, photo apps, food delivery), so you must become a more original person.
- Ways he lists to become original:
  - Travel, which exposes you to new cultures and problems.
  - Work lots of different jobs, such as a factory or coffee shop, to see problems on the work floor.
  - Spend time in fringe subcultures.
  - Be early on fringe topics. Nomad work was very niche in 2014. He concedes it's a gamble but "better than being the same."
- His own ideas came from travel. The flip book idea came from seeing a shop in Hong Kong. He says sitting at home with the coffee machine produces no ideas.
- Start small with a hook rather than a "spaceship company." Example: instead of a generic travel tours app, a group travel app for women, which gives a story to pitch to press. You can expand later.
- Nomad List started as a social network for digital nomads and is expanding to travelers.

## Building: do it yourself, keep it simple, automate, work alone

- Don't outsource development. Changing a button color through a remote team takes meetings and days, while doing it yourself takes five seconds. He wants a fast feedback loop.
- Learn just the basics, just in time. He compares it to learning to ride a bicycle, not racing like Lance Armstrong. Skip big frameworks like Meteor and Angular, which he says are a year-long rabbit hole and make some startups take six months to get a landing page online.
- Use the tools you know. Suggested MVP tools:
  - Public spreadsheets (his own started as one).
  - Typeform, which can even take payments.
  - Squarespace with e-commerce.
  - WordPress or basic HTML and PHP.
- Automate from day one. His sites run cron jobs, "robots on my server," that fetch weather or find co-working spaces and add them to the site, freeing him to build features and validate ideas.
- Put a buy button in the MVP, even a fake one. If someone enters card details, show a pop-up saying "we're not live yet, we haven't saved your card, we'll email you when we're done." That validates demand. He cites a startup that began with a "plans and pricing" button and an email box.
- Work alone. Competing VC-funded teams of 20 in Silicon Valley take months to add a feature. His "meeting" is thinking in bed what to add tomorrow, then adding it. Hire contractors later if needed.

## Launch tactics: prepare it, press, Product Hunt, Hacker News, Reddit

- Launch is "the most important stage." If you mess it up, product quality won't matter.
- An MVP is ready when the bugs are gone and basic functionality works. He criticizes launching a landing page with only an email box, because nothing has been built.
- Preparation checklist:
  - Add analytics (he admits he forgets and later finds zero data).
  - Collect emails, because launch day brings 10,000-20,000 visitors who mostly won't return.
  - Add a feedback form (he used Olark) so bugs reach him immediately.
- Press: find journalists relevant to your niche, not just TechCrunch. Make it a personal story, and keep it to one or two sentences, three at most. Send to 100 journalists and "maybe five will write about you."
- Product Hunt: email your existing list, tell them it's live, and don't ask them to vote. Upvotes can follow naturally.
- Hacker News: you need a few people to upvote early in the first hour, then the rest push it up. If it doesn't catch, it fails.
- Reddit: pick a subreddit relevant to the product that is also on the front page. Don't write a boastful title like "best app ever." Say plainly what you built and that it might be relevant.
- For niches, go to specialist forums (his example: a motorcycle forum), write a humble post, and keep interacting. Build requested features quickly, even on launch day, to get users engaged.
- After launch, check analytics (his example: 2,000 visitors, 6,000 euros). Traffic always drops afterward, which is normal. If the idea didn't work, scrap it.

## Growth: skip growth hacking, relaunch often, spin off features, re-engage

- He says "[ __ ] growth hackers" and calls the scene sleazy, full of former SEO spammers and addictive social patterns. He prefers organic growth where the product is the marketing.
- Don't pay for traffic early. Paid users hide whether the startup is validated, and when the money runs out the users leave.
- Launch repeatedly. Instead of one launch, ship a new feature every few months and send it to press and relevant communities. He shows traffic spikes in May, July and August, with drops between that require new launches.
- Spin off features as standalone side projects:
  - After Brexit he rapidly reskinned Nomad List into an "escape the Brexit" site and sent it to UK press, getting lots of coverage and traffic.
  - He spun the co-working spaces feature out as a separate site, Place to Work, submitted it to Product Hunt and Hacker News, and it now sends traffic back to Nomad List.
  - Pick features that can live on their own and take them out.
- Tell stories to press about new features, such as strategy or a bigger market, rather than "we added a feature."
- Build in public, like Buffer sharing revenue and every good or bad event.
- Make sharing easy. Generate dynamic preview images for every page (for example a San Francisco city page) so links get far more clicks on Facebook and Twitter.
- Re-engage old users with web push notifications and emails. When he launched Nomad List 2 he emailed users, asked them to reply directly, and got traffic and engagement.
- Keep shipping like Snapchat's weekly new filters. If nobody uses a new feature, scrap it and focus on what is monetizable.

## Monetization: charge early, recurring memberships, and other models

- If you make zero dollars "you're doing a hobby and it's not a real startup." He says developers feel guilty charging, which is wrong given the weeks spent building.
- Free apps lose more money as they grow and when they explode. Paid apps make more money as they grow. So charge immediately and think about monetization at the start. He says his early Play My Inbox never earned because he didn't think about it.
- Business models he discusses:
  - Affiliate: poor. Hotel bookings on Nomad List gave about five bucks for five bookings.
  - Display ads: "dead."
  - Native text ads: work. Sell them yourself or via networks, or add a simple text link.
  - Sponsorships: Matt Mullenweg of WordPress/Automattic emailed to sponsor Nomad List on launch, giving about 2,000-4,000 dollars a month immediately and making him profitable. He suggests cold emailing companies to sponsor.
  - Paid membership: gate the chat group behind a "join a community" paywall. He made it recurring, about $30 a month, and says user count builds up so revenue "almost double[s] every year."
  - Patronage: a friend's YouTube channel about Japan has 835 patrons paying about 3,000 a month. Overcast dropped premium features and asks for optional payment.
- Put buy buttons on everything to validate demand.

## Build for future trends and when to ditch an idea

- Be ahead of the curve. Trends he names:
  - Short-term relationships and less marriage.
  - Less home ownership, more renting and travel.
  - Changing family units and co-living.
  - Local food and robot farms.
  - An "existential crisis" from comfort, so entertainment or products that address it.
  - Basic income.
  - 3D, VR and AR, which he is currently learning (building an interactive Japan street in Unity).
- Ditch rule: if it isn't making money or getting traffic after a few months, scrap it. He criticizes people who spend years on something that will never work and call it determination.
- On boring work plus life: grinding four hours with headphones, then going salsa dancing, was a good combination of boring and amazing. He says living in a place for more than two weeks changes your identity, since it's shaped by your environment.
