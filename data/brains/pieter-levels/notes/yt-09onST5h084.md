## Remote work will turn some home-workers into nomads, and the 1 billion nomads prediction

- Pieter's 2015 prediction was 1 billion digital nomads by 2035, driven by cheap air travel, more freelancers, remote workers, the sharing economy and better internet. The prediction predates his later work, but he still stands by it.
- His definition is loose: a remote worker who works from outside their home country for at least part of the year. He says full "bouncing around from city to city every week" nomads are rare, and "people go crazy if you do that".
- Evidence he cites: he says about 70% of startup teams in Silicon Valley were part or mostly remote when they started out. Regular companies were forced into remote work by Corona.
- His logic for the transition: first people work from home, then from cafes, then from another city, then from another country. He did this himself while finishing his thesis in San Francisco and running a YouTube music channel.
- His estimate: perhaps 5-10% of work-from-home remote workers will make the jump. On a US labor force of 150-200 million, that is roughly 20 million people potentially working from other cities or countries.
- He points to "Zoom towns" (remote workers moving to smaller towns and booking Airbnbs) as the early sign.

## Why Nomad List exists: solving his own problem with criteria quality-of-life rankings ignore

- Nomad List started as a personal problem in about 2014, when he was nomading through Chiang Mai, Bangkok and Vietnam. These places did not appear in typical quality-of-life rankings, which favor Scandinavia and Vienna.
- He set criteria specific to remote workers: internet speed, safety, weather (mild or warm), and cost.
- He collected the data and ranked cities against it. Later he added social data, so the ranking reflects whether other nomads actually live there and whether you can make friends or find community.
- His principle: with a laptop-based life, the places that win are the ones with a community of similar people, not just good infrastructure.
- Pattern worth noting: he built a product by picking a problem from his own life and quantifying the criteria nobody else measured.

## Company culture: half-remote doesn't work

- He agrees with David Heinemeier Hansson and Matt Mullenweg that hybrid companies (some people in the office, some remote) have a cultural problem. Remote people are left out of social gatherings that can't be replicated online.
- His view: a company should be either a physical-office company or fully remote.
- He expects many regular companies, such as the Dutch ones he sees, to drift back to a split team after lockdowns and hit this problem.

## Fringe to mainstream: how remote work went from a subculture to normal

- Five years earlier remote work and nomadism got huge pushback and were a "fringe subculture". Interviewer's company had to hide being remote from investors in 2016; by 2020 it was a plus.
- His insight: debates and arguments don't decide adoption. "You can always find arguments against things when that's not your culture. But then when the culture changes, you're like, 'Okay, I'll do it, too.'"
- Every fringe subculture has the potential to go mainstream, and early adopters should expect resistance at first.
- He compares it to underground drum and bass: a scene either becomes mainstream or dies out. A business built on a counterculture needs it to grow, even though growth costs the "romantic" early feeling.
- Startups work the same way: you start something because it is missing from the market and culture, you push it, and by pushing it you create the market.
- Consequence for him: he has to find the next fringe thing, which he says he hadn't worked out yet and might take 5-10 years.

## Bootstrapping versus venture funding

- He has never raised funding or taken bank loans. Revenue goes back into the business, and mostly into food and coffee so he can code more. He avoids hiring and uses only short-term contracts.
- Exit odds argument: he cites odds of roughly 0-10% for a startup exit. If a startup takes about 5 years, "you'd need to be doing startups for 50 years to get one exit".
- On VCs: he says most VC funds return less than an S&P 500 ETF, VCs earn on management fees (he gives roughly 3-5% per year, e.g. $5 million a year on a $100 million fund), and pension funds put around 10% of their money into high-risk venture portfolios because they need the exposure.
- His advice: unless you have a really unfair advantage, start small and bootstrap.
- Market signal: when bootstrapped, anything you change shows up immediately in revenue, a very direct connection to the market. With VC money in the bank there is little incentive to build revenue early, so "you can do a lot of BS as a startup".
- He says he could raise VC money on his revenue now but would rather reinvest it himself.
- Valuation note: a website broker told him he typically pays three to four to five times annual revenue. Founders think their product is special; to the market it is a normal business. He wouldn't sell Nomad List: "It's my baby".

## Why people go nomad, and what is still unsolved

- His reasons: it became possible, boredom with a routine 9-to-5, wanting different cultures and adventures in his 20s, and the chance to be around people who fit you better than your birthplace's community does.
- Purpose-based moves: anime fans to Japan, jiu-jitsu fighters to Brazil, snowboarders to ski towns (Lake Tahoe, Denver, Boulder), surfers to surf towns. He expects cities to differentiate more by interest, self-reinforcing.
- Waves: first wave around 2007 (Tim Ferriss), second around 2014, third after Corona.
- Startup requests he names:
  - Kids' education for nomad families: central online coursework from a certified provider (e.g. Harvard), plus affiliated local schools running the practical classes with other kids. Nomad parents often want alternatives like Montessori.
  - Community building. Even slowmading between Bali and Seoul makes friendships harder than staying in one place for 20 years.
  - Facilitating remote work hubs physically with services, which he calls a good business, though he isn't sure of the form.
- Against building villages: he says communes in the 1960s often broke up from infighting, and he doesn't think privately built villages or castles work. He recommends iterating on existing places: build roots in a hub, open a school, a nice co-working space or cafe, and "make sure the money goes back to locals".
- Hubs form organically: he felt the closest to this in Canggu (Bali), a bit in Chiang Mai, and cites Tulum. He expects nomad hubs to become remote work hubs, and doesn't think artificial places will work.

## Happiness, meaningful work and picking projects

- He argues the lifestyle marketing around nomadism (infinity pools, beaches, "buy this course for $3,000") is boring in practice and is essentially materialism, as is Instagram experientialism.
- What he says actually makes people happy: the right people around you, meaningful work, health and sports, therapy, and flow from making things.
- Meaningful work doesn't have to be the main job. Volunteer work, even online (building a website for a library), can supply it.
- His own path: music was most meaningful but had weak market fit ("I wasn't good probably enough"). He then built sites to solve his own problems, which made them meaningful. He calls it partly luck that his money-making products are also meaningful.
- Nomad List is more meaningful to him; Remote OK is "more boring, but it's very necessary" as a job board for remote jobs.
- His advice: don't focus on finding perfectly meaningful work as your main job, since market fit is hard. Find meaning first from people.
