## Pieter's decision framework for a $45K acquisition offer on Go Fucking Do It

In June 2014, during his "12 startups in 12 months" challenge, Pieter received his first offer to buy one of his startups, Go Fucking Do It (gofuckingdoit.com), his second project of the series. The buyer opened with interest and, when asked for a price range, named $45,000.

- The final offer was $45,000, about EUR 33,000.
- Dutch tax would take 52%, leaving him about EUR 16,000.
- The site had only been about a month of work, and he found the experience of being asked "kinda excited" and "very flattering".
- He did not sell. He read the offer as a sign he was "on to something here that I can scale up".

## How he decided: price against the site's potential

Pieter judged the offer against what the site could become, not against the effort already spent.

- His reasoning: if he can scale the site 10x, it could generate double the offer in annual revenue alone.
- Because of that, any sale would need to be "a strong multiple" of $45K before he would consider it.
- The offer felt too low for the site's potential even though it was well above the month of work invested.
- Tax mattered to the real number: a headline of EUR 33,000 became about EUR 16,000 after Dutch tax at 52%.

## Passion and freshness as a reason to keep building

He also weighed how he felt about the project, not only the money.

- He was "having way too much fun with it still"; the concept and execution were "still so fresh" and did not bore him yet.
- Selling a project he still enjoys would trade a learning and growth opportunity for a modest payout.

## Why a small exit is a trap for a builder

Pieter compared a sale to simply working for money as a freelancer.

- He noted that he is "trying to build startups here, not be just an expensive web developer (at EUR 16k/m)".
- Taking EUR 16k after tax for a month's work would pay like a high-priced contractor, which is the opposite of building assets that compound.
- His point: small exits turn a founder back into a service provider, selling time at a premium instead of owning something that scales.

## The five goals he uses to evaluate any offer

The offer made him write down what 12 startups in 12 months is actually for. In order:

1. Have lots of fun and learn startup development and marketing.
2. Meet lots of interesting people in the startup world (which he already had).
3. Maintain his living standards with his projects without needing a job.
4. Attract investment for one project and work with a team to scale something to 1M in revenue.
5. Sell one project for an actual good amount of money, giving a few years of runway and financial independence to build new things while traveling.

An offer only makes sense if it advances these goals. A $45K sale funded about a year of living but ended a project that could serve goals 3 and 4.

## What he took away and did next

- Being asked to sell was validation that the project had real value and could be scaled.
- His response was to double down: his closing line was "let's go fucking grow it", pointing to a public challenge on his own site, "Grow my startup", with a 2015-01-01 deadline.
- He treated the offer as a signal to grow rather than as an exit, and kept the option of a sale later at a much better multiple.
