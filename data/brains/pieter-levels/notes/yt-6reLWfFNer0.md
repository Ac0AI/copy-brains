## Pieter's origin story and the 12 startups in 12 months project

- Pieter graduated in business in the Netherlands. He ran a YouTube channel for electronic music making about $2,000-$3,000 a month, then sold his stuff and traveled Asia with a laptop.
- The YouTube income fell from roughly $3,000 to $900, then $700, then $500 while he traveled. He returned to his parents' house with anxiety, depression and panic attacks.
- His fix was to "do 12 projects in 12 months", one month each, as a way to keep busy and build something.
- Early projects: a playlist app for music shared by email (no revenue), and animated GIF flip books via a Malaysian printer (margin only 2-3%, probably losing money after tax).
- The first that made money was a goal-commitment app. You set a goal and deadline (e.g. quit smoking by 1 January), add a prize and a credit card via Stripe, and a friend confirms by email. If the friend says no, the card is charged $50-$100 and the money goes to him. It took him from about $200 a month to about $700 a month.
- The press picked up the "12 startups in 12 months" project (The Next Web, Tech in Asia). Thousands of people emailed him and followed him on Twitter. He says he "cracked this little marketing thing accidentally".
- He did only about 7 of the 12. Nomad List took off and he chose to grow it rather than keep launching new projects. It took "hardcore effort" and constant feature work.

## The shotgun approach and what stuck

- Pieter's philosophy: "shotgun", shoot lots of projects and see which one sticks.
- **Nomad List** began as a public spreadsheet of cities (internet speed, temperature around 26°C, cost of living). He accidentally left it editable, so people added data and it went viral, reaching about 75 cities. He turned it into a website and launched on Hacker News, where it hit number one. Now about 1,250 cities and 250,000 crowdsourced data points, making $15,000-$25,000 a month in membership fees.
- **Remote OK** is a remote job site, since December the number-one remote job site in the world with almost a million monthly visits, making about $10,000 a month.
- **Hoodmaps** is a crowdsourced map tagging neighborhoods (hipster, rich, tourist).
- The recurring loop he describes: idea, build, launch, grow, monetize, automate, exit, repeat.
- Only one product in the early batch stuck, and he treats that as normal.

## Finding ideas and validating them

- Solve your own problems. "You are the expert at your own problems", which is a competitive advantage (a gardener knows gardeners' problems). Nomad List came from wanting to know which cities to go to; Hoodmaps from being lost in tourist centers.
- Because people are homogeneous, they get the same ideas. To be more original, do unusual things (skydiving, long solo jungle treks, fringe subcultures). Taboo areas have less competition.
- Start small: fix a small niche problem, make money, then grow the niche. Elon Musk started with PayPal, not a space company. Nomad List is now expanding into the whole travel market, which he estimates is 10-100x bigger.
- A $100 product needs only 10,000 customers to make $1 million.
- Keep a concepts list of every idea. The ones that keep coming back are worth building; some remote work ideas came months before he built them.
- Competitors: an existing app does not mean there's no market. If nothing exists, usually there is no market. Existing apps are often broken, ugly and low-usage, so you can make a better one. He is "a little bit arrogant and naive" and jumps straight in; it works about one in ten times. Think from first principles about the problem you want to solve.
- Do it yourself, no co-founder. Groupthink is dangerous; people hype each other's ideas. Working alone ships faster.
- Share your ideas. "Nobody's gonna steal your idea, it's all about execution." Sharing lets you talk to potential customers before you build.

## Learning to build and keeping the prototype short

- Don't do coding bootcamps (he calls them "a little bit of a scam"). Google "how to make a website". The core skill is learning how to learn and figure things out yourself.
- He started with spreadsheets and basic WordPress themes and learned just in time: when a problem appeared, he googled it. "The only other option of not learning it was my entire startup failing."
- He uses PHP and vanilla JavaScript and finds JS frameworks needlessly difficult. Even professional developers spend half their time googling and copy-pasting from Stack Overflow.
- No-code options for non-coders: Typeform (forms that accept Stripe payments) and a no-code site builder built by his friend AJ.
- Prototype in a maximum of one month. Months of polishing with no customers doesn't work; you need to validate by launching.
- He thinks coding is essential: "if you're not coding you're gonna be unemployed".

## Launching: Product Hunt, Hacker News, Reddit and niche communities

- Launching is the validation step. Do Product Hunt, Reddit and Hacker News on the same day to concentrate traffic and create a compounding effect, plus tweet and share on Facebook.
- **Product Hunt**: about 10,000 visits, roughly 10% convert or less. Make the listing look good, add an animated GIF, write a very simple slogan (ask friends). It runs on San Francisco time, so submit just after midnight SF time to rank high that day. Reply to comments, fix bugs immediately, be human, not marketing.
- **Hacker News**: its comments can "destroy your whole startup". Use a "Show HN", be frank, honest and personal. No voting rings or bots.
- **Reddit**: about 100x bigger than Product Hunt and Hacker News, and the mainstream launch platform. Post in niche subreddits (e.g. r/horses for a horse management app), friendly and asking for feedback. Front-page traffic can reach 400,000 to half a million visits. He hit the front page twice (including Hoodmaps).
- Make the site static HTML rather than PHP or JS and load test it, since servers die at page two or three of the front page.
- Niche forums and websites for your audience may convert highest because they're so relevant.
- For non-web or offline products, go where customers are. Patrick McKenzie (patio11) sold his appointment reminder SMS app by walking into barber shops and hair salons.
- After launch, traffic spikes then drops, which is normal. If everyone is gone within a week, it may not be validated.
- To judge whether to continue, he keeps a Google bookmark of his product name in quotation marks filtered to the past 24 hours or week to see press mentions. Give a product a few weeks, not months: "just do new stuff".
- Keep relaunching: launch features and major versions to the press every few months to stay in people's minds.

## Organic growth, feedback loops and building in public

- He hates "non-organic" growth: Instagram bots, Twitter follow/unfollow bots, bought email lists and ads. Paid traffic fades when you stop paying, and it hides whether the product is any good.
- Organic traffic is the "ultimate test" of product quality. If you don't get it, the product isn't good enough.
- Every site has a feedback box ("be nice"). He adds features weekly (e.g. moved a search box because someone said it looked ugly). Users become ambassadors through co-creation. Intercom works but is paid.
- Capture emails to re-engage users after launch spikes, e.g. daily job alerts and a newsletter for Remote OK. Use sparingly.
- Build in public. He live-streamed building Hoodmaps (about 100 viewers made him code faster) and cites Drew Wilson doing it. It brings attention and press, and few others do it.
- Press and Twitter: he warns to ignore haters and not engage.

## Monetization, pricing and business models

- Charge early: aim for the first dollar within about two months, ideally on launch day. Without revenue the idea isn't validated.
- Ignore the "this should be free" complainers, especially on Reddit. You compete with free alternatives by being premium.
- Validate with fake doors:
  - Buffer put up a landing page with plans and a pricing button that collected emails, validating demand before building.
  - Pieter built a fake Stripe payment box for a feature he hadn't built. It saved no card data and said it was a test, collecting emails of those who paid. Then he built the feature and those people paid. "Put buy buttons on everything."
- Charge limits from the start; it's harder to limit free features later. He regrets giving too much of Nomad List away free and says he could have charged $10 for all features.
- Side-product models: the main product can be free while something adjacent earns.
  - Nomad List data is free; about 7,000 people pay for membership (the social network for travelers).
  - Dribbble is free for designers, but employers pay $299 for 30 days to post jobs.
  - Sponsorships: Matt Mullenweg's Automattic (WordPress) sponsored Nomad List with a banner and was hiring, paying a few thousand dollars a month. It's hard to get by outbound emailing.
  - Patreon/supporter models: a Japan YouTuber makes $3,000 a month from 800 supporters; Overcast has a patron tier at around $12.
- Nomad List membership: he started charging because of spammers in the chat (people selling courses). He put $5 behind a Typeform, then raised it to $25, $50, then $100, and people kept paying. Spam nearly disappeared. Now about $1 a day or $99 a year, and you can browse read-only but need to pay to have a profile and interact.
- Prefer recurring revenue. With 25% yearly sales growth, a one-time $75 payment gives $75,000 in year one and about $183,000 by year five, versus almost $2 million a year with subscriptions.

## Automation, solo operations and exits

- Pieter gets bored of products after 1-2 years, so he automates. His server runs 180-700 robots (cron jobs and parallel processes) at once, e.g. fetching weather for Nomad List, collecting remote job posts, and processing refunds.
- He prefers scripts to hiring because humans are hard to fire and get sick. The one human role: one full-time person on standby to monitor servers and alerts, so you aren't woken at 4 a.m.
- Software scales: 40 visitors or 4,000 on Reddit with no change and no hiring. He tried a social media manager (Buffer posts) but sees little future in content work.
- Work comes in cycles of intense sprints, then little work. Products get dusty as trends change.
- Legal: one company holding everything as projects, because a Dutch BV/LLC would cost at least about $5,000 a year in bookkeeping.
- Exit: a business with $100,000 a year and 25% growth could sell for about $500,000, or around $1 million with higher growth. He'd want a 4-5x revenue multiple and has declined offers because he wasn't happy with the price. He notes the emotional attachment to a "baby" product.
- Mistakes he names: trusting TechCrunch-style advice (raise millions, hire a team, get an office) instead of his intuition, and engaging with haters. He advises trusting your own intuition and being nicer.
