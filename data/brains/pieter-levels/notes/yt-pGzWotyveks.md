## Shipping early and building for yourself

- Pieter's advice to his younger self: "ship my products earlier, don't perfect them." He says to just ship.
- His own first project was a huge analytics dashboard that took 1.5 years of work, and nobody paid for it.
- Marc, a co-panelist, describes the opposite lesson. Pieter's takeaway is the same: ship things people actually want before putting in a year of effort.
- His current approach is to make sure someone will actually use a product before pouring time into it, rather than building maximum functionality without asking whether anyone needs it.
- He frames this as the Lean Startup approach.

## Small steps beat big visions

- Pieter visualizes the future vividly (where he wants to be in ten years, how the house looks, how it feels). He says visions already exist in you and you "dig into yourself" to find them.
- He ties this to his nomad startups: he remembers being in the Philippines at night, picturing many people traveling and working, before building Nomad List-type products.
- He says you can pursue a big vision with small websites and small steps.
- He recommends a monthly float tank session (about 40 dollars in Amsterdam) as a way to get visions.

## Data, A/B testing and intuition

- Pieter says he uses very little data for his products. He sees many people A/B testing and tracking events, but he doesn't really trust it.
- He prefers intuition: have a feeling for your product and how you would use it. "Humans are not numbers."
- He argues that optimizing mostly improves a local maximum; creativity and art make the big jumps, and art "never listens to the people that watch it."
- He cites Apple as a company that doesn't rely on focus groups, and Ford's "faster horses" idea as the reason not to over-rely on customer asks.
- He warns that over-optimized landing pages can produce customers who "hate" you: they got pulled to a green button, paid, and don't know what your site even is.
- His preferred stance is a modest approach: happy customers and a business built to enjoy life, "it's not all about sales."
- He personally tracks his own life with the Gyroscope app (connected to RescueTime and health data) for feedback loops like step counts.
- Other panelists argue for mixing hard and soft data; Pieter's position is intuition-first.

## Press as a sustained acquisition channel

- Pieter says press is almost completely the reason he is invited to events like this one.
- It only works if the product backs it up: "if it sucks they're not going to care, but if it's a good product and the press is there, it's so important and it keeps giving traffic."
- Example: a TNW (The Next Web) article keeps sending traffic for years because people searching his product's name still find it, as a top-three search result.
- He calls press one of the biggest sustained sources of traffic.
- Keep launching new features and products to keep generating press.
- Retargeting didn't work for him: he spent around 2,000 dollars on Facebook retargeting and says it hardly worked, so he is distrustful of it.

## Spinning off small products as a gateway to bigger ones

- Pieter says engineered marketing works like a "gateway drug": small products or features attract a new audience, then you upsell to the bigger product.
- Apple example: the iPod brought people in, then iTunes worked well, then they might buy a MacBook or iMac.
- Big companies can use it too: if you have an expensive or hard-to-sell product, start with easier-to-buy products, establish a relationship with the customer, and later upsell.
- Spin-offs help you reach new markets beyond your brand's current audience (e.g., reaching business travelers beyond digital nomads).
- Note: an in-room panelist claims he coined the "gateway drug" framing; Pieter's point is the tactic itself.

## Finding eyeballs and channels for a new product

- His blunt first answer: build something really valuable and interesting.
- He lists places to launch: BetaList, Product Hunt, Twitter, Medium, Hacker News.
- He stresses the concrete question to ask is: where are your customers, and which channels, conferences, websites or agencies do they use?
- For a product aimed at laid-off or unemployed people, he suggests:
  - Go to where customers are and strike a deal with intermediaries (e.g., lawyers, HR departments, companies doing layoffs), possibly offering commission.
  - Study competitors using tools like SimilarWeb, Google, and their Facebook and LinkedIn pages.
  - Target on Facebook or LinkedIn, and consider job boards.
  - Use referrals, since one customer in a community usually brings more.
  - Consider a chat or coaching service format.
- He also suggests building a small tool that solves a customer's problem, connecting with them, getting first value, then onboarding them to the main product.
