## Why Pieter builds 12 startups in 12 months

- He spent about two years perfecting one product and launched it to four free-trial users who then left. He calls it "horrible" after "I worked so hard on this."
- Building something every month forces him to finish things and keep them minimal. He says he has always procrastinated on finishing, and the deadline fixes that.
- The aim is to shift products faster and find out quickly which ones work, "like throwing spaghetti on the wall and seeing what sticks."
- He is candid that the label is partly "a marketing ploy." They are MVPs built in a Lean Startup spirit, not full startups. He built the site, got market fit if possible, monetized fast, then moved to the next.
- He argues most people share the same ideas ("we all have the same ideas and it's just about execution now"). He keeps a years-old Trello board with lists like plans, work in progress, complete failure and success, and drags ideas right until they finish or fail.

## How he builds fast and keeps the quality high

- He does the front end and back end himself, so he says he cannot be great at anything in particular. Keeping things minimal makes them look clean and saves time, which gives a better design aesthetic.
- Ideas come mostly from his own problems. Nomad List came from not knowing which cities to work and travel from.
- He borrows layouts from existing sites. Nomad List took Product Hunt's list-based layout and applied it to cities, with modifications.
- Idea sourcing is roughly 50/50 between his backlog and actively hunting for the next one while building the current month's product.
- His advice against perfectionism: the customer only sees the finished product and never the mess behind it. "Just make it look as if it works and then that's enough." Everyone's back end is a mess, so don't be scared to build something because you think you can't.

## Revenue from the early projects

- Most of the projects earned revenue at that point. He estimates about three of four or five were generating revenue actively.
- One site (a swear-word-titled domain, "effingdoit.com") earns completely automatically with no work from him.
- Nomad List was his most recent and most successful, monetized through simple ads: two paid advertisers for remote jobs. It ranks cities for working and living remotely by cost of living, weather and similar factors.

## Using a "12 in 12" narrative to get press

- Press needs news and a spin. "They're not going to write about something that's common." Pieter says to build a narrative around your products or personality that genuinely fits you, because you can't fake it.
- He tried for ages to get coverage the year before, and it didn't work. Having a narrative made it much easier.
- The first contact was John Russell of The Next Web, who reached out because Pieter blogged about working on a startup from Chiang Mai, Thailand. After that he sent every new site to John, and the first covered was Go F*** and Do It.
- He considers press essential to getting users, even for non-tech products, because "everything is tech these days."
- The framing was partly luck: he wrote "12 projects in 12 months," then backspaced "projects" and typed "startups" as a joke. He admits it felt a little sleazy, but argues it is a delicate balance and that "startup" gives the dreamy feeling that something might become something.
- Each month's launch gives a new follow-up story, since the series builds a narrative over time.

## Handling criticism of "trivial" startups

- A Wired journalist asked whether nomadic work in developing countries exploits people, and why he builds trivial first-world products.
- Pieter's view on travel: it is beneficial in the way tourism can be, with money coming into lower-income countries, helping create middle classes (he cites Thailand's tourism in the 80s and 90s) as long as it is done respectfully.
- He concedes the journalist has a point that builders sit in a Western bubble, and it inspired him to think about solving less trivial problems. He sees that as a long-term project, since he was 28 and just starting.
- His practical argument: you need cash flow to solve big problems. A person in a nine-to-five job should build their own products for monthly cash flow, then focus on something big.

## Focusing on the nomad market and what's next

- His approach is to see which project sticks, and Nomad List was sticking. He saw it as a market he is part of: people selling homes and quitting rent to move to South America or Southeast Asia, which he sees as becoming a feasible lifestyle.
- He wants to remove friction in arriving somewhere new, such as SIM cards, transit cards and finding co-working, which can take days to sort out in a place like Bangkok.
- Planned projects: Giftbook, which prints animated GIFs as flipbooks shipped to your house (he searched and found nobody had done it, with only a competitor doing stereoscopic cards), and Nomad Kit, a bundle for arriving in a city (Austin, Chiang Mai, Bali) with co-working, hotel stay, SIM card, transit card and a guide.
- He expected more nomad-related projects among the 12.
