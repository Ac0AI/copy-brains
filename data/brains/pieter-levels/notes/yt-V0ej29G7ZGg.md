## Pieter's one-person, contractor-based business model and numbers

- Pieter runs several products as the only full-time person. His stated numbers in the interview:
  - Nomad List: past roughly 100K a month, close to a million dollar business. It showed a 73% profit margin on his public open page.
  - Remote OK (job board): about 1.6 million a year, his biggest earner, at about 94% pre-tax margin.
  - Rebase (immigration/relocation agency for remote workers): his newest business.
  - The ebook (Read Make): about 4K a month.
- His public revenue meter targets 3 million a year. The interviewers cited 2.7 million at the time.
- Team: a part-time customer support contractor who covers all projects, a Slack community moderator, and a devops friend on an SLA-style arrangement.
- The devops contractor gets alerted if a server goes down and handles security updates. Pieter says it "never goes down anymore."
- He argues moderation cannot be fully automated: "you need a real person there" because Slack communities have drama. He tried automating it first.
- He hosts on a VPS (DigitalOcean and Linode), not Amazon.
- He describes the sites as "heavily automated," roughly 99.9%, and says he barely needs to work on them.
- His reasoning for part-time contractors: keep margins very high, so revenue is almost profit, which is what makes a sale multiple worth it.

## Simple stack and fast feedback loop

- Pieter builds with PHP, jQuery, JavaScript and a database. He picked PHP because it was the language he already knew from making a blog.
- He gets criticized for the stack and doesn't care: "it doesn't matter what you use, but as long as you use something that's really fast."
- Speed of the iteration loop is the point. He can add a button and deploy in about 20 seconds. Developer friends on heavier stacks (Kubernetes etc.) sometimes need an hour or days per feature.
- He ties this to lean startup: customer feedback has to loop fast.
- Fast shipping makes customers happy because they see their own request live and feel they "influenced this product."
- He builds features from talking to customers about what they want.

## Build for yourself, spot a "discongruence," and surf waves

- He describes himself as "user zero." He builds what he needs.
- His idea trigger is a gap between what he sees and what society says. Most people assume something is wrong with them. He assumes society may be wrong and makes a small site to test it.
- Example: he tweeted about inflation about two years earlier, people dismissed it, so he built inflationchart.com to show higher real inflation numbers.
- Travel feeds product ideas indirectly. Different places and different shop products inspire him.
- The origin was not a master plan:
  - He started blogging about nomading in 2013 for his mom.
  - Posts about bootstrapping as a nomad in Asia took off on Hacker News.
  - Twitter followers grew organically from there.
  - His inspiration was Patrick McKenzie (patio11), an indie developer who shared revenue from small projects.
- He positioned against the "shady" online business scene he saw in Thailand and wanted reputable, mainstream remote work.
- On timing: he gave a talk in 2015 predicting 1 billion remote workers by 2030 and was ridiculed. COVID changed that.
- Pieter says he had no idea it was coming, and for years the chart "doesn't really go anywhere."
- His lesson: "You cannot control the market at all... Just surf waves." Pivoting is steering the surfboard.
- Movements start with freaks, then developers, then the mainstream, as with crypto and electronic music.

## Revenue as an asset, small starts, and why he doesn't sell

- He tweeted that an app doing 20,000 a month is "probably already a millionaire." It implies about a 1M asset at a 4-5x multiple.
- He thinks 20K a month is approachable with high automation, high margins, and part-time contractors instead of a big team.
- Advice for small ideas: one feature done really well solves one problem, brings the first customers and cash flow, then you add a second feature and scale toward a real product toolkit.
- Why you rarely see multi-million bootstrapped founders: they raise VC once past 1M and go for a billion-dollar outcome. He chose not to.
- Why he hasn't sold:
  - Bootstrapped businesses usually fetch 3-5x, "too low for me."
  - He said 10x is where it gets interesting.
  - Most acquisition processes bounced off.
  - The sites run themselves, so he can just wait.
  - Nomad List is his "baby" and he expects buyers to ruin it. Remote OK is less personal because it's "just a job board."
- He used to be obsessed with selling because of startup movies, until his own cash flow made it matter less.
- He publishes /open pages with live stats, P&L and an estimated valuation based on a multiple of profit.

## Pricing and automated high-ticket sales on Remote OK

- Job post prices range from about 100 to 1,000 dollars, with dynamic pricing. He changes job post pricing based on how many people post jobs on the site.
- He sells job post bundles via Stripe with no sales calls. Customers use a slider on remoteok.com/buy-bundle to build their own bundle and get a discount.
- Bundles are roughly 30% of revenue. Examples:
  - 25 jobs for 22,000 dollars.
  - Typical large purchases of about 40-50K.
  - The biggest ever was around 49-50K.
- Companies pay with company cards. The bundle idea came from companies asking if they could buy job posts in advance.
- The sales page is packed with text and emojis. He keeps adding content daily and says it "looks like a circus" but works.
- Hidden complexity: invoices for every little thing, edge cases, special features and dynamic pricing. This is why clones that copy the look don't take off.
- Self-serve customers (credit card on Stripe, no forms or NDAs) are, in his experience, more modern, pay more, and are easier to support. Customers who ask many email questions and want W-8 forms convert less and pay less.
- Sales team debate: the hosts argued sales teams create demand. Pieter conceded it might work but said he hasn't found good salespeople, fears managing them, and fears outbound spam damaging his reputation. He's optimizing for freedom and happiness rather than max growth.

## Radical transparency as a recurring marketing engine

- Pieter publishes revenue and stats openly (revenue meter in his Twitter bio, /open pages).
- He says this "recurring marketing machine" is a big reason his businesses and Twitter audience grew: "I cannot deny that."
- His motivation is radical honesty and countering founders who only share the good news. He wants to show the ups and downs of a small indie company.
- He admits it's now part of his identity and hard to stop.
- The hosts' critiques (Buffer-style transparency gets harder at scale, and privacy concerns) were their views, though Pieter agreed it's hard to stop.
- Content format thoughts: he is wary of the weekly-content treadmill and burnout. He is considering short talking-head videos, as Derek Sivers does.
- He closed his DMs because most are low-quality asks from people who don't invest time, and he explained his routine in a blog post instead.

## Product opportunities Pieter sees in slow nomad and remote worker markets

- Digital nomad is "a horrible word." His data says people travel slowly. The average stay is about seven months, and many have partners, kids and families.
- He says "slowmads" are the real market and are underserved. Moving weekly doesn't work for them.
- Opportunity ideas he gave:
  - Serviced apartment hotels for long stays, aimed at high-earning remote workers (100-200K). He spends about 2-3.5K a month on them and says they solve cleaning, breakage and quality variance versus Airbnb.
  - Furniture rental with a configurable "click and arrive, already set up" experience.
  - Homeschooling and education for remote families.
  - Pet-friendly travel and services, since tech workers have pets and often no kids.
- He prefers being a customer rather than owning real estate. He's a software guy.
- He sees remote work as still niche and growing as physical jobs get automated.

## Lifestyle, spending, and constraints

- Pieter spends about 4-5K a month and invests the rest after tax, mostly in ETFs (Vanguard, S&P 500, some Asia exposure) plus Bitcoin and Ethereum.
- He benchmarked and found his S&P 500 ETF beat his own stock picks over two years. He sold roughly 70-80% of his individual stocks.
- He avoids lifestyle inflation deliberately, citing hedonic adaptation research and "golden handcuffs" he saw in corporate friends.
- Where he spends: food (organic, grass-fed) and experiences, and business class only on rare long-haul flights.
- He argues constraints help creativity and focus. He owns little: a backpack, a small suitcase, clothes, iPhone, MacBook Pro, backup phones for 2FA, and two Stadia controllers.
- He now mostly stays in one place (Portugal) with nomad friends nearby. His routine is coffee, coding a little with a friend, gym, sauna and swimming. He says he doesn't get bored as long as he ships a bit on his sites.
- He admires Derek Sivers (CD Baby founder, who sold it for 30 million dollars) as a model of a simple, philosophical life.
