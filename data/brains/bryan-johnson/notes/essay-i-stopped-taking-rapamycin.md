## Why Bryan stopped rapamycin

On September 28th, Bryan decided to stop rapamycin, ending almost 5 years of experimentation with the molecule for its longevity potential. He and his team concluded that "the benefits of lifelong dosing of Rapamycin do not justify the hefty side-effects."

- Despite the immense potential seen in pre-clinical trials, the trade-off did not hold up in his own data.
- He stresses that longevity research on experimental compounds keeps evolving. He and his team watch both the research and his biomarkers constantly.

## Protocols Bryan tested

Over almost 5 years he tried several dosing schedules to optimize rejuvenation and limit side effects:

- Weekly dosing at 5 mg, 6 mg and 10 mg
- Biweekly dosing at 13 mg
- Alternating weekly dosing at 6 mg and 13 mg

Changing the dose or schedule did not remove the side effects.

## Side effects that drove the decision

- Intermittent skin and soft tissue infections
- Lipid abnormalities
- Glucose elevations
- Increased resting heart rate

No other underlying cause was identified. The team suspected rapamycin, and because dosage adjustments had no effect, they discontinued it entirely rather than keep tuning the dose.

## Research concerns Bryan cites on metabolism

- Preclinical and clinical research indicates prolonged rapamycin use can disrupt lipid metabolism and lipid profiles.
- It can induce insulin and glucose intolerance.
- It has been linked to pancreatic beta-cell toxicity.

These findings match the lipid and glucose changes he saw in his own biomarkers.

## Cancer and immune surveillance concern

- Bryan acknowledges anecdotal evidence that rapamycin slows tumor growth.
- He points to research showing rapamycin inhibits natural killer cells, which raises concerns about anti-cancer immune surveillance and cancer risk over the longer run.

## Epigenetic clock evidence

- On October 25th, a new pre-print indicated rapamycin was one of a handful of supposed longevity interventions that caused an increase or acceleration of aging in humans across 16 epigenetic aging clocks.
- Bryan notes this evaluation is the first of its kind. Most longevity interventions have been tested against only one or two aging clocks, which leads to "invisible biases and potential intended 'cherry picking' of favorable clocks."
- Testing across many clocks is a stronger check on whether an intervention truly slows aging.
