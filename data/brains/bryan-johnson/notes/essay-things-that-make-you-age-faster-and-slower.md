## Study design: 16 epigenetic clocks used to rank interventions

Bryan summarizes a December 2024 study that scored interventions against 16 epigenetic age clocks.

- **Slowed biological aging:** anti-TNF therapy, metformin, ketamine, AC11 supplement, TruLacta, gastric bypass, smoking cessation, kidney transplant, HBOT (large average effect, not statistically significant), vegan diet, green Mediterranean diet, Mediterranean diet, low-fat and low-carb diets.
- **Accelerated biological aging:** rapamycin, senolytics, buckwheat extract, follistatin gene therapy, kidney dialysis.
- His main takeaway: "continuous measurement and optimization for actual biomarkers and performance indicators automatically consolidates the interventions that best slow down epigenetic biological age." Many findings matched what his protocol already did.

## Rapamycin: why he stopped

- In September 2024 he stopped rapamycin because his lab results showed metabolic side effects: higher cholesterol, blood sugar and resting heart rate.
- The study showed rapamycin raised biological age on 2 clocks and did nothing on the rest.
- Rapamycin is consistently the strongest drug at extending mouse lifespan in gold-standard ITP studies. Bryan says this "puts to question the validity of rodent data in predicting longevity interventions in humans."

## Metformin and anti-TNF: what he keeps and what it signals

- He continues taking metformin.
- In the study, 5 of 16 clocks showed a significant reduction or slowdown in biological age, and no clock showed the opposite.
- Counterweights he considers outweighed:
  - Mouse ITP studies failed to show lifespan extension.
  - Claims that it blunts muscle growth. He says he is top 0.1% of his age group for muscle volume.
- Supporting evidence he cites: aging clocks in non-human primates (particularly in the brain) and lower cancer incidence in retrospective population studies.
- Anti-TNF therapy lowered biological age across most clocks. He reads this as showing the importance of "inflammaging" in driving aging.

## Diet: green Mediterranean, grain-free, and the buckwheat result

- A green Mediterranean diet did best, slowing aging on 10 of 16 clocks. He says it is the closest to his Blueprint diet.
- He stays grain-free. Buckwheat extract produced a slight but significant acceleration in aging clocks, which he takes as validation.
- His explanation for buckwheat:
  - It is rich in B vitamins, which drive the methylation cycle.
  - That raises genome-wide methylation, as seen in a large elderly cohort study, potentially accelerating age-related epigenetic change.
  - Long-term B9 (folate) supplementation raised cancer incidence and all-cause mortality, with the effect correlated to blood folate concentration.
- Organ-system clocks and DNAm proxies reproduced known diet effects, for example vegan and Mediterranean diets reducing inflammation and low-carb reducing HbA1c.

## Follistatin gene therapy: his personal data

- The study found follistatin gene therapy increased biological age, driven mainly by the PACE clock.
- Bryan received the therapy from Minicircle, and it raised his circulating follistatin.
- After the therapy, his PACE score dropped from 0.70 to 0.64. He calls this associative, not proof of causation, but reassuring that it had no undesired effect on his biological age.
- He is investigating whether the studied cohort was healthy or disease-specific, since that changes how the result should be read.
- Minicircle's preprint found no PACE change. It did find a mild reduction in Extrinsic Epigenetic Age, strongly correlated with T and B immune cells.

## Which aging clocks he trusts and how he tracks himself

- He continuously tracks his epigenetic age with DunedinPACE (PACE), which he calls superior in reliability and sensitivity, especially for shifts in healthy people.
- He says he is, as far as he knows, "the most epigenetic-measured person in the world."
- In the study's reliability comparison, DunedinPACE always agreed with the majority of other clocks or was the only one to detect a change.
- Why healthy-cohort sensitivity matters:
  - Disease treatment gives larger, easier-to-detect improvements.
  - True longevity interventions should lower biological age in healthy people too, since they address aging itself.
- Three clocks detected effects in healthy cohorts, including DunedinPACE. GrimAge (mortality-predicting) worked in both healthy and disease contexts.
- He describes the newest "Gen X" (explainable) clocks as linking aging to specific organs (organ-system clocks) and biomarkers (DNAm proxies), much as conventional studies identify a drug's mechanism.

## Limits of aging clocks

- Clocks are a surrogate endpoint for lifespan. Waiting on mortality data alone "would delay the field by several decades."
- Clocks were not designed to predict therapy effects, so they can isolate negative effects while missing beneficial ones.
- He argues single-clock approaches and animal data are unreliable for judging human longevity outcomes, which is why multiple clocks and real biomarkers are needed.
