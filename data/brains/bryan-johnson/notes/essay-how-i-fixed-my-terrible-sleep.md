## Sleep as the top priority

- Bryan says high-quality sleep changed his life. It took years to build the habits behind eight months of 100% perfect sleep, which he believes is the best in the world.
- His advice: "If you only do one thing for your health: sleep."
- He tells people to see themselves as "a professional sleeper": make sleep the #1 priority, plan the day around it, treat it as the most important calendar appointment, and get buy-in from others by explaining the priority.
- He dismisses the idea that sleep is for the weak; those people are "drunk from sleep deprivation."

## Why sleep deprivation matters

- Being awake for 18 or 24 hours is equivalent to a blood alcohol content of 0.05% or 0.1%. In the U.S., 0.08% is legally intoxicated.
- Averaging six hours of sleep or less makes you 4.2x more likely to develop the common cold.
- After one night with 4 hours of sleep, natural killer cell activity fell 70% in 18 of 23 participants. One night of normal sleep restored it to baseline. NK cells kill cancer cells, among other functions.
- Preclinical studies link lack of sleep to inhibited immune surveillance and increased tumor growth in animal models.
- Sleep deprivation also reduces cognition, slows reflexes and worsens decision-making. It is linked to heart disease, diabetes, obesity, anxiety, depression and weakened immunity.

## Wind-down routine (30-60 minutes)

Set a specific time each evening to start winding down. Options he lists, with the evidence he cites:

- Relaxing activities such as reading or a warm bath. An online randomized trial of 991 subjects found reading a book in bed improved self-reported sleep quality in a majority of participants.
- Mindfulness or deep breathing. A wide meta-analysis found mindfulness meditation outperformed controls and was equivalent to medications for sleep disturbances.
- Soothing music. In a study of 94 students, calming classical music beat a negative control and an audiobook for sleep quality and depression. A meta-analysis of 9 studies with 489 older adults showed improvements in sleep latency, duration and efficiency.

## Light management

**Morning**
- Get outside within 15-30 minutes of waking to set the circadian rhythm and boost mood.
- Use a light therapy device if natural sunlight isn't available.

**Evening**
- Dim lights one to two hours before bed and use warm lighting; red light is even better.
- Install blue light filters on devices (e.g. f.lux) and consider blue light-blocking glasses.
- Limit screens for at least an hour before bed and black out the bedroom.

## Bedtime consistency and sleep environment

- Pick a bedtime that allows 7-9 hours in bed, and keep it consistent, including weekends. Avoid scheduling late nights.
- Set an alarm for winding down.
- Keep the bedroom between 60-67°F (15-19°C). Use breathable bedding and light sleepwear, fans or cooling devices, ventilation, and a temperature-controlled mattress if possible. A warm bath before bed can help.
- Keep the room quiet, dark, cool, clean and clutter-free, with a comfortable mattress and pillows. Use a noise machine or earplugs if needed.

## Meal timing

- After years of testing, Bryan landed on an 11 am final meal as best for sleep. He goes to bed at 8:30 pm, by which time primary digestion is done and his resting heart rate is about 47-49 bpm.
- If he eats later, his resting heart rate is 55-58 bpm because he is still digesting, and sleep quality drops by about 30%.
- For others: eat the last meal at least two hours before bed and avoid large meals near bedtime. Experiment with 4 and 6 hours and see whether sleep improves.
- Late eating creates large metabolic demands, causes blood glucose fluctuations, reduces melatonin production, and disrupts the natural drop in core body temperature.
- He found foods like pasta and bread reduced his sleep quality, and was surprised how much food affected sleep both positively and negatively.

## Stimulants

- He suggests avoiding caffeine, alcohol and other stimulants at least 10 hours before sleep. He consumes neither, because each disrupts his mood and sleep.
- Caffeine has a 6-hour half-life, so coffee 6 hours before bed is like half a cup right before bed.
- Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, which stops the brain sensing declining energy that it needs to switch to sleep mode. It can also interfere with sleep phases later in the night.
- Alcohol may speed falling asleep, but it reduces REM sleep, increases fragmentation (frequent awakenings) and can worsen existing medical conditions.
- Evening alternative: herbal teas or other relaxing drinks.

## Track and iterate

- Track sleep with a journal or sleep tracker, use the data to adjust the routine, and keep monitoring and tweaking.
- He frames it as a journey: take it one step at a time, starting with reshaping your relationship with sleep.
