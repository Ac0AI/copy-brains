## Evening exercise timing and the Whoop sleep study

- The Whoop study covered 4.3 million nights of sleep from about 15,000 Whoop members. Bryan says it is large-scale data the field has been waiting on.
- Findings he cites:
  - Strenuous evening workouts can delay sleep onset, shorten sleep duration and reduce sleep quality.
  - They elevate nocturnal heart rate and lower heart rate variability.
  - Workouts that end at least 4 hours before bedtime showed no negative impact on sleep.
- Bryan cites the highest-intensity exercise within 2 hours of bed:
  - It raised resting heart rate by 3.9 bpm (6.8%).
  - It cut HRV by 32.6%.
  - Recovery was reduced for any exercise 2 to 6 hours before bed.
- The effect is dose dependent. Higher intensity and longer duration (Whoop's "strain") hurt more the closer the workout is to bedtime. Light activity such as yoga or a slow walk more than 2 hours before bed did not meaningfully affect sleep.
- Bryan says the study matches his own data, and he has told others to follow these protocols for years. Late, high-intensity exercise raised his resting heart rate and lowered his HRV and recovery.
- Practical rule: do strenuous exercise at least 4 hours before bed, ideally 6 or more. Inside that window, keep it to light activity.
- On trade-offs, he first challenges whether there is truly no other time to work out. If a person can only train hard in the evening, some days they should do it anyway. Exercise has a huge longevity payoff, for example high VO2 max with a 300 to 400% lower mortality risk.
- He also argues sleep deserves the number one spot in life, "above family, above work, above everything. It is the fuel source of existence."
- He argues people should look for the rule before the exception: "we have more in common than we do indifferent," so find the power law that applies to everyone, then peel off exceptions.

## Resting heart rate before bed as the single most important biomarker

- Bryan calls himself the most measured person in history, and of everything he measures, resting heart rate before bed is the one he values most.
- His reasoning is that sleep is the most effective performance-enhancing drug available, and pre-bed resting heart rate is the biggest determinant of sleep quality.
- It is the lynchpin because almost everything that harms sleep raises it:
  - heavy meals
  - alcohol
  - stress and conflict
  - stimulants
  - doom scrolling and screens
  - evening exercise
- This sets off a vicious cycle: worse sleep, less willpower, less exercise, worse eating. The reverse is a virtuous cycle: a low resting heart rate leads to great sleep, which leads to morning exercise, better eating and more willpower. Exercise in turn lowers resting heart rate.
- Resting heart rate also tracks cardiovascular fitness, stress and overall health. It rises with BMI, falls with fitness, and relates to total sleep.
- Cardiovascular risk data cited:
  - About 10 beats per minute above 60 corresponds to roughly 10% higher cardiovascular disease risk.
  - A resting rate of 90 is roughly 30% higher risk.
- For longevity, the target is under 65, ideally the low 50s. Women average about 2 bpm higher than men. The rise with age is mostly due to lost fitness, not aging itself.
- Bryan's own pre-bed rate:
  - He puts his head on the pillow, opens the app, and does about 60 seconds of breathing.
  - Typically he sees 44 to 55 bpm.
  - 44 means a perfect day and "a guaranteed perfect night sleep."
  - 55 to 57 means a rough night. Causes include international travel, a messed-up food schedule, or evening exercise such as a dance party.
  - After hundreds of repetitions he says he can predict REM, deep sleep and HRV from this number alone.
- His overnight resting heart rate was about 46 and is now about 50, since he recently increased calories and exercises heavily. It has stayed between 46 and 50 for the past couple of years.
- His 12-month sleep stats:
  - 8 h 35 min average sleep
  - 94% sleep efficiency
  - 3 h 54 min of restorative sleep (REM plus deep)
  - 69% recovery
  - HRV of 48
- He uses resting heart rate as an accountability partner: before grabbing a chip or spoonful, he reminds himself it will push the number up.

## How to measure resting heart rate without a wearable

- Lie down, take a few deep breaths, and give yourself about 60 seconds to calm down.
- Put fingers on the side of the neck, count beats for 6 seconds, and multiply by 10.
- Example challenge: if tonight's reading is 60 bpm, aim to drop it 10% (to 54 bpm) within a week.
- Bryan expects results quickly, "dare I say immediately," especially for someone with poor fitness, late meals, high stress or poor sleep. Dramatic changes can show within a couple of months.

## Protocol to lower resting heart rate: food

- Food is the first lever. Bryan eats his final meal around noon, about 8.5 hours before bed. He ran hundreds of experiments eating 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 hours before bed and found noon optimizes his resting heart rate.
- For beginners: if bedtime is 10 p.m., start with the last meal at 8 p.m., then try 7 p.m. and 6 p.m., and keep moving it earlier.
- Eat lighter. Heavy foods like pizza or lasagna make the body work harder to digest than steamed vegetables. Learn which foods raise or lower your own rate.
- Expect some hunger before bed. He says he enjoys that feeling and that people can learn to get comfortable with it.
- Alcohol raises resting heart rate. He cites Oura data of about a 7% increase, and says even one drink raises his by 4 to 5 beats.
- On the social argument for alcohol, he says alcohol, croissants and sugar are not necessary to connect with people, and you can bond over healthy food. He argues from biochemistry: nothing about a croissant's molecular makeup produces oxytocin or good health.

## Protocol to lower resting heart rate: wind-down routine

- Screens off 60 minutes before bed, as a minimum. Bryan calls phone use an addiction and expects withdrawal symptoms.
- Replace screens with reading, a light walk, conversation, breath work, meditation, a hobby, journaling, a bath or sauna.
- Avoid blue light. Use red and amber light.
  - The app f.lux removes blue from screens.
  - Phones have a red mode (iOS or Android).
  - He uses a triple-tap shortcut that cuts the screen to near-zero light.
- Keep a consistent bedtime and a consistent routine. Consistency itself lowers resting heart rate.
- Ten minutes before bed, he holds a book in his hand and reads.

## The "Bryan personas" technique for evening willpower and anxiety

- Bryan fixed his nighttime overeating by naming his different selves and writing down each one's emotional state, problems and typical arguments.
- Evening Bryan arrives after the kids are bathed and in bed, exhausted and stressed, and wants to soothe himself with food (brownies, graham crackers, whatever is in the kitchen). He shows up when other Bryan has spent all the willpower.
- Rule: Evening Bryan cannot eat from 5 p.m. to 10 p.m., the highest-risk window. "You can't negotiate with evening Bryan."
- He applies the same method to other selves:
  - Ambitious Bryan shows up at 7:30 p.m. with new ideas, and Sleep Bryan says "we love you... we're going to write down your idea. Then tomorrow..." and logs it for the next day.
  - Anxious Bryan replays embarrassing moments (such as something said on a podcast), and Bryan acknowledges the concern and addresses it before bed.
  - Father/Dad Bryan is another persona.
- His reasoning: unresolved thoughts about work, family, relationships and grievances loop when the head hits the pillow, leaving only an hour or two of sleep before waking and looping again. Resolving them during the wind-down prevents that.
- Overeating then exercising late (burning it off) is a double whammy. A full stomach plus a pounding heart means no sleep, and he then woke up feeling awful and low on willpower. He broke this cycle only by prioritizing sleep above all else.

## Other points on calorie restriction and appearance

- He loves caloric restriction. At roughly 900 calories a day (about 25% restriction) his resting heart rate was very low and he felt high energy.
- He became too thin, lost facial fat, and found it hard to get back. He says people associate a gaunt face with age and a fuller face with youth, so "unless you've got fat in your face, it's very hard to have a conversation about health."
- He wishes he could sustain restriction while keeping facial fat, so his markers would be believed.
- Bryan says he and Kate share resting heart rate screenshots daily and that the number tells a thousand-word story about her emotional state and how to prioritize the day. He wishes he could monitor friends' overnight rates to ask how they are doing.
