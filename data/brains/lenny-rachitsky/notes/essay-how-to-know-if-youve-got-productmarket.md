## Pre-product: what counts as early PMF evidence

Lenny collects the most actionable PMF definitions he's found, split into pre-product and post-product signals, "sorted from the most concrete to least concrete." He notes most people (himself included) have only a vague grasp of what PMF is, and few chase it systematically.

Pre-product signals he highlights:
- **Visible excitement.** Steve Blank's test: "do someone's pupils dilate when they use your stuff?" Elad Gil's version: strong feedback from even a small group, such as "literal love letters" from customers at Color.
- **Willingness to pay now.** Ask people directly, and try to get them to pay before the product exists (even send an invoice for early access). "Nothing will be a better signal of interest and PMF if you can get people to put down money before you have a product." Try it even for consumer apps you won't charge for, since it shows how much value you create.

## Post-product: retention and the "very disappointed" survey

**Retention curve.** Lenny's favorite quantitative signals are cohort-based:
- Plot % active users over time per cohort. If the curve flattens, you've probably found PMF for some market or audience (Brian Balfour).
- A steep first-month drop is fine. A flattening means a group of customers finds value. Casey Winters adds that PMF is real when you can acquire users for less than the money that retention curve earns.
- Jeff Chang: once several cohorts level off at a vertical-specific number, you have PMF. Benchmark against comparable products that grew significantly, since the right retention level varies by product type.

**Surveys.**
- Sean Ellis / Rahul Vohra: ask "How would you feel if you could no longer use the product?" If over 40% answer "very disappointed," you have PMF.
- Enterprise version (Doug Leone / Andy Rachleff): pull the product at the end of the free trial. "If the customer doesn't scream, you don't have PMF."

## Organic growth and pull from the market

Signals that the market is pulling the product:
- **Exponential organic growth** from word of mouth in consumer apps (Andy Rachleff).
- **Explosive usage** (Michael Seibel): customers using it "in an explosive and destructive way."
- **Spontaneous referral** (Sam Altman): do any users love it enough to tell others unprompted?
- **Share of organic traffic** (Merci Victoria Grace): ideally more than 50% of new accounts come from direct or organic traffic.
- **Major brands adopting you organically** and paying (Elad Gil's examples: Apple at PagerDuty, Facebook at Zeplin, many brands at Airtable).
- **Customers clamoring** (Marc Andreessen, Steve Blank): customers buy as fast as you can make it, usage grows as fast as you can add servers, people grab the product out of your hands.
- **Emmett Shear's boulder test:** "Pushing a boulder: don't have product/market fit. Chasing a boulder: have product/market fit."

## Efficiency metrics: burn multiple, sales yield, CAC vs LTV

Lenny includes several economics-based definitions:
- **Burn multiple** (David Sacks) = Net Burn / Net New ARR. Lower is more efficient. Generating $1M ARR by burning $2M is more impressive than burning $5M, because the market is pulling the product rather than the startup pushing it.
- **Sales yield** (Andy Rachleff, enterprise): contribution margin of a sales team divided by its total cost (a team may cost around $500k-$600k). Above 1.0 signals PMF.
- **CAC < LTV:** Elizabeth Yin: you can "repeatably acquire customers for a lower cost than what they are worth to you." Nikhyl Singhal: value of each user exceeds the cost of bringing them in. Casey Winters: retention that creates enough dollars or virality to drive sustainable acquisition.

## Strong-signal tells: it works even when it's broken

Lenny lists the "you can screw it up and still win" signals:
- Elad Gil: high retention with a broken product, such as Twitter's fail-whale days when no one left.
- Brett Berson: in consumer, growing quickly "despite poor execution and management."
- Don Valentine: customers want it so badly "you can screw everything up and still succeed."

## Guides and bonus tips for finding PMF

Lenny's recommended guides:
1. Dan Olsen, the Lean Startup playbook for achieving PMF (Lenny corrected the attribution after Olsen pointed out he wrote it, not Eric Ries).
2. Brian Balfour, "The Never-Ending Road To Product Market Fit."
3. Marc Andreessen, "The Only Thing That Matters."
4. David Rusenko, "How To Find Product Market Fit."
5. Rahul Vohra, "How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product/Market Fit."

Bonus tips he shares:
- Naval: the top predictor of finding PMF is a high shipping cadence.
- Justin Kan: you probably lack PMF if you aren't growing, have high churn, the product is hard to sell, or customers don't seem to care much.

## PMF is not a single moment

Lenny closes with Ben Horowitz: "Product/market fit isn't a one-time, discrete point in time that announces itself with trumpet fanfares." Competitors arrive and markets segment and evolve, so it is hard to know you're headed the right way before accelerating. He opens with Marc Andreessen's framing: "The only thing that matters is getting to product-market fit."
