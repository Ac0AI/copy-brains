## Quality and consistency are the whole growth model

- Lenny says the things that don't matter are design, title, strategy, growth plan and vision. What matters is quality and consistency.
- "There's no trick to making something like this grow." Growth comes from publishing something valuable, over and over, that people want to share with friends and colleagues.
- If a newsletter isn't growing, he says it is most likely a quality problem or a consistency problem.
- Quote: "people don't want more emails. They want better emails."
- His growth path to 500,000 subscribers (Sep 2023):
  - First 100: a Medium post about his time at Airbnb, which grew his Twitter audience, then a tweet announcing the newsletter.
  - Next 1,000: guest posts on First Round Review and Andrew Chen's blog.
  - Next 10,000: nine months of useful weekly writing, plus tweeting summaries and word of mouth.
  - Next 100,000: two more years of the same.
  - Next 200,000: more of the same, week after week.
  - Last 200,000: the same plus Substack's recommendations feature.

## Pick a job to be done and do it better than anyone

- Lenny buckets every great newsletter by the job it does for readers: help me parent, cook, understand tech or the world, inspire me, entertain me.
- There is room for many winners in each category, so you needn't be the best in the world, but you should strive to be.
- His own job: help readers get better at work, specifically building and growing their product and career. Do it well and consistently and they keep reading.
- Two questions to start:
  1. Who exactly is your audience? Think of one specific person and what they'd find extremely useful.
  2. What concrete job are you doing for them (entertainment, making money, inspiration, understanding the world)?

## Choosing a topic: follow your energy

- Writing consistently is a grind, and you may spend years thinking and writing about one topic. If you aren't genuinely curious you "will create a job for yourself that you will hate."
- Three filters, in order:
  1. Follow your energy: which topics give you energy and which sap it. Do more of the former.
  2. Base it on real-life experience. The best newsletters come from people with deep experience in the space. Look for the center of the Venn diagram of your skills and background.
  3. Pay attention to what people value. He puts this third on purpose, because many people start here and it's a trap. Look at what people ask you advice about and where you find pull.
- Find a niche (a "personal monopoly") but don't go too narrow, or you'll get bored.
- He deliberately stayed broad (building products, growth, career, startups) instead of just product management, because he doesn't care about PM that much. It made the newsletter harder to explain but kept him excited.
- Rule of thumb: first get 1,000 people to think your newsletter is the best in the world. At 1,000 paid subscribers he could make a real living from it.

## Spend more time on each piece, and nothing else

- Lenny says 99% of content isn't very good, so there is always room for better. You have to rise above what exists, and that takes work.
- He spends a median of 10 hours per post. Some take hundreds of hours, like his B2B startups series. He sees a strong correlation between time spent and how well a post does.
- That's why it's hard to do well alongside a full-time job.
- Any newsletter time that isn't writing, or making the writing better, is wasted. Many writers optimize their site, build a brand or craft a big strategy, and their growth slows.
- He values Substack because he can focus exclusively on writing while they handle everything else.
- Substack's recommendations feature is, in his view, one of the most impactful growth features in history and changed his trajectory. Over 9,000 other newsletters recommend his, not from anything he did actively. It's the quality-spreads-itself point again.
- He says that because of recommendations there has never been a better time to start a newsletter.

## Writing craft: cut 30-50% and add something new

- With no writing background, the biggest lesson he learned was "cut, cut, and cut some more." He credits On Writing Well, Nobody Wants to Read Your Sh*t and Several Short Sentences About Writing.
- Tactics:
  - Cut every word that isn't absolutely necessary.
  - Make the intro 50% shorter and get to the meat fast. He quotes Wes Kao: "Start your story right before you get eaten by the bear."
  - In nearly every guest post he edits, deleting the first paragraph of the first draft makes it stronger.
- Contribute something new. Most writing rehashes what's been said. Ask what new ideas, insights and concepts you add.
- Most of his top posts came from tens of hours of primary research that surfaced insights not shared before. He's doing a lot of work on the reader's behalf.
- That requires real experience. He keeps doing advisory work on the side so he doesn't lose touch with the real work.
- If you're considering going all-in on content, he advises doing the work for many years first (e.g. 10), which also builds career optionality. He endorses Elena Verna's point that a leadership title should be a step toward career optionality (for her, solopreneurship with her brain as the product), not the end goal.

## Cadence: pick one you can sustain for years

- A deadline is an essential forcing function. He picked a cadence, shared it publicly and tried hard to stick to it.
- Weekly is a good starting point. People often publish too fast and burn out. He can sustainably write one great post a week.
- Daily, monthly, or even one incredible post a year can all work depending on goals. The key is a cadence you can keep up for years.

## Just start, and the real downsides

- Quote he cites from Joan Didion: "I don't know what I think until I write it down." He writes to mold a rough idea, not to share a finished thought.
- Start with something you want to get out of your head, write it, share it, and don't overthink it. Post on Substack or similar so you start collecting email addresses in case it takes off.
- His first public piece was a write-up of what he learned at Airbnb, which people found valuable, so he wrote more.
- Downsides he is candid about: no PTO, benefits or 401(k) match, and nobody to write if he's sick. For parental leave he worked doubly hard for a couple of months to build a content backlog. In theory he must write an awesome newsletter every week for the rest of his life.
- Despite that, he calls it the most fulfilling work of his life and highly recommends it. His mission is to help people get better at work and increase net happiness in the world.
