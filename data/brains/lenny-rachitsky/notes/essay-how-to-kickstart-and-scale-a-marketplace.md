## Constraining the marketplace: the "go small to get big" rule

Lenny's research across 17 marketplaces found that, with one exception, every marketplace constrained its initial scope to reach critical mass faster. He frames this as counter-intuitive: "the best way to get big is by first going small."

- There are two ways to constrain: by **geography** and by **category**.
- If supply and demand must meet physically, the constraint is always geographic (a single market).
- Otherwise the constraint is category-based (for example, handmade goods).
- The sole exception was Thumbtack, which launched with all categories and all geographies.

## Marketplace definition and why it is a great business

Lenny defines a marketplace as a business that:
1. Connects demand (people who want a thing) with supply (people who have it).
2. Leads to a financial transaction.
3. Generally owns no supply, provides no products or services directly, and eventually handles the money.

- **Marketplaces:** Airbnb, Uber, Lyft, Etsy, eBay, DoorDash, Rover, Thumbtack, TaskRabbit, Craigslist.
- **Not marketplaces:** Delta (owns inventory), YouTube (no transaction), Slack (no supply).

Why they are great businesses:
- **Network effects:** more users make the product more useful or cheaper.
- **Barrier to entry:** hard to replicate once the network effect is strong.
- **Efficiency:** no inventory means cheaper operation.
- **Scalability:** no inventory means easier scaling.
- **Flexibility:** easier to pivot (for example, Uber Black to UberX).

## The chicken-and-egg problem

- A working marketplace has supply serving demand. At launch you have neither.
- You must convince one side to commit before the other. Without restaurants, a hungry customer has no reason to open your app; without customers, restaurants have little reason to onboard.
- Solving this is "one of the biggest barriers to launching a marketplace business."
- Lenny's phase 1 steps: constrain the marketplace, pick which side to focus on, drive initial supply, drive initial demand.

## Geographic constraint: examples from location-based marketplaces

- **Rover:** started and stayed "hyper-focused" on Seattle: dog-friendly, early adopters, techy working professionals who travel (David Rosenthal's account). It helped them figure out the product quickly.
- **Airbnb:** focused first on New York. When it expanded internationally in 2011, it built critical mass in just a few markets where supply and demand could be unlocked quickly.
- **Uber:** the Launcher team had a playbook for new markets that started with supply. Goal: over 30 drivers, with an ETA under 15 minutes.
- **OpenTable:** density mattered because "dining is local." Rule of thumb: 50-100 concentrated restaurants in a city gave diners a meaningful consideration set.
- **Instacart:** picked markets with likely demand, such as Chicago in winter (high household income, fewer households with cars, bad weather).
- **Zillow mortgages:** started in a single market, because rates were entered manually by brokers responding to emails.

## Breather's lesson: duplicate exactly what works

Julien Smith's account, which Lenny includes as a marketplace lesson:
- With short transactions (a 2-hour room booking), proximity was critical.
- Dense areas like the Flatiron District saw new units booked immediately, which was clear product/market fit.
- Their approach: "This cafe, at this location, at this time, is working. Duplicate this exactly." Don't launch a new city yet, don't change anything; look for ways to extend or double the transaction.
- The lesson: if you're in a decent-sized market, "just double down on what's working." Airbnb did the same, recreating New York "again and again."

## Category constraint: examples from non-location marketplaces

- **TaskRabbit:** always a broad platform, but deliberately built liquidity in its most popular categories: handyman tasks (including IKEA assembly), house cleaning, and moving help.
- **Eventbrite:** found its core use case in tech mixers and conferences, based on where early traction appeared. Invites carrying the Eventbrite name made it "a thing."
- **Etsy:** began with only three categories: vintage items, craft supplies, and handmade items.

## The Thumbtack exception, and caveats on the research

**Thumbtack** (Sander Daniels) deliberately did the opposite: all categories and all geographies from the start.
- It didn't get funding for many years in part because investors thought it was "boiling the ocean."
- Breadth in category raised usage frequency from once every couple of years (hiring a house painter) to 8-12 times a year (average American household services).
- Breadth in geography let them scale as fast as possible, giving the revenue, traffic, and experimentation velocity to bootstrap the product.
- Per Sander: "In our case we wouldn't have survived had we done it any other way."

Lenny's caveats:
- Many of these companies succeeded mainly through great product/market fit; growth levers accelerated and controlled growth, but they may have succeeded anyway.
- Past behavior is not predictive. Focus on ways of thinking, not specific tactics, since many past growth levers no longer work.
- The research is based on interviews about events many years ago, so memory may be faulty.
- He warns against leaning too heavily on a single company's experience (including his own Airbnb background).

## First-principles reminder from DoorDash

The opening quote Lenny leads with, from Micah Moreau (DoorDash VP of Growth):
- Everyone looks for the growth hack or channel that unlocks something big.
- Every time DoorDash looked for why it wasn't growing, "it always came back to the basics: selection, delivery quality, pricing."
- "Always come back to first principles. Whenever we made a mistake, we forgot this."
