## Benchmarks: what counts as GOOD vs GREAT retention

Lenny defines user retention as the percentage of users who signed up and are still active (using the product, buying, posting) six months later. He and Casey Winters surveyed 20 growth practitioners (Andrew Chen, Elena Verna, Fareed Mosavat, Brian Balfour, Dan Hockenmaier and others) and combined their answers with public company data. The result is a set of benchmarks by business type.

User retention at 6 months:
- Consumer social (Snapchat, Twitter, Instagram; free, ad-supported; denominator is registered users): ~25% GOOD, ~45% GREAT
- Consumer transactional (Airbnb, Lyft, TurboTax; one-off purchases; denominator is users with at least one transaction): ~30% GOOD, ~50% GREAT
- Consumer SaaS (Netflix, Spotify, Hulu; denominator is users who started a paid subscription): ~40% GOOD, ~70% GREAT
- SMB/mid-market SaaS (Asana, Slack, Atlassian; companies of roughly 100 to 1,000 employees; denominator is companies on a paid plan): ~60% GOOD, ~80% GREAT
- Enterprise SaaS (Salesforce, Workday, ADP; over 1,000 employees): ~70% GOOD, ~90% GREAT

In the detailed section, the enterprise GOOD figure is labeled ~75%. The expert answers range from 70% to 85% for GOOD and 90% to 95% for GREAT.

## Benchmarks: what counts as GOOD vs GREAT net revenue retention

Lenny defines net revenue retention (NRR) as the current month's MRR from a cohort of customers divided by that same cohort's MRR one year ago. In other words, how much revenue one cohort of customers drives over time. The categories differ from the user retention ones because the type of customer changes revenue retention:
- Network effects in bottom-up SaaS often push retention up.
- Very small businesses (VSB) churn involuntarily because many go out of business.
- Land-and-expand models raise revenue per user, which can offset high user churn.

NRR at 12 months:
- Consumer SaaS: ~55% GOOD, ~80% GREAT
- Bottom-up SaaS (Slack, Figma, Zoom; self-serve prosumer product sold to individual contributors): ~100% GOOD, ~120% GREAT
- Land and expand VSB SaaS (Gusto; companies under roughly 100 employees): ~80% GOOD, ~100% GREAT
- Land and expand SMB/mid-market SaaS (Atlassian, Box, Zendesk): ~90% GOOD, ~110% GREAT
- Enterprise SaaS: ~110% GOOD, ~130% GREAT

## Public comps for user and customer retention

Lenny pairs the expert ranges with public figures from big companies:
- Consumer social: Facebook 60%-70% at 6 months. Instagram 50%-60% at 6 months. Snapchat 33% at 3 months and 30% at 24 months. Twitter 31% at 3 months and 22% at 24 months.
- Consumer transactional: TurboTax 77% at 12 months. Lyft 22% at 12 months.
- Consumer SaaS: Amazon Prime 93% at 12 months. Dropbox ~80% at 12 months. Spotify 72% at 6 months. Netflix 66% at 12 months. Hulu 53% at 12 months.
- SMB/mid-market SaaS: Atlassian 98% at 12 months. Slack 90%-95% at 12 months. QuickBooks 79% at 12 months.
- Enterprise SaaS: Workday 95% at 12 months. Salesforce 90% at 12 months. ADP 90%+ at 12 months.

Note that some comps use a 12-month window while the benchmark is 6 months.

## Public comps for net revenue retention

- Bottom-up SaaS: Twilio 140%-170%. Zoom 140%. Slack 135%-155%. PagerDuty 139%. Datadog 130%. New Relic 115%. Dropbox ~100%.
- Land and expand SMB/mid-market: Atlassian 100%-148%. Box 130%. Zendesk 123%. SendGrid 116%.
- Enterprise: Alteryx 135%. Fastly 130%. Okta 124%. Anaplan 124%. Workday 100%+. ServiceNow 97%.

Lenny's point is that good NRR is above 100% for bottom-up and enterprise products, because expansion revenue outgrows churn.

## Where expert opinions spread (and how to read the ranges)

Lenny shows each expert's numbers instead of only an average, and the ranges are wide:
- Consumer social GOOD runs from 25% (Jeff Chang, Casey Winters, Brian Rothenberg) to 50% (Andrew Chen). GREAT runs from 40% to 75%.
- Consumer transactional GOOD runs from 15% (Casey Winters) to 30%. GREAT runs from 35% to 70%.
- Consumer SaaS GOOD runs from 40% to 70% (Elena Verna). GREAT runs from 60% to 80%.
- Bottom-up SaaS NRR GOOD runs from 100% to 120%. GREAT runs from 110% to 140%.
- Enterprise NRR has only two expert answers: Jeff Chang (110% / 120%) and Naomi Ionita (120% / 140%).

The published benchmarks sit near the middle of these ranges. Treat them as guides, not hard cutoffs.

## When low retention is OK

Lenny says the benchmarks look high because the bar for a massively successful business is high. Retention "doesn't live in a vacuum", and he lists three cases where lower retention is acceptable:

1. **You're just starting out.** Use the benchmarks to decide between retention and acquisition work. But "startups rarely increase retention significantly."
2. **You have low CAC and marginal costs.** Growth balances CAC, retention and unit economics. If you acquire users cheaply (SEO, word of mouth, virality), you can afford to lose more of them. He points to Dan Hockenmaier's thread on why low retention is OK for businesses like Shopify and Twitter.
3. **You're not building a venture-scale business.** These benchmarks come from people who built iconic, massively scalable companies. That level is not required for product-market fit or a sustainable business. A flat retention curve that supports a scalable acquisition strategy is enough to keep a business alive, though the upside is limited.

He quotes Fareed Mosavat: "Ultimately, what matters is that your retention supports sustained growth."

## Why retention matters most

Lenny calls retention the most important metric to get right when building or investing in a business, and one of the least understood. Most people rely on anecdotes, dated blog posts and misguided benchmarks. He quotes Casey Winters: great retention is "the scalable way to grow a product." It is the best indicator of product-market fit, the most important factor in lifetime value, and it drives the best acquisition strategies ("growth's equivalent of the triple word score"). In his summary he says no other metric is as telling of whether a business will thrive or die.
