## Deciding if product management is the right role

- Lenny calls PM "often a thankless, nerve-racking, and all-consuming" role, but says that when it works, "you feel like you were born to do it." He started as an engineer, moved into product seven years before this 2019 post (one of Airbnb's first few PMs), and says he couldn't imagine another role now.
- Become a PM if you are fulfilled by:
  - solving problems for users and for your team
  - driving business growth
  - working closely with many kinds of people
  - developing a strategy
  - getting things done
  - leading through influence, not authority
  - communicating often and broadly
  - making decisions
  - creating great experiences
  - being organized, detail oriented and prepared
- Do not become a PM if you are primarily fulfilled by:
  - appreciation
  - having your way
  - being left alone
  - always being right
  - designing or building things yourself
  - everyone liking you
  - flow states
  - avoiding meetings, email or people
- You don't need to match every item on either list, but the gist should feel right.

## Four common paths into product management

Lenny has helped people move into product from Operations, Data Science and Finance. He says every PM working today made this transition in some form.

1. **Internal transition at a large company.** Usually the easiest and fastest. It needs three things: an internal transfer process, a chance to demonstrate the PM skills, and an internal PM champion (generally your new manager). If two of the three exist, find a way to make the third happen. Otherwise pursue another path.
2. **Junior PM role at a large company.** Probably the most common route, but limited to companies with APM or internship programs. It is increasingly common for MBA graduates, though he says an MBA is not necessary. Without an MBA, he suggests programs like Product School and General Assembly, communities like PMHQ, and practicing the core skills in your current job. The key is clearly showing you are smart, driven and have raw strengths in a handful of the core skills.
3. **Joining a startup with a burning need.** Needs connections with founders, a lot of hustle, and delivering when given the chance. Look for jobs on HN and AngelList, find ways to meet founders, and build the core skills. A strong growth mindset matters because you learn the job on the fly. You can start as a founding PM or transition into it later; Airbnb's original head of product got in this way.
4. **Starting your own company.** The most work-intensive path and rarely planned, but it is how Lenny got into product. CEOs often become PMs after an acquisition, and founders can take the PM role as the company grows. He wouldn't recommend prioritizing this route.

## The seven core PM skills, in order of importance

Lenny says the role varies by company and team, but these seven are the most crucial to build early. Don't try to be incredible at all of them: "double down on your strengths and fill in the gaps."

1. Strategy: take any problem and develop a strategy to resolve it
2. Execution: getting things done
3. Communication
4. Leadership through influence
5. Decision-making informed by data
6. Building great products and having taste
7. Always being prepared

His bonus skills to build over time:
- **Vision:** set your team's north star, articulate it, and get buy-in.
- **Business sense:** understand what drives the business and build the right things in the right order.
- **An obsession with impact:** connect everything you do to its effect on the business and customers.
- **A growth mindset:** see yourself and others as capable of improving.

## Strategy and execution

**Strategy.**
- A PM's job is to marshal the team's resources to drive business value. The team is handed problems (grow adoption, reduce churn, increase conversion) and the PM guides the team to make the problem smaller.
- A strategy paired with a clear vision is the route from problem to solution.
- He endorses Richard Rumelt's definition: "A good strategy is a set of actions that is credible, coherent, and focused on overcoming the biggest hurdle(s) in achieving a particular objective."
- Ways to build it:
  - Ask the best PMs you know to walk you through the vision and strategy of a recent initiative.
  - Take a problem at your company (or a friend's) and break it into solvable chunks with a framework such as an opportunity-solution tree.
  - Learn the difference between vision, mission and strategy.
  - Practice Amazon's working-backwards approach.
  - Read *Good Strategy, Bad Strategy* by Rumelt.

**Execution.**
- A PM who is good at nothing else but execution is valuable; a team with a PM who can't execute is better off without that PM.
- Tactically this means a roadmap everyone is aligned behind, setting and hitting deadlines, and "ruthlessly unblocking blockers."
- His advice for new PMs is to start practicing immediately, because you only learn it by doing.
  - Ask a friendly PM to take the project-management duties on one of their projects. Never call a product manager a project manager.
  - Study people who are good at shipping: how they run meetings, handle issues as they arise, and keep the team aligned.
  - Learn OKRs and goal setting (*Measure What Matters* by John Doerr).
  - Regularly ask, "how am I going to move my product forward today?"

## Communication and leadership through influence

**Communication.**
- "Engineers code, designers produce designs, and product managers … communicate." Everything a PM does happens through writing, speaking and meetings. Quoting Boz: "communication is the job." You can never be too good at it, and it is very hard to over-communicate.
- Emails: look at it at least once before sending; there is always something to cut or clarify.
- Docs:
  - Get feedback from at least one person before sharing widely.
  - Keep formatting clean and consistent.
  - Close out comments before sharing with execs.
  - Make it easy to scan.
- Meetings:
  - Put the primary goal in the invite, ideally with an agenda.
  - Invite as few people as possible.
  - Point it out if a meeting isn't productive.
  - Leave with clear action items, and follow up by email with action items and owners.
- Presentations:
  - Check you need a presentation rather than an email.
  - Make clear whether you want a decision, feedback, or to share information.
  - Get feedback first and keep it short: "no one ever wished that presentation went longer."
- Storytelling is a meta-skill that improves all of the above. He recommends the situation-complication-resolution framework for laying out a pitch.

**Leadership through influence.**
- PMs generally have all the responsibility without actual authority. To succeed you must build trust, make decisions while giving everyone a voice, and keep morale up regardless of circumstances.
- The best PMs become the de-facto team leaders, "not because of any actual authority, but because they help everyone on the team do the best work of their lives."
- Ways to build it:
  - Watch how PMs get things done without managing anyone.
  - Lead a small project, ideally with a PM mentor.
  - Build team culture with fun rituals, outings and a lasting team identity.

## Decisions, product taste and being prepared

**Decisions informed by data.**
- Teams look to the PM to reach decisions; expect dozens of them daily.
- Two aids: a clear set of principles the team aligned on beforehand, and hard data, both quantitative and qualitative. "The less opinions you have to rely on, and the more facts you have at your disposal, the easier your life gets."
- Ways to build it:
  - Study how Airbnb, Uber, Netflix and Pinterest decide through experimentation, and launch an experiment or two where you work.
  - Watch how fast successful leaders decide, what they ask first, and how they explain their reasoning.
  - Always have your own point of view, but be ready to change your mind on new information.
  - Learn Type-1 versus Type-2 decisions.

**Building great products and taste.**
- This means building an instinct for what makes a product great, balancing art and science, and working well with other disciplines.
- He endorses Merci Victoria Grace's line: "Product intuition is a skill: it is the observation of human behavior, trained by data, and applied to software."
- Ways to build it:
  - Build and ship a product yourself or with friends, solving a small problem for someone (or yourself). "There's absolutely no better way to learn than to do it yourself."
  - Notice what makes you like or dislike a product and keep notes.
  - Learn design thinking.

**Always being prepared.**
- He calls this one of the most underrated and valuable PM traits, the "I got this" aura: everyone should feel that if you take something on, it will get done and done exceptionally well.
- It comes from being detail oriented, being more prepared than anyone else, and keeping a higher bar than those around you. He started off very bad at it and saw a lot of career growth once he prioritized it.
- Tactics:
  - Never come to a meeting without spending a few minutes preparing.
  - Keep a high bar for everything, including the communication practices above.
  - Build "spidey sense": look around corners, ask what could go wrong, and plan contingencies.
  - Learn from very well-organized people by asking what they concretely do to stay on top of things.

## How to move forward

- Lenny boils the PM job into four words: "Figure out what's next."
- His advice: start developing and demonstrating the seven skills, then push forward on one or two of the four paths. Read, process and put learnings into action, since creativity is part of being a PM.
- Be as prepared as possible for when an opportunity arises. The transition may take a year, two or three. If you're not getting anywhere, try another route.
- Meet PMs, listen to their feedback and act on it. "If you truly believe this is the right role for you, find a way."
