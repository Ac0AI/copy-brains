## Senior PM: three differentiators (strategy, autonomy, nuance)

Lenny shares Jackie Bavaro's framing, "the best framing I've come across" for leveling up to Senior PM. Senior PMs aren't much better at the regular PM's craft. The biggest differences are behind the scenes: teammates who only see specs and product choices may not understand why someone got promoted. The three differentiators are strategy, autonomy and nuance.

## PM career ladder and how long each step takes

Lenny says the PM career is "unnecessarily shrouded in mystery". The path has three milestones after starting as an IC:
1. Become a "senior" IC PM.
2. Become a manager of PMs (Group PM or Product Lead).
3. Become a manager of PM managers (Director).

His estimate of time per step:
- Entry-level PM to Senior PM: median about 3 years. This is the longest step.
- Senior PM to manager of PMs: 1-2 years.
- Manager of PMs to Director: 2-3 years.

His conclusion: accelerating the move to Senior PM has the most impact on the overall PM career.

## Strategy: the senior PM develops and evangelizes it

Senior PMs are responsible for a strategy that leads to meaningful customer and business success. They paint an inspiring future, find the best path to it, make sure people understand and agree, and convince executives to spin up the teams they need.

- Newer PMs prioritize by how much incremental value work adds to the product.
- Senior PMs prioritize by how much work moves the product toward the long-term vision.
- Example: the top-requested feature may delight current customers, but features that expand the customer base can be more strategic.

How to start:
- You don't need a promotion to create a strategy. Propose what the team should work on next.
- Make space for it. Block off half a day (Jackie jokes: pretend you're attending a Zoom wedding).
- If the engineering team needs you too much to find time, first level up core PM skills so day-to-day work goes faster.
- The strategy doesn't have to be perfect. It will change as you learn, but having one improves your moves now.

## Three parts of a strategy: vision, framework, roadmap

A strategy has (1) a vision, (2) a strategic framework and (3) a roadmap. Start with whichever appeals most.

- **Vision:** Storyboard future customers using the future product and show how much better their lives are than the status quo. Model it on an infomercial, spending time on how painful things are today. Include a few strategic insights or bets so it feels believable. Example: "Ouch! I stepped on a lego brick... Imagine a future where cleaning up toys is a game and your kids race to be the fastest!"
- **Strategic framework:** Write down the target market, their pain points, and the bets or differentiators you believe will win. Write alternative approaches and why yours is better. Example: enter the positive-parenting market with a fun game that solves a top pain point (putting away toys, picky eating, staying in bed), then expand into other pain points. A game rather than a tool or book expands the market beyond parents to anyone buying the child a gift.
- **Roadmap:** Write a rough plan for the next few years, grouped into strategic themes. Then imagine someone asking "why?" about each part; the answers become the start of your framework. Example: this quarter launch the cleaning game, next quarter the picky-food game, next half two more games, next year license new games to expand faster.

## Autonomy and the template for sharing work with your manager

Senior PMs can run a team independently: keep it running, work with stakeholders, resolve conflicts and ship successful products without their manager guiding them. They ask for lots of input, and they know when to question a direction they were handed instead of treating it as fixed.

- Autonomy is also about earning the trust to be allowed to work independently, through proactive communication and a track record of successful launches.
- The irony: high autonomy can make trust harder to earn, because your manager doesn't see your good decisions and hard challenges. You must share them intentionally.
- Sharing can backfire: you sound self-promotional, your manager thinks you're stuck, or they're frustrated you're not asking for advice.

The template that avoids these problems:
1. "Hi manager, let me tell you about a challenge I ran into ___"
2. "Here's how I'm thinking of handling it (or did handle it) ___"
3. "Any thoughts?"

It shows the challenge and how you handled it while opening the door to feedback. If the solution was good, the manager says "Great work!". If not, proactively asking shows you can be trusted to seek input when needed. Use it in your next one-on-one.

## Nuance: structured reasoning when "it depends"

The more senior you get, the more decisions have "it depends" as the right answer. Senior PMs:
- Recognize these decisions and reason through them in a structured way, without falling back on rigid frameworks.
- Understand goals and constraints of departments across the company and don't assume their own team's goals outrank others'.
- Decide quickly, laying out their thought process in minutes thanks to product expertise and rich mental models.
- Rapidly pick the most important problems in an ambiguous area and drive them to tangible impact.
- Have constructive discussions with leaders, explaining tradeoffs and defending a recommendation without becoming defensive.

## Practicing nuance: example and exercise

Teammates and stakeholders are the best guides. Take their concerns seriously and look for hidden complexity: when would they be right, and what information might they have?

Example: launching a feature, the head of customer support complains it can't be controlled by her team's operational scripts. Instead of brushing her off with "I'll update the scripts after launch", keep asking until you understand why. You might learn that without scripts, reps take longer on tickets, which lowers customer satisfaction, which correlates with cancellations. You might then delay the launch until the scripts are updated.

Exercise: when you make a decision, write "It depends. If X, then A is the best choice. If Y, then B is the best choice. I think this situation is X, so A." Share the longer answer only when it's useful, so you don't annoy teammates.

## Three things to start today

1. Block time on your calendar for strategy.
2. Use the autonomy template in your next meeting with your manager.
3. Look for the "it depends" answer to practice on.
