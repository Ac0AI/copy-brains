## Constrain the marketplace first (geography or category)

Lenny's step one for cracking the chicken-and-egg problem is to build the smallest viable marketplace so a flywheel can form.
- Constrain by geography if the service needs physical interaction (pick up food, ride in a car, stay in a home). Constrain by category if it doesn't.
- Of the 17 marketplaces he interviewed, about 70% started with a geographic constraint and the rest with a category constraint.
- Geography examples: Airbnb started in New York. Rover started in Seattle (Amazon is a big employer, very dog friendly). Instacart picked markets with high expected demand, such as Chicago in winter. OpenTable found it needed 50 to 100 restaurants in a concentrated area for a market to work, then moved city by city.
- Category examples: Etsy started with handcrafted goods, Eventbrite with tech events and mixers, TaskRabbit with handyman tasks, housecleaning and moving help.
- TaskRabbit later felt it was still too broad and would have focused further (it eventually narrowed to IKEA furniture assembly).
- Thumbtack is the exception. It went wide on both category and geography (nationwide, all services) and brute-forced it, taking four or five years to crack. Lenny thinks it would likely have done better starting with a geographic constraint.

## Pick one side to focus on, usually supply

Step two is deciding whether supply or demand is the initial constraint. Every company he studied picked one.
- About 80% focused on supply first, so supply is the likely default.
- Lyft knew supply growth was the unlock from the start. DoorDash studied Uber-style models, saw they start with restaurants, and saw that supply drives demand.
- Thumbtack compared Amazon, eBay and Craigslist and noticed that all three had all the supply and none cared much about brand or product. So it focused on getting all the supply and came back to product later.
- Only three companies found demand was the constraint. Rover had no supply problem, and its value prop was compelling: "$50 to have a dog over for one night." Zillow bootstrapped its supply from existing home data, so it never had to convince anyone to sign up.
- Patreon realized it wasn't really a marketplace. Creators loved it, but demand-side users didn't want to hunt for creators to pay. It removed the demand side from the roadmap and became a SaaS tool for creators, which was a huge win.
- Lenny's takeaway: you may not have a demand problem to solve, and you may not actually be a marketplace.

## Levers for kickstarting supply

Step three is driving supply. He found 12 common levers, and the top ones were not what most people guess.
- Direct sales was by far the biggest. About 60% of companies (10 of 17) used it as their primary early lever. Airbnb was heavily direct-sales driven early on, and OpenTable staff walked restaurant to restaurant pitching and setting up the service themselves.
- Piggybacking on an existing network was next, almost always Craigslist. Uber went to Craigslist for drivers. Airbnb allegedly solicited people renting homes on Craigslist and got them to switch (before Lenny's time, unconfirmed).
- Referrals were third. Lyft got double digits of supply from referrals, Uber a third of trips, and Lenny worked on referrals at Airbnb.
- Word of mouth was fourth. OpenTable restaurants talk to each other. Eventbrite got 36% of supply from word of mouth. On Patreon, creators follow creators.
- Subsidizing supply: Lyft and Uber paid drivers a salary at first.
- Single player mode: build a tool that is useful to one side before the other side exists. OpenTable built an excellent reservation system for restaurants that worked with no demand. The pitch was about 90% reservation system and 10% "we'll drive you demand," then slowly flipped toward demand.
- Performance marketing and SEO were low on the supply side early on (only Lyft/Breather-type cases and Eventbrite for SEO).
- Companies used only about 2.5 supply levers on average (median 2). His advice is not to try all twelve. Pick the few that work and double down.

## Levers for kickstarting demand

The demand-side ranking looks different from supply.
- Word of mouth was by far the top driver, which Lenny reads as proof of how much product-market fit matters. At Airbnb, a 2014 measurement showed about 50% of growth came from word of mouth (70% on the host side). Lyft's pink mustache drove word of mouth.
- Supply driving demand: your own suppliers bring buyers. DoorDash restaurants promote "download DoorDash to order," Etsy sellers and Eventbrite organizers share their pages. This worked for 7 companies and is a strong lever because "they do all the work for you."
- SEO is big on the demand side (7 of 17). Thumbtack's founder sat next to an SEO expert at a bar, who then joined the board, and SEO became 80 to 90% of growth. GrubHub got 30% of new users from SEO as its top lever.
- Performance marketing: Breather's founder Julien ran Twitter ads as himself ("we're launching in New York, message me"), got thousands of DMs, and hand-onboarded people through a seven-step funnel. Twitter called him in because the ad converted so well, at about a penny per click.
- PR was the fifth most common lever. TaskRabbit and Zillow relied on it as their number one channel.
- Loops: supply drives demand drives supply. GrubHub built menu pages that convert traffic into bookings, then tells restaurants "we're sending you traffic, sign up," which brings more supply and more demand.

## Know whether you are supply or demand constrained at scale

- Constrained means which side, if you added more of it, would unlock growth. Adding an already-sufficient side does nothing.
- About 40% of companies were always supply constrained (DoorDash, Uber, Lyft). About 20% were always demand constrained (OpenTable, and TaskRabbit, which had a waitlist of would-be taskers and started charging them a fee). The rest changed by market or category, so they built a model or heuristic.
- GrubHub sorted markets by the percentage of restaurants online and orders per restaurant.
- Thumbtack found NPS rose significantly once a search returned at least three results, so "at least three results per search" became the goal.
- Airbnb first used occupancy (e.g. 70% occupancy means supply constrained), then looked at where conversion drops (in some markets above 50% occupancy), then built a sophisticated revenue model.
- Uber's surge pricing started when a Boston GM emailed drivers promising double pay for 3 a.m. Friday rides. It worked, other cities copied it, and it became a product feature.

## What works at scale, and keeping quality up

Fewer levers scale than work early, since early tactics don't scale. A combined supply and demand view looks like this:
- Performance marketing became number one (roughly 50% of growth at one company, and Zillow added it later with big results).
- Geographic expansion was second (a top-two-or-three lever for Instacart).
- Conversion/funnel optimization was third, though less of a huge lever than he expected.
- SEO often arrived late (OpenTable) but worked once adopted. Referrals were the sixth most common, 10 to 15% of growth at Airbnb.
- You can hold levers in your back pocket and add them later. Quote from Caviar's Gokul: there's no single silver bullet, "a lot of light bullets" that add up.
- Quality tactics: set a standard and remove or warn people below it (Rover removes hosts under a star rating); onboard early supply (Airbnb used a 12-point checklist on photos, pricing and descriptions, and DoorDash and Caviar still walk restaurants through onboarding); use search ranking to demote low quality and promote high quality.
- Find early quality signals: TaskRabbit gave a mini Myers-Briggs test and correlated personality with success. Rover found the question "where does the dog sleep at night?" predicts great sitters, since "in my bed" is a good sign.
- DoorDash noted that keeping french fries and buffalo wings hot and crispy is the white whale of delivery.

## Regrets, funding, and early-stage advice

Asked what they'd do differently, the interviewed founders mostly said:
- Focus more. Breather tried to be a big consumer brand and a lot of things, but was really on-demand meeting rooms.
- Be more aggressive. GrubHub said it should have spent more.
- Think longer term. OpenTable only showed restaurants on its own network, and Yelp won demand because it listed everything.
- Use data sooner. Etsy had most of its team on a part of the product driving about 700 bookings a day while few worked on the part driving about 100,000.
- Add the adjacent product earlier. Rover waited on dog walking, and Wag got there first, even though it served the same customers and had better frequency economics than boarding.
- Advice from the Q&A:
  - Don't hire a "growth person" early, since everyone is growth. Hire people who build (engineers, designers, sometimes data scientists) rather than strategists.
  - Wait a long time before adding a new business line. Airbnb's founders resisted pressure for "Airbnb for dogs" and boats, and hired a Pixar storyboard artist to draw the guest and host journeys, which showed years of travel work left.
  - Marketplaces need product-market fit on both sides, which is "crazy." If you have it on only one side, cutting off the other side (Patreon) is probably the best bet.
  - Investors look at fill rate, frequency of use and dollar amount per use. High frequency, even at a low dollar amount, is often a good opportunity.
  - Airbnb's early growth was flat for about three years, driven by a combination of things rather than one spark: hosts in New York, meetups, host photography, PR (the cereal box story), cheap travel and word of mouth. After a trashed-apartment incident, liability insurance went from a planned $10,000 to $100,000 (after Marc Andreessen said to "add a zero") and later $1 million.
  - Trust is a huge part of marketplaces that this talk doesn't cover.
  - A moat comes from being the marketplace people already use, and from aggregating supply first.
