## Growth for a newsletter and podcast comes from consistency and quality, not hacks

- Lenny led supply growth at Airbnb, then the team that optimized the booking experience, so he spent years on conversion and growth. He says very few of those skills transferred to growing a newsletter or podcast.
- His view: "nothing helps it grow other than just consistency and quality of content."
- He tried referrals, paid ads and guest posts. Some helped early on, but "if you just look at the trend line of growth, nothing makes a dent other than just keeping at it and keeping the quality bar high."
- Referral programs ("send this to all your friends, get a hat, get stickers") don't work for his kind of newsletter. He thinks they suit a different personality of newsletter, such as Morning Brew.
- There is no real moat. Others are copying his welcome emails, advice-column format and intros. He doesn't think about defensibility much. If you keep providing valuable stuff, people keep wanting it.

## Subscriber growth timeline for Lenny's Newsletter

- Goal at launch: He called it "Project Avoid Getting a Real Job". The first target was $100K a year, later raised to $300K, and he says it has gone far beyond that.
- First ~100 subscribers: A popular Medium post brought Medium followers. These turned into Twitter followers, and he tweeted nuggets from the post.
- Next ~1,000 subscribers: Guest posts on newsletters and blogs with the same audience, namely the First Round Review and Andrew Chen's blog. They worked because he had interesting things to say and they wanted to share it.
- Next ~10,000: Writing every week for nine months. The growth chart was linear over that period.
- Paid plan: He launched it about 8-9 months after starting, at roughly 10,000 subscribers. Covid hit, he was making no money, and Airbnb stock looked doubtful. Another year or two of weekly writing kept growth going past $300K.
- Latest inflection: Substack's recommendations feature. About 1,000 newsletters now recommend his, and new signups see 10 newsletters he picked himself. He says he is likely in the onboarding flow as the top business newsletter, and the result was a "hockey stick of growth".
- Those recommenders get no affiliate commission. They recommend organically.
- He says "there's never been an easier time to grow large newsletter" if you write awesome stuff and people recommend you.

## How Lenny picks topics and decides what to write

- He started the newsletter as an advice column ("Dear Abby" style). Readers sent questions and he answered them. This rooted the content in real problems people have, not in his pontificating.
- He frames content as a job to be done: you need to nail one job better than anyone else. Newsletter jobs he lists: entertain me, help me make money, help me be better in life, help me be better at work. His is helping people build and grow product, one niche within the "better at work" job.
- He admits this framing came in hindsight. Getting there was "90% stumbling".
- His rule after leaving Airbnb was to notice what gave him energy and do more of it. Writing gave him energy. Startup work and advising calls drained it, so he cut them.
- A friend told him that doing something people value and keep reading is rare, and not to take it for granted. That pushed him to double down.
- His first post was what seven years at Airbnb taught him about building a company. He wrote it to remember what he learned.
- He says writing is thinking: "I don't know what I think until I've written it down." He doesn't start with the answer. He dumps thoughts into a doc, gathers quotes and talks to people, and the conclusion forms during writing.

## Research process: primary research and mini case studies

- The model came from his first big series. After leaving Airbnb, people asked how to build a marketplace. He didn't know if Airbnb's approach was the way or worked "in spite of ourselves". So he talked to the biggest marketplace companies to find patterns. This became a five-part series on kickstarting and scaling a marketplace business, and it did very well.
- His takeaway: he does primary research on behalf of readers at very low cost to them. He is spending tens of hours answering a question for them.
- Steps for a post like the growth inflections piece:
  1. Pose the question (for example, what causes an inflection in growth?).
  2. List the companies he'd most love stories from.
  3. Email people he knows at those companies with the question and ask what they can share.
  4. Collect the responses and see what conclusions emerge.
- A flywheel: the more of this he does, the more connections he builds and the more companies he's close with. He tries not to bug people too much, and often they are excited to contribute.
- He is "doing a lot of work for people that would cost a lot of money". Paid readers feel like insiders.

## Quality bar: time, ruthless editing, and knowing when to stop

- Direct correlation: the time he puts into a piece correlates with its success. Being full-time on this gives him far more time than people with jobs.
- Time per piece: the newsletter takes 10 hours minimum per post, some 20, some 100. A podcast episode takes about 3-4 hours of prep and recording, with a producer who edits.
- Most of his writing is editing. He starts with content and bullet points, shapes it, and reviews it up to a hundred times. He emails it to himself and reads it on his phone.
- The test is whether there is nothing he can cut and nothing that consistently confuses him.
- Influence: On Writing Well. Its conclusion is "cut, cut, cut". He tightens, simplifies, and uses bullets, charts and tables.
- He tries to open with a surprise, an interesting point or a story. His advice is to skip long intros and get right to it.
- Knowing when to stop: when he looks again and finds nothing to tweak. He may come back later in the day, maybe tweak the title, and when nothing changes it's ready. It won't be perfect forever, with weekly publishing and a $150-a-year subscription.

## Podcast: launching it, running interviews, and sequencing with the newsletter

- He long resisted a podcast, a book and a course. Harry Stebbings told him after a 20VC recording that he should do one, and that got him over the hump.
- He finds the podcast easier than the newsletter. In the newsletter he must produce unique insights and craft them. In the podcast he extracts them from someone else's brain.
- He also says the podcast is more lucrative and more energizing.
- Sequencing: every episode is shared with his large newsletter audience, which makes the podcast easier to grow. His advice: "build an awesome newsletter and then launch a podcast". Doing it the other way is harder.
- Interview technique:
  - Keep following up when an answer is vague, to get concrete.
  - Don't react to every answer with "awesome, great, that was so interesting". Move on to the next question.
  - Watch your own interviews to find what is annoying.
- Guest vetting: He relies more on referrals from past great guests and on watching their other talks and videos. He admits you never know until you talk. He hasn't yet cut an interview.

## Creator-life tradeoffs and advice for starting

- Upsides: easier angel investing, since founders want him involved. He also gets access to founders and companies, regular notes about how his work helped people, and a large network. He controls his own calendar.
- Downsides: no PTO, no 401K match, no health insurance, no disability coverage. It is also lonely, and it's an endless treadmill. A viral hit fades within days, and you must keep publishing.
- Exit: paid annual plans are bought every day, so stopping means losing income. He doesn't see an exit path for a creator business named after himself.
- His advice: work on something you're genuinely curious about and care about, or you create a job for yourself that sucks.
- The name doesn't matter. He called it Lenny's Newsletter because it was Substack's default suggestion when he signed up with no plan. People waste months on names. Find something you like, have a process and grind it out.
- On starting: "just do it. Stop thinking about it." Write one thing on Substack, which is free. Write what you want to get out of your head or remember, and see whether you enjoy it and whether people value it. Writing is easier than video.
- He hasn't written a book. He sees it as a year of intense work with little money, and says a collection of newsletters won't be easy. He may do it someday.
