## Culture as a competitive advantage

Lenny credits Airbnb's strong culture, clear values and quirky rituals as a real competitive advantage. It helped the company hire top talent, move fast when opportunities arose, push through adversity, and let the team hold leaders to the long-term mission.

He names three ingredients:
- **Founders obsessed with culture.** This shapes who the first hires are (who co-create the culture) and which values get modeled, knowingly or not. It matters more as you scale.
- **A strong sense of self.** Airbnb codified core values through a small task force about three years in. They are used to measure success, hire (a core values interview group vets every candidate), evaluate performance (baked into peer review) and assess large deals. Everyone at the company could recite them verbatim.
- **Rituals.** Cookie time Tuesdays, new hire tea time, hosted bar, human tunnels, fun facts. They create space for bonding and joy. His advice: "Don't overthink your rituals; experiment and see what sticks."

Takeaway: be obsessed with company and team culture.

## Nail the problem statement

Lenny calls crafting and aligning on a problem statement "the single most important step in solving any problem." He saw simple projects with vague problem statements circle for weeks or months, while complex projects with strong ones "sail smoothly."

Tools he uses:
- A one-pager template he refined over the years to crystallize the problem and opportunity for his team and stakeholders.
- The situation-complication-resolution framework, to tell the story to a wider audience.
- The jobs-to-be-done framework, to make sure real customer needs are being addressed.

Takeaway: crystallize the problem and align the whole team behind it.

## Setting wildly ambitious goals

At the end of each year, Airbnb was often shocked at how close it came to "seemingly impossible" goals. CEO Brian Chesky was known for doubling proposed goals and often pushing teams to 10x.

Lenny's five ingredients:
- **Set uncomfortable goals.** Pick one that makes you uncomfortable while clearly understanding why hitting it would be incredible for the business. Ask: what would need to be true to hit it, and what could we accomplish without barriers (budget, people, dependencies)?
- **Make someone directly accountable.** "If a number doesn't have a person's name next to it, it's not going to happen."
- **Think long-term.** Airbnb looked five to 10 years ahead to set each year's goal, for both growth and mission.
- **Give a cross-functional team ownership of how to achieve it.** A leader's number-one job is assembling the right team, pointing it in the right direction, and unblocking it.
- **Celebrate success, don't punish failure.** Goals are meant to push you, not kill you. If a team gets close, congratulate them and move on to the next goal.

Takeaway: when setting goals, think bigger.

## Start with the ideal and work backward

Lenny describes a variation of Amazon's working backward method that worked well at Airbnb: envision the perfect user experience first, then close the gap.

**Snow White example.** Inspired by how Disney developed the original film, the founders treated Airbnb as a story with a beginning, middle and end. The team storyboarded the ideal guest and host experience and identified key emotional moments. The storyboards exposed the biggest gaps and informed early company strategy.

**Instant Book example.** Booking had many steps, including a wait while the host reviewed the request. Rather than micro-optimizing parts of the funnel for months or years, the team defined the ideal: a guest instantly books any home with no waiting.
- Only about 5% of bookings were instant at the time, and getting every host to allow booking without approval seemed impossible.
- It was clear this was where the business had to go, so the team put all its resources behind the bet.
- Over a couple of years the marketplace shifted so the vast majority of bookings are now instant.

His process:
- **Write or draw the ideal experience.** The team sketched the ideal booking flow on paper and wrote a sample blog post announcing it as if it were real.
- **Create a framework to make the problem tractable.** The biggest gap was giving hosts control over who could book instantly. The team split it into "can" problems (am I able to use it?) and "want" problems (do I want to use it?) and worked through them in priority order.
- **When it feels uncomfortable, get more data.** Many people assumed instant bookings would hurt quality (less communication, more transactional). A quick data dive showed otherwise, which, with a few other data points, cleared the path for internal buy-in.

Takeaway: look for step-function change by imagining the ideal state and working backward.

## Treat org design as a product

Lenny argues that as you move up in product leadership, "the most important product to get right is how you organize your people." Structure can multiply or hinder the mission.

- **Optimize for dedicated cross-functional teams with a clear mandate.** He calls this the single most impactful thing leaders can do when setting up a team. Teams should be self-contained and autonomous. A missing resource (designer, data scientist, budget), an extra cross-team dependency or a conflicting surface area cuts impact sharply, often invisibly until later. Minimize how often a team must meet with or wait on another team. Well-functioning teams "feel like a black box that outputs regular updates and amazing work."
- **Get the goals right.** Teams underestimate this. Good goals are:
  1. limited in number, ideally one or two
  2. quick to show impact
  3. directly tied to top-line business growth
  4. easy to understand
  5. uncomfortable
- **Accept there is no perfect org design.** He went through almost a dozen reorgs at Airbnb and never saw a plan that fixed every issue and pleased everyone. Address the biggest pain points, future-proof where you can, then move forward. Note the flaws (overlapping product ownership, two teams sharing a key metric, a team owning too much), build systems to work around them, and set the expectation that the org will change again.

Takeaway: build autonomous units with well-defined goals and get out of the way.

## Maintain a high bar for everything

Coming from startups, Lenny was used to moving fast, settling for good enough and thinking short-term. An early Airbnb manager taught him the power of a high bar, which he says "profoundly impacted my career."

Practices:
- **Emails:** reread before sending; there is always something to cut or clarify.
- **Shared docs:** get feedback from at least one person before sharing widely, keep formatting clean and consistent, close out comments before sharing with execs, make it scannable.
- **Meetings:** put the goal and ideally an agenda in the invite, invite as few people as possible, call out unproductive meetings, leave with action items, and follow up by email with owners.
- **Presentations:** ask whether an email would do. State whether you want a decision, feedback or just to share information. Get feedback first, and keep it short: "no one has ever wished that presentation went longer."
- **Hiring:** "The people you bring in determine the company you become." Only hire people you feel "hell yes" about. "If it's a maybe, it's a no."

Questions to keep asking: how could we go a bit bolder, make this slightly better, make this meeting more productive, make this doc crisper, set the bar higher?

## Keep teams focused

Lenny found a small supply growth team spread across a long funnel at Airbnb, seeing wins but unable to build momentum. He saw the same on a trip quality team. In both cases, narrowing the problem space and giving a focused mandate improved impact and morale.

- **Supply growth:** split into focused units (referrals, top-of-funnel organic growth, performance marketing, and so on), then grew each with resources matched to its problem.
- **Trip quality:** dedicated a quarter at a time to one aspect (host response rate, guest review rate, etc.). When a large opportunity appeared, the team doubled down the next quarter.

The same applies to product. Giving users fewer things to think about drove some of the biggest guest conversion gains he saw:
- opening listings in new tabs (avoids getting lost while exploring)
- extending session length (fewer logins)
- removing links within the payments flow (fewer distractions)

On the host side:
- always showing a "recommended" tag when presenting options
- defaulting settings by host persona
- adding inline tips so users don't bounce

Takeaway: "Focus. Focus. Focus."
