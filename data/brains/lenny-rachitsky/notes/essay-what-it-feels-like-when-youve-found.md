## Three forms of product-market fit pull

Lenny asked founders of 25 iconic companies when they first knew they had product-market fit (PMF). Every company had a clear moment of recognition, but not every company saw it immediately. He groups the stories into three signs:

1. Sudden, significant pull from the market.
2. Steady, compounding pull.
3. A meaningful milestone proving the idea works.

He says pull shows up in many forms:
- An inflection in organic growth.
- Customers asking to pay before you ask.
- Users flipping from excited about what you have to mad about what you don't have.
- Customers complaining when your site goes down.
- People using the product even when it's broken.

Founder quotes he highlights:
- Drew Houston (Dropbox): "like getting pressed into the back of your seat by a fast car or a plane taking off."
- Calvin French-Owen (Segment): "When we hit PMF, we started feeling 'pull' for the first time." Before that, "we had to nag people to use the product."
- Henry Ward (eShares/Carta): a prospect wrote "Why won't you take my money???" after a phone misconfiguration hung up on sales callers for three weeks. "That's what product market fit feels like."

## How long PMF takes, and what determines its intensity

About half of the companies found PMF right after launch. The other half spent months or years iterating:
- Netflix: 18 months
- Segment: 1.5 years
- Airbnb: 2 years
- PagerDuty: 2 years
- Superhuman: 3 years
- Amplitude: 4 years

Once they got there, it became obvious.

Lenny's model is that the intensity of the pull is a function of two things:
- **Fit:** how good the product is at solving the user's problem.
- **Initial market size:** niche or broad.

Examples:
- Dropbox, Netflix and Tinder were 10x better products in a huge market, which gave sudden, broad pull.
- Instacart, Superhuman and Substack were 10x better products for a narrow set of initial customers, which gave steady, compounding pull.

## Three requirements for "True" product-market fit

Lenny stresses that feeling PMF does not guarantee a successful business. True PMF needs three things:
1. **Make a product people want.** This is the focus of the post.
2. **Make a profit delivering it at scale.** He cites Cherry, Luxe and Exec as cautionary tales.
3. **Find and keep these people sustainably.** This is the quantitative side: retention and sales yield.

He notes that the first is the most likely to kill a business: not building something people want.

## Sudden pull: stories from consumer and viral products

Lenny includes these examples of sudden pull:
- **Netflix (Marc Randolph):** 18 months and hundreds of failed experiments. Then they tested "No Due Dates, No Late Fees, and Subscription." Within days they knew. Traffic surged, inventory wasn't enough, engagement soared and churn dropped.
- **Uber (Ryan Graves):** zero marketing budget, uncontrollable word of mouth, and free media coverage because the story was compelling.
- **Tinder (Jonathan Badeen):** downloads picked up in late December 2012 and skyrocketed by early to mid January 2013. The swipe was added within weeks of the August or September 2012 launch, with very few product changes before takeoff.
- **Robinhood (Vlad Tenev):** about 600 concurrent visitors on a waitlist site nobody knew about. That led to 10,000 sign-ups on day one, over 50,000 in the first week, and almost 1 million in the first year.
- **Stripe (Patrick Collison):** friends invited friends. A waitlist was put up and demand took off over a couple of months.
- **Patreon (Samuel Yam):** after launch, patrons and creators started writing in, with a level of passion he had never seen.

## Sudden pull: signals that customers want to pay

- **GitHub (Tom Preston-Werner):** the private beta was free, and users wrote asking "Can we pay for this??" That was the first sign it would work. Early users were Ruby community enthusiasts, and being embedded in that community was critical. After individuals came SMBs, then enterprises.
- **Eshares (Henry Ward):** the phone misconfiguration story above.
- **Datadog and Segment:** Segment's customers moved from receiving the product to requesting more (a second, third, fourth and fifth problem solved), so early iteration was mostly answering customer requests.
- **Dropbox:** the demo video hitting the top of Digg, then Reddit.
- **Another company (Jeffrey Queisser):** described nearly 100% inbound sales, "like Geoffrey Moore's Inside the Tornado."

## Compounding pull: slower, narrower PMF

- **Substack (Chris Best):** no single moment. Steady growth made "how-do-we-keep-up" anxiety so large there was no time left to worry about PMF. Retention curves looked good early, especially for paying readers and writers with paying readers.
- **Superhuman (Rahul Vohra):** found pockets of PMF with specific segments (founders, managers, executives, business development professionals) and focused the whole company on them. Vohra argues narrowing the target doesn't necessarily cap growth. His moment came when updated analytics showed every metric looked great: growth, DAU/MAU, PMF score, NPS, virality, CAC payback, CAC:LTV, activation and long-term retention. A month later the product was in the New York Times, the Economist and the Guardian.
- **Instacart (Max Mullen):** early PMF with a small sub-segment who wanted groceries delivered ASAP from any store. Most customers wanted their favorite store, so Instacart partnered with top retailers, which drew customers and, in turn, more partners. "Initial signs of product-market fit feel a bit like a calm breeze, while true product-market fit feels like a powerful wind at your back."
- **Nextdoor (Sarah Leary):** an hour of downtime with only about ten neighborhoods live brought panicked calls within ten minutes. "If your users are urgently calling and demanding access to your product, you have clearly built something of real value."
- **Datadog (Olivier Pomel):** no sudden influx, but a regular stream of sign-ups from the moment they flipped from closed to open beta, growing as key features like alerting were added.
- **PagerDuty (Alex Solomon):** beta in summer 2009, paid version December 2009. They added incident tracking in early 2010, then saw over 10% month-over-month growth in 2011 and roughly $1M ARR. By mid-2011 they felt confident, because they were solving a "hair on fire problem."
- **Looker (Keenan Rice):** about 10 customers from 12 months of founder-led selling, who were engineers stuck doing data projects. Fit arrived because the market shifted: a new generation of product and data people had the same revelation, and AWS Redshift made data warehouses easily accessible.
- **Another company (Tomer London):** realized in hindsight, with NPS of 80+, low churn and record month-over-month organic growth despite gaps in marketing, product and care.

## Milestones that proved the idea worked

- **Product Hunt (Ryan Hoover):** overhearing strangers at a cafe discuss Product Hunt and their upcoming launch.
- **Airbnb (Joe Gebbia):** "When my mom booked her first Airbnb."
- **Caviar (Jason Wang):** the first large company (Kabam) replaced its lunch service with Caviar, which made them sustainable and "ramen profitable."
- **Amplitude (Tai Rattigan):** PMF came when the company shifted from mobile analytics to product analytics for product teams. Over 1,000 people from product teams attended its first conference, Amplify. The sign was hearing a bank team member say "I've been in Product for 10 years and this is the first time a company has ever focussed on us."
- **Canva (Cliff Obrecht):** with fewer than 200 templates, they saw designs published on social media, including Guy Kawasaki using it for social posts.
