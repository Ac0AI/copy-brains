## Starting from the first users: who, what to pitch, where to find them

Lenny frames the "grand unifying theory of growth" as a playbook for building any company, consumer or B2B, from zero to a million users. It has four steps:

1. Identify an early adopter segment that acts as a wedge into a broader market.
2. Find a value prop that resonates with that segment.
3. Work out where those users are and pitch them there.
4. Build growth engines that sustain growth long term.

The first three steps get the first users. The fourth is what keeps growth going afterward.

## Step 1: pick the wedge customer

Lenny's examples of initial wedge customers:
- Netflix: DVD fanatics.
- Cameo: C-list celebrities.
- TikTok: bottom-tier influencers.
- WhatsApp: Russian immigrants.
- Substack: successful online freelance writers.
- Tinder: party-loving college students.
- Airbnb: conference goers in San Francisco.
- Robinhood: techy millennials.
- Yelp: females aged 25 to 35.

On finding the ICP:
- Take a best guess at who has the biggest pain point and take a swing.
- The more niche and focused the segment, the better. "Millennials" is too broad.
- Useful framing: what pain point does the user have, what do they use now to solve it, and what are their demographics.
- He admits he hasn't spent much time on this step.

## Step 2: find a value prop that is "remarkable"

Lenny's examples:
- Robinhood: no-commission stock trading.
- Spotify: all the music in the world, free with ads.
- Dropbox: throw away your USB drive.
- Netflix: no due dates, no late fees, unlimited movies.
- DoorDash (sales-driven): "more revenue with no risk" for restaurants.

He likes the word "remarkable": a value prop worth remarking about, so people want to tell others. How to read the signal:
- "Magic happens when you get the first two steps right."
- Some companies hit it immediately. Robinhood and Dropbox posted on Hacker News and traffic exploded.
- Others take a long time. Netflix needed a while to land on the right combination. Superhuman took about four years.
- He estimates roughly 70% of companies know quickly when they've found something people want, especially in consumer.
- If it isn't clicking, "try to change something radically".
- If it works, it's "very clear": "there goes", unlike anything experienced before.

## Step 3: the concentric circles of where to find users

Lenny models channels as concentric circles moving away from the founder. It gets harder the further out you go. Try all of them in small ways, but most success comes from a few.

1. **Friends and colleagues.** LinkedIn (Reid's investor friends), Pinterest (Ben emailed friends), Facebook, Slack (Stuart emailed friends), Gusto, Stripe (a Collison friend was the first customer), Salesforce (Marc Benioff's former colleague), Workday.
2. **Targeted strangers.** Cameo hired $10/hour interns to DM C-list celebrities. Behance's Scott Belsky emailed 100 designers. Figma and Instagram DMed designers on Twitter. DoorDash went door to door. Substack approached successful writers. Udemy approached experts.
3. **Online communities.** Netflix used DVD forums, Dropbox used Hacker News and Reddit, Robinhood used Hacker News, Facebook used the Harvard mailing list.
4. **Offline communities.** Etsy at craft fairs, Lyft and Uber at startup offices, Tinder on college campuses, Snapchat at malls.
5. **Building your own community.** Rare. Examples: ProTocons (email list), Stack Overflow (podcast), TikTok (creator community).
6. **Influencers.** Spotify targeted music bloggers. A celebrity, Ronnie Radke, nearly broke Cameo by bringing in thousands of signups, but people loved it anyway.
7. **PR.** Worked for Instacart, Udemy, Zillow, Spotify, Airbnb.
8. **Physical presence.** DoorDash flyers at Stanford, Instacart logos on delivery bags, Grubhub signage in restaurants.
9. **Partnerships.** Netflix with DVD manufacturers, Kayak with AOL (he's not fully certain on that one).
10. **Marketplace dynamics: supply drives demand.** Suppliers promote the platform to their own customers. Examples: DoorDash restaurants, Caviar, Uber Eats, Etsy, Eventbrite, Substack writers.

## Friends and colleagues: bootstrapping trust

- The reason early users come from friends is "bootstrapping trust". Nobody trusts a new CRM or HR product, so you need trust from someone they know.
- This matters most for B2B. Many of the biggest B2B companies' first 10 customers came from friends, former colleagues and their communities.
- It's less useful for consumer, which needs millions of users. Friends won't move the needle, so founders quickly move to other layers.
- This is part of why YC works for B2B. Stripe, Gusto and Amplitude drew early customers from YC.
- The friends-and-family trap: don't let them drive what you build. Start with what you want to work on and where the opportunity is, then find the users. Friends are just a way to get started.

## Communities, audience and authenticity

- Don't join a community to pitch. Provide value, or become someone they already trust.
- Netflix hired Corey, a "black ops" person, to infiltrate online DVD forums and build trust before launch.
- A company Lenny invested in finds success posting useful how-tos on Reddit.
- You can't credibly join a community just because you'll start a company in two years. Be part of communities you're genuinely interested in. Shopify started when Toby sold snowboards and Ruby on Rails people asked to use his store software.
- Follow energy and interest. You'll commit years to the company.
- Build a following by sharing value (make people smarter, laugh, or make money). He didn't do this deliberately but calls it "really helpful". It's optionality and trust for whatever you launch later. With a big following you can also ask hundreds of people questions.
- Twitter approach: don't overthink it. Be authentic, share what's interesting, and don't be "thirsty about likes and followers".
- Patreon's Jack Conte already had a big YouTube audience and understood the problem.

## PR and new channels

- Lenny is not a PR expert. His principle: find a channel that isn't saturated yet. A new channel is great until it gets crowded.
- PR is now crowded with funding announcements. Twitter, podcasts (including advertising), and newsletters are newer, undervalued channels.
- He likes Balaji's idea that every company will want a chief content officer, especially for SEO, which needs good content.
- Finding the new channel is where creativity comes in.

## Step 4: the four growth engines

Everything above produces a spike, not sustained growth. Lenny's metaphor: push the car to get it rolling, then add an engine. He says there are only four sustainable engines:

1. **Performance marketing.** Spend to acquire customers who generate more revenue than they cost. Examples: Wish, Booking.com, most DTC businesses, TikTok (spent heavily on paid ads early), Redfin.
2. **Virality / word of mouth.** Users bring users. Examples: Robinhood, Airbnb.
3. **Content / SEO.** Best when users generate the content: more users, more content, more users. Examples: Pinterest, Grubhub.
4. **Sales.** Mostly B2B. Doesn't make economic sense for consumer. Example: DoorDash (sales gets restaurants, restaurants bring customers).

Key points:
- Every successful company has one engine that primarily drove its success, with others layered on later (everyone eventually does some SEO and some paid).
- You need to be the best in the world at one engine in your market. In travel, Booking.com owns paid, Airbnb owns word of mouth, and TripAdvisor and Expedia ended up SEO and content driven.
- Pick the engine most likely to work, then commit. Don't dabble: SEO on the side takes a long time to figure out. He links heuristics for choosing in the first round piece.

## Airbnb as a case study

- Lenny joined about five years in, when nobody knew where growth came from. Everything was working because organic growth was strong.
- Gustaf created the first growth team and measured it by asking visitors "how did you hear about us".
- Result: roughly 50% of guests and about 70% of hosts come from word of mouth. Referrals were about 20% and paid about 20%.
- Early on, growth came from PR and community: host meetups, plus the Obama O's moment. Word of mouth followed because the experience was great at a low cost.
- Referrals were "an optimization" layered on top of word of mouth. Nothing special was done to cause word of mouth, other than making the product great.

## Open problem: the messy middle

- Lenny has thought less about the transition from unscalable tactics to a scalable engine, and wanted input.
- His advice for companies in it: figure out which engine is most likely to work, test it, and get good at it.

## Humane growth and his own newsletter

- Humane growth is a topic he wants to write about. Businesses must grow, so the question is how to grow without harming people.
- Problems arise when customers are the product (advertising businesses). Things work better when the customer and business interests align.
- Company principles help. Example: Wealthfront's stated value of putting customers above the business and thinking long term. Founders must set rules and hold to them under growth pressure.
- On the newsletter: anything beyond writing good stuff is rarely worth it. Twitter ads and cross-newsletter exchanges did nothing. What worked early: guest posts in other communities, plus befriending people with followings and asking them to tweet at launch. SEO is a small share of traffic.
