## Starting the newsletter: following the pull

- Lenny left Airbnb after 7 years with several plans: start a company, join a startup as first PM, join a big company as PM, or consult. Writing a newsletter was nowhere on the list.
- He kept writing on the side while exploring startup ideas. His first post, on what he learned at Airbnb, did very well. Medium featured it and Brian Chesky shared it with the whole company.
- A friend (Lee Jacobs, now a VC) pointed out that "you seem to be enjoying it" and people valued it, a rare overlap worth doubling down on.
- Lenny says he would otherwise have been "on the struggle bus of startup life" and, once that failed, joined a company as a PM.
- He had never written online before and describes himself as an introvert who liked staying behind the scenes. The newsletter began during COVID, so he could just type and publish from home.

## The 9-month weekly commitment and the Lindy effect

- He moved to Substack and committed to a weekly newsletter, announcing it as an experiment.
- After 9 months of weekly posts, he reasoned that if he'd kept going that long, he could probably do it another 9 months. He cites the Lindy effect: something that has lasted a given time will most likely last at least that long again.
- He had many more ideas to write about, so he kept going.
- That was the point he added a paywall. COVID had hit Airbnb's stock, so he needed income from the newsletter.
- The paywall worked: he made "meaningful dollars" about a month in.
- He now has 1.2 million subscribers, and his podcast is regularly in the top 10 of tech podcasts.

## Enjoying it, and the treadmill downside

- His test for a path worth pursuing is the overlap of three things: it's good, people like it, and he enjoys it.
- He can't imagine doing anything more fulfilling: writing about interesting things, sharing other people's insights, interviewing people and extracting wisdom, all in his own business.
- He cites the artist Finch's post on becoming an artist: once it's a thing you have to do, it changes it.
- A post and a podcast episode every week is a treadmill. His image is "the Indiana Jones boulder is chasing me constantly": once a post ships, the question is immediately what comes next week.
- Some weeks he loves it, some weeks he'd like to skip. He says you can't survive in this life without doing something consistently and making it good every week.
- He has no answer for where this goes long term, since there's no obvious end state for a Substack writer and podcaster.

## Keeping the business small and avoiding a job he'd hate

- He misses having someone next to him working on the same thing, now that he works from home. He has a team, but not coworkers in the room.
- He's trying hard to never have full-time employees and to keep things simple.
- His warning: it's easy to build this into something complicated, or to create a job you hate by doing what people want or by chasing things that "feel big."
- He is careful about what he commits to.

## Business crisis: the product pass fraud attack

- Subscribers get a bundle of about 23 products (he names Linear, Mobbin, Lovable, Replit, Gamma). He used to read every one at the end of his podcast, but there are now too many.
- About a year before this recording, he launched a perk giving a free year of Cursor, Lovable, Bolt, Replit and V0. He says it was "way too good an offer."
- It attracted fraud rings, mostly in China, which built attacks to exploit the free goodies, found exploits in the API his team built, and spread the offer virally through student networks.
- He worked with Stripe and Substack to shut the attacks down. His engineer Este barely slept for a week patching holes.
- The stress was real: he feared the whole thing could unravel if people stopped trusting him, and he slept badly.

## Managing stress and happiness

- He thinks he's more stressed than he looks (he gets headaches) but less than the average person, and credits maybe 70% genetics plus deliberate work on how he thinks.
- During his wife's complicated delivery, when a emergency happened and he was left without information in a hospital hallway, he kept telling himself "it's going to be okay" and trusting that the doctors knew what they were doing. He calls it probably the scariest moment of his life.
- A University of Pennsylvania online course on the psychology of happiness had a big impact on him. Its key idea: everyone has a baseline level of happiness, good and bad events move you away from it temporarily, and the main lever is raising the baseline.
- The practical tools he took from it: think more optimistically and don't let your mind spiral into "it's going to be terrible." He says his baseline has gone up.
- On exercise, the research he remembers (from around 15 years earlier, possibly outdated) is that it doesn't make you happier but lifts you out of the negative, from below zero to zero.
- He has also done a 10-day silent meditation and breathing work.
- A psychedelic experience in Joshua Tree around the time he started the newsletter, where the phrase "I have wisdom to share" came through repeatedly over about 3 hours, gave him confidence that he had things to share.

## Why the best content comes from real experience

- He says the best stuff comes from doing the thing for real, then sharing the advice, not from "floating in the clouds pontificating."
- That's why most of his posts are now guest posts where someone shares the best thing they've learned in their career, and he focuses on practitioners on the ground.
- He agrees with the idea that to write, you read, but adds that lived experience is the source of the best material.
- His quality bar: he knows a piece is ready when it feels really interesting and good to him, without waiting for others' approval.
- His process is heavy iteration. He reads through and improves a newsletter post about 50 times, then it goes to his editor, a copy editor and a designer.
- Sitting on work and coming back to it often reveals what will make it better.
- He compares this to how David Sedaris says yes to everything to collect stories: you can't just sit and generate ideas, you have to try things and live.

## Product management, in his words

- His definition: a PM's job is "to deliver business impact by prioritizing and solving the most impactful business problems."
- He still believes a PM is a mini CEO: the PM should think the way the CEO would about what helps the product and business succeed.
- The five words he first reached for: impact, collaboration, judgment, alignment, coordination.
- What carried over from PM work to his current job: being organized, a very high bar for quality, and communicating points succinctly so people grasp them.
- He is not an intuition person; he asks what the smartest people have already figured out about a problem (for example, shortening bedtime) and applies it.
