## Early adopters: the seven ways to find your first 1,000 users

Lenny reviewed the early growth of more than 100 consumer companies and found only seven reliable ways to find the first 1,000 users. He treats them as a menu: pick two or three that look appetizing, try them, and double down on whatever works. Almost every startup got most of its early growth from one or two channels, and some from a single one (Udemy and PR, Etsy and communities, Twitter and influencers). His advice is to focus, not to order everything.

The seven strategies, with roughly how often each drove early growth:
- Reach out to friends and colleagues (about 20% of startups)
- Reach out to targeted strangers (about 30%)
- Go where your target audience hangs out, online or offline (over 50%, the most popular and highest-ROI)
- Enlist influencers, paid or organic
- Get press (over 20%)
- Create viral content (about 10%)
- Get physical placement such as flyers, stickers and signs (about 10%)

He says these seven account for 99% of early B2C growth. Rarely, a company wins "off menu."

## How to choose a tactic: three archetypes

Lenny maps common startup types to a starting order:
- **Typical consumer startup:** friends and colleagues, then go where the audience hangs out, then influencers.
- **Marketplace or platform (DoorDash, Cameo, Substack):** reach out to targeted strangers (DM celebs, email owners), then physical placement, then get supply to drive demand.
- **Remarkable story (Airbnb, Spotify, Superhuman):** get press, then try to create viral content.

He frames the strategies as concentric circles with the founder at the center. The further out you go, the harder and less likely to succeed a strategy generally is. So start with the easy stuff and move outward only if nothing works.

## Strategy 1: Reach out to friends and colleagues

- Easiest and fastest route, a large driver for about 20% of startups. Lenny cites Paul Graham: "you don't need to get the word out. You need to get the word *in*."
- Best for products most people find useful (Reddit, Facebook, Lyft). The key is that your friends resemble your super-specific "who."
- Takeaway question: "Have you asked your friends to try out your product yet?"
- Examples he cites: Yelp reached roughly 1K users two degrees out from ex-PayPal coworkers. Lyft sent personal email invites before its waitlist. Strava got about 100 users from direct friends and about 1,000 within 12 months by word of mouth. LinkedIn's founders invited their contacts on launch day (a couple thousand individuals), and most of the roughly 12,000 first-week signups were 1st or 2nd degree connections. Substack's Hamish McKenzie recruited Bill Bishop as the first publisher, then kept asking friends and each writer for more recommendations.
- Caveats: friends may not match your "who." WhatsApp's Jan Koum demoed to friends and none liked it. Patreon's founders emailed 40 creators they knew and all 40 said no.

## Strategy 2: Reach out to targeted strangers

- Important for about 30% of consumer startups, used almost exclusively by marketplaces to bootstrap supply. He reasons that one unit of supply is worth enough to a new marketplace that manual cold outreach has high ROI.
- Best for marketplaces and platforms where each recruited user is very valuable (a Cameo celebrity).
- Takeaway question: "Would it be worth having someone on your team ping 100+ people directly?"
- Examples:
  - Cameo: "$10/month interns" DM'd talent on Instagram and Twitter.
  - YouTube: summer 2005 outreach to bloggers, video bloggers, photographers and pet communities.
  - DoorDash: the founder went door to door to restaurants.
  - Thumbtack: emailed local service pros and then sent them job leads once registered.
  - Uber Black: phoned limo companies, pitching a guaranteed minimum income if they kept the app on.
  - Airbnb: direct sales let local teams choose which supply (neighborhood, size, price) to acquire.
  - Udemy: after manually creating successful courses, returned to the same experts with proof and landed Zed Shaw and Bess Ho, then 5 to 10 more that summer.
  - Behance: contacted the 100 designers they admired most, offered an interview for a productivity blog, then built each a portfolio. Nobody declined; the result was a v1 with about five projects per top creative, which "solved our chicken-or-egg problem."
  - Nextdoor: picked the Lorelei neighborhood and approached homeowners association board members.
  - Shef: asked friends where they bought authentic dishes, got intros to those cooks, and joined their Facebook groups.

## Strategy 3: Go where your target audience hangs out

- Lenny calls this the single most popular (over 50%), effective and highest-ROI strategy. It is why a very specific "who" matters: you need to know where they gather.
- Places include niche forums, events, transit hubs, Hacker News, Product Hunt, college campuses, malls, even an REI.
- Key: give real value instead of spamming your pitch. Takeaway question: "What are 1-2 places where your super-specific who already gather?"
- Online examples:
  - Netflix: an early hire joined DVD forums as an enthusiast and seeded mentions with moderators and top commenters months before launch.
  - Discord: a Final Fantasy subreddit member mentioned the app.
  - Etsy: reached Craftster.org's founder (about 100,000 members) and had thousands of sellers ready at launch, with fee-free listing for the first few months.
  - Behance: used StumbleUpon, Reddit, NotCot and Pinterest, and sent personal "I noticed your work on..." emails (more personal converted better). The practice was to bring in 10 amazing new members a day.
  - Dropbox: Hacker News in April 2007 (top for two days), Digg and Reddit in March 2008, public launch September 2008, a million signups by May 2009.
  - Robinhood: a waitlist accidentally hit #3 on Hacker News, with about 600 concurrent visitors on a Saturday.
  - Facebook: a message to a mailing list of 300 Harvard students.
- Offline examples:
  - Morning Brew: a hub-and-spoke model, pitching business professors and club presidents at Michigan (lectures of 75 to 500 people) for a couple thousand readers.
  - Etsy: a team at craft fairs nearly every weekend across the US and Canada, buying sellers lunch and dropping off "craft show kits."
  - Lyft: free Bi-Rite ice cream sundae drop-offs at startups, with Lyft credits.
  - Uber: street teams handing out referral codes at Caltrain.
  - Hipcamp: about 100 users from a fold-up table and stickers at REI.
  - Pinterest: the founder changed Apple Store demo computers to Pinterest.
  - Rent the Runway: trial trunk shows on college campuses.
  - Wealthfront: educational seminars at Facebook and LinkedIn aimed at engineers, with the pitch dropped entirely ("80% education and 20% selling" had to become all education).

## Tinder and Bumble: the campus playbook

Lenny highlights Tinder's launch as a detailed offline recipe:
- A launch party of about 300 USC students where you had to download Tinder to get in.
- The next Monday, a pitch in every sorority and fraternity; then SMU, UCLA, UCSB and Boston.
- A "Tinder University Program": pick influential students per campus, send swag (T-shirts, stickers, cups), and make them brand ambassadors, measured by the percentage of people downloading in an area. About 15 universities at a time, adding another once an area saturated.
- Bumble reused the playbook: sororities pitched "from the heart," pizza boxes and cookies wrapped in Bumble stickers for fraternity members.
- Snap spread from a high schooler in Orange County: just over 2,000 users in December 2011, 20,000 in January, 100,000 by April.

## Strategy 4: Enlist influencers

- Underutilized. Figure out who your super-specific who is influenced by and get them genuinely excited, paid or not (bloggers, newsletter writers, tweeters, Instagrammers, celebs).
- Takeaway question: the center of the Venn diagram of who your audience is influenced by and which of them you can get excited about what you're building.
- Examples:
  - Instagram: seeded the prototype with people with large followings inside the designer community, not necessarily large overall.
  - Tinder: prominent Instagrammers posted authentically.
  - Product Hunt: personal emails inviting influencers to contribute.
  - Spotify: seeded with journalists, musicians and tech CEOs to build pent-up demand.
  - Cameo: Ronnie Radke, found via famousbirthdays.com, got booked about 500 times in days; his poor seven-second videos still got positive reviews, showing the magic is the fan-talent relationship.
  - Pinterest: a blogger collaboration ("pin it forward") after the Alt Summit conference.
  - Twitter: Om Malik's post drove more than 250 signups the next day, when the service had under 600 users.
  - Reddit: Paul Graham's essay forced an unplanned launch and passed 1,000 users in a day.

## Strategies 5 to 7: press, viral content, physical placement

**Press** (over 20%). Best for products or founders with a great story and a drumbeat of news. Takeaway question: what's the most interesting or surprising thing about your company to someone who doesn't care?
- Instacart's Max Mullen: "PR is an untapped growth channel for early-stage consumer startups."
- Airbnb's 2008 DNC bump lasted only a week, so the founders sold Obama O's and Cap'n McCain's cereal at $40 a box; they pitched the smallest bloggers first as social proof for bigger outlets.
- Udemy's first 10,000 registrants came directly from its press launch. Spotify relied on PR per country launch. Superhuman landed pieces in Wired, TechCrunch and Cheddar.
- Instagram contacted press directly instead of using a PR firm and got coverage including the New York Times.

**Viral content** (about 10%). Best for founders with an existing audience who can make something remarkable. Takeaway question: what story would get thousands of people to share it?
- Duolingo: the CEO's TED talk led to 300,000+ private beta signups, then no ad spend.
- Calm: donothingfortwominutes.com collected about 100K emails in two weeks.
- Patreon launched via a YouTube video to the founder's fans. Superhuman's longform essays drove early growth. Substack's founding essay was picked up by NiemanLab before the product existed.

**Physical placement** (about 10%). Best for local products (food delivery, dating); the key is hustle. Takeaway question: where would you put flyers, stickers or signs?
- DoorDash printed tens of thousands of flyers ("Dubar delivers, get hummus delivered!") and stuffed them in delivery bags, because customers cared about the restaurant, not DoorDash.
- Instacart delivered in logo-branded reusable grocery bags.
- Grubhub tried in-store signage (copied from Yelp's stickers), sandwich boards, menus and delivery bags.
- Tinder's Whitney Wolfe Herd printed about 1,000 "Find out who likes you on campus" flyers at FedEx and paid people $20 to place them.

## Off-menu tactics

Lenny lists four rarer approaches:
- **Build an audience first:** Product Hunt began as an email list and reached 100 users in its first week; Stack Overflow seeded a private beta from its founders' existing communities; Patreon launched to a creator's fanbase.
- **Let supply drive demand:** DoorDash restaurants made their own placards and window stickers; Etsy sellers did grassroots marketing and were pushed to promote their shops; also Cameo, Behance, Product Hunt, Substack.
- **Be first on an emerging platform:** Noom was the fourth app in the Android store; TikTok (Musical.ly) used a very long App Store name to rank in search; WhatsApp's status pings turned it into messaging after Apple's push notifications launched in June 2009.
- **Host a launch party:** Hinge spent its last $25,000 on a 2,500-person party in D.C. where everyone had to download the app to get in.
