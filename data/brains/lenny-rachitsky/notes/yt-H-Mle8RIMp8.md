## Why Lenny says high-quality content beats every other growth tactic for a newsletter

- Lenny says that anything he does besides creating high-quality content does very little for his newsletter's success. He says, "any time not spent creating high quality content is not time well spent."
- He tried Twitter ads, a referral program and cross-promotion with other newsletters. None of them did much.
- He says that over the long run, "if you provide value to people they're gonna want it and they're gonna subscribe and follow and pay."
- If a newsletter isn't growing, he would ask how to provide more value to readers "again and again and again." He says it won't happen overnight.
- He says there are no growth hacks. The hack is to "show up consistently" with consistent quality.
- He notes that a referral program worked well for Morning Brew, so non-content tactics can help in some cases.
- His growth is now almost entirely word of mouth.
- Early signals that the newsletter was working were qualitative. He got messages about how valuable the posts were, many from smart, respected people. Growth also continued when he wasn't doing anything, and people didn't unsubscribe.
- His quality test: "If I'm a founder reading this, is this actually useful to me, or is this a bunch of fluffy stuff?" He keeps it concretely actionable and immediately valuable. He cuts anything that isn't.

## How Lenny got his first 1,000 subscribers

- He wrote Medium posts about what he learned at Airbnb. The first was featured by Medium, and Airbnb's CEO sent it to the whole company. That drove views and a Twitter following.
- He tweeted small nuggets from the posts, or ideas that didn't fit in a post. He had a few thousand followers at first, then about 10,000.
- He moved to Substack for the usual reasons: owning the audience and not giving the benefits to Medium.
- He launched by tweeting "I'm launching a Substack newsletter, you should subscribe. I don't know what it's going to be yet but don't miss it." That got his first few hundred subscribers.
- Two guest posts, one on Andrew Chen's blog and one in First Round Review, plus Twitter, got him to the first 1,000. By then he had about 20 valuable posts.
- He had no plan to start a newsletter. He wrote to collect his thoughts and crystallize what he'd learned, and the newsletter grew from there.

## Guest posting: how to do it, and how Lenny handles guest posts now

**Writing guest posts early**
- He recommends guest posting early because you go where the audience already is. Show how good you are on someone else's audience and they'll follow you.
- Pick the right blogs and newsletters, and do a great job.
- Put your best work in, not leftovers.
- His Andrew Chen piece was "28 ways to grow supply in a marketplace." He worked on it for months, with many examples and quotes, and it was the best thing he'd written to that point.
- He showed Andrew the piece because they had gotten to know each other on Twitter. Andrew asked to republish it, and they did a 24-hour exclusive.
- First Round doesn't allow republishing. He had an outline and many of the posts done when he showed it to them, and that got them excited.
- Guest posts did little directly later on. He says "all these things add up."

**Running guest posts in his own paid newsletter**
- A paid newsletter means writing every week forever, so great content he doesn't have to write himself helps.
- He recruits the best person in each field to write a definitive answer on a topic readers keep asking about.
- He reaches out to them himself. He says the people he wants are often the ones not interested in guest posting, and so far no inbound pitch has met his bar.
- Rule of thumb: "If someone reaches out to me to try to do a guest post, that automatically means they're not going to do a guest post."
- His advice for pitching him is to send the entire article pre-written. A draft shows where it will land, and it's hard to turn someone down after starting with them.
- Even with top people, he pushes them through back-and-forth to make the piece more concrete, actionable and tactical.

## Getting supporters to amplify your launches

- He recommends an advisor-style group, an idea he got from the Groove blog. The writer sent drafts to about 20 smart people for feedback, then emailed them when the piece went live. Those people then tweeted and shared it because they had a stake in his success.
- Lenny did this for his paid-plan announcement. He ran it by Andrew Chen and others with big Twitter followings to get feedback on framing.
- They were supporters along the way, and they tweeted about it. He says that helped a lot.

## The downsides of going paid, and when Lenny decided to do it

- He says going paid has downsides he didn't think about beforehand.
- First, you're stuck writing every week, in theory forever, because people pay annual plans. Stopping would mean refunding everyone, which is psychologically hard. Be ready to commit for a few years.
- It took him nine months from the free newsletter to paid. Much of that was building confidence that he'd have a topic every week that he was excited about.
- Second, the same work now sits behind a paywall. Early on it feels like it "goes into a black hole" with few paying subscribers. He reminds himself that people are paying for it.
- He went paid about a year after leaving Airbnb, around the start of COVID, when he had no income and his stock was down.
- His goal was to earn more than he did at Airbnb and then stop worrying about growth.

## Lenny's weekly writing process

- He compares it to "a boulder is always chasing me." It was stressful at first. After about a year and a half, he's confident he'll find something great each week.
- He keeps a Coda doc with about three months of topics, each on its own page with a headline. Near-term posts get bullet points. The next month or so is fleshed out in more depth.
- The format is an advice column, so readers send real questions from founders, PMs and growth people. That gives him a large backlog, and he can also revisit old topics and do better.
- He aims to be a few weeks ahead but never quite gets there. The next post is usually about 80% done a week ahead. The following ones are 10-20% done, and the rest are just headlines.
- He blocks his mornings and takes no meetings until 3 p.m. He says that's hard to do with a day job, and he doesn't know how people write a good newsletter while working full time.
- He sends on Tuesdays so he isn't in the Monday morning inbox or at the end of the week.
- He balances content types (product, growth, people) so one audience segment doesn't leave. He thinks unsubscribes happen when people feel "not what I thought it was going to be," but that's anecdotal.
- He suggests asking readers for their biggest frustrations, which gives topic prompts.
- Rule: roughly "80% what do I actually care about this week" and 20% "is this top of mind for anyone." Otherwise you've "created a job for yourself you don't like." He rearranges his topic list constantly based on what he's drawn to, to avoid burnout.

## Pricing, conversion and numbers

**Numbers at the time**
- About 45,000 free subscribers and just over 3,000 paid, plus comps, so the real total is higher.
- Revenue is over six figures, more than he made at Airbnb.
- His first target was 1,000 paid subscribers, which roughly equated to $100K after fees and discounts. He calls it a "livable wage in San Francisco," borderline.
- Free-to-paid conversion is under 10%. At launch it was about 2%, with about 10,000 free subscribers and 200-300 paid.

**What he did on conversion**
- Paid subscribers get the email weekly. Free subscribers get one email a month, which "has to be really good because that's your sell."
- Sometimes he sends a peek at that week's paid post. Free posts also include "here's the three things you missed," and Twitter shares what's happening in the paid posts.

**Pricing**
- His advice: "priced higher than you think you should." People default to $5 a month and don't think their work is worth more.
- He looked at the Substack leaderboard. Analysis and "make me better at what I do" newsletters charged $10-20 a month, so he picked the middle.
- He started with a big launch discount at $10 a month for a year. Then he moved to $12 a month or $120 a year, and later to $15 a month or $150 a year. He says he still doesn't know if that's right.
- A 25% cyber-monday/holiday discount drove his biggest single day of subscribers. His takeaway is to price a bit higher and discount on occasion.
- He gives students a 50% discount and occasional steep discounts to people who value it but can't afford it.
- He gives free subscriptions to groups like Women in Product, a Black product managers group and Latinx VC.
- About 20% of subscribers expense it, he guesses. Proactive B2B sales did little. Companies and VCs come to him asking for discounts, and VCs sometimes offer it as a portfolio perk.

## Adding a community and a course, and staying simple

- To justify $15 a month, he offered a private community for paid members. He calls it maybe the best thing he did that year. About 2,500 people are in it, helping each other all day.
- He turned the best conversations into their own weekly newsletter. For some people the community is the reason they subscribe.
- He's building a product management course for the next year. He cites Hunter Walk's idea of starting with one SKU, the newsletter, and adding more later.
- He tries not to pile on more work like a podcast, a book or a course. He wants to stay a solo operator and not become a workaholic again.
- He has contractors: a designer, a part-time PM who helps curate, and a friend who runs community events.
- His philosophy: "just keep it really simple," and you end up making more and being happier.
- Platform choice matters less than creating value consistently and enjoying it. He says reaching 1,000 subscribers is a meaningful bar, and below that the platform doesn't matter. He credits Substack for making it effortless to go from a post to a newsletter to paid.
- Substack's fees at scale are "very painful." He's loyal to them for now.
- Spin-offs from the audience: asking questions on Twitter gets great answers, and about 80% of founders he meets as an angel investor know the newsletter, which helps him get into deals.
- He suggests topics that help people make money, aimed at people who have money, such as product management and growth for founders and PMs.
