## Make their goals your goals

Lenny's first influence skill is to connect what you are asking for to what the other person is already trying to achieve. People are more likely to help when they believe the request serves their own goal.

- Spend about 10 seconds before any ask: what are they trying to do, and how does my ask connect to it?
- Their goals can be hitting a metric, making a customer they care about happy, helping their career or performance review, or making their team more efficient.
- Example from the Airbnb Superhost project: the search team pushed back on adding a Superhost badge to search listings, worried it would confuse ranking. Lenny made improving booking conversion one of the project's goals, which was exactly the search team's goal, and they immediately agreed.
- He ties this to empathy: it is the same skill as customer empathy, applied to engineers, designers and other PMs.
- When a solution is handed down with no stated goal, he works out the "why" behind it and frames it around outcomes others care about.
- When two teams' metrics hurt each other, he builds a trade-off heuristic with numbers (for example, how many extra bookings justify some number of cancellations).
- He notes that Superhost ended up moving almost no metric except one version of NPS, yet "it's hard to imagine Airbnb without Superhost". Sometimes you build things everyone agrees are good.

## Charge your trust battery before you need it

Trust is not binary. Lenny uses Toby Lucky's "trust battery" idea: it charges and depletes as you build or lose trust, and the higher it is, the more you can influence someone.

- If you wait until the big ask, it is usually too late. Decide who you will need to influence later and invest in them now.
- Ways to charge it:
  - Do what you say you will do and follow up on promises.
  - Listen, and show you care about their opinion and take their feedback into account.
  - Help them with no return expected.
  - Celebrate contributions they made.
  - Get to know them as a human.
- A practical weekly takeaway: pick one influential person and do one thing this week that charges your battery with them.
- Pick your battles so you do not spend trust on small decisions.
- Cold asks with nothing given first rarely work. Lenny says a thoughtful ask works, such as someone who reviewed his last three podcasts and offered three specific improvements.
- Remote work: the same behaviors apply (follow through, help when needed). He admits he has never been a PM remotely and the micro-interactions of in-person work are harder to replicate.
- For a new PM joining a team: ask everyone what is broken or bothering them and fix it in the first couple of weeks.

## Help them see what you see

Lenny's model is Gandalf, who spent days explaining his reasoning to Frodo instead of ordering him to go. The goal is to walk people through the journey that convinced you.

- He quotes Stephen Covey: "slow is fast and fast is slow". People who are rushed may pretend to be on board and then change their minds later.
- Tactics:
  - Loop people in early.
  - Explain the why behind your asks.
  - Invite them to project kickoffs.
  - Communicate regularly about what is changing and how your thinking evolves.
  - Let them give feedback.
- At project start, decide who needs to be bought in, so you do not discover late that a stakeholder (like marketing) has never heard of it.
- If you forgot to loop someone in, say so: "I should have looped you in at the beginning", then spend an hour talking through how you got here.
- If others do not feel your urgency, tell them the timeline and explain what happens if you miss it and the impact on the company.
- When you lack hard evidence for a new idea, walk people through whatever convinced you, and look for comparisons such as other companies doing it, or research.

## Show success and bring evidence

Two skills that build credibility. People follow those with a track record, and are persuaded by a convincing case.

**Show success**
- Lenny cites Amazon's "be right a lot" principle. Senior PMs get things done efficiently because people have watched them succeed.
- Hit goals consistently, ship successful products, make customers happy, make good decisions, and highlight your team's wins so people notice.
- Example: Airbnb's Instant Book took two and a half years and moved the marketplace from mostly request-to-book to over 80 percent of bookings being instant. It kept getting resources because the team kept moving the metric it promised, and kept showing it was not hurting host churn or cancellation rate, which were the feared downsides.

**Bring evidence**
- Disagreement is often not personal; you simply have not made a convincing case.
- His rough ranking of evidence, strongest first:
  1. Quantifiable numbers.
  2. User research showing patterns.
  3. A specific, vivid story or example.
  4. A smart person's opinion.
- The most effective way to convince someone is to help them see it themselves.

## Leverage authority, and how to say no

Lenny's sixth skill is borrowing authority, as Gandalf's backing made hobbits and warriors trust Frodo.

- Use it sparingly. The more you rely on "the CEO says so", the more your own authority wanes and you become a middleman.
- Authority can be team leads or a few senior people, not only the CEO. Figure out the one key person (like a top engineer or architect) whose buy-in makes others follow.
- On pushing back, he shares a rule from a former manager: "prioritize and communicate".
  - Don't quietly deprioritize an ask without telling anyone.
  - Don't flatly say you can't do it.
  - Put the ask where you think it belongs in your priority list, show your manager the list, and ask whether it should go higher or which of your other items should be pushed off.
- This exposes trade-offs and gives them something actionable instead of a bare "no".

## Be likable, but not at the expense of impact

The seventh skill draws on Robert Cialdini's book Influence: people are more influenced by those they like.

- Ways to be more likable:
  - Say hello and care about people as humans.
  - Smile and look like you are enjoying the work.
  - Give compliments.
  - Show up at events.
  - Bring fun to people's days, such as organizing celebrations.
  - Stay positive.
- He admits effective leaders can be unlikable (his "Gollum" example) but says that route is harder and less fun.
- Caveat: "As a PM you don't want to optimize for being likable. You want to optimize for business impact." Not everyone will like you, and if you are right over the long term, people will like and respect you anyway. He points to Ken Norton's podcast episode on this.
- He agrees that arguing over every small decision makes you less likable and spends trust, so pick your battles.

## PMs have more authority than they think

Lenny pushes back on the idea that PMs have no authority.

- He believes PMs actually have more authority than other functions, and that people underestimate it.
- Even when you do have authority, he says, you should still use influence rather than just directing people. This is why CEOs also benefit from influence skills.
- To learn soft skills, he recommends studying people who are excellent at them. His framework uses Frodo Baggins, who had no formal power but got the most capable people in Middle-earth to follow him.
