## Three sourcing strategies cover every early B2B customer base

Lenny's research (40+ hours across old interviews, founder DMs and warm intros, covering about twenty hyper-growth B2B companies) found that the first ten customers came from only three sources:
1. Tapping your personal network (friends, investors, former colleagues, incubator peers).
2. Seeking out customers where they already spend time (communities, forums, events, door to door).
3. Getting press.

- He contrasts this with consumer apps, where he found seven strategies. In B2B the choices are "easy, yet limited."
- He uses "first ten" because that is when founders are most scrappy and "doing things that don't scale."
- His planned follow-ups look at the first 100 and first 1,000 customers, and at how these companies closed their first ten.

## Most B2B companies combine network and community, not one or the other

- Lenny's point is that almost every B2B business hits up its personal network and also goes to where potential customers spend time. The question is how far your own network takes you before you move on.
- A strong personal network is a big B2B advantage. He says you can build one by bringing on a connector investor or joining an incubator such as YC.
- Network matters even more if you have to "sell" the product (not bottom-up), likely because early customers need a reason to trust you.
- Press is rarely the way to get started.

## Tapping your personal network: the key question and examples

Key question: who in your network could be a potential customer?

- Slack: Stewart Butterfield "begged and cajoled" friends at other companies, getting 6 to 10 companies to start. The pattern was to share with progressively larger groups and amplify feedback at each stage.
- Asana: almost all of the first ~15 users were people the founders were personally close to. They focused on making them happy (features, bugs, performance) before rolling out gradually.
- Salesforce: the first pioneer was Blue Martini Software, a company Marc Benioff had invested in. Everyone on the team was encouraged to contact anyone they knew. The second client came from a product manager running into a former colleague in a grocery line who said their sales process was "ACT! and Excel... It's a mess."
- Carta: early customers came from angel investors' companies and from sister portfolio founders, then "working the network."
- Gusto: the first 10 came from friends, mostly YC batch startups plus non-tech small businesses (such as a children's swimming camp) known through family. The three founders told everyone they knew what they were building and asked for referrals.

## Network tactics: Stripe's "Collison installation," Looker's evangelists, Intercom's manual outreach

- Stripe: Lenny quotes Paul Graham's "Collison installation." Rather than "Will you try our beta?... we'll send you a link," the Collisons said "Right then, give me your laptop" and set people up on the spot. Friends then asked to invite their friends, which spread by word of mouth.
- Looker: got 10+ customers via networks, including customers' and investors' networks. Two tactics: spend endless energy making early customers successful, and set up simple programs so they could evangelize.
- Intercom: Des Traynor said they reached out first to people from earlier businesses who would listen. He emailed people all day by hand, showing what Intercom might look like for them and gathering feedback, and said he would still do it by hand.
- Okta: used contacts from Salesforce and past jobs, alma mater networks on LinkedIn, and angel investors' LinkedIn networks. One founder targeted 15 to 18 new IT contacts a month for six months.
- Amplitude: early customers came from the YC network, other companies in the cohort that had retention questions existing tools couldn't answer.
- Workday: early customers were midsize-company CEOs who knew the founders. They "bought the friendship, not the software."
- Lesson from Coda: its first alpha customer, a startup run by a former YouTube colleague, churned entirely in one day. The feedback and a long list of fixes produced a much better product: "the customers you lose may be the source of your best feedback."

## Seeking customers where they already are

Key question: who are your target customers, and where do they spend time, online or offline (communities, forums, offline events)?

- Shopify: Tobi Lütke's colleagues in the Ruby on Rails community asked to license the code from his snowboard store, which led to Shopify. A waitlist of a few thousand emails (2006) fed a beta of a few hundred stores.
- New Relic: targeted the small but fast-growing Rails market, which was not too big to attract well-funded competition. Endorsement from community leaders "can't be bought; it needs to be earned."
- Plaid: grew mostly by word of mouth in developer and PM communities, with time spent on forums, IRC, meetups and startup accelerators, meeting fintech founders to learn their problems. The founders were their own first customer.
- Atlassian: open source communities (mailing lists, newsgroups, IRC), constant marketing, great customer service, and "uneconomic prices."

## Where to find early users online: Twitter and Hacker News

- Figma: Dylan Field talked to practically every designer he knew, then used Twitter data to find the most influential designers. He filtered that list to people personally inspirational to him, cold emailed or found intros, and showed them Figma. He kept them in the loop as the product evolved, and many amplified the launch. Coda (then Krypton) was the first team to adopt Figma.
- Segment: launched as an open-source library on Hacker News and blew up overnight. First customers were small companies and founders there who wanted better analytics instrumentation.
- Airtable: friends and family first, then the beta was posted on Hacker News. After that, word of mouth was by far the biggest mechanism.
- Dropbox: Drew Houston posted a demo video on Hacker News in April 2007 ("My YC app: Dropbox – Throw away your USB drive"), which brought the first users.

## Go where customers are, even physically (Square), and use press carefully

- Square: friends and "Clubhouse"-type users from the network did not create repeat usage or become real customers. Local merchants the team could walk to (a flower shop, a coffee shop) were the first everyday-use customers. Visiting them multiple times a week let the team see what worked and refine the product.
- Press (key question: what is a unique, untold story you can pitch?):
  - Canva: high-profile investors led to press around its "stealth startup" and a 50k-person waitlist.
  - Twilio: launched on TechCrunch in private beta in November 2008, after Dave McClure rickrolled TechCrunch's Michael Arrington with the product.
  - Slack: a press blitz based on the team's prior experience brought 8,000 invitation requests on day one and 15,000 two weeks later. Lesson: "Don't underestimate the power of traditional media when you launch."
- Lenny still says press is rarely how companies get started.
