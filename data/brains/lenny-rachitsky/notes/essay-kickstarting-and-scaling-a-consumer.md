## Most consumer startup ideas come from paying attention, not from sitting down to ideate

Lenny studied how 50+ of the biggest consumer businesses (TikTok, Reddit, Uber, Airbnb and others) came up with their idea. He says "ideas don't matter" is wrong and quotes Sam Altman: "I myself used to believe ideas didn't matter that much, but I'm very sure that's wrong now."

- He finds five common strategies, all "rooted in paying attention":
  - Your own problems: ~30%
  - Your curiosity: ~20%
  - What's already working: ~18%
  - Paradigm shifts: ~15%
  - Brainstorming with friends: ~15%
- Fewer than 10 of the 50+ founders actively brainstormed ideas. Most ideas arrived organically.
- He says this doesn't mean you shouldn't spend time ideating. It just isn't where most of the biggest consumer ideas came from.

## Surprising facts about the founders behind big consumer businesses

From the same dataset of ~50 companies, Lenny reports:

- Half of the founders were under 30 when they started, and 80% were under 35.
- Fewer than a third of the ideas came from founders solving their own problem. He expected the majority.
- Fewer than a third were actively ideating when the big idea hit.
- Only 18% of winning ideas came from pivots. He expected this to be much higher.
- Fewer than a third of founders had a unique or specific skill that enabled the build.
- Just over half of the ideas felt trivial at the time (Twitter, Snap, Pinterest, Coinbase, Tinder, Calm, Airbnb).
- Over 75% had 2+ founders, 68% had an engineering co-founder, 42% had previously started a company, and 11% had a co-founder with an MBA.
- Only one company, DoorDash, found its idea by actively talking to potential customers.

## Strategy 1: Solve your own problem (~30%)

The largest group of companies began with founders fixing something that annoyed them, then realizing others wanted it too.

- Examples:
  - Dropbox: Drew Houston kept forgetting his thumb drive.
  - Airbnb: Gebbia and Chesky needed rent money and rented out an air bed during a design conference.
  - Uber: Garrett Camp couldn't reliably get a cab in San Francisco.
  - Hipcamp: Alyssa Ravasio found campsites hard to research and book.
  - GOAT: Daishin Sugano bought fake Air Jordans on eBay.
  - Warby Parker: the founders kept losing expensive glasses.
  - Calm: Michael Acton Smith used meditation to cope with his own pain.
- He quotes Paul Graham: "Pay particular attention to things that chafe you."
- His advice:
  - Build self-awareness about your day-to-day challenges.
  - Pay attention to problems that drive you to action.
  - Don't take your ideas for granted. If you find it valuable, others likely will too.

## Strategy 2: Follow your curiosity and tinker (~20%)

About 20% of the businesses came from founders following their intuition and quickly building a prototype to see if there was a "there" there.

- Examples:
  - Coinbase: Brian Armstrong read the Bitcoin white paper and built a wallet.
  - Google: Page and Brin found the web interesting for its mathematical graph structure and built a crawler.
  - OpenSea: shipped a beta within a couple of months of the ERC721 standard.
  - Musical.ly: Alex Zhu saw teens combining video and music on a train and built a prototype.
  - Twitter: built a prototype in two weeks.
  - Tinder: prototyped at a hackathon.
  - Duolingo: began as an academic project.
- He quotes Paul Graham: working on projects that seem cool leads you to build things that are missing.
- His advice:
  - Give yourself time to tinker.
  - Build a prototype rather than just thinking.
  - Keep it simple and test as cheaply and quickly as you can. Your first idea is unlikely to be exactly right.

## Strategy 3: Double down on what's already working (aka pivot)

Just over 15% of the companies launched something, noticed which element unexpectedly worked best, and focused on it.

- Examples:
  - Instagram: Burbn's users bounced off the check-in app but kept posting filtered square photos. Kevin Systrom's team cut everything but photos, and got 25,000 users on the first day of the new version.
  - YouTube: began as a video dating site.
  - Lyft: grew out of Zimride's long-distance ridesharing.
  - Pinterest: Tote users saved collections of items rather than buying.
  - Discord: the best part of a game was its voice and text chat.
  - PayPal: email payments started as an add-on "user acquisition engine".
  - Twitch: grew out of Justin.tv.
- Lenny says the best part of this path is that it should motivate you to launch whatever excites you most now, because even a failed idea may lead to the bigger one.
- He quotes Elon Musk on PayPal: when two paths aren't obviously different, "just pick a path and do it rather than just vacillate endlessly."
- His advice:
  - Kill your darlings. If not enough other people love your idea, it won't work.
  - Don't take for granted any part of your product people value, because that is a rare find.
  - Try lots of things. The more ideas you put out, the more likely one sticks.

## Strategies 4 and 5: Work backward from paradigm shifts, or brainstorm with friends

**Paradigm shifts (~15%):** Founders noticed a shift in where the world was going and built for it. Lenny quotes Mike Maples Jr. ("Start from the future and work backwards") and Paul Graham ("Live in the future, then build what's missing").

- Examples:
  - Amazon: Bezos saw web usage growing 2,300% a year and chose books as the first product.
  - Spotify: Daniel Ek saw that only a better-than-piracy service would work.
  - 23andMe: genetic data had suddenly become cheap.
  - Instacart: on-demand rides and delivery were emerging.
  - Stitch Fix: retail was moving online.
- Elad Gil, quoted approvingly: the proof is in the growth rate or extrapolated technology curve, not "the number sitting in front of you."
- His prompts:
  - What will be very different in 5, 10 or 20 years?
  - What hasn't software eaten? Look for a big market with a bad customer experience.
  - What would be amazing if it were possible? Start from the ideal experience and work out what it would take.

**Brainstorming with friends (~15%):** 8 of the 50 companies found their idea this way.

- Examples:
  - Thumbtack: brainstormed weekly for a year. Its first idea, a financial aggregator, was beaten by Mint.
  - Netflix: Marc Randolph pitched ideas like personalized surfboards and custom dog food first.
  - Yelp, Reddit, Nextdoor (a standing 10 a.m. meeting for "billion-dollar ideas"), DoorDash, Bumble and Eventbrite.
- Lenny points to Michael Seibel's advice on this path.
- From Randolph's account: "for every good idea, there are a thousand bad ones."

## What makes a good idea

Lenny says no one can truly know whether an idea will work, but the more of these elements an idea has, the likelier it is to succeed:

1. **You can't stop thinking about it.** Startups only really fail when the founder gives up, and passion makes that less likely. He quotes Sam Altman: founders often "wish they had waited to start a startup until they came up with an idea they really loved."
2. **You start building a prototype immediately and have the skills to do it.** Most companies came from a scrappy experiment within hours, days or weeks. This is likely why over 80% had an engineering co-founder (elsewhere in the piece he cites 68%).
3. **You have unique insight into the opportunity.** This usually comes from prior experience. It is why the best ideas often sound crazy, trivial or impossible to others. User research rarely drove early ideation; ideas came from experience, vision and gut instinct.
4. **The idea is easy to understand.** Every product he looked at can be explained in a sentence or two.

- The right founder (obsessed, with unique insight and the right skills) plus market timing creates the opportunity for massive consumer businesses.
- This is also why he says you shouldn't worry much about someone stealing your idea. The chance that someone else has that combination at the right time is very low.

## What to do once you have an idea

Lenny's advice, quoting Walt Disney ("quit talking and begin doing"):

1. **Prototype cheaply and quickly to validate.**
   - Stitch Fix's Katrina Lake used SurveyMonkey and toted garments to customers' homes for a $20 styling fee.
   - Netflix mailed a used music CD to test DVD-by-mail overnight.
   - DoorDash built a static HTML page with a Google Voice number and PDF menus, offered $6 delivery, ran a small AdWords campaign, and got its first order within hours.
   - Rec Room built its first game in 90 days.
   - He points to Todd Jackson's post on validating startup ideas.
2. **Talk to potential customers to refine the idea.**
   - Rent the Runway's founders cold-emailed guesses of Diane von Furstenberg's address and got a meeting the next day.
   - Rec Room set up in its WeWork lobby for testers.
   - Discord's founders jumped into voice chat with anyone who showed up.
3. **Figure out who you're building for.** This is the focus of the next post in the series (Step 2: a "super-specific who").

The series he is launching has six steps: Insight, Audience, Hook, Reach, Retain, Scale.
