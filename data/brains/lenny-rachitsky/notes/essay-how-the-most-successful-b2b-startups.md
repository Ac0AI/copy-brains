## Idea sources: past pain, ponder and probe, present pull

Lenny's research on how two dozen of the biggest B2B companies found their original idea (based on dozens of 1:1 founder interviews) found three reliable paths: past pain, ponder and probe, and present pull. In all three, founders look for two signals: pain (an important problem exists) and pull (people actually want your solution). Several of his findings are counterintuitive:
- The majority of founders had no special skill or background in the problem space.
- Only about 40% of ideas came from past pain at a previous job, which he did not expect.
- Every prosumer product (Notion, Figma, Airtable, Miro, Slack, Coda) took two to four years of wandering before something worked.
- Founders spoke to a median of 30 potential customers before committing.
- About 40% of startups pivoted at least once before landing on the winning idea, often more than once.
- About 20% were solo founders.
- Cold outbound was the second most common way to get early customers.

## The three ingredients of a great B2B idea

Lenny says a great idea combines three things:
1. **The problem is important.** Many people must be willing to spend a lot to solve it. Most startups fail because the market is too small, not because the idea is bad. His rule of thumb for venture scale: a clear path to $100M a year in revenue and eventually $1B a year. Example signal: Robinhood agreed to a large contract with Sprig before it was built and installed the first version as it was being made. He points to Hunter Walk's LUV framework and Sequoia's market-curve guide for sizing.
2. **The market is underserved.** He cites Kunal Shah's (CRED) Delta-4 test: ask customers to rate the existing solution 1 to 10, then rate yours. Your solution must be at least 4 points better for anyone to care. If the incumbent scores 6 or higher (e.g. Excel), it is very hard to replace. Strong signal from Gusto: when asked about their payroll provider, over half of people "started cursing, unprompted."
3. **You are excited about it.** You may spend years on it. He endorses Dylan Field's view that many good ideas exist, so pick one you care about, because even if it fails you will have learned from it. He also quotes Paul Graham: "When in doubt, optimize for interestingness."

## Path 1: Past pain (about 40% of great ideas)

Identify a large pain you experienced at a previous company, then build the solution. Examples he gives:
- **Gong:** co-founder Amit ran Sisense and found that why deals were lost lived in salespeople's heads; the CRM only showed outcomes, not reasons.
- **Retool:** David Hsu kept rebuilding internal tools at each company and realized they share the same building blocks.
- **Linear:** founders at Coinbase, Airbnb and Uber felt "a nagging feeling of disappointment" when their companies defaulted to Jira.
- **Persona:** Rick Song worked on identity at Square, where the team joked someone would build this well in 10 years. He saw vendors assume one technique would work, while every business needs a multi-modal approach.
- **Hex:** Barry McCardel started as a buyer, asked around for a tool, heard "No, but if you do, let us know," and eventually concluded no one else would build it.

Reflection questions he poses:
- What did you or others build at past jobs that proved very valuable to teammates?
- What tool did you wish you had and would have paid a lot for?
- What internal product have you built again and again?

Appendix examples include Snyk (security shifting left with DevOps), Canva, Stytch (a "Stripe for authentication" after Auth0 and Cognito pain at Plaid), Snowflake, and Ramp.

## Path 2: Ponder and probe

Pick a space you are interested in, whiteboard and tinker, and talk to many potential customers looking for pain and pull. Lenny notes a wrong and right way to whiteboard: ideating in a silo is wrong; quickly validating with potential users is right. Focus on a space that is important, underserved, and exciting, ideally rooted in your experience (though that is not required).

Examples:
- **Figma:** after the Thiel Fellowship ($100,000 over two years), Dylan Field tested drones, then computational photography, then settled on design with WebGL. Field's "why now?" lens: a societal, regulatory or technological change that opens new possibilities.
- **Vanta:** Christina Cacioppo picked two spaces (team collaboration and security), banned herself from shiny objects, and learned the whiteboard trap: "if you can do it on a whiteboard, someone has probably done it. There's no $20 bills on the sidewalk." Startups she asked said they knew they should do security but didn't until customers asked, so she did it for them manually, which became Vanta.
- **Notion:** began as a no-code website builder that "didn't go anywhere for years." Four years in (early 2017) it launched Notion 1.0 focused on docs and wikis, with templates users could click into, which became the wedge.
- **Zip:** the sixth or seventh idea (earlier ones included fractional US equities for India and an accounting staffing platform that made $200k). Their YC partner advised looking for a large market with entrenched, mediocre incumbents, which led to procurement (valued over $1B).
- **Databricks:** Berkeley researchers saw Silicon Valley companies using what became Apache Spark for AI on data; open-sourcing it did not get enterprise uptake, so around 2012 they started a company.
- Others in the appendix: Coda (a 20-year-old idea revived with a friend), Gusto (whiteboarding small-business problems, then payroll), and Census.

Census's Boris Jabes learned that sitting in a room generating startup ideas "is really bad" because the ideas were not tied to a specific user pain; writing code forced them to talk to people.

Reflection questions: What emerging trend is underserved? What new technology is underused? How many customers have you spoken to? Rate problem importance 1 to 10, and how underserved the market is 1 to 10, looking for a delta of at least 4.

## Path 3: Present pull

Keep building, but watch for a feature or side project users pull on strongly, then pivot fully to it. Examples:
- **Amplitude:** built an internal analytics tool for a voice-texting app (Sonalight), and other founders at Y Combinator wanted it.
- **Sprig:** Ryan Glasgow built a small SDK on an Airtable backend rotating three survey questions. Founders he shared it with said they'd be his first customer, and he quickly pivoted to surveys.
- **Loom:** two failed products (an expert-feedback marketplace, four months; an embeddable user-test tool, three months). Then a client recorded a video summarizing the user tests, a Product Hunt launch a month later drew thousands of extension downloads by day's end.
- **Segment:** about 15 months of failed analytics products. A growth-hack library, Analytics.js, got attention; with about six months of runway left, they built it for a week and launched on Hacker News, where it hit the top.
- **Slack:** after shutting down the Glitch game (45 people, $17.2M raised), the team's internal IRC-based communication, with added archiving, search and integrations, became the product.

Reflection questions: Which feature of what you've built has the most pull? What problem in your own startup do you wish someone would solve? What side project have you wanted to build?

## Summary checklist

Lenny's recap: look for ideas that are important, underserved, and exciting to you; choose one of the three routes; and you know you're onto something when you notice both pull and pain. The next step after the idea is validating it.
