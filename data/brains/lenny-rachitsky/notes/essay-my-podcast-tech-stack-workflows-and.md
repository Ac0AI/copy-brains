## Start with 100 people who love it, then widen

Lenny's founding podcast principle: instead of making something many people like, make something 100 people love. He says it is the same advice as starting a company.
- 100 sounds tiny, but most podcasts can't reach it. A small focus lets you give those people exactly what they want.
- Once you have that foothold, fans tell friends, better guests say yes, and you improve at the craft.
- "Love" matters because you must displace another show in someone's rotation, and they only do that for a show they keep loving.
- For his own show, he knew at least 100 people would want a product management podcast, so he made it the best one for them. Then he expanded into sales, storytelling, design, fear and productivity, and the audience grew with him.

## Aim for B+ at the start, and don't pre-record too much

Lenny says a podcast boils down to one job: create a great audio file, meaning A+ content and A+ audio quality. He does not think you need to start at A+.
- What got him over the hump was low pressure: basic equipment, audio only (YouTube came later), and telling early guests it was an experiment to scrap if it went poorly.
- His advice is to aim for B+ and work up. Many famous tech podcasts had weak first episodes.
- He pre-recorded about 10 episodes before launch and calls that a mistake: after launch you learn what resonates and spot audio, video and speaking issues, but the first batch can't be improved. He recommends recording only three to five ahead.

## Interviewing tactics

Lenny's interview tips (he says he has much left to learn):
- **Be the listener's home base.** Mirror back what the guest says, ask the questions the listener has in mind, and reframe when the conversation gets complicated or tangential. Don't interrupt, because "some of the best stuff comes at the end."
- **Get to the gold quickly.** Spend little time on backgrounds and intros unless the guest is famous or wildly fascinating. Listeners want to learn something.
- **Look for "dropped facts."** Guests mention something interesting offhand and move on. Come back to it; they often want to talk about it. He learned this from a book on interviewing.
- **Prepare.** He spends one to two hours per guest (more for famous ones): reading their posts and social media, listening to past interviews, exploring LinkedIn.
- **Just ask the next question.** Skip "So interesting!" and "Thank you for sharing" unless you have something to add. He points to Tyler Cowen as maybe the best interviewer.
- **Go off-script.** Follow unexpected threads; guests don't mind, and you can cut it in post.
- **Keep your ego in check.** "Don't try to sound smart. Make your guests sound smart."
- **Be yourself.** Early on he imitated hosts he admired; things improved when he stopped.

He sends every guest these questions beforehand:
1. Are there 2-3 topics you'd especially love to talk about?
2. Any frameworks, methods or processes you've found especially useful that others may find useful too?
3. Anything you have a contrarian opinion about that would be fun to share?
4. Any fun, surprising or wild stories from your career or life?
5. Anything you haven't shared elsewhere that could make the conversation extra-special?
6. If this were the one episode you'd send anyone who wants to learn how you think and operate, what would you want in it?

## How he finds and books guests

- **Work backward from a wishlist** of dream guests and slowly find a connection to each, the same way the best products are built from the ideal experience. Current wishlist names include Satya Nadella, Tony Fadell, Jony Ive, Gwynne Shotwell and Nikita Bier.
- **Mix famous and under-the-radar guests.** People won't listen to names they don't recognize, but known people are already everywhere. The mix brings listeners in while surfacing wisdom-rich unknowns. He also makes an effort to include a more diverse mix of guests than the typical tech podcast.
- **Most people say yes**, often to a simple cold DM or email, though not the most high-profile on day one.
- **Use your unfair advantage.** His is seven years at Airbnb plus newsletter collaborators.
- **Say no to most inbound requests.** He gets one to three a day, many from PR people. Some became great episodes, but it is the least useful channel.
- **Keep trying.** Brian Chesky took a year to agree and is now the most listened-to episode.

How his most popular guests came on:
- Brian Chesky: knew him from Airbnb, floated it around big launches, agreed a year later.
- Shreyas Doshi and Marty Cagan: wishlist from day one, approached before the podcast was live (Shreyas pitched as first guest alongside Julie Zhuo; Marty by cold email).
- Elizabeth Stone: a friend's introduction, before she became CTO of Netflix.
- Gustaf Alströmer: former Airbnb colleague, then YC Partner, via email.
- Nikhyl Singhal: suggested by past guests; introduction from one of them.
- Matt Mochary: cold DM.
- Ethan Smith: several founders credited his SEO work; asked for an intro.
- Ethan Evans and Geoff Charles: collaborated on popular newsletter posts first.
- Marily Nika: Maven reached out to pitch her.
- Claire Hughes Johnson: replied "Come on the podcast?" to her tweet about her book; her people followed up. He says "This method works really well."

## Tech stack and studio setup

Software:
- Recording: Riverside (what most podcasters use); Podcastle as backup; Descript for solo portions like ads and intros.
- Editing: Descript (his editor uses it too).
- Hosting: Substack, which makes sense if you run a significant Substack newsletter. Alternatives: Spotify for Podcasters (free, limited analytics), Transistor, Simplecast.
- Analytics: Podstatus for charts, Chartable for general analytics, plus the host's built-in data. He says podcast analytics "stinks."
- Website: Podpage (behind LennysPodcast.com).

Starter hardware: Shure MV7 USB mic, Sony MDR7506 headphones, Opal camera (he is an investor), Lume Cube Edge light, and a portable tripod as mic holder (he suggests trying a few options).

Six months in, he took the Dream Studio course and upgraded: Canon EOS M50 Mark II with a 22mm f2 lens, a three-light setup (key light with soft box, hair light, side light), a teleprompter rig so he looks into the camera, quiet Logitech keyboard and mouse, and an Elgato deck for muting and DND. His favorite lesson: the more work your studio seems to have taken, the more professional it looks. For the room, he cites Descript's advice: pick a smaller room with soft surfaces (carpet, curtains, upholstery) and avoid large empty spaces and hard surfaces that echo.

## Outsourced help and per-episode workflow

His team:
- Pen Name for core production (editing, titles, thumbnails, show notes).
- Supermix for Instagram, TikTok and Shorts clips and trailers.
- An EA from Double for guest scheduling.
- A newer researcher for famous guests.
- Earlier, an audio engineer, Tommy Harron, when it was audio-only.

What the help saves:
- Editing used to take hours; now 10 to 30 minutes to review the producer's work.
- Titles, thumbnails and show notes used to take 1-2 hours; now 10 to 20 minutes to approve, often after many title and thumbnail iterations.
- Guest prep: a week before, guests get detailed instructions (no AirPods, avoid bright windows, turn on DND), an offer of a free mic to keep, and an optional audio/video setup call.
- Guests review the final audio, video, title, description, show notes, thumbnail and shareable assets before publishing.
- Organization: Notion production calendar, Coda for questions, Slack, Google Calendar.

Time per episode is about 4 to 5 hours total:
- Schedule with guest: 30 minutes.
- Prep questions: 1-2 hours, with a draft agenda sent to the guest 3-7 days ahead.
- Record interview: 1.5-2 hours in Riverside.
- Record guest intro: 15 minutes. Record ads: 15 minutes each.
- Ship and promote (including X and LinkedIn posts): 10-30 minutes.

## YouTube as the discovery channel

Lenny says YouTube is a must eventually: "There's no better way for new people to discover your podcast than through YouTube's algorithms."
- Over half of his views come from YouTube sending new people his way; he cites 39.5% + 15.1% + 7.8% = 62% from YouTube's discovery features.
- He started about six months in, at his producer's urging, and wishes he'd started earlier, though he says not to let that stop you from starting; video can be added later.
- Video means more work, especially editing, but it enables TikTok, Instagram and Shorts clips (he mentions Opus.pro for automating clips) and posts on X and LinkedIn, plus diversification across platforms.
- Results at the time: over 10M downloads, 3M YouTube views, a top 10 global technology podcast, and a 5.0 average rating across nearly 4,000 reviews.
