## How Lenny decides what to write about

- Lenny never planned to be a writer. After leaving Airbnb in March 2019 (about seven years there, company sold to Airbnb in 2012), his plans A through D were: start a company, consult or advise, join an early-stage startup, or join a big company as a PM. A newsletter was not on the list.
- He started by writing down what he learned at Airbnb, turned it into a Medium post that did well, then moved to Substack and tried a weekly cadence.
- Two pieces of advice from friends shaped the pivot: spend more time on what gives you energy and less on what saps it, and don't take for granted a thing that is working for people. Writing gave him energy and startup exploration did not.
- He draws topics from four sources:
  - things he wants to get out of his head
  - reader questions (an advice-column format "gives me a huge backlog," since each question is a problem at least one person has)
  - his own curiosity ("a good excuse to learn it")
  - what resonates on Twitter (a tweet thread on performance reviews got more attention than expected, so he wrote a deeper dive)
- He warns against writing only what others ask for, because "then you just end up with a job that you don't like again." The more interested he is, the better the piece gets.
- He deliberately keeps a broad scope instead of the classic advice to pick one niche. Because the audience is a mix, he rotates growth, product, startup/business and leadership topics. If a reader sees nothing useful for about three weeks, "you just turn."
- His test for a niche is Brian Armstrong's point about Coinbase: few people combine engineering, economics and cryptography. Find the combination of things you are good at that few others share, as long as enough people care.
- A flop on a personal-interest topic is fine. His Buddhism-and-product-management post did not resonate, but there was no wave of unsubscribes. He says people give you "two or three chances in a row," and he looks for a consistent problem, not a one-off.

## Why quality is the whole strategy

- Lenny argues there is a huge amount of free content, so you only stand out by being much better, not a little better: "oh wow, this is so good." Otherwise "you'll just get ignored."
- He is continually surprised that basic topics are poorly covered. Examples are how to start a consumer app, get your first customers, launch a B2B business, get into PM, and PM templates. His explanation is that most people have little time to write, so "if you have time to write, you're going to stand out."
- Time invested correlates directly with how well a post does. His 8-part marketplace series took at least 100 hours over a few months. It was his most popular post and an early source of growth.
- His bar is "is this actually going to be useful for somebody?" A PM should be able to read it and use it on their team. He avoids pontificating and fuzzy high-level ideas, and favors concrete, actionable steps and examples.
- Formats that do well: pieces that answer questions he hears repeatedly, and visual frameworks (for example, how B2B companies kickstart).
- He wants honesty over sounding smart. He asks whether he has seen this work in practice, and pushes founders on whether something actually had impact or just sounds like a PR pitch. For a retention post he was after "concrete stats and numbers and real things that worked."

## Research process for deep-dive posts

1. Start with a single question to answer (for example, how do you start a marketplace business). It came from people asking how Airbnb did it, when one company's experience may not generalize.
2. List the companies he'd like to include, then find a person at each and get a warm introduction.
3. Ask them all the same question by email. He skips Zoom to keep the time he asks for small.
4. Paste every answer into one big doc and stare at it until a pattern emerges: steps one, two, three, or a few distinct approaches. A story and framework take shape from that.
5. Visualize it and write it so readers aren't overwhelmed. Keep it structured, simple and focused.
6. Keep pushing for access. He says people are more open than expected, as long as they can review their section before publishing.
- He says roughly 80% of his writing is him learning the topic, and the deep-dive posts teach him the most.

## Editing and finishing a piece

- He reads each piece 10 to 20 times, on both his computer and his phone. A different device "tricks your brain into seeing things again for the first time," and he is always surprised how many typos he missed.
- He knows it's ready when each pass produces only a couple of changes and he stops finding typos or reasons to move things around.
- He keeps a book on writing well next to his chair and opens it at random as a reminder to cut: remove words, adjectives and adverbs, make it shorter.
- He keeps a short list of high-level rules, such as: is it interesting, is there a story to add, is there something to cut.
- He has no editor or review group, and wishes he had a group to run posts by. After publishing, reader comments often lead him to add a point or cover it in a follow-up.

## Distribution: word of mouth, Twitter and repurposing

- He spends very little time on distribution. About 90% of growth has been word of mouth, because better writing spreads. He suggests that if you are spending a lot of time on growth, "something's off about the content" or you lack any distribution channel.
- Twitter is his main channel. He built a following by posting nuggets of value, either from his Medium posts or things he didn't turn into posts. When he launched the newsletter he tweeted it, and that brought his first 500 subscribers. His lesson: find places to add value, build an audience there, then direct it to your newsletter.
- At launch he also seeded it by telling people with large, respected followings the day he'd launch and asking them to share it if they liked it. He says this worked well, and the sharing came because they found it valuable, not because he pushed.
- On cold outreach, messaging people to read your work is fine, but be wary of writing only what you think people want.
- He starts with the long post, then tweets it with an independent nugget (a story, an image, a short thread). Only about 10% of the time does a tweet become a post. Twitter's constraint forces a simple, consumable thought, and threads give people a reason to share and save.
- Give away the best material up front, especially on Twitter. Don't build to a reveal, because "nobody's going to read it." Post the most useful parts in the tweet, and readers then click through and subscribe.
- He has experimented with Twitter ads and with discounts for groups, but they are minor.

## Paid newsletter, expensing and community

- He ran a free weekly newsletter for nine months before going paid. That gave him confidence he had plenty to write about, and a big free base at launch. He says there is no shortcut to that confidence.
- The paid launch price was $15 a month. Because that felt like a lot for writing alone, he added an invite to a private community as an extra reason to subscribe.
- He moved paid mainly because COVID hit and he needed income. In hindsight he thinks he waited longer than he should have, but also that launching with a large free base was valuable, so not too early either.
- His emails encourage readers to expense the subscription, but he straddles the line so people who can't expense it don't feel worse about subscribing. He doesn't pitch team or bulk plans, and notes Ben Thompson has the same pattern where one person subscribes and shares internally.
- Community: he picked Slack and invited 10 to 20 people at first. It grew to about 600. His philosophy is to bring awesome people together and hope good things happen. He spends little time moderating and it is largely self-sufficient.

## Cadence, sustainability and platform choice

- For anyone nervous about committing to a list, he suggests framing it as an experiment: "I'm going to try a weekly newsletter, we'll see how it goes," and run it for about three months. Nobody remembers when your last email arrived. The pressure gets real once people pay for yearly plans.
- Early on he answered three reader questions per week to pack in value, but he learned it isn't sustainable. He now sticks to one topic per post and saves the rest for later.
- He doubts daily newsletters, noting he can barely do a really good weekly one, and says people "don't really want more email," so weekly is often a good cadence.
- On Substack, he values that he doesn't have to think about anything but writing. His custom domain means he could move with the same URLs and keep SEO. Substack takes 10%, which at scale is a lot of money, so the question is whether the services it adds justify it. He says he wouldn't be where he is without it, and he'd consider alternatives like ConvertKit mainly if selling courses or books.
- He advises that if you're not worried about SEO, staying on Substack to build an email list is easy and worthwhile.
