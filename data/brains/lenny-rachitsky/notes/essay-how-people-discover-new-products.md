## Seven channels people use to discover new products

Lenny posits that people become aware of new products in exactly seven ways:

1. Friends or colleagues (e.g., an invite)
2. Browsing online, organically (e.g., SEO)
3. Browsing online and seeing a promotion (e.g., Facebook ads)
4. Out and about, organically (e.g., a store shelf)
5. Out and about and seeing a promotion (e.g., a billboard)
6. At home, seeing a promotion (e.g., direct mail)
7. Someone reaching out to you (e.g., sales)

He frames the list as a way to develop a growth strategy beyond the obvious channels like Facebook ads and SEO.

## Word of mouth from friends and colleagues

- Lenny calls this "the oldest growth channel in the book." It can be free and organic ("I LOVE my new spatula, check it out!") or incentivized through a referral program or even an MLM program.
- He says it is "by far the cheapest and best way to grow if you can pull it off."
- Invest in it if the product is innately better with friends or colleagues (Snapchat, Slack), is innately fun to share (travel photos, homes for sale, crime nearby), or is remarkable, meaning "something worth remarking about."
- He warns that most products do not grow this way and that it is "nearly impossible to layer on" if the product isn't naturally suited to it, so founders shouldn't be sad if it doesn't work.

## Organic discovery while browsing online

- The next cheapest way to be discovered: people on the internet talking about the product organically. This includes social posts, press, influencers sharing unprompted, forum comments, Wirecutter articles and organic search results.
- Products that grew primarily this way: Clubhouse (people on Twitter), e.l.f. Cosmetics (TikTok influencers raving about it), Zillow (press articles), Animal Crossing (people posting on Facebook).
- Bet on organic traffic if:
  - The product is remarkable and people are naturally excited to share it (e.g., Instapot).
  - People are already searching for the product category, such as Googling "best CRM" or "best credit card."
  - The product or company makes a good story that reporters would want to cover.

## Paid promotion while browsing online

- Covers ads (Facebook, Google, Twitter, YouTube, Instagram), paid influencers, affiliate programs and channel partners.
- Companies that grew primarily through this channel: Wish, Casper, Calm, Blue Apron, Hims.
- Most effective when:
  - Customers aren't naturally looking for the product (e.g., Impact Gum), so you have to go to them.
  - The product generates revenue directly from new users (a purchase or subscription), which can fund more ads (e.g., jeans).

## Offline discovery: shelves, products in the wild, and out-of-home ads

**Organic, out and about**
- People stumble on products in the world: a new cereal on the shelf, a Tesla driving by, a Peloton in a friend's home.
- Most CPG products grow this way via shelf placement, as do retailers (foot traffic) and some consumer electronics (the white iPod headphones).
- Works for physical products that look great, are prevalent, and are impulse purchases.

**Paid out-of-home (OOH)**
- Billboards, bus ads, taxi ads, backs of trucks, flyers: you pay to reach people who spend time around a location.
- ROI is traditionally hard to measure, but done well it can "build a massive brand moat" (Nike, Apple, Coke).
- It is also a way to target a specific audience in a specific place. Example: Brex used billboards and bus stops in SF to reach founders.
- Best for companies that need to build strong brands, sell to a broad mainstream audience, or want to make a splash.

## In-home promotions: TV, mail, podcasts and newsletters

- Goes well beyond TV: direct mail, ads on packaging, flyers inside packaging, radio, podcast ads, newspaper and magazine ads.
- Direct mail has become a staple of DTC growth strategies, and podcast and newsletter ads are an increasingly popular way to reach new customers.
- Best suited to:
  - Subscription companies or high average order value (AOV) products that can sustain the cost of this advertising.
  - Companies that get a lot of value from geo-targeting.
  - Companies that can be creative with the platforms (e.g., Geico).
  - DTC brands.

## Outbound sales

- Someone comes to you to pitch a product, by phone, email, DM, LinkedIn message or door-to-door.
- Large enterprises grow mainly this way (Oracle, Workday and ADP each have outbound teams reaching out daily), as do telemarketing- and door-to-door-driven businesses like Cutco and many insurance companies.
- Best suited to products that:
  - Have high AOV and high LTV, to support the cost of a sales team.
  - Require hand-holding to be successful.
  - Are just getting started and looking for initial customers.
  - Began with a bottom-up sales motion and are ready to layer on sales.
- For founders doing sales, he highly recommends the book Founding Sales.

## Five-step process for choosing where to invest

Lenny suggests working through these steps to pick a channel:

1. **Who are your early adopters?** e.g., middle-aged men in the Midwest, new dog owners in cities, HR leaders at growth-stage companies.
2. **Where do they spend time?** A community, newsletter, event or physical location.
3. **What's the quickest way to get in front of them?** e.g., a mailer, a LinkedIn DM, a billboard, Facebook ads.
4. **What's the cheapest way to get in front of them?** e.g., shelf placement, encouraging existing users to send invites, micro-influencers.
5. **How can you stand out?** What's remarkable about the product, story or vision? What big problem do you solve? What's most surprising about the offering?
