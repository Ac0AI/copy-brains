## Lenny's path from Airbnb to writing full-time

- After leaving Airbnb in 2019, Lenny gave himself 6 months to tinker. His rule: "do more of what gave me energy, and do less of what sapped me of energy." After every call, project and meeting he noted whether he felt more or less energized.
- His plans were A) start a company, B) advise full-time, C) join a startup, D) return to a big company as a PM. "Not even Plan Z was to write full-time."
- He ran two-week sprints: write three work goals and three personal goals, email them to three friends, then circle back at the end. The goals kept pulling him toward writing.
- Two wise friends pointed this out, so he leaned into writing and paused the rest. He called the path "Project: Avoid getting a real job," aiming for low stress and good-enough income.
- It became the most he has ever earned. He says he makes more than he did as an Airbnb product lead, stock included.
- He describes himself by what he does (newsletter, podcast, job board, angel investing) rather than calling himself a "writer."

## How Lenny chooses topics and writes each post

- He keeps a list of about 50 topics and a rough plan for the next 2-3 posts. Each week he picks the one he's most excited about: "I optimize for my own curiosity vs. what people want, because that always leads to the best stuff."
- Every post is framed as a question, since it's an advice column. It opens with a concrete, direct reader question, such as "what is a good retention rate" or "how did the biggest consumer apps get their first 1,000 users."
- Each topic gets a Coda page where he dumps bullets, quotes and stats.
- For expert quotes, he lists the people he wants and emails each individually. This needs 2-3 weeks of lead time.
- For his own research, he throws findings in messy and keeps adding.
- Then he looks for patterns, frameworks and simple ways to share conclusions, drafts the post, and adds charts, tables and visuals.
- He rereads dozens of times, emails himself a copy to see it with fresh eyes, and has a copyeditor review it. A designer sometimes makes a graphic. It's ready around the 50th read.
- Median time is 10 hours per post, ranging from 3 hours to 100+.

## Writing principle: density over "writing"

- He didn't set out to be a writer. He focused on "a density of high-quality, easy to understand, advice."
- He says people fill writing with long intros, useless context and pontification. His rule: get to the point, synthesize clearly, and get out of your own way.
- Books he credits: "On Writing Well" (cut, cut, cut some more) and Steven Pressfield's "Nobody Wants to Read Your Sh*t."

## What performs best

- Time invested correlates strongly with performance. All the 100+ hour posts are in his top 10.
- Exceptions: two of the top five (Favorite Templates, and Mission → Vision → Strategy → Goals → Roadmap → Task) took under five hours.
- Many top-20 posts are guest posts. They take much less time (mostly editing and refining with the author) and are among the most popular, which he calls a win-win.
- He calls a paid newsletter "the ultimate meritocracy": people subscribe, pay and tell friends if it's useful, and unsubscribe when it isn't.

## Why he stays on Substack despite the fees

- Substack's discovery features, especially recommendations, drive 12% of his new paid subscribers and 78% of his free subscribers.
- Their fee is 10%, so "they now pay for themselves."
- He has seen people leave to avoid fees and then see growth slow. He prefers optimizing for long-term growth over "eeking out every last %."
- Time on anything other than great content is wasted, and time spent drives quality, so he values not handling administration.
- If starting again, he'd change little: maybe charge sooner, and pick a better name than Substack's default.
- Surprises: nobody was doing this well and there's still lots of open space; how long he's kept going; how fulfilling it is (at least one message a day from someone whose career benefited); and how lucrative it is.

## Newsletter niches and the math

- Four jobs to be done in newsletters: entertain people, help people make money, help people be better at work or life (his bucket), and inform people (news).
- His advice: "Pick a niche and pick a JTBD and there's an opportunity there."
- The math: 1,000 paid subscribers at $10/month is over $100k/year. "How many niches have 1,000 people willing to pay something for great content?"
- Engineering newsletters (Gergely Orosz, Alex Xu) are now covered. He sees open opportunities in B2B sales, negotiation and angel investing, and has a "random feeling" about gardening.

## Angel investing and personal investing

- He started about five years ago by investing in friends leaving Airbnb to found companies, then other friends' companies.
- As the newsletter grew, he became helpful to more founders and saw better deals, and he followed that pull.
- He aims for about 10% of net worth in angel investing, which he calls "a very expensive hobby." It's too soon to know if it will pay off, though he's optimistic.
- His three buckets are index funds, individual stocks and angel investing. After the market came back to earth, he's "increasingly convinced index funds are the way to go."
- He read The Bogleheads' Guide to Investing in high school and put most money in index funds early. In hindsight he'd put more in index funds.
- He eventually hired a professional financial advisor because he lacks time to optimize.
- Best spending since liquidity: trips and being generous with family and friends. Beyond that, only a great laptop and phone noticeably improved his happiness.
