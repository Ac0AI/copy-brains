## Writing as solving a "job to be done," not as self-expression

- Lenny frames all content through jobs to be done. People buy a product, listen to a podcast, or read a newsletter "because they have a job they want you to do for them" (learn a skill, make money, advance a career, be entertained, stay informed).
- His own job for readers: help them get better at building product, grow their product, and accelerate their career. Readers are mostly founders, product managers and product teams.
- He treats the newsletter like a product. He imagines a "puzzle board" of every question founders and product teams have, and his job is to fill in each piece. Popular tactical posts include how to get your first thousand users, what good retention is, and how to kick-start and scale a B2B business.
- Milky Way vs Snickers example: Snickers is bought to feel full fast, Milky Way for comfort. They look like competitors but do different jobs.
- Newsletters that fail tend to just "pontificate" on ideas. The "be smarter at dinner parties" job exists, but the bar is very high (competing with Tyler Cowen, Noah Smith, Ben Thompson).
- Check that enough people have the job. He found about 800,000 product managers in the US and 2 million worldwide, and he also writes for founders, designers and engineers.
- A niche can be enough. At 1,000 paid subscribers he was making $100,000 a year, which he ties to Kevin Kelly's "1,000 true fans".
- He wants to be what people think of when they have that problem, like Amplitude or Mixpanel for analytics. His niche is specific enough that someone can recommend it by word of mouth ("he writes really well about startups, growth and product management").
- He rejects a single narrow topic. He lets himself roam across growth, product, career and starting a company, because boredom would kill consistency.
- Pick topics you are curious about. Writing only what you think will grow an account creates "a terrible job for yourself." Aim for the overlap of what you love and what people value.

## The research process for his 100-hour posts

- Lenny spends about 10 hours on a typical post and sometimes 100 or more. He says time is the main edge, because most people don't have much of it. Doing it full-time creates a flywheel: more time, better posts, faster growth, more income.
- His first big series came from leaving Airbnb and being asked how Airbnb grew supply, judged home quality and fixed booking conversion. He didn't trust that Airbnb's path was the right answer, so he interviewed founders of the biggest marketplaces.
- He interviewed 17 founders or early employees (including Etsy and AngelList). He found a four-step pattern: constrain to a focused problem and geography, then focus on supply, then focus on demand.
- His six steps:
  1. Pick a topic people are curious about.
  2. List the dream target companies.
  3. Find warm intros through his network or LinkedIn connections, or cold DM/email.
  4. Ask every interviewee the same questions and paste all the answers into a Google Doc.
  5. Stare at it and look for patterns.
  6. Turn the patterns into a story or framework, then look for a chart or a great quote.
- For targets, he starts with the wish list. He skips the very top (no Elon Musk at the start) and goes to a founder, early employee or someone who was there. Each round of interviews grows his network.
- For the B2B series, he interviewed companies like Amplitude, GitHub and Slack for about an hour each. He also jots down the questions builders will have: how to get the idea, first customers, product-market fit, first hires.
- Example pattern: for product-market fit, "get one company to love you." Figma's early customer story was that they drove back to a customer's office when the product "broke," and the actual problem was the customer's Wi-Fi.
- He pushes on interview claims using his product background (e.g. "How long did it really take?"), because founders' stories can be too tidy.
- He admits he keeps wanting "one more" interview and has to say "I'm just going to ship this."
- People are surprisingly open about early-days details. He says it's good marketing for them, and there's more to a big business than one tactic.

## Writing style: high signal to noise, answer first

- He has no writing training and had never written online before starting. The less he thinks about "the beauty of the writing," the better things go. He doesn't call himself a writer and says "I write a newsletter."
- His formula is "high signal to noise, actionable, succinct, useful information."
- Put the answer first. Introductions are "way too long." Lead with the answer, then give detail. For the product-market-fit post he opens with a chart of how long it took each company, then four steps, then quotes and stories below.
- He edits by reading from the top again and again (10, 20, even 30 times), looking for what to cut, simplify or move, until he sees nothing else to fix.
- Editing guests: cut introductions by about 50% and cut anything that isn't interesting or goes off track.
- For friction, he tests like a smoothie through a straw: where does the reader get stuck? He follows "kill your darlings" and saves cut ideas for later posts.
- Intro checklist at the top of his template: What's surprising? What's a good story to open with? What's unexpected?
- Titles: "What 7 years at Airbnb taught me about building a business" worked because it had a time investment (credibility), a brand, and a clear promise. A friend told him it would go viral.
- Pop culture metaphors: he's bad at them, so he asks ChatGPT. He describes the point, asks for fictional-narrative references, and narrows over 7-8 rounds (e.g. Rocky IV, Ocean's 11 for mission/vision/strategy/goals).
- Quality bar: he credits Airbnb's culture of asking "how do we 10x this?" (designer asked to build something the internet had never seen days before launch).
- Quality and consistency: "just be high quality and do it again and again and again and good things are going to happen." Aim to be one of the best in the world on a focused topic.
- Not every post lands. A hiring post didn't do well because it wasn't surprising and its four points weren't unique, and the quotes weren't strong. He aims for "mostly doubles and triples" with a home run every month or two. He does let himself off the hook sometimes.
- The quick-win post: a templates roundup (strategy, vision, PRD, one-pager, roadmap) is a top-10 post. It reused work he'd already collected.
- A rule of thumb: a post's views correlate strongly with the time put in, though templates are an exception.

## How he grew to 531,000 subscribers (distribution)

- About 99% of growth is word of mouth. Newsletters spread because they're easy to forward. He tried Twitter ads, Facebook ads, LinkedIn ads and cross-promotion, and says they're "a blip" next to word of mouth.
- Origins: he started on Medium with his Airbnb post, which got retweets from Andrew Chen and some VCs. Medium featured it on its homepage and it got lots of claps. That helped build his Twitter following.
- Move to an email list as soon as possible, since Medium doesn't give you your readers' emails.
- Guest posts on Andrew Chen's blog and First Round Review helped. Those came from serendipity: chatting with First Round's editor and being brave with Chen as he left Airbnb.
- He had the advantage of an Airbnb brand but says great work needs to be put in front of people.
- Substack Recommendations: about 9,000 other newsletters recommend his, and growth had a huge inflection the day the feature launched. He thinks it worked especially well because he was a known quantity when it launched, though anyone with a successful newsletter benefits.
- Early growth was slow: the first nine months were slow, and about a year to reach 10,000 subscribers. He notes newsletters grow faster now.
- Promotion channels: Twitter/X, LinkedIn and the newsletter. LinkedIn is "shockingly powerful," driving more traffic than X for his content because it's business-oriented. He starts short on Twitter and adds more on LinkedIn.
- He doesn't track email conversion. He watches views per post and asks why a post underperformed.
- He doesn't spend time on growth strategy: "see what's working, follow that pull, double down."
- Don't over-optimize setup. People who spend time on hosting, naming and design, or hop platforms, see growth slow. "Anytime you spend on that is just waste of time you could be spending on writing awesome stuff."
- Growth is linear and flattens when he's away: during about three months of paternity leave growth was flat, even though he had a backlog of posts, because he didn't promote them.

## Business model, goals and saying no

- Goals were small milestones, not a master plan. First: $100,000 a year from the paid newsletter ("avoid getting a real job"). Next: about $300,000, roughly his Airbnb salary and stock. He has no vision for an "empire" and deliberately avoids building a media company.
- He had a list of things he'd never do (podcast, course, book). The podcast came after Harry Stebbings encouraged him to try it. It generates more revenue than the newsletter and grew faster than the newsletter. He also made a course, and a book is the one thing not done yet.
- Annual plans mean he owes readers a year of weekly quality, which he describes as pressure like the Indiana Jones boulder.
- He publishes every Tuesday and aims to be done over the weekend. He never pulls all-nighters. If he's behind he takes a week off, using a self-invented PTO policy (for example, four weeks a year).
- Make writing less work with guest posts, which are a growing share. He picks the smartest person on a topic and heavily edits. He also keeps "easy" posts (like a library of essential PM books) in his back pocket.
- A copy editor costs about $100 per post and catches roughly 100 things every time. He says writers are blind to their own text right after writing it, and last-minute tweaks after the copy edit always introduce errors.
- Guard creative time: no meetings until 3 p.m. He quotes Naval: as you become successful you have less time to do the thing that made you successful. He avoids talks, events and interviews via policies, and uses excuses like a new baby ("can we chat in six months?"). Simply not replying is another tactic.
- Advice for people writing at work: write to remember what you're learning in the moment, because it fades after you leave. Tweets are enough as a start.
- Writing prompts for ideas: what do I want to learn, what do I want to remember, what's interesting to me now, what did someone ask me that I couldn't answer well, what resonated on Twitter. The last one produced his newsletter, because people asked marketplace questions he couldn't answer.

## Tools and workflow

- Coda is home base: a page per future post, a content calendar, about 50 idea stubs, about 5 in progress, plus a doc of writing advice to himself.
- He moves drafts to Substack once fleshed out, to see headings and formatting.
- Guest posts: Google Docs for comments and edits, then a copy editor.
- Focus: brain.fm (binaural beats), headphones, Wi-Fi, a comfy chair, sparkling water, coffee, and Magic Mind. He works in different rooms and cafes, and admits he checks Twitter and LinkedIn about 30 times while writing. He deleted the Twitter app and uses mobile web.
- Handling criticism: he mostly looks for what he did wrong. Example: Hindi subtitles on his podcast (India is his second biggest YouTube market) drew pushback that Indian tech audiences speak English. He also had to correct dates on a product-market-fit timeline of about 30 companies after companies pinged him. He avoids errors because corrections hurt credibility at a scale of 500,000+ readers.
