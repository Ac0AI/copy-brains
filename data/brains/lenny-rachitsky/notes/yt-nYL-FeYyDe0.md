## Series A criteria and what founders can influence

- Lenny says investors focus on six things at the Series A: product market fit, revenue, growth rate, how amazing the founders are, market size, and why now is the right time for this kind of company.
- Only three of these can change between the seed round and the Series A: signals of product market fit, revenue, and growth rate.
- He calls the Series A "the product market fit round". It is where a startup moves from searching to raising money, "pouring fuel in the fire", and scaling.
- His advice is based on research into "basically every successful company" he could get hold of. He interviewed founders and early growth employees. He also draws on about 70 angel investments over five years, and on his seven years at Airbnb (supply growth, conversion, Superhost, reviews).

## How to tell if you have product market fit

- He defines product market fit simply as whether there is a market for the product you are building.
- From his founder interviews, he sees three main signs: a sudden and significant pull, a gradual but compounding pull, and cohort retention curves that flatten.
- **Sudden pull** is the most common story and is hard to miss.
  - Dropbox: after launching on Digg and Hacker News, Drew Houston felt pulled back in his seat as the car raced away.
  - Netflix, Uber (uncontrollable word of mouth) and Segment (analytics.js, its third iteration) had the same experience.
  - GitHub: people asked to pay for it before it charged, because they didn't want it to go away.
- **Gradual, compounding pull** is easier to miss.
  - Substack: the founder was obsessed with measuring product market fit, then got too busy with growth to keep checking.
  - Instacart: it started with a thin market of people who don't care which store their food comes from, then expanded.
  - Superhuman: it found a sliver of the market (founders, managers, executives) and expanded to more personas.
  - Datadog: there was no explosion, but sign-ups never stopped.
- **Timing:** it takes longer than founders expect.
  - The shortest period he found was about a year and a half.
  - Segment took three tries.
  - Amplitude took four years of iterating.
- **Three early signals** to use before the main signs appear:
  - The Sean Ellis survey: over 40% of users say they would be "very disappointed" if the product went away. He says it is not necessarily true, but it is a good signal and a useful stat for a deck.
  - Steve Blank's test: people's pupils dilate and they get visibly excited. This suits idea and prototype stages.
  - People keep using the product even when it is broken or down, and are sad when it is down.

## Measuring retention by cohort

- Retention is, for him, "the ultimate measure" of whether you are building something people want.
- There are two ways to measure it.
  - Monthly churn: the percentage of active users lost each month.
  - Cohort retention: track users who joined in the same period and watch them over time.
- He prefers cohorts. Blended churn mixes new and existing users, so you can't tell whether a new onboarding flow or landing page had an effect.
- Example table: of about 168 users who joined in one week, 14 were still around at 10 weeks and eventually only 6%. He calls that "not great".
- Plot each cohort as a line chart by day, week or month.
  - If the curve flattens and doesn't hit zero, you have product market fit with some segment of the market. The flat level shows what share of the market finds value.
  - If it goes to zero, you don't have product market fit, even if you are growing fast.
  - Cohorts stacking higher on each other means retention is improving.

## Good vs great retention benchmarks at six months

He surveyed many top growth, product and investing people and triangulated their answers. Good / great at six months:

| Product type | Examples | Good | Great |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer social (ad-supported) | Twitter, Snapchat, Instagram | 25% | 45% |
| Transactional consumer | Airbnb, Lyft, Chirp, Tax | about 30% | about 50% |
| Consumer subscription | Netflix, Spotify, Hulu | about 40% | over 70% |
| Mid-market subscription (100-1,000 employees) | | about 60% | above 80% |
| Enterprise software | | 70% | 90% |

- **Why they differ:**
  - Acquisition cost rises roughly linearly down the list. Costly customers must be retained or the unit economics fail.
  - Small companies fail and switch products more often. Enterprises sign year-long contracts and train everyone, so enterprise churn means something is badly wrong.
- **Three reasons it's okay to fall below the benchmarks:**
  - You are early and haven't built the core yet. He warns it is not a long-lived excuse, because retention usually jumps once the core of the product is in.
  - Acquisition and marginal costs are near zero, as with Twitter and Shopify.
  - You aren't building a venture-scale business. He notes that Series A founders likely aren't in this group.

## The growth engine framework (race car)

- He frames a startup as a race car with four parts: engine, turbo boosts, lubricants and fuel.
- **Engine:** a self-sustaining growth loop that drives most long-term growth. He says only four exist, and one will drive the majority of growth, so pick it and get really good at it.
  - **Performance marketing:** spend on ads, ads bring customers, customers bring revenue, revenue funds more ads. It fits when you earn revenue immediately, or when people aren't searching for you (e.g. a mattress in a box).
  - **Content:** users create content (reviews, answers), SEO brings visitors, who create more content. It fits when content is generated by the product or you have unique data for millions of pages (Thumbtack, Yelp). He also names editorial content, where employees write SEO blog posts. User-generated is best because it feeds itself.
  - **Sales:** hire salespeople, they bring customers, revenue funds more salespeople. It is like performance marketing but suits higher revenue per sale or products that need training. It is the usual B2B model for non-bottom-up businesses.
  - **Virality:** users invite friends, who invite friends. It fits products that are innately better with friends or fun to share. It also works for bottom-up B2B SaaS, which combines a cheap viral engine with enterprise contract size. He says you can't just bolt it on.
- **Examples:** Airbnb grew via virality, then performance marketing. Thumbtack grew via SEO, then added performance marketing. Booking.com started with SEO and shifted almost entirely to performance marketing.
- **Turbo boosts:** one-off lifts like PR, events and brand marketing. They are useful early. His warning is that a common founder mistake is repeating them without investing in an engine.
- **Lubricants:** improvements to conversion, activation and retention. They make everything run better but won't make or break the business alone. Retention is special once you have product market fit.
- **Fuel:** what feeds the engine: money, content or users.

## Getting your first thousand users: three steps

He calls this a new, still-developing framework. The steps are: identify customers who care, build a remarkable value prop, then find where those customers are.

- **Step 1: Customers who care** (a narrow wedge of early adopters).
  - TikTok: bottom-tier influencers.
  - Netflix: online DVD fanatics.
  - Tinder: attractive, party-loving college students in LA.
  - Uber: tech employees in San Francisco.
  - Ask who feels the most pain without it, who would benefit significantly, and who would pay.
- **Step 2: Remarkable value prop.** "Remarkable" means people remark on it.
  - Robinhood: zero-percent trading commissions.
  - Dropbox: "throw away your USB drive".
  - Netflix: rent as many movies as you want, no late fees.
  - DoorDash to restaurants: make more money, no risk.
  - Ask what the alternatives are, what is unique, and what superpower it unlocks for the user. Most companies don't get it right at first.
- **Step 3: Find them.** He says almost every company found its first thousand customers through one or two of 10 channels:
  - Friends and colleagues, then targeted strangers.
  - Infiltrating offline communities, then online communities.
  - Building your own community.
  - Influencers, PR, partnerships, and physical placement.
  - The list is concentric circles out from the founder. The further down it, the more friction, so use the earlier ones when you can.
- **Examples:**
  - Friends and colleagues: Yelp, LinkedIn, Substack (writer Bill Bishop, a colleague of a founder), Salesforce (Marc Benioff's former colleague).
  - Targeted strangers: Cameo hired interns at $10 an hour to DM celebrities. DoorDash went door to door to restaurants. TikTok DMed influencers. Udemy pitched Ruby experts.
  - Offline: Etsy at craft fairs. Lyft handed out balloons and cupcakes at startups. Tinder threw parties at USC. Pinterest's Ben loaded the site on Apple Store laptops.
  - Own community: Product Hunt (Ryan Hoover's email list), Stack Overflow (podcast and blog), Patreon (musician friends), TikTok (influencer community).
  - PR: Instacart used it as a primary lever (a PR event with each new city or big feature). Udemy, Spotify and Airbnb (Obama O's/McCain's cereal story) also used it.
  - Physical placement: DoorDash flyers on campus and in delivery bags. Instacart put its logo on bags.
- **FOMO as a layer:** you need cool insiders sharing how great it is and a "velvet rope" such as a waitlist. One without the other doesn't work.
- **Takeaways:**
  - Consumer startups usually got their first 10,000+ users from one strategy, and usually by going to users directly.
  - For B2B, only four work most: friends and family, targeted strangers, infiltrating communities, and PR.
  - A big network is a large B2B advantage, which is part of why YC is valuable. Trust matters, e.g. "Mark Benioff, he's great, he's not going to screw me".

## Experiments, patience, prototypes and positioning

- **Growth hacks:** as an investor he wants organic growth and market pull. "The less hacks you need the better." Hacks suit one-off turbo boosts, and Twitter is a good lever for a spike.
- **Low traffic:** startups can't run valid A/B tests for long. Experiments on little data are "complete nonsense". Talk to people qualitatively instead.
  - Paid ads can give traffic but at low intent. If low-intent users get excited, that is an even better sign.
  - For sample sizes, use the Optimizely experiment sample size calculator.
- **How long to persist:** he has no great answer. His heuristic is to give it double the time your instinct says, then consider a pivot. Pivoting sooner is often better than running out of money. Airbnb had about two years of nothing before it took off.
- **Positioning:** Amplitude thought it was building a tool for marketers testing copy. Once it pitched product teams, a product manager at a conference said it was the first product built for what he needed. The product barely changed; the positioning did.
- **Prototypes in B2B:** pitching a prototype is "super realistic". B2B customers are clear about their problems, you can look for the pupil-dilation reaction, and you can ask them to pay now for first access. Many companies raise with just Figma files or decks.
- **PM teams:** he favors companies where product teams are empowered and cross-functional, with a say over vision, strategy and priorities, rather than feature teams told what to build.
- **No-code:** he says tools like Webflow and Coda work well and will widen who can build software.
