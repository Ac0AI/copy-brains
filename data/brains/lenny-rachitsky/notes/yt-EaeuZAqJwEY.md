## How Lenny went from a Medium post to a paid newsletter

- Lenny left Airbnb about a year before this talk (October 2020). He wanted to write down what he had learned there so he would not have to relearn it at his next company.
- He started with Evernote bullets, which became a Medium post. It did far better than expected, landing in roughly the top 300 most-clapped Medium posts. Brian Chesky sent it to all of Airbnb, and Medium featured it and paid him.
- That gave him confidence there was something to share and maybe money to make. He wrote more Medium posts, then moved to Substack on friends' advice. He first used it only as a blog, with no newsletter plan.
- He also tweeted small nuggets on product, growth and people management. Those grew his Twitter following, which later became central to newsletter growth.
- He then experimented with writing weekly, announced it on Twitter, and got his first ~500 subscribers that first week.
- By October 2020 the newsletter had about 30,000 free subscribers and about 1,400 paid. It launched in June 2019 and went paid in April 2020. It is a six-figure income.
- He named it "Lenny's Newsletter" because he expected to rename it later, and the name stuck.
- He says plainly that "I don't actually know what I'm doing, I'm just kind of figuring this out as I go."

## Subscriber growth milestones and what drove them

- Twitter announcement of the weekly newsletter: ~500 subscribers in week one.
- Guest post on Andrew Chen's blog, whose readers match his core audience: another ~500.
- Switching to an advice-column format, inspired by Julie Zhuo's similar column for designers: ~1,000 more, mostly from Twitter and word of mouth.
- A guest post for First Round: roughly 1,500 more. Guest posts are very valuable early on.
- An eight-part series on building marketplaces took over 100 hours of research. It roughly doubled his subscribers.
- At one point half of his growth came from two big posts: the marketplace series and "how to get your first 1,000 customers" for consumer businesses. The other half came from all his other posts.
- His lesson is to mix smaller, less time-intensive posts with occasional epic ones. Hard work on something substantial pays off.
- Most growth is word of mouth (Twitter, Facebook, LinkedIn), with a little SEO. Valuable content gets talked about, and there is no growth hack beyond that.
- He started with three questions a week, found that unsustainable, and found readers don't consume that much. One question done well is enough.
- He watches the unsubscribe rate. If growth slows, he wonders whether he is doing something wrong.
- He gives discounts or free subscriptions to people who can't afford a paid one. He says he is not very strategic about it and focuses on content.

## What "value" means: five jobs and three writing rules

Lenny's core claim is that the secret to newsletter success, and probably to anything successful, is delivering consistent value. He sees five jobs a newsletter can do:

- Entertain
- Make people smarter
- Help them make money
- Keep them informed
- Help them feel part of something bigger

Every successful newsletter does at least one extremely well, especially a paid one. Before publishing, he checks three things:

- **Actionable:** Can someone actually use this in their job, or is it fluffy and high-level?
- **Close to what he knows:** He stays close to topics he knows. If he doesn't know one, he finds someone who does, which is where guest posts come from.
- **Adds something new:** He tries to add something to the conversation rather than rehash it.

## Finding topics that are new, and why time is an edge

- Things that seem obvious to him are often valuable to others. You don't need to know everything to create value, and sharing what you've done is often enough. The key is to articulate it clearly and make it easy and engaging to read.
- His method for finding gaps: when someone asks him a question, he looks for an answer. If none exists, that signals an opportunity for primary research.
- Example: no one had written how the biggest B2B businesses got their first 10 customers, which surprised him.
- Many newsletters don't do primary research, meaning reaching out to people to find answers themselves.
- Time is a major advantage: "the more time you put into a thing... the better it's going to be than anything else out there." Most people don't have time to research how companies started. Just spending the time puts you ahead, even without a big network.
- He thinks he could only do this after leaving his full-time job.
- Many of his questions come from advising companies and from Twitter.
- The marketplace series started from a selfish motive. People kept asking how Airbnb succeeded, and he wanted better answers. The research simply grew into a multi-part series.

## The advice-column workflow

- The format is a Dear Abby-style column. Readers submit questions through a form linked in each issue, and the answers land in a spreadsheet.
- Periodically he moves questions into a Coda doc as a topic list, sorted by what he most wants to write about and thinks will be useful.
- Over time he adds notes and thoughts under each topic, then fleshes it into a draft with the question and the full story.
- He moves the draft to Substack and scans it about ten times to simplify, cut words and make it punchier. He sends it to his phone to review, because he spots things there that he misses on his computer.
- He has three post types:
  - **Answering the question himself:** about half of posts, such as career advice drawing on his own path.
  - **Guest posts:** when he has no good answer, he finds an expert. For an SEO question he got a world-class SEO friend to write the post, then helped edit it.
  - **Research posts:** like the marketplace series.
- Guest posts on his own newsletter give writers a chance to build an audience and give him a week off.
- Tools: Coda for post outlines, Mem for personal notes, Grammarly, and the Centered app for focus. He keeps mornings meeting-free until 2pm to write.
- He sometimes gets distracted wanting "one more company" to interview. That is easier and more fun than solving the puzzle of the post itself.
- Charging for the newsletter helps: a post is due every week because people are paying.

## How he runs a research post

- Make a wish list of companies and people he wants to talk to, ideally the best possible group.
- Find warm intros through LinkedIn and friends, or cold DM or email founders. A VC friend intro'd him to Thumbtack's co-founder, and they talked for half an hour.
- Throw interview notes raw into a messy Google Doc, organized by company. Keep a checklist tracking who has answered.
- Stare at the answers to see what emerges. The marketplace series became a four-step framework for starting a marketplace business, backed by stories and quotes from each company.
- Clean up each quote and run it by the person for approval.
- Getting many perspectives matters. Existing writing on solving the chicken-and-egg problem is usually one company's story (OpenTable, Uber, Airbnb). He looked for the common approach across all of them.
- He was working on a similar post on going from a B2B company's first 10 customers to its first 100.
- On examples: he asks whether it is something the reader can do, whether it is interesting or surprising, and whether it is concrete and actionable. If many companies did the same thing, he groups them together.
- He has not done a post going deep on a single company. Readers want to know what to do at their company, and one company's exact playbook rarely transfers. A group of similar companies doing the same thing is more useful.

## Distribution: the Twitter and newsletter flywheel

- His distribution rests on one belief: a good, high-quality, valuable newsletter does well, and there is no hack.
- The flywheel runs in both directions.
  - **Newsletter to Twitter:** he extracts the "juice" of a post and puts it on Twitter, giving away the good stuff instead of teasing it. He does this even for paid posts, because people ignore tweets with nothing interesting. He did a thread on a B2B post and summarized a long retention post in one tweet that got a lot of love. People then subscribe to read more.
  - **Twitter to newsletter:** sometimes a tweet he thinks is interesting does well, and he then writes a post about it.
- His publishing steps: send the newsletter, tweet a summary that gives away the goods, copy it to LinkedIn, and encourage sharing via buttons in the post.
- He squeezes as much value as he can from each post. While writing the B2B first-customers post, he tweeted screenshots of companies at launch just for fun, and they were widely shared.
- After publishing, he tweeted out individual founder stories from the long post. One post led to five or six tweets. He did this because he didn't want people to miss the interesting parts, not to maximize likes.
- Distribution tips:
  - Because most content is paywalled, he runs one free post a month. That post has to be really good, since it is what grows the newsletter.
  - Repurpose content.
  - Do guest posts.
- Guest posts: Andrew Chen asked to distribute a post he'd written for his own newsletter. The trade-off is giving content away versus the subscribers it drives. He now puts most energy into his own newsletter, which drives more traffic directly. He still does them occasionally, such as a new First Round post.

## Which posts worked and how he picks topics

- His most popular posts were, in order: how the biggest consumer products got their first 1,000 customers, how B2B companies got their first customers, then the marketplace posts.
- His takeaway is that people want practical answers to problems in their company. Founders especially ask "what should I actually do," not "what's the future of tech." He asks whether someone can use the post tomorrow to start something, brainstorm, or talk to their manager.
- He avoids chasing what readers want at the expense of what he enjoys writing. He looks for a balance of topics he finds interesting and that people will value, so he doesn't create a job he dislikes.

## Structuring and editing posts

- His usability rule of thumb as a product manager: "imagine all your users are drunk and don't know what they're doing, just fumbling around clicking buttons." He applies it to writing.
- He makes posts very easy to scan:
  - a simple summary image at the top and bottom
  - big bold headings
  - visuals
  - breaking up or cutting any large block of text
- He asks what the natural flow of the reader's thoughts is.
- He keeps *On Writing Well* next to him, which teaches him to cut everything he can. His posts are still very long, but he cuts anything that adds no value.

## Why Airbnb's design mattered

- Two of Airbnb's three founders are designers, so design value was in the company from the start.
- Airbnb competed with Craigslist, HomeAway, VRBO and later Booking.com. Lenny says there are only a few ways to differentiate: be cheaper, or be a much better experience that people feel good using and find easier.
- Every product launch had to be reviewed by Brian Chesky in a weekly meeting. The bar stayed very high, which doesn't scale but helped keep quality up.
