## Why Lenny launched a newsletter and how it started

- Lenny spent seven years at Airbnb, left to start a company, and began writing notes on what he learned there "so that I don't have to relearn it again when I start a company."
- The notes became a Medium post that did very well (top ~300 most-clapped). He wrote six Medium posts before moving to Substack on advice from others, to build his own email list and collect long-term SEO benefit.
- He started with no concept and named it "Lenny's newsletter" because he had no idea what to do with it. He tweeted an experiment: "I'm going to send a newsletter every week, I don't know what it's going to be, but you should subscribe."
- He settled on an advice-column format, inspired by Julie Zhuo's design advice newsletter, applied to product and growth.
- He wrote weekly for nine months for free. COVID hit about a year after he left Airbnb, with no job and a collapsing market, so he tried paid. He launched paid in April and is now at a six-figure income that covers his entire living.
- He says he never imagined making a living from writing. He was an engineer and product manager with no public writing history.
- He credits having savings: roughly six months to build confidence, an audience and a rhythm before income started.

## Five jobs to be done for a newsletter

Lenny's framework for why people subscribe: "entertain me, make me money, make me smarter, keep me informed, or help me feel like I'm part of something bigger."

- If a newsletter creates real value in one of these buckets, people subscribe and pay, especially in a professional setting.
- Lots of people write about product and growth, but little of it is consistently good, because people lack time to research and go deep.
- Lenny's edge is time. He had left his job, so he could do the research that makes posts high quality.
- He thinks doing this part-time alongside a fast-growing startup would be very hard, for time and for "brain energy." He suggests this is why so much VC content exists: VCs have the time.

## Picking topics and building a content pipeline

- The advice-column format is his "cheat code": readers send questions, and he keeps a backlog of about 50.
- Other sources: questions from startups he advises and angel invests in, and topics he wants to learn about. The newsletter is "a really good excuse" to study them.
- His process:
  1. Dump all questions and topics into one big doc.
  2. Sort by his interest and timeliness.
  3. Turn the sorted list into a content calendar for the next couple of months, adjusted for interest and blockers.
  4. For each piece, start with the question plus quick thoughts and links, then flesh it out in its own page, move it into Substack, run it past a few people for feedback, and ship.
- His topic range is broad (growth, product, management, org). He says broad is "normally a bad idea" but works here. Some readers come for only growth, others only product, so he intermixes topics to avoid long stretches that lose one group.
- He doesn't answer every question he gets. He weighs usefulness to readers but stays close to what he finds interesting: "if it's interesting to you, it's probably going to be interesting to other people."

## Staying consistent and getting better at writing

- Paid subscribers create accountability: "there's always the next week's post." He calls it a boulder always chasing him.
- He publishes on Tuesdays and keeps every morning free for writing. The content calendar means he always has a sense of what is next.
- Public commitment works. He tweeted the weekly experiment to create accountability.
- Writing about things he's curious about is what sustains the cadence.
- To improve as a writer, he:
  - keeps a book on writing well by his side
  - has a friend who assigns him well-written books (he has been reading Malcolm Gladwell as a model of compelling non-fiction)
  - joined a writing group (Compound) and a Typehouse group where people give feedback on posts
  - keeps writing and editing, and runs drafts past others

## Growing the audience: guest posts and the Twitter flywheel

Lenny says most growth is now word of mouth: if he just publishes, it grows. He got started with two moves:

- **Guest posts:** He wrote guest posts on Andrew Chen's blog and on First Round Review, which got him his first 500 to 1,000 subscribers. The idea is to find the audience you want and write something strong in their newsletter or blog. He now accepts guest posts in his own newsletter, which lightens his week and gives new writers a platform.
- **Twitter flywheel:** Newsletter and Twitter feed each other.
  - A tweet that does well becomes a newsletter topic.
  - Each newsletter post is tweeted with its best parts.
  - Early on, while writing on Medium, he tweeted small nuggets that weren't full posts, which built the audience he later used to launch the Substack.
- Rule for tweeting: "give away the best stuff on Twitter and not entice people to go read the post, because no one's going to read it." Tweet the best parts of the post (sometimes as a thread) with a link, and people subscribe because they conclude "this guy knows some stuff."
- He calls Twitter and Word of Mouth the top of his newsletter funnel.

## When and how to go paid, and pricing

- Lenny went paid at about 8,000 free subscribers. About 250 converted to paid, roughly 3%, which he says was below average. Typical conversion is 5 to 10% of free subscribers, and he thinks his big free list may have lowered the rate.
- Once most content is behind a paywall, he says you can't use it to grow beyond teasing it, so build the free list first.
- Only go paid if you want the newsletter to be your main income. Otherwise use the newsletter to drive something else you do.
- On pricing: he spent a long time on it and got input from many people, and priced above $10 a month, believing most writers price lower than they should. He experiments with discounts and says there isn't enough data to know the perfect price, so he goes with what feels right and is working well enough.
- Cadence matters to price. Ben Thompson's daily output at about $12 a month sets a low bar for everyone else, and Lenny can't price at $20 as a non-daily writer.

## Avoiding overthinking, and the realities of newsletter life

- Misconception: people overthink what to write and never start. His advice is to just do it, tell people it's an experiment, and follow what interests you.
- He tries to "stay close to the things that give me energy" and do less of what drains him. That drew him to writing over starting another company.
- He has stayed focused on a high-quality newsletter and says no to a podcast, a book, and joining a bundle. He thinks readers don't want more email, and since the newsletter is going well he has no reason to bundle. He sees bundles as a better deal for readers and more sensible for writers starting out.
- Self-employment has practical gaps: health insurance, 401(k), and getting a mortgage, which typically needs years of tax returns.

## What Lenny values in Substack versus Medium

- Posts reach people's inboxes immediately instead of through an arbitrary push or a lucky discovery.
- You own your audience: an email list you can take anywhere.
- You own your Stripe account, so you are building your own business and can leave if you want.
- On Medium, his old posts still earn about $30 a month. He says the promise of Medium sending readers delivers little, and you end up building Medium's user base, traffic and SEO rather than your own.
- Medium's newsletter feature still doesn't let writers own their audience, which he thinks is the gap.
- He wishes Twitter let him own his audience, and says he'd pay thousands of dollars to reach his followers directly.
- On quality: he pushes writers in a fellowship to hold a very high bar and give direct feedback ("no, this isn't good enough, keep going"). Content that works is concrete, new, different and easy to read, not high-level, fluffy pontificating.
- He can tell when readers dislike something because they unsubscribe, but it rarely happens in mass when a post isn't perfect.
