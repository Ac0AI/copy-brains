## Growth engines: the four ways a startup grows long term

Lenny describes the "race car" growth framework, built with Dan Hockenmaier. A company is a race car with four parts:
- **Engine:** the most important part. It is a self-sustaining loop that keeps growth going.
- **Turbo boosts:** one-off hero moments that give a burst and then fade.
- **Lubricants:** things that make the engine run more efficiently.
- **Fuel:** the input that powers the car.

He says there are only four engines to choose from:
- **Performance marketing:** spend on ads, ads bring customers, customers bring revenue, revenue funds more ads.
- **Virality:** users bring in new users, who bring in their friends.
- **Content:** SEO, viral content and user-generated content.
- **Sales:** hire salespeople, they close customers, revenue funds more salespeople.

Other options are partnerships and physical space (retail placement, as Casper did). He treats them as outside the core four, and says a partnership only counts as an engine if it keeps driving traffic rather than being a one-off.

His central point is that a company has to become one of the best in the world at one engine in its market to have a real chance. Airbnb won travel through virality, Booking.com through performance marketing and TripAdvisor through content. He says the common mistake is spreading effort thinly across many channels, which "is unlikely to work".

## Choosing an engine and layering a second one

- Most companies' growth is explained by one engine at first. Later they layer on a second, and maybe a third.
- Lenny frames it as an S-curve. An engine ramps up, then plateaus, so you should anticipate the plateau and plant seeds for the next engine before it arrives. He cites Jeff Jordan's "layer cake" of growth curves.
- He reads "first-time founders focus on product, second-time founders focus on distribution" as a mental model. Work backwards from an engine you can be unusually good at, then build a product that suits it.
- Sales gets added eventually by almost every bottoms-up business. The question for enterprise B2B is when, not whether. His example is Dropbox waiting too long to build sales while Box moved early.
- Brand work, PR, events and a Super Bowl ad are turbo boosts. They give a spike that goes away.

## Performance marketing and sales: when each fits

Performance marketing:
- Anyone can try it in a couple of weeks with a Facebook or AdWords account.
- He advises avoiding building the business on it if you can, because it is brutally competitive and a sliver of success invites competitors. Booking.com reportedly spent over a billion dollars a year on Google search ads.
- Two rules of thumb for when it fits: you earn revenue from users immediately, so it can feed back into ads, and people aren't already searching for your product. His example is a Slanket, which no one searches for.
- Winners go very deep. Wish had a founder who came from Google search ranking. Credit Karma and Casper are other examples.
- Paid ads are one of the worst ways to get retention, because those users have the lowest intent.

Sales:
- It fits when average order value and lifetime value are high and customers need hand-holding, education and onboarding.

## Virality: types, fit and how to measure it

Lenny lists several kinds of virality:
- Word of mouth because the product is remarkable (Airbnb).
- Inviting friends to do it together (Snapchat).
- Passive exposure through shared links (Dropbox).
- Incentivized referrals (Dropbox's extra storage).
- Collaboration invites (Slack, Figma, Airtable, Coda).

Rules of thumb for fit:
- The product is better with friends or colleagues using it.
- It is innately fun or remarkable to share.
- He likes the word "remarkable" from Seth Godin: worth remarking about.

Measurement:
- K-factor (above 1 means viral) is hard to use in practice, since many invites happen offline.
- He prefers asking new users how they heard about you and measuring the share that came via friends, family or invites.
- He says you want over half from word of mouth, and closer to 70% means virality is truly your engine.

Bottoms-up B2B pairs cheap consumer-style viral growth with enterprise contract sizes, which is why those companies grow fastest.

FOMO works when you have both parts: great people or a great value prop inside, and a velvet rope outside. Clubhouse had people like Naval and Marc Andreessen inside. Robinhood offered free trades.

## Content as an engine

Lenny maps content-driven growth on two axes: user-generated versus company-written, and SEO versus viral.
- User-generated and SEO is the best case: Quora, Reddit, Glassdoor.
- Editorial and SEO: Ahrefs' SEO blog posts.
- Data-driven or machine-generated SEO: Thumbtack's "best plumber in [city]" pages, and Zapier's pages for every app-to-app connection. This works if you have unique data nobody else has.

Rules for company content:
- Decide up front whether you are optimizing for SEO or for viral sharing. It is one or the other.
- Viral one-offs work for creators like MrBeast, and Superhuman's early blog posts built traction.
- Most companies should focus on SEO, since that loop keeps giving.
- Pick one primary KPI per piece of content and treat the rest as secondary.

## Lubricants, activation and retention

Lubricants are conversion, activation and retention. They matter, but founders over-invest in micro-optimizing them before they have an engine.
- **Conversion:** anything that increases someone completing the next step, including sending an invite.
- **Activation:** the moment a user understands and feels the product's value. For Airbnb hosts it was getting their first booking.
- **Retention:** he calls it "the most important metric to watch" because it is the ultimate test of product-market fit.

To improve retention, change who you target:
- Target "customers who care", a phrase he credits to April Dunford.
- Retention is largely an audience-fit problem.
- On his own newsletter, Hacker News traffic converts to paid far worse than other sources.

Benchmarks:
- He surveyed about 20 growth leaders and investors to set thresholds, and it ranges from roughly 25% to 90%.
- Consumer social is 25% okay and 45% great.
- Consumer subscription is 40% okay and 70% great.
- Enterprise SaaS is 70% okay and 90% great.
- The spread follows acquisition cost. Expensive sales-led customers must stick around, while cheap consumer users can churn more.

## Getting the first users: turbo boosts and customers who care

Early on, the work is mostly turbo boosts that ignite the engine, not designing loops. This is "do things that don't scale": reaching out to strangers, getting some PR, infiltrating online communities. Building the perfect engine from day one is a pitfall.

Step one is defining customers who care, the narrow early-adopter wedge:
- TikTok: bottom-tier Instagram influencers.
- Cameo: C-list celebrities.
- Netflix: DVD fanatics.
- WhatsApp: Russian immigrants in San Jose.
- Substack: writers already doing well online.
- Tinder: attractive, party-loving college students in LA.

For his own newsletter, about 99% of growth is word of mouth. His first thousand subscribers came from a couple of guest posts, a few retweets from influencers and Twitter. Ads, a referral program, cross-promotion and B2B offers did nothing meaningful. He says writing consistently valuable content is the only thing that moves growth. When he taught a course for three weeks and wrote less, growth slowed noticeably.

## Building a newsletter: the numbers, topics and value

Numbers (as of this conversation):
- About 55,000 free subscribers and about 5,000 paid.
- He launched about two years earlier and started charging about one year earlier.
- He says he makes roughly twice his Airbnb tech salary.

Choosing what to write:
- He rejected "pick one niche". He writes across product, growth and startup challenges because he wants to stay excited.
- He modeled it as an advice column after Julie Zhuo's newsletter, so reader questions give him an endless queue of topics.
- A creator's pick should sit in a Venn diagram of what interests you and what creates real value for others.

He asks of each post whether it will help someone with their work. He sees five jobs content can do: make you better at your job, entertain you, help you make money, help you feel part of a cause, and others. The Browser and Pomp are his examples of value that isn't purely work help.

On platform and strategy:
- He stays on Substack despite paying an "obscene" amount, because it lets him just write and he can switch later.
- He tries to do less. He avoids a podcast or book and focuses on what is working, such as a job board and a private subscriber community.
- He treats a subscription as a promise to deliver every week.
