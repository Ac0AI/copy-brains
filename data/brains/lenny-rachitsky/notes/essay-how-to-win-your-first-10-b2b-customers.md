## The sequence Lenny recommends for finding your first 10 B2B customers

Lenny's recommended order for finding early B2B customers:

1. Reach out to your network for people who match your ICP.
2. Go cold outbound, but be strategic about it.
3. Tap your investors' networks.
4. Participate in relevant communities and network.
5. Put out compelling content and build a following online.
6. Get press.
7. Just launch.

The simple version:
- Start with former colleagues, both yours and those of your early employees.
- Collect your investors' contacts who match your profile, then go cold outbound creatively.
- Find a community where your ICPs hang out and participate.

His main takeaways:
- "None of these seven strategies scale. That's why they work." In B2B, "it always starts with hand-to-hand combat."
- Cold outbound works if it is done creatively.
- PR can work, but rarely does.
- Many founders found early customers by building an online following first.
- In communities, add value first. No one wants to pay attention to someone who is only there to pitch.
- For the first customers, trust is the key.

## Start with your personal network and trust circles

Lenny puts the personal network first because it carries the most innate trust and the least skepticism. The downside is that friends may not give you a true read on the idea.

His examples from founders:
- **Figma:** Alpha customers were mostly friends and friends of friends of Dylan Field. The first company to use Figma was Coda, then code-named Krypton.
- **Gong:** All of the first dozen customers were personal connections of the founders or early hires. A part-time contractor (the third or fourth employee) called people he knew, such as at Greenhouse Software.
- **Coda:** The "Alpha 2" customers were recruited "one or two steps out from friends of the company." They included a jewelry shop run by an employee's wife, a tech company started by co-founder Alex DeNeui, and Box, which came via the head of recruiting.
- **Census:** Half of the first 10 came through co-founder Sean Lynch, who targeted ex-Dropbox people at new product-led companies. The other half were people Boris Jabes knew, including Fivetran, which he knew from YC.
- **Hex:** A few of the first 10 came from the founder's data-space network. Barry McCardel is confused by founders who start something far outside their wheelhouse, because a domain you know well comes with a built-in network.
- **Okta:** The founders used Salesforce and past-job contacts, alumni networks, and their angel investors' LinkedIn networks. They targeted 15 to 18 new IT contacts at different companies per month for six months and hit 85%-plus of that quota.
- **Gusto:** The first 10 were friends starting businesses in California. Most were tech startups from the YC batch, plus non-tech businesses such as a children's swimming camp.
- **Ramp:** Early customers were people with a close, trusting relationship, such as an early employee from the founders' last startup. Later ones came from co-building (Candid, Ro), friends, finance teams, semi-cold outreach and VC introductions. Truebill, one of the first 50, was a cold email to someone they had met years before.
- **Notion:** The first 10 were friends, family, fellow San Francisco startup founders, and investor connections.

Shishir Mehrotra of Coda disagrees with the common advice that early customers must not be connected to you. Lenny quotes him: honest feedback is "actually higher if they know you."

## Cold outbound works if you make it creative and targeted

Lenny calls the effectiveness of cold outbound in the early stages one of the biggest surprises of his interviews. His advice is that if responses are poor, get more creative and more targeted.

His examples:
- **Figma:** Designers pick tools by seeing what other designers use, so Figma had to go bottom-up. Dylan Field built a custom script to find the most influential designers on Twitter and cold-DM'd them a Figma demo.
- **Retool:** David Hsu bought a Crunchbase account, shared with five other companies to save money.
  - They filtered by last fundraise date and vertical.
  - They skipped SaaS, because SaaS companies don't build many internal tools. They wanted operationally heavy companies such as delivery and fintech.
  - They emailed the CTO and the VP of operations, since engineering feels the pain of not being able to build tools and operations feels the pain of not having them.
  - This landed DoorDash, Rappi and Brex.
- **Zip:** The founders chose cold outbound over friends on purpose, so the first 10 would buy because they saw value and not out of obligation. They used only LinkedIn and asked for advice, not a sale, until they knew who to target. Their first customer, Deserve (80 people), paid roughly $1,000 to $10,000 after the CFO's calls. They "landed on seven, I think."
- **Gusto:** The founders hustled for every customer. One came via a swimming instructor's brother, and another from asking a flower shop owner what she used for payroll.
- **Persona:** "We would build anything for anybody," as long as it related to identity.

## Tap your investors' networks by doing the digging yourself

Lenny says this bucket is small but real, especially with YC. The tactic is not to ask investors for introductions. Dig into their networks, including YC batch-mates, cold-email those founders, and ask them for intros to other founders who fit.

- **Vanta:** Christina Cacioppo says the first 10 customers came through YC, and "this is why we did YC." She skipped the current batch, since those companies were too early to care about SOC 2 in 2018. She went through the list of older YC companies and prioritized it with her YC partner. She also emailed anyone who had said "compliance" on Bookface, YC's internal forum, in the prior decade.
- **Amplitude:** Spenser Skates warns against selling to your own YC batch: "A YC company is a speculative investment, not a business yet." Amplitude's first community was ex-Zynga product managers. An investor introduced them to the CTO of Super Lucky Casino, who spread the word in Zynga community groups.
- **Sprig:** All of the first 10 customers came from cold emails to YC companies. Thunkable was the first customer with no personal link, and installed the product during the demo. First Round Capital runs a "customer discovery program" that works like an SDR service. It sends cold emails to your target customers, aims for five meetings a week, and reviews feedback every two weeks. The goal is to reach $1M ARR before hiring salespeople.
- **Carta (eShares):** Early customers came from angel investors, such as Andy Palmer of Tamr, and from sister portfolio founders.

## Join communities and networks, and add value first

Lenny reports that a surprising number of startups found early customers through old-school networking and community, offline and online. Sometimes the founders created the community.

- **Snyk:** Snyk launched a freemium beta and had no paid version for nearly a year. It promoted the beta to open-source maintainers and dev communities. The first users came from meetups, conferences, online content and outreach to dev thought leaders. It reached thousands of users quickly, and the first (tiny) customers came from that base a year later.
- **Plaid:** Growth came from word of mouth among developers and PMs. The founders met fintech founders to learn their problems, and spent time on forums, IRC, meetups and startup accelerators.
- **Databricks:** The founders ran AMP Camps, hackathons using their open source software, with hands-on help for machine learning. About 70 people came.
- **Persona:** The founders went to every First Round event and pitched anyone with identity needs. That's how they landed Urban Sitter. The first customer was a friend of a friend's vape shop that needed age verification.

His rule for communities: add value first, because no one wants to hear from you if you're only there to pitch.

## Build an audience with content before or after you start

Lenny was surprised by how many founders found early users through their online presence. Some started sharing before founding the company, and some after.

- **Front:** Mathilde Collin "inundate[d] the market with content" (writing, Hacker News, Twitter) and got 3,000 beta sign-ups, mostly B2B.
- **Linear:** The founders announced on Twitter when they went full-time, linking to a landing page with an email signup. They built a wait list from their existing Twitter followings.
- **Hex:** The founders wrote about the problem they were solving and built a wait list. They sent feature-update emails, got replies like "I'd love to try it," and spun up accounts by hand. Even with a large wait list, reaching 10 customers took a while, because a technical, complex product needs enough surface area before you feel good onboarding people. Some "tastemaker" users then spread the product to others.

## Press and launches: PR rarely works, but some launches do

Lenny says PR can work but rarely does. Three startups he studied had meaningful early PR success.

- **Amplitude:** A TechCrunch launch in early 2014 brought a handful of customers, including Keepsafe. Spenser Skates still calls PR "normally... a terrible channel" compared with communities and word of mouth.
- **Canva:** Funding announcements made press easy to get.
- **Slack:** A press blitz, built on the team's prior experience, drew 8,000 invite requests on day one and 15,000 two weeks later. The lesson Lenny quotes: "Don't underestimate the power of traditional media when you launch."

Sometimes you just launch and users come:
- **Segment:** It launched on Hacker News, and engineers tried it on side projects and then told the founders what they needed to bring it into their companies. The advice: "launch, launch, launch."
- **Stytch:** A non-friend developer put it into production eight hours after a 20-minute call and API keys, before the public beta existed. Later, publishing the seed raise on the company blog and a retweet from Benchmark's Chetan Puttagunta drove demo sign-ups. About 40% to 50% converted to demos, and roughly 20% of those onboarded.
- **Loom:** A Product Hunt launch brought thousands of extension downloads by the end of the day.

Lenny says Canva did all of the above. Its two-year wait list reached about 10,000 people, and it lined up embargoed press with TechCrunch and ZDNet. Launch night was anticlimactic, with users dripping in. Converting press into users is hard, because articles often don't link to the product. The first week brought a couple thousand sign-ups and the next about 5,000. Growth then compounded from many small channels (Cameron Adams: "never one silver bullet"). Talkative early users, such as social media managers and bloggers, drove the first 6 to 12 months.

## Trust is the secret weapon: start close and move outward

Lenny sees the seven channels as concentric circles of decreasing trust and increasing distance from the founder. His takeaway is to start with the highest-trust channels and work outward.

Supporting points from founders:
- **Figma:** A recommendation from someone you trust beats a marketing team's. Figma deliberately mapped who designers listen to, using Twitter connections.
- **Ramp:** Early customers took a chance because of a close trust relationship, and the founders promised to obsess over saving them money. The scale was small enough, and they had backups, that the risk was acceptable.
- **Census:** Knowing the founders from YC meant skipping the "niceties of doing customer discovery."
- **Salesforce:** Marc Benioff found it hardest to convince the first prospect, since most people don't want to be first. Salesforce targeted pioneers who wanted to take part in something new. The first was Blue Martini Software, a company he had invested in.
- **Persona:** For security products, big mature companies won't trust a tiny startup with a PII system. That limit forced the founders to keep asking "Why would this person buy us?"
