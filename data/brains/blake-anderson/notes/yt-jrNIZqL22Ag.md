## Start doing: Blake's 80/20 rule against analysis paralysis

- Blake says founders stuck in "endless research and studying and devising the perfect launch strategy" never launch. Studying marketing without doing it gets you nowhere.
- Spend 80% of your time doing and 20% studying and reflecting. When you study, use real case studies, not theory.
- He has "never purchased a course on marketing in my entire life". The best marketers he knows didn't learn from courses.
- The loop is: try, fail, succeed, analyze what worked and what didn't. This applies to any domain.
- He rebuts three common excuses:
  - "I need to learn more first": you learn by doing.
  - "I don't want to waste money": you can create free content yourself.
  - "I don't know any creators": start DMing them.
- His own first attempts failed too. The answer to failing is to keep getting shots up, not to study more.

## Marketing the emotional benefit, not the features

- Blake defines marketing at its most primitive as understanding how people think and what motivates them, then using that to capture attention.
- The most successful apps tap core desires: sexual attraction, social status, self-improvement and entertainment.
- His examples:
  - Rizz GPT went viral by tapping people's desire to be charismatic with members of the opposite sex.
  - Umax went viral because it validates how attractive people think they are.
  - Cal AI went viral because it helps people reach their fitness goals.
- Marketing should focus on the emotional benefit and the problem solved, not features: "don't tell me what your app does tell me how it will make my life better".
- His context is single-player utility apps: apps that solve a problem and need no complex backend. Viral marketing aims to minimize friction between launch and millions of users.

## Rapid iteration: test many hooks and let data decide

- The goal is to optimize for learning until you find scalable strategies.
- Umax example: early on he wrote a list of hooks and tested 5 to 10 across the company's own accounts and across influencers.
  - "POV you use the Umax app to glow up": a creator shows a side-by-side of face ratings before and after, for example 55 to 95.
  - "Here are the best haircuts for your face shape": creators scan their face and show the recommended haircut in the app.
- They doubled down on the winners. This took Umax to over $600,000 in monthly revenue within 3 months.
- He says that even though he's good at marketing, he can't come up with a hook that works every time. Don't get emotionally attached to any single angle; let the data guide you.
- Pottery class story: one class had a whole semester to make a single best pot, the other was graded on the number of pots. The quantity class made better pots too, by learning through trial and error. When marketing a new product "you have unlimited pots", so make more and learn faster.

## Method 1: Internal UGC (most profitable, least scalable, best place to start)

- Internal UGC is content created and posted to company-owned accounts. It is the most profitable way to market an app and the best starting point with little to no capital.
- Rizz GPT example:
  - Tens of accounts were named things like "get Rizz GPT" or "Rizz GPT app".
  - It started with the main account, now named Plug AI, run by his co-founders Alex and Evan.
  - They began with video demos of the app, then shifted quickly to Rizz slideshow content. In the first month of that shift the account alone generated nearly 50 million views.
- Once winning strategies were known, they hired teams of UGC creators to run accounts. Four of five of those accounts flopped, but one produced a 20x return.
- Process:
  1. Study viral hooks and content in your app's niche.
  2. Start creating content yourself and figure out what works.
  3. Use what you learned to hire UGC creators to run their own accounts and scale it. Retain those who do well and replace those who don't.

## Method 2: Influencer partnerships (very scalable, hard to do right)

- This is the next most profitable way to scale. Blake says you should probably study psychology and statistics to get good at it, and he plans future videos on it.
- Finding influencers:
  - You need a strong understanding of the content and influencers in your niche.
  - "If I DM'd 10 influencers and nobody responded", the answer is that you need to DM hundreds.
  - Once you work with them, ask them to refer you to other creators they know.
- Pricing:
  - Pay based on average view count. A creator averaging 100,000 views might get roughly $100 to $1,000 depending on niche.
  - A contract (for example 4 posts per month) is usually much cheaper than one-off videos.
- Vetting: analyze the comment section. An engaged, largely American audience is a good sign. A comment section full of bot accounts leaving fire emojis will not convert.
- Attribution:
  - Direct attribution for organic social is "currently next to impossible".
  - Instead, watch your app's conversion dashboard and the influencer's post in real time, and assess how well views convert into downloads and purchases.
  - Build an intuitive understanding of each creator and how well they convert.

## Method 3: Platform ads (most scalable, lowest profit share, needs capital)

- Platform ads are the most scalable method but the least profitable as a percentage of revenue, and they need the most upfront capital. Blake would not prioritize them for a new app developer.
- Main channels: Meta, TikTok, Google and Apple Search.
- Steps:
  1. Compile a large number of creatives: a mix of videos and images, in different content styles covering every angle you can market the app from.
  2. Install the necessary SDKs for attribution.
  3. Run campaigns with small budgets first to find winning creatives and ad strategies, then scale up.
- Ads work best for apps with high LTV (a lot of money per user). Low-LTV apps work better through organic marketing, which is why he recommends UGC and influencer partnerships for people getting started.
