## Low-maintenance consumer apps at 50%+ margins

- Blake built the first two App Mafia apps, RizzGPT and UMAX. Each does roughly $4M a year, and the wider group (with Cal AI and Quitter) is just over $50M in annual revenue.
- His thesis: "If you build a good product, you don't have to maintain it. The code continues to provide the service on its own." With the marketing system set up correctly, "it'll just continue printing."
- These two apps run at over 50% profit margins. Each has one full-time employee, a social media manager, plus a VA. Blake says he personally does nothing on them.
- Revenue is flat rather than growing, and he reports no churn problem. The apps still run on new downloads: the revenue is "primarily almost entirely for new users."
- The model is subscription (weekly plans are mentioned), fed by continuous new-user acquisition.

## How RizzGPT launched: ChatGPT wrapped as a consumer app

- Blake first tried a college social marketplace app with a co-founder. It found some traction at his own school, failed to scale to other schools, and fizzled.
- Facing graduation and not wanting a job, he asked "what is the quickest thing I can build that's going to make some money."
- His reasoning:
  - People should be packaging ChatGPT into apps.
  - More consumption happens on phones than laptops.
  - So build a product and market it on social media.
- The product was RizzGPT, an AI dating assistant for guys who can't talk to girls. The user uploads a screenshot and it suggests what to say.
- He expected $10-20K a month, enough to live on while building other businesses. Instead it hit about $80,000 MRR within 3 days of launch.
- The driver was influencer marketing with underground, niche, underpriced creators who had cult-like followings.
- This was the very infancy of the AI app game. Before that, mobile was mostly social apps (NGL, TBH, Gas, Instagram wrapped).

## Three macro trends behind the app boom

Blake argues the opportunity comes from three forces, and nearly every new player uses at least two, usually all three:

1. **New technology.** There was no major technology shift from the App Store's advent until ChatGPT (2022-2023). Most big apps (MyFitnessPal, Headspace, Calm) date from the early 2010s. LLMs let you embed real functionality into a single-player product.
2. **Easier building.** Previously you had to hire developers, raise capital or be highly skilled. Now you can code with LLMs, and database providers like Supabase are much easier to use.
3. **Dropshipping-style marketing.** It is easy to get a product in front of people through creators.

## Is the app space saturated, and does influencer marketing still work?

- Blake concedes the low-hanging-fruit AI app ideas have been taken. But "if you have a novel idea there's still plenty of opportunity on the marketing front."
- On influencer competition: it is easy to think more apps means more bidding for creators. Yet the vast majority of influencer promos are for physical products, not apps.

## How Blake responds to cold DMs

- He shares the example of Zach Yadegari's cold DM, which led with credibility and asked for nothing up front: "I'm a 16-year-old founder and CEO of an unblocked gaming website that has gotten over 4 million users and is monetized with AdSense."
- Zach then referenced Blake's "over 900,000 downloads in 3 months" and asked to learn from a mentor.
- Blake says leading with credibility or value beats a vague ask. Being 16 and already building also appealed to his wish to help younger founders.
- The failing version is "yo bro, thinking about building an app that does this, what do you think?" Blake's reaction: "no chance, dude, I'm not answering that."
