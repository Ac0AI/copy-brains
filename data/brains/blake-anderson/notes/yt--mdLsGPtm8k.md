## How Blake finds and validates app ideas

- He builds around core human insecurities or desires, not around an "overintellectualized use case" he then has to make viral. Examples: RizzGPT (AI tells you what to say on dating apps), UMAX (AI rates your face and says how to become more attractive), Cal AI (AI calorie tracker). Making an over-intellectualized idea viral is, in his words, "a much much harder task."
- Being first to market on a new AI capability is the biggest source of virality. When RizzGPT launched on TikTok, people reacted with "wait, you can have AI tell you what to say on dating apps?"
- He times launches to model releases. He saw the looksmaxing niche growing and GPT Vision about to ship. He built UMAX so he could plug in the API the day GPT Vision became production ready, and launched about 2 days after it hit production-level rate limits.
- Looksmaxing was a tight-knit niche (mostly guys aged about 16 to 24) with almost no monetization. UMAX had "immediate product market fit."
- He thinks one-function viral AI apps were easier in 2024 than 2025. Simple use cases (talk to girls, rate your face, track calories) are taken. Newer viral products are more complex (he cites Cluely, which helps people cheat on interviews).
- He stays at the frontier of AI and moves on quickly. He warns against putting "complete blinders" on for years while ignoring new AI developments.
- He has worked full-time on five projects in about two years: RizzGPT, UMAX, Cal AI, Apex and 10X.
- On whether to stick or quit: many people stay on wrong projects too long, and many quit before a project matures. He asks himself whether he is moving on for good reasons or just wanting new excitement. He cites Speechify, which took years before it took off.
- He avoids copying what everyone else is doing ("mimetic desire"): "you are generally going to get the same results as everybody else." He follows curiosity and optimizes for learning.
- He avoids projects where failure teaches nothing transferable, such as day trading (unless you also sell content or courses about it).

## Distribution-first thinking and pricing

- He says it helps to know your distribution strategy before building. Engineers tend to ask only "is it technically feasible?" and growth people only "does the channel exist?" He advises understanding both sides.
- His shorthand: study Silicon Valley for product, study dropshipping for marketing. Dropshipping differentiates on how cheaply you can generate views. Silicon Valley puts much more weight on product. The value is at the intersection.
- Distribution channel informs pricing:
  - Paid ads suit a higher LTV and a higher price point, for example $20 a month, or a free trial then $100 a year.
  - Organic suits a lower price point, for example $30 a year or $4 a week.
- The product direction and the distribution channel feed back into each other.
- He applied the TikTok Shop playbook (creators and affiliates making viral product videos) to his own apps. He chose not to move into TikTok Shop because he was optimizing for learning and wanted to stay in AI.

## Influencer and organic marketing tactics

- He takes many shots. He says if you aren't "DMing hundreds of influencers per day" and wonder why partnerships fail, you aren't taking enough shots. Don't fixate on a handful of names.
- Reach out to every creator whose audience overlaps your target demographic. That is how you learn whether influencer marketing works for your app.
- Pricing: he usually pays a flat rate with a minimum-views clause, for example $10,000 with the expectation of at least 2 million views.
- Watch your target CPM. His example was a $5 target CPM. Some niches cost far more: edtech is expensive, and finance and fintech creators run $50+ per 1,000 views. Find niches that aren't too expensive and check view quality.
- If the app isn't inherently viral, influencer marketing is an uphill battle because you depend on organic recommendation algorithms.
- Early on, UMAX hit $100,000 revenue in month one with roughly 8 to 12 creators (he says "maybe less, maybe like eight"), who had high follower counts and decent views.
- UMAX revenue went from $100,000 in month 1 to $200,000 in month 2 and $700,000 in month 3.
- RizzGPT launched in July 2023 and went from zero to $83,000 MRR in about three days.

## Choosing creators: the comment-section test

- His main selection metric is engaged comment sections, not follower count or views. History: followers mattered first, then views, and now content quality matters most.
- A video can get a few hundred thousand views in 6 seconds and not convert at all. An engaged comment section signals one or both of these:
  - The creator has a deeply trusting, dedicated fan base.
  - The content is informative or engaging enough that people leave thoughtful replies.
- What to look for:
  - Thoughtful replies and insights, not "a bunch of fire emojis."
  - Top comments with a good number of likes.
  - Sub-replies and conversations. Many comments with no likes on any of them is a bad sign.
  - "You want a community to exist within the comment section."
- He says it isn't a perfect one-to-one mapping, but it is "a very underrated metric."
- Viral views alone are not the goal, because viewers who won't engage in the comments are unlikely to click through to the product.

## Tracking organic campaigns and briefing creators

- Organic has no platform dashboard, which he sees as part of the opportunity: "If it was easy, everybody would do it."
- His team uses automated spreadsheets. They input video links and track views, likes and engagement over time, then map that to downloads. They also compute median view count, average likes and average comments. Deeper judgment stays intuitive.
- For beginners, he says you don't need heavy tooling. Watch views and downloads over time, and build an intuition for which hooks and creators convert.
- He says good social media marketing comes down to statistics and psychology: psychology for hooks, messaging and negotiating with creators, and statistics for inferring what converts.
- Content brief for every creator, shared as a PDF before they start. It includes:
  - Ways to integrate the product, plus example ideas.
  - Links to videos that did well.
  - Basic stipulations, for example show the product for at least 2 seconds and before the 15-second mark, and tag the brand.
- Creators send the video for approval before posting. He says you can relax this as you scale and trust certain creators.
- The brief saves both sides time and signals professionalism.

## Co-founders and how he structures partnerships

- He is a big advocate for co-founders. His apps were built with partners:
  - RizzGPT: two co-founders, 3 people in total.
  - UMAX: his older brother (part-time, backend help from a data-analytics job) and Sam Zia, a looksmaxing influencer (part-time at first). Both went full-time after revenue took off.
  - Cal AI: four co-founders. Zach and Henry came to him after seeing his success. Zach gave him a list of ideas and Blake suggested "AI calorie tracker." Jake Castillo joined later as chief marketing officer and fourth co-founder.
- Cal AI structure:
  - Blake, Zach and Henry started with a three-way equal split.
  - Blake acted as coach, providing marketing strategy and capital from earlier ventures, with less day-to-day involvement.
  - Zach covered growth, marketing and design, with some engineering. Henry covered engineering.
  - He says Cal AI will do over $30 million in revenue in 2025.
- His reasoning on equity: an unequal split would have demotivated people, and someone might have left. "It's a lot better to have 50% of a watermelon than 100% of a grape." Great co-founders are "multipliers." Alone, the RizzGPT team might have made $10k to $20k a month; together they hit $250k a month within three months.
- The upside is capped by whether you can capture it, not by your share. Co-founders can also go bad; he has worked with people where it didn't work out.
- Define expectations clearly from the start. The biggest issues come from unclear roles or mismatched ambitions (one wants a billion-dollar business, another wants $50,000 a month). Also address non-delivery on engineering.
- Finding co-founders is hard. He says they usually come from organic relationships over time. Rushing to find someone yields lower-quality candidates.
- A founder split he went through: on RizzGPT he and a co-founder disagreed on product direction and "one of us has to leave." He left, kept his equity and later rebuilt the friendship.

## Opportunities he sees for builders

- Zero to $10k a month: become highly skilled with AI tools, since the skill applies to design, copywriting, development and marketing. He gave the example of a designer who out-produced nine others in a 2-hour paid test using an AI icon tool.
- Specifically, build and distribute AI-generated content across platforms. If you can generate a few million views a month for a company, you can charge $10k+ a month. He thinks few people do this well. Another version: start a UGC agency and work with app founders.
- App-to-web payments agency: a recent US court ruling lets apps offer web payments. Apple takes 30% (15% for apps under $1M a year in revenue). He and others tested it and see at least a 20% increase in proceeds, and "well over 20%" in his own case. He didn't share the exact number.
  - He says it is an unsolved problem space with many things to test (he has about 10 ideas).
  - His agency idea: offer to optimize an app's web flow for free in exchange for a reference, then source paying clients who pay only if results are good. He thinks an agency could scale past $50,000 a month in profit.
- "Sell the pickaxes" logic: building AI apps has a lower probability of success, but more people overall will make life-changing money from apps than from servicing them. Servicing has a higher chance of success and is less grindy. He cites Cursor and Lovable as servicing companies that became multi-billion-dollar businesses. He builds consumer products because he finds them fun.
- AI hardware (his current side direction): building little devices with his brother using local inference (an open-source LLM on a small processor with SD storage, no network).
  - First project: an AI pocket recorder, built from an ESP32, a microphone, an SD card, Bluetooth and a lithium-ion battery. He cites Plaud as a model: about $160 for the device with roughly $25 cost of goods, plus a $250 a year subscription with inference costing "pennies," making multiple millions a month.
  - Next idea: an AI smart speaker running a local model on a Jetson-class processor. He expects powerful LLMs to fit in a watch within 18 to 24 months.
  - He says the chance of this becoming a business is not high, but he learns either way.

## Principles for taking advice and working

- Optimize for learning: aim to finish a project with either material success or at least something new learned.
- Do what you find interesting, because it is where you will put in the most hours and creative output. Have intellectual hobbies, since you can't predict how they will help professionally later.
- Treat advice cautiously. People are "very complex systems," so universal principles are hard to create and much general advice doesn't apply. His approach is to test everything and keep what works for you, and to rely on close relationships with people who know you well. He cites Peter Thiel as someone who famously avoids giving advice.
- His own habits: he dislikes networking, turned off Twitter DMs, and keeps a small close circle. He doesn't claim this is advice for everyone, and notes Zach is better at relationships.
- Background: he wrote an ebook at age 9 ("How to Make Money as a Kid"), mowed lawns, and sold candy at school. He also tried dropshipping, Instagram meme pages and Ethereum mining. Earlier ventures made between $100 and about $20,000 each time. Before RizzGPT he built a college marketplace app (with a technical teammate) that made no money. Prototyping the first RizzGPT came from a roommate asking what to message a girl, which he tried in ChatGPT with prompt engineering.
