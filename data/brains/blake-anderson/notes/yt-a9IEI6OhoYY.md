## Background and credibility

- Blake Anderson was 23 at the time. He "stumbled into" the AI mobile app space a little over a year earlier.
- His apps:
  - Riz GPT (now Plug AI): a little over 3M downloads, about $200K monthly revenue.
  - Umax: over 10M downloads, about $500K monthly revenue now (closer to $400K), peaking around $700K/month.
  - Cal AI: launched a little over 6 months before the interview, about 2M downloads, over $1M monthly revenue.
- He is launching a new content-heavy company called Apex.
- His thesis: the space sits at the intersection of new AI functionality and novel distribution tactics pioneered by e-commerce people. A small group of 18 to 22 year olds runs it.
- AI lets you deliver personalized functionality updated in real time. It also lets you build things that used to need costly proprietary tech. Umax's facial analysis used GPT Vision with essentially zero upfront development cost beyond his own time.

## Iterative virality and influencer relationships

- Don't bet everything on one trendy way to go viral. Run a highly iterative process from early on:
  - Come up with new hooks and new positioning for who you market to.
  - Split test them continuously across niches, videos and creators.
  - Track views per hook and conversion per view. Over time this builds a mental or real statistical model that informs distribution.
- This is the same approach Meta and TikTok apply to creatives, applied to influencers, in-house UGC and hook generation.
- Example for a career app: find ed-tech, study-tip, job-application and productivity influencers. Write different hooks per niche and test them across creators.
- He does not use influencer marketplaces, databases or agencies. He calls the messaging that you should "outright wrong".
- What he does instead is build personal relationships with influencers:
  - They get hundreds of DMs and emails daily and can't tell serious companies from unserious ones.
  - Once you're embedded in the industry, they connect you to whoever you need.
  - Creators like Sam Zia (220K followers), Dawson Gibbs (170K) and Carter (280K) comment on his posts without being paid.
- To solve the cold start, prioritize both quantity and quality early:
  - Reach out to thousands of creators.
  - For a creator you really want, DM them on every platform, "message their mom," message their girlfriend.
  - Then treat them well and become friends.

## Turning an idea into a product

- Ideation starts with virality: which tools in the product will do well on social media? That informs the design.
- For Umax, the six-factor rating system drew the most likes, comments and engagement, so it went viral.
- With an all-in-one product, split test product hooks too. For example, test three tools on social to see which goes most viral and engineer around it.
- Design process:
  - Make wireframes.
  - Have a professional designer or an Upwork freelancer turn them into fleshed-out UI.
  - Then build.
- Building:
  - He used ChatGPT to teach him to code Riz GPT and the first Umax, starting with SwiftUI and pasting code into Xcode.
  - He now codes in Cursor with React Native, a month in.
  - He did not code Cal AI.
  - If you have capital or a great CTO, he says you don't need to code yourself. There is no one-size-fits-all answer.
- Blend functionality with virality. Take a functional feature and make it shareable, e.g. a six-factor score for each section of a resume.

## Idea 1: Career AI

- Career tools today are fragmented: resume analyzers, personality-based career quizzes, application courses. Combine them into one app.
- Pricing: about $50 a month for an all-in-one tool "feels pretty obvious."
- Why it works: universities sell themselves as setting you up for a career, and finding jobs and internships is hard.
- Features:
  - Resume analysis with a six-factor score per section, designed around popular ed-tech content and shared via ed-tech creators.
  - Career quiz ending in a shareable result screen.
  - A trend such as seniors getting recommended computer science and asking why it took until senior year to realize they shouldn't study it.
- Distribution: Instagram Reels and TikTok aimed at students, plus Twitter/X and LinkedIn as relatively untapped channels.

## Idea 2: Journal AI and idea 3: Language tutoring AI

- **Journal AI:**
  - An "AI second brain" framed as a journal, with memory storage and a UI built for journaling.
  - Analysis covers productivity, emotional support and relationship analysis.
  - It need not be technically robust: essentially ChatGPT with more complex memory and a journaling-friendly UI.
  - Thesis: people already use ChatGPT as a journal. Journals give nothing back, and this returns objective analysis.
  - Brand it as a journal, not a therapist. He might name it something friendlier like "Juny" or "Journey".
- **Language AI:**
  - Compete with Duolingo by integrating new AI functionality faster and building a dynamic UI instead of static modules.
  - Voice model costs are high now but he expects them to fall.
  - Learners are high-intent. Taking about 1% of a roughly $1B market would be a $100M app.
  - His answer to "Duolingo will build this": incumbents' incentives aren't aligned. Cal AI heard the same about MyFitnessPal, whose engineers and social media managers probably hold little equity after repeated sales.
  - Cal AI's calorie estimates from images are significantly better than MyFitnessPal's, and it pioneers new social marketing.
  - Few people try to beat entrenched players, which makes it easier to succeed with these tactics.
  - Frame it as an app that tutors through its UI, not as an "AI tutor."

## Naming and the "AI" suffix

- Appending AI helps when innovating in a stagnant industry (Cal AI in calorie tracking), where AI signals sophistication.
- Skip it where accuracy is critical, such as a medical document service, because people may fear errors.
- His co-founder Zach tweeted that adding AI is a negative signal. They resolved it with the distinction above.
- Cal AI is the name he's proudest of.
- Umax was named for a planned all-in-one self-improvement ecosystem. In retrospect, with that plan he'd have called it "looksmax AI," as a copycat did.
- Naming is not his strength; he points to others for that.

## Paywalls and pricing

- He uses Superwall (not affiliated) and no longer uses RevenueCat. It makes split testing easy and gives all the data.
- Most of the industry optimizes users multiplied by profit per user. He optimizes users raised to a power greater than one, multiplied by profit per user.
- He accepts lower LTV per download for more people using the product. Reasons:
  - Dominate the market faster.
  - Increase word of mouth, which is nearly impossible to measure once you run ads constantly.
- Standard industry practice: take users through an onboarding flow (quiz, push notification permission, personal info "to customize the app") and hit them with the paywall at the end. It converts far better than other placements, and he has done this on his apps.
- He's ambivalent about the ethics ("I don't feel good about it"). He argues it doesn't move the needle much and that the money funds what he's building.

## Finding more app ideas

- Use Sensor Tower to size the other players in a space.
- Hike or walk, where he thinks clearly.
- Deliberately scroll social media with burner accounts curated to one topic. When building Umax, the account had only looksmaxxing content. Put yourself in the mind of the content's consumer.
- Obvious ideas are often best: calorie tracking, language learning, journaling. He rates them "10 out of 10" because they're nearly impossible to fail with.
- Riz GPT was a 10 out of 10 for a first small win, with a lower ceiling.
- Follow the new styles of execution, build functionality and distribution, and build small MVPs rather than boiling the ocean.
