## From a dating-app "wingman" to Rizz GPT: how the first product launched

- In June 2023 Blake moved back home after graduating from Tulane. He worked from his attic and a coffee shop, with no income and no plan B.
- The idea came from a college roommate who kept asking what to say to girls on dating apps. ChatGPT had launched a few months earlier, and Blake believed it could generate those replies programmatically.
- The product, Rizz GPT, lets a user upload a screenshot of a conversation and get suggested replies.
- His co-founders had social media marketing experience but none with consumer apps.
- Launch results: instant product-market fit and virality. $50 promos with underground "rizz" accounts on TikTok drove 200,000 downloads and $80K MRR within a week.
- He later scaled the app to a little over $200,000 MRR, then split amicably from his co-founders over product direction and kept his equity.
- Tools and skills: he double-majored in finance and CS, but says Tulane's CS program was weak. ChatGPT acted as his tutor and wrote most of the early code, in SwiftUI.
- The launch version lacked basics like notifications, reviews and a formal back end (no user table). He caught up by asking people who had built successful apps what to do after finding product-market fit.
- He argues going full-time beats a side hustle. He says people who plan to take a job and pursue entrepreneurship later find their expenses scale and quitting becomes impossible.

## The repeatable playbook behind Rizz GPT and UMAX

Blake describes one strategy used for both apps:
1. Identify a popping niche on TikTok.
2. Build a product that solves a consumer need in that niche.
3. Scale through creator promos.

- UMAX came after the split, while he was hopping between friends' couches. He saw the looksmaxing trend and brought in Sam as a co-founder and his older brother.
- The need it serves: guys want to know how attractive they are and how to become more attractive (hair, skincare, etc.).
- After the first few promos, he says it became "a game of scaling from there."
- The best month so far was February, at about $700,000 in revenue.
- Across all his apps he is at roughly $1 million a month in revenue.
- He describes a two-part timing edge:
  - Market timing: Google Trends for looksmaxing was steadily rising.
  - Technology timing: the GPT Vision API had just been released. It let him build a face-analysis app without custom-training an ML vision model, which he says could cost tens to a hundred thousand dollars.
- His framework for ideas: ask what new technology was just enabled, and what popular space it can be applied to. Where both meet there is little competition.
- He calls UMAX an "opportunistic play" for a trend and not a guaranteed 30-year market. He says "ready, fire, aim" suits that kind of play.
- He says to take your time if you are building an empire where public perception matters from the start.

## Fighting a copycat competitor with a $200K influencer blitz

- A competitor copied UMAX's outputs and ratings feature almost exactly. They reached in about 2 weeks the downloads UMAX took a little over a month to reach, and pumped influencers from the start.
- Blake had wanted to go slower and improve the offering, including a course-like section on skincare and self-improvement. The competitor cared only about the ratings, which he says are the most viral component.
- His decision: either let them become the top name and try to overtake them later, or go big immediately. He chose to go big.
- Over about 3 days he deployed a little over $200,000 on influencers, which was all the money the company had made to that point.
- Tactics:
  - He made a list of the biggest influencers in the niche and DMed them.
  - He asked for exclusive contracts, so they could not work with the competitor.
  - One example: a $50,000 wire for a month, for a set number of posts, with approval rights and no view clauses (very favorable to the creator).

## Influencer marketing math: RPM vs CPM

- A promo is profitable if its RPM (revenue per thousand views) after variable costs exceeds its CPM (cost per thousand views).
- Step one is to find the average RPM within a niche. Example: if an influencer's 1 million views produce $5,000 in sales, the RPM is $5.
- With a $5 RPM, he would target influencers at about a $3 CPM, paying $3,000 per million views.
- Variable costs differ by business. For physical goods they are the cost of goods, shipping and Stripe fees. For his apps the main one is Apple's cut.
- Apple takes 15% up to $1 million in sales and 30% after that.
- He says Android users notoriously don't convert, so Apple's marketplace is where paying users are.
- Apple also gives free distribution once an app does well. About half of UMAX's downloads now come from Apple pushing it out, driven by past downloads and positive reviews.
- Hit rate: DM about 100 influencers, about 10 respond, and perhaps 3 produce profitable ads. Most people fail because they can't identify those 3.
- Knowing your market and being your own customer helps you judge which influencers and messaging will convert.
- Lessons from his teenage ventures:
  - From selling counterfeit NBA jerseys he learned to negotiate promos with Instagram meme pages.
  - Those deals paid a set amount as long as the post hit at least Y views, with an incentive if it exceeded Z.
- Paid ads: Rizz GPT and UMAX used little paid advertising because they are inherently viral. A promo post tends to outperform the creator's average, since people comment "no way bro is using AI to analyze his face." For Cal AI, his most recent app, he has started paid ads and is still assessing ROAS.

## Why apps: the gap between developers and social-media marketers

- Blake sees an opportunity in the overlap between two groups. Software developers don't understand social media virality, and "internet money" kids don't understand software development. Few people sit in the middle.
- His argument: in a niche where physical products earn around a $2 RPM, a competent app can earn about a $5 RPM. The people viewing that content have usually never seen an app for the space.
- With that higher RPM, you can pump money into creator promos faster than competitors who sell physical products. This works especially well if you also know how to generate views.
- He adds that consumer apps are largely a game of maximizing revenue per user and getting as many users as possible.
- Some developers he knows make millions a month and say they don't care what they build.
- He says he doesn't plan to stay in the consumer app space, because it isn't mission-driven and he doesn't find it deeply fulfilling.

## Iteration, doubling down and when to quit

- Blake's teenage ventures:
  - Raking leaves and shoveling driveways.
  - Selling candy.
  - Selling Madden Mobile coins.
  - Botting RuneScape.
  - Building an Ethereum mining machine.
  - Running Instagram meme page promos.
- Each made roughly $50 to a couple thousand dollars.
- His lesson: iteration is valuable because you learn fast, but eventually you must double down on your skills. He did lots of learning and little doubling down as a teenager.
- That wide skill set paid off when Rizz GPT took off.
- On the college marketplace app he was building before Rizz GPT, Harry Gestetner (founder of FanFix, who built a college marketplace app before FanFix) told him, kindly, that he wouldn't continue it. Blake says "sometimes you got to call it quits" is advice that is not prominent enough online.
- He says failure is acceptable if you learn. It is better to have failed and learned than to have failed without learning or never tried.
- He says the biggest driver of entrepreneurial success is not intelligence or hard work but risk tolerance and willingness to sacrifice.
- On his own edge: he says his superpower is thinking differently from most people. He credits creative logical insight and spotting new opportunities for Rizz GPT and UMAX, and says he is not the hardest worker or the most intelligent.
- His grind in 2023: after graduating he worked only on Rizz GPT, from a Starbucks from about 6am to 6pm, then continued at home. He says it didn't feel like a sacrifice at the time. After that period, he says, you can spend your time more freely, and he now works less than his 9-to-5 friends.

## Reinvesting profits and the Apex plan

- Blake's thinking on capital: he views money as a tool and leverage. He cites Walter Isaacson, his former professor and the biographer of Elon Musk and Steve Jobs, on mission-orientedness and risk tolerance. His example is Elon repeatedly pouring winnings into the next venture (Zip2 into PayPal, PayPal into SpaceX and Tesla).
- He quotes Peter Thiel's takeaway that maybe "the rest of us don't take enough" risk.
- He argues the highest-return investment is combining capital with his own skill set, rather than the S&P 500 or real estate.
- He says to work on things where, even if you fail, you come out ahead. In drop shipping or trading, failure leaves you with essentially nothing. A hard mission-driven pursuit leaves you with skills and knowledge even if it fails.
- Apex is his next company: an all-in-one male self-improvement ecosystem. It will include free apps (workouts, meditation, nutrition and others) and social media content from a creator house.
- The mission is to reorient how self-improvement and entrepreneurship are framed online, away from "get jacked to get girls" and "get money for materialistic things."
- Funding:
  - $500,000 from HF0, the San Francisco accelerator, for a few percent equity plus a SAFE note.
  - At least $500,000 of his own money from UMAX profits, probably more.
  - Spending on development, the house, food and flying influencers in to film.
  - Some profits go to his parents. UMAX and Rizz GPT earnings are being set aside to fund Apex.
- On HF0: it is a house of about 20 people and roughly 10 startups. He says it taught him what it means to be truly mission-oriented.
- His 10-year goal is to influence society positively by redefining self-improvement and entrepreneurship for young people.
- On co-founders: he says to pick people with moral integrity above all, and says it matters most when partners part ways. He notes the right choice depends on the time horizon and structure.
