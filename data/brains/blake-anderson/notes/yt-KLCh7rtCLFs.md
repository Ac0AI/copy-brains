## Why Blake leans into controversy and social capital

- He argues the value of social capital and notoriety is only increasing, and he sees it most clearly in hiring: "The more algorithmic reach and distribution that you have, the higher quality talent that you can attract."
- He posted a reel/TikTok saying he would start being "more of a douchebag on social media." His reasoning is that avoiding controversy is ego preservation: people stay risk averse to protect their image, which blocks the influence they could gain.
- His examples of people who rose through controversy: Trump (via The Apprentice), Andrew Tate, Elon Musk, AOC and Bernie Sanders. Some of it is calculated and some is just who they are.
- He is bullish on Cluely and Roy Lee for this reason. He thinks Cluely's product has a way to go, but "getting a crack team in place is most important," and controversy-driven reach lets you build that team and bigger companies over time.
- Case study: Zach (Cal AI) first contacted him via DM after seeing RZGPT and UMAX organically. Blake had posted an egotistical tweet about UMAX's initial traction (UMAX was his second app). He says inbound like this brings in smart, hardworking, mature people.
- The group's launch logic for their first product (an education/course offering) is the same trade: sacrifice some social capital and take backlash in return for attention and a following.

## Why software is the opportunity, and the thesis behind App Mafia

- Blake notes that short-form "entrepreneurship" content is dominated by social media marketing, drop shipping, day trading, meme coins and copywriting. He calls many of these poor long-term industries: zero-sum, heavily competed, low margin, and the money often comes from selling courses rather than running the business.
- His question: why is there no hype around building software? His answer is that it was inaccessible for decades.
  - Ten years earlier, an AI calorie tracker like Cal AI would have meant training custom ML vision models from scratch, needing lots of data and millions in funding, with no guarantee it worked.
  - Even a simple mobile app five years ago meant hiring developers or agencies (generally a bad strategy), needing capital, or having a CS degree.
- Today AI plus infrastructure like Firebase and Supabase means almost anyone "sufficiently intelligent" can build. None of the group has a CS master's or a big dev team.
- The aim of App Mafia is to make building software cool and accessible.

## Bootstrapping, risk and "yolo" spending

- He thinks raising money is wrongly treated as the default way to start. He also pushes back on the idea that bootstrapping means being a solo indie hacker with no team.
- His college experience: he took a Tulane entrepreneurship class that was essentially all pitch decks while he was working on a college marketplace app (which failed). His question was why such classes aren't about "just doing the damn thing."
- He thinks most institutions are built to be risk averse and protect the downside, so people study and analyze far more than they act. He adds that this isn't always wildly inefficient, since it keeps things from collapsing.
- His view of teachers: his own professor ran a drop-shipping tea store doing about $10k a month. Founders who really succeed rarely stop to teach. He did get Walter Isaacson (Elon Musk and Steve Jobs biographer) as a mentor and professor, and Isaacson's best advice to him was roughly "most advice is [bullshit]. Do things, lean into what works, take risk."
- Downside of bootstrapping: every dollar spent comes out of your own pocket, whereas VC-backed companies can take bigger swings. He says the group would still yolo everything into a play they believe in.
- He frames risk as a power law: for a ~$500k influencer spend, the Cal AI team expected to get back at least about $300k, counting direct response plus word of mouth, with upside in the millions. His rule: "whenever we get these opportunities we'll take them 10 times out of 10."
- It took the team a while to learn this. The first example he cites is Alex Eubank: roughly $100k a month on a deal when Cal AI was at about $500k a month, followed within months by a jump to over $2M a month, which he credits largely to Eubank and the influencers that deal brought in.
- On being risk tolerant: if everything burned down, they could lock themselves in a room for months and rebuild.

## Stepping-stone path and the team

- He says the "work on the hardest problem immediately" advice suits some people but not all. Examples he gives: Elon built Zip2 and PayPal before Tesla and SpaceX, Sam Altman did Loopt and YC before OpenAI, and Steve Jobs sold blue boxes before Apple. Bootstrapping fits this progressive path, whereas taking venture capital carries an implicit expectation of pursuing a decacorn-sized outcome.
- On solo founding: before his first full-time hire he bought into "one person, one billion-dollar company." After hiring one person, "I watched the productivity... skyrocket overnight," and he now calls the solo narrative "a complete scam" for most businesses. His advice is to find cracked individuals and hire them.
- He believes in knowing how to do almost everything without needing to be the best at it. Each founder in the group can code, design and market, which makes it easier to identify good hires. He has seen non-technical founders repeatedly hire terrible engineers because they can't tell a bad engineer from a good or great one, and the same applies to marketing.

## Prioritization and shipping fast (RZGPT as the example)

- His first successful app, RZGPT, launched as a mess: no reviews, no notifications, the app name on download was "RZGPT-roduction," and API keys were exposed in the front end. It still hit about $80K MRR within 3 days. Case studies were written about it.
- His lesson: identify the core components of the business and let everything else wait. The core is getting people to download, converting them, and giving them a good experience so they stay.
- He sees indie hackers optimizing ASO and tiny design tweaks while stuck around $400 MRR, which is effort on the wrong things.
- As a company grows, the principles you dismissed early (running a tight ship, more process) start to make sense. He compares it to understanding your parents' rules as you grow up.
- Perfectionism and low confidence slow people down. His view is to take life less seriously, accept you'll embarrass yourself and break things, and recognize that more decisions mean more data and stronger intuition. Someone who does nothing never builds mental models.

## Mindset: complex systems, advice and doubt

- He thinks everyone should study complex systems, because people are complex systems, not single-dimension optimization curves. Two people can take the same actions and get vastly different results, so there are no universal rules. His advice is to lean into your own attributes and strengths.
- He notes that successful founders are very different archetypes, and it is easy to pick three similar ones and ignore everyone outside that pattern.
- He points out that Elon's companies nearly went bankrupt several times. One example he cites: in 2008, when Tesla couldn't make payroll, the investors in the room declined to invest. Elon then said he would do the entire round himself despite having little money, and the room's dynamic shifted so investors wanted in. Survivorship bias hides the identical situations that ended in failure.
- His biggest challenge was doubt. He always felt he had massive potential but had nothing to show for it. By 22 and graduating college he had only worked DoorDash, tutoring, taking tests for other kids and mowing lawns: no internship, no W2, no second-round interview.
- Years of failure followed, even though he was smart, worked hard and took risks. He says most people never get out of that state, but accepting it and pushing forward usually pays off over a long enough time horizon.
- He believes the difference is perseverance, risk tolerance and sacrifice: most people try once or twice half-heartedly, then go back to the standard path. His own freedom framing is that step one is achieving freedom, after which you can go in any direction.
