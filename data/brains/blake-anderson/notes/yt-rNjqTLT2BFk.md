## Blake's path into AI apps and the RZ GPT launch

- Blake graduated from Tulane (finance major, some CS classes, never really learned to code). His senior-year college marketplace app, built with friends and a CTO, flopped. Of social apps he says: "Social apps are very hard to scale. I do not recommend building social apps, especially if you're a first-time founder."
- After graduating he moved home, was borrowing grocery money from his brother, and needed income. He spotted a niche of people showing off their "RZ" (chat game) on dating apps. A roommate on Hinge kept asking what to say to girls, and it was obvious ChatGPT could answer.
- He searched the App Store and found no apps doing it successfully. He built it by asking ChatGPT how to build it (Swift UI, API calls) and pasting the code into Xcode, in the early days of "vibe coding" before Cursor existed.
- Launch: about June 2023, roughly five months after ChatGPT's release. It hit $80,000 MRR within weeks and about $250,000 a month within three months.
- The app was renamed Plug AI after a lawsuit from a company already using the term "RZ" that had little traction.

## How RZ GPT went viral: dropshipping-style organic content

- Blake says the app went viral because "we took the dropshipping organic content strategy using these sort of underground influencers, UGC, whatever it may be. We applied it to AI apps."
- He pairs distribution (UGC, creator and influencer content, iterating hooks) with a product built around a trend already visible on social media.
- His marketing summary: identify the correct creators, identify the correct hooks for UGC, create the content, put it out into the world, continue to iterate, and double down on what works.

## UMAX: spotting the next wave and shipping on a new API

- When RZ GPT flatlined around $250,000 a month, Blake saw looksmaxxing taking over social media.
- He knew OpenAI's Vision API (image analysis) was about to launch, so he built the app first: take a photo of your face, get ratings and recommendations on how to look more attractive. The moment the Vision API dropped he plugged it in and launched.
- UMAX reached about $700,000 monthly revenue within roughly one and a half to two months.
- Unit economics he gives: API costs are marginal; Apple takes 30%; influencers are generally 30-40%; then team costs. Every app he has built has had a profit margin above 30%. At $700,000 a month in revenue, he says they took home over $200,000 a month in profit.
- Product design principle ("inspire action without creating insecurity"): UMAX has a floor rating of 55 out of 99; most users score 70+; a "potential" score is always 5-10 points above their rating; recommendations are mundane (go to the gym, eat less calorie-dense food, wash your face). No mewing, bone smashing or hormones.

## Cal AI: partnering and compounding growth

- A 16-year-old Zach Yadegari DM'd Blake after seeing RZ GPT and UMAX. After weeks of talking, Zach sent a list of ideas and Blake picked the AI calorie tracker. They built Cal AI together.
- Cal AI was not an immediate hit like RZ GPT and UMAX, but "the ceiling has been so much higher." Per Blake it roughly doubled month over month early on ($50K, $100K, $200K, $400K) and is now at about $3.5 million a month in revenue and over 10 million downloads.
- Pricing: about $20-30 a year (just under 10 cents a day), versus My Fitness Pal at roughly $20 a month for the ad-free version.
- Distribution: working with bigger, more reputable creators. They paid MrBeast between $500,000 and $1 million for an ad, and also work with Alex Eubank.
- Product positioning: the viral feature is photo scanning (about 90% accurate by their testing against a verified food database of images, macros and calories). They also offer a manual food database and barcode scanning. He argues the AI scan is more viral for social promos and removes the friction that makes people quit manual tracking. His framework: "don't let perfection be the enemy of improvement."
- On competitors and copycats: he says there are "hundreds, thousands of copycats" and treats mission-driven ones as allies; his focus is improving the product, lowering cost, and getting distribution.

## Principles for finding and validating app ideas

- Start with problems you or people close to you experience. Do market research, identify key pain points, look at what other apps in similar industries do, then build for the specific problem.
- Blake's own space is self-improvement, which he has studied for over a decade.
- Use existing apps as case studies. Everything is "open sourced" in the sense that the funnel is visible: download Cal AI or search it on TikTok and you see what the consumer sees.
- Copy-and-adapt works: he says tens if not 100+ other apps making over $100,000 a month rebuilt the experience for other domains (quitting sugar or alcohol, scanning supplements, skincare routines for women).
- Macro positioning: AI created new opportunity in consumer apps and social media made distribution easier, so he argues merging those trends was the key decision versus taking a finance job.
- The ground has moved: basic chat is commoditized, so value sits in narrow, specific applications on top of the models.

## Building skills, hiring and bootstrapping

- Learning to code: use ChatGPT as a personal tutor, not a copy-paste machine. Ask it to explain concepts (MVVM in Swift UI, backend security, API calls, state management), implement, iterate. Copy-pasting alone teaches nothing.
- He says he IQ tested people he has worked with and found roughly 100 IQ people building successful apps; some baseline intelligence is needed, but beyond that "it's really a problem of agency."
- On fundraising: they have all bootstrapped, with starting budgets in the low thousands, and get VC offers "all the time." His view is that VC suits multi-billion-dollar outcomes, and in this space "the bottleneck is not cash." The difference between $100,000 and $5,000 to start is small, mostly speed.
- He believes founders should learn the core skills themselves rather than start with cash and hire people, in this space: "you're actually kind of shooting yourself in the foot if you don't learn how to do the thing yourself."
- He says App Mafia portfolio apps collectively do over $70 million a year. Alumni of his team, such as people who worked on RZ GPT and his UMAX CTO, went on to found studios or apps doing $10 million or more a year.
