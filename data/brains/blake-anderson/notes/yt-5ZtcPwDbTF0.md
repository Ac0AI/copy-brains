## How Blake chooses what to build: new trends plus newly possible technology

- Blake uses two frameworks to decide what to build. The first is "what are people interested in today and this year that they were not interested in last year" (the trend component). Google Trends is one quantitative input.
- The second is "what products can I build today and this year which I could not build last year." He says his successful products capitalized on trends, but "more than anything they're capitalizing on new technology."
- His advice to a would-be founder: ask what newly advanced models and deep tech now make possible that wasn't possible even in 2024, then build with it.
- Examples he gives of current angles: OpenClaw wrappers, or applying the Claude Code style lexical-search agent to different industries. He says he has a few theses of his own but doesn't spell them out.
- His apps map to trends: Riz GPT (AI dating assistant, now Plug AI) to the dating/"rizz" wave, UMAX (face scan with hair and skin recommendations) to looksmaxxing, and Cali (AI calorie tracker) to the protein/fitness wave.

## Why single-function apps are no longer the play

- Blake says the opportunity in consumer apps still exists, but it is no longer in building a single-function application like his early products.
- Single-function products can still work as consumer interests shift, but he calls a more sustainable strategy "to identify a very clear problem that people experience that is either unsolved or poorly solved and then build an amazing product around that."
- He says people copy his early success with "super niche specific products" and that this is the wrong lesson.
- Example: if he rebuilt an AI dating assistant today, he would not make a screenshot-in, reply-out tool. He would build a suite covering dating profile optimization (photos), message replies, and date planning.
- He observes that the most long-term successful apps on the App Store, before and after AI, "are usually pretty fleshed out, high quality products. They're not cheeky single function products."

## Product vs. distribution vs. engineering

- Blake's view: great product still wins and always will.
- His analogy: the quality of the product determines the ceiling of the outcome. Distribution and engineering are what actualize that potential and get the company to the ceiling.
- He treats distribution and engineering as equally important.
- He predicts a world with fewer people who are truly great at distribution than people who are great at engineering, which makes distribution skill scarce.

## Consumer app distribution channels and how to test them

- Blake says the most popular consumer growth approach is short-form social, built on three channels: influencer marketing heavy, UGC heavy, and paid ads heavy.
- He says they are not mutually exclusive and are often mutually advantageous: working one channel makes the others easier.
- His advice: "try them all," then see what you are best at and what your customers respond to most.
- He calls social media the greatest distribution channel on the internet right now. Founders who grew up on it have a leg up, which advantages younger founders.
- He says there are "many ways to skin a cat" for growth, so no single playbook applies.

## Starting small: the first $100 of marketing

- Blake launched Riz GPT with limited money. He corrects the "$100" claim to roughly $2,000 total, saying the first $100 of marketing spend was the first that actually worked, and that is what got the product going.
- The takeaway he implies is that early validation comes from finding the first marketing spend that works, not from a large budget.
- He bootstrapped the apps with no outside funding.

## Results and track record he cites

- Background: studied finance, didn't want to work in finance, built apps in school that failed, then built Riz GPT after graduating to pay living expenses.
- Riz GPT (now Plug AI): scaled to a few million ARR, still around $3M per year in revenue.
- UMAX: built a couple of months later, scaled to a few million ARR.
- Cali: the most successful, about $30M revenue last year and about $6M in the most recent month.
- Combined, he says his apps have over 50 million downloads and about $60M in revenue.

## Building as a young founder, college, and hiring

- Blake says if he were a teenager starting again he would 100% build consumer software, and he thinks it is still the place to be.
- He gives two reasons younger founders are advantaged: they grew up on social platforms (the best distribution channels), and they have built with AI tools for years and so build faster.
- On college: it depends on the goal. It is valuable for fields like theoretical physics (being in the room with peers), and socially. For a CS degree, he says you're probably better off spending most of your time building side projects, for more economic upside.
- As someone who hires and talks to other engineering hirers, he would pick the CS major who built many projects and got a mid GPA over the one who ground for a high grade.
