## Origin story and early hustles

- As a kid Blake wrote an ebook titled "how to make money as a seven-year-old", and mowed lawns, raked leaves, shoveled driveways and sold candy.
- As a teenager he moved to manipulating game marketplaces: selling Madden Mobile coins, botting RuneScape (he ran the feather market at about 13), selling counterfeit NBA jerseys, building an Ethereum mining machine at 16, and scaling Instagram meme pages.
- His senior-year college marketplace app made no money. He had no job plans, a few thousand dollars invested, and his older brother loaned him money for groceries.
- A roommate asked him what to say to girls on dating apps, a few months after ChatGPT launched. He decided to have ChatGPT generate replies programmatically, with zero software experience and nothing in his bank account.

## RizzGPT: value plus virality

- The product was simple: upload a screenshot of your messages and get suggested replies.
- He had two co-founders with social media experience. They launched on TikTok and found an untapped niche of "rizz" content, where creators show off how good they are at talking to girls.
- They paid two faceless accounts $50 promos. They got about 200,000 downloads in roughly 5 days and about $80k MRR right away.
- The app was a mess: no notifications, no review prompt, a "horrible" paywall. His takeaway: "the most important thing was we provided value to people and we made it viral and in this space that's really all you need to get started."
- It launched at the start of July and scaled to a bit over $200k MRR over the next few months (he agreed to roughly $2.5M ARR). He parted ways amicably with a co-founder over product direction and kept his equity.
- ChatGPT did about 95% of the code for RizzGPT and about 90% for Umax. He used it as a personal tutor.

## Umax: finding the niche and partnering with an influencer

- He scanned social media for what was popping and underserved, and landed on "looks maxing": men trying to become more attractive. He argues it cuts through the real motive behind weightlifting.
- He saw two things people wanted: to find out how attractive they are, and how to become more attractive. The GPT Vision API had just come out, so he could send a face photo in and get an analysis back.
- He became obsessed with the content himself first, so he knew which influencers looks maxers would actually listen to.
- He brought on Sam Zia, a top looks-maxing influencer, plus his older brother for engineering. For a partner he looks for someone "hardworking, intelligent and a true entrepreneur", not just a creator.
- The launch started with one Sam video that got about 100,000 views and a few thousand downloads. The first month brought about $100,000 in revenue.
- He got Sam through an intro. Lucas Pactor connected them after Blake messaged him about the app.

## The copycat and going big early

- In mid-January a copycat copied the marketing, the outputs and the scoring metrics, and passed Umax in the App Store.
- Blake had been holding off on scaling to improve retention first. His rule is not to "burn through the entire Forest before we had optimized the app for retention for LTV."
- The copycat didn't care about user experience, so he decided it was better to burn through the forest and pursue the long-term vision later than to be beaten in the short term.
- In 3-4 days he put over $200,000 into influencer partnerships, more than all the revenue the app had made up to then. He calls it a massive gamble.
- Umax went to over $500,000 MRR. The copycat hit in two weeks the downloads that took Umax a month and a half, but Blake says Umax then had about 4x their downloads and 4x their revenue.
- He chose not to add features that might pull focus from the core loop: download, scan your face, get results.

## How he approaches influencer marketing

- Most influencers produce negative ROI, but some produce 10x ROI. Running as many of those as possible is highly scalable.
- Paid platform ads let you split test 100 creatives and double down. Influencer attribution is hard, so you need a "Spidey-like sense" of who converts.
- He looks at what an influencer's comment section says about the strength of their community and whether it follows their recommendations.
- Companies fail by sending 100 DMs, taking every yes, or going through an agency.
- For one-off promos you have to know the space deeply. For a deep partnership, pick someone who is also a builder.
- Cal AI uses paid influencer marketing with no partner. It competes against big incumbents in a huge category, and no single influencer could out-return what the team can do in-house. A partner makes more sense in a smaller niche like looks maxing, where the top person carries the market.

## Outreach: be unignorable, lead with value

- Don't send one DM and give up. Try TikTok, then Instagram, then Discord, and even their mom: "you have to make yourself unignorable."
- The same message on four platforms makes a response far more likely than on one. It takes about 2 minutes. He says there is a spammy way and a good way: lead with real insight.
- Quality beats quantity. The best cold messages give something thoughtful before asking for anything, rather than "can you give me advice on X."
- Zach slid into his DMs at 16 with a specific insight ("you're doing X, you should be doing Y"). After a few calls Blake was working with him as an equal partner.

## Cal AI and the playbook model

- He met two 17-year-olds around March or April. They saw calorie tracking as one of the biggest App Store categories, but a static one, so the thesis was to apply new AI to make tracking more accurate and easier.
- It launched a bit over a month before this recording and had done about $100,000 in revenue in that time.
- Zach Yadegari is CEO and Henry Langmack is CTO. Blake provides high-level advice and the playbooks that made RizzGPT and Umax work, without a multi-year time commitment.
- He says the strategy is "100% replicable" and invites skilled, hard-working people with good ideas to DM him.

## Finding ideas and what not to take as advice

- New AI capabilities have expanded what can be built, and building is cheaper than ever. He built his first two apps with ChatGPT.
- If you think and say what everyone else does, you reach the same conclusions. To find novel ideas, orient your life differently and read between the lines.
- He avoids crowded categories like AI CRMs and email agents, which he sees as bloodbaths. He cites Peter Thiel: "competition is for losers."
- Early wins are a way to fund bigger plays later, such as a broader male self-improvement vision.
- He doesn't think advice transfers. What worked for him may not work for others, and people urging the safe path mean well but don't know you like you do.
- He rejects the idea that you must work 16 hours a day, and wants time for the gym, friends and being outdoors.
