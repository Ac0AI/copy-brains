## Why technical skill matters more in the AI era

- Blake rejects the idea that learning technical skills is pointless because AI will write the code. He says "there has never been a more high leveraged time to become technical in human history."
- The form changes: less scanning Stack Overflow to debug, more using AI to code and build. He compares it to abstraction layers (machine code, then interpreted languages, now largely human language), but the underlying engineering knowledge is still needed.
- Common trait of successful founders he sees: they apply AI to everything they do.
- Example: he ran a two-hour paid trial for a 10X designer on Upwork with 5 to 10 candidates. One produced far more and better work, including custom icons and a design system, because he knew which AI tools generate clean mobile app icons.
- Other examples: content creators using ChatGPT for hooks and scripts, engineers for code, designers for icon libraries.
- He sees the gap between people who can apply these tools and those who can't as a large market inefficiency.

## Where the AI app opportunity has moved

- Blake's contrarian take: more mobile apps hit $1M+ ARR in 2024 than will in 2025 on "one or two key AI functions," because GPT Vision let builders upgrade old app categories and that innovation has largely been done.
- His examples of the earlier wave: Cali's photo-to-calorie logging was a function that was previously unsolved.
- He expects more apps overall, but not the super simple one- or two-function ones. He sees too much copycatting of already-popular experiences.
- His advice: stop over-indexing on one viral feature. Chain multiple prompts and user steps into fully AI-native experiences, because "the level of complexity necessary to succeed will continue to increase as will the size of the outcomes."
- Because new capabilities arrive faster than products get built on them, he says there is more alpha in being close to the ground and knowing which tools and resources emerge first.

## How he's building 10X (version 2.0)

- 10X's long-term vision is to redefine personalized education.
- The first version was a Duolingo-style "learn anything" app. He built it in about two weeks and launched with 2 to 3 days of marketing. It hit $10K MRR, but RPM to CPM didn't make sense. He knew how long and how much capital it would take to reach $1M ARR, so he paused it and went back to ideation.
- He built it during PMF or Die, a challenge to hit $1M ARR in under 90 days. He left after 36 days locked in a New York apartment on a 24/7 livestream. He says the mental toll hurt his ability to think clearly, and he doesn't think it is a conducive way to build a business.
- He and his co-founder on that product parted ways ("not the right fit"). He also names a flawed UX design as a cause of the failure.
- The new innovation angle is customized, auto-updating content:
  - A long onboarding flow that captures what the user wants to learn.
  - A generated assessment during onboarding to gauge knowledge by topic.
  - A feed auto-populated from those results, updating as the user learns.
- His pattern across products: pick one thing to innovate on per app and keep the rest standard. For Cali it was photo calorie logging.

## Distribution thesis: UGC is saturating, account volume is the next lever

- Almost two years ago he tweeted that UGC demand would rise exponentially as AI lets more small teams build apps. He now says everyone knows this and "there is relatively very little alpha left" in it, though he still believes in AI-generated UGC.
- His view of the bottleneck: creating thousands of pieces of AI UGC is no longer the edge. Getting them posted on warmed-up accounts is.
- His example system: 100 warmed-up accounts, 3 videos per account per day, so 300 videos a day. At a conservative 500 views each, that is 150,000 views per day as a floor, before counting videos that hit millions.
- Reasoning: social algorithms follow a power law, so one viral video produces outsized profit for the creator, the UGC creator or the brand. More shots on goal means a higher chance one lands.
- He cites Temu's affiliate system, with a huge volume of content going out daily, as the best example of this volume approach.
- He believes this volume-and-warmed-accounts play is a major opportunity in viral content, and says he is thinking about it deeply for 10X.

## Killing projects fast and keeping failures public

- Scorecard he gave: UMAX was just over $5M ARR a year earlier and is now over $3M. RizzGPT was just over $2M and is now about $1.5M. Both dipped because he put little time into them.
- Cali went from $100K MRR to over $2.5M a month, which he says is over $30M annualized.
- He spent early time scaling Cali, then moved to Apex, an all-in-one self-improvement ecosystem built on free apps, with content and CPG (supplements) as the long-term play. He had two co-founders on it.
- He decided Apex had a flawed macro thesis and wasn't the right project. He also wanted to be building AI technology.
- He defends moving on quickly as closing the feedback loop: "I'm not going to allow my feedback loops to lengthen... when I know what the end answer is going to be." If the loop says no, he kills it and moves on.
- He admits he got too confident and cocky, assuming he could copy and paste earlier formulas onto bigger ideas. He now wants bigger-impact products, which he says don't come from one-function apps.
- He says he failed for years before earning a full-time income, then did it back to back to back. He now aims to build a legacy and says years of repeated failure won't make him quit.

## Cali's co-founder structure

- Blake describes his role arc at Cali: early on he was "the coach." He put in most of the capital, handled UX, and brought his marketing strategy and influencer relationships from previous projects.
- Over time Zach became the CEO and the one running the company. Blake now acts as a resource, covering cash flow and the Apple payout timing and helping with high-level strategy, while the team runs daily operations.
- Roles he describes:
  - Zach: CEO, leads from the front and is the public face. Blake calls him exceptionally mature and hardworking, a 17-year-old CEO of a $30M ARR business.
  - Henry: CTO, engineers the product and accepts that he doesn't need to represent the company.
  - Jake: head of growth, leads influencer campaigns and the internal growth team.
- Why it works, in his view: no one needs the spotlight, and everyone knows they can claim the credit when they eventually step back, with full support from the others.

## Learning, college, advice and skill over followers

- On college: he dropped out after a year and a half, still thinks going was a good decision, and thinks he left at the right time. He argues that for an intelligent, hardworking person, self-learning something you care about is faster than school.
- Zach example: a ~34 ACT, president of his robotics club, rejected from about 20 schools, accepted to UT Austin, U Miami and Georgia Tech, and now runs a $30M ARR business.
- Two learning priorities for 2025 and the next five-plus years:
  1. Become proficient with AI tools in your own industry (chatbots, image generation, industry-specific tools) and start creating immediately.
  2. Keep building time-tested foundational knowledge (calculus, statistics, psychology, philosophy). AI tool skill alone won't carry a career; you need a developed worldview to know which questions to ask.
- He cites Ed Thorp, who studied physics and math, rejected the consensus that roulette was unpredictable, and built a device to predict where the ball lands.
- He says people will tell you what you can't do with AI, such as building a full app with ChatGPT, which he did, and still don't believe him.
- On advice: people rarely give it hoping for the worst, but consider the source. He cites Peter Thiel as someone who rarely gives advice because what worked for one person doesn't transfer.
- His closing lesson: reject what he says too, learn foundational knowledge, build your skills, and "follow your gut." If it fails, you at least did it your way and learned the lesson.
- He deleted Twitter, Instagram and TikTok because of the "skill to follower ratio": he doesn't want followers to outpace his actual skill.
- His current routine: coding every day, reading textbooks on agentic and complex systems, and studying multivariable calculus with Math Academy.
- His view of the bottleneck: for builders it is skill, not capital or hiring, because "most of the best talent is unhirable."
