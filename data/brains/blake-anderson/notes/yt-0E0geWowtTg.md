## How Blake Found and Validated AI App Ideas

- Blake launched Rizz GPT, an AI app for dating-app replies, in July 2023, a few months after ChatGPT came out.
- The idea started when a roommate asked what to text a girl.
- Blake typed the transcript into ChatGPT by hand and the answer was decent.
- He then tried prompt engineering and decided it was a viable product.
- The product let users screenshot a dating-app chat and paste it into the app for a response.
- The app went from zero to $83,000 in MRR within about three days of launch.
- He had tried many ventures before: an ebook at age nine, a lawnmowing business, candy sales in school, dropshipping, Instagram meme pages, an Ethereum mining machine, and a college marketplace app. Individual ventures earned from hundreds of dollars up to about $20,000, but nothing sustainable.
- He says his products benefit from being first to market and from targeting "core human insecurities or desires". He does not start with an over-intellectualized use case and then ask how to make it viral, which he finds much harder.
- Rizz GPT drew on people's wish to get dates. UMax, where you photograph your face and the AI rates your attractiveness and how to improve it, drew on appearance insecurity.
- On timing, he built the whole UMax product beforehand so that when OpenAI's GPT Vision became production-ready he could plug in the endpoint and submit to the App Store. He launched about two days after it hit production-level rate limits.
- He had seen the looksmaxing niche growing and had several market theses at the time. He calls it luck but says he was deliberately analyzing the factors.
- UMax made $100,000 in its first month, $200,000 in the second and $700,000 in the third.
- The looksmaxing community was tight-knit, large and not yet monetized, so he got "immediate product market fit".
- He worked with roughly 8 to 12 creators with large followings to reach the first $100,000.
- He believes more single-function viral AI apps reaching millions in annual revenue were built in 2024 than will be built in 2025. The obvious use cases are taken: AI to talk to girls, rate your face and track calories (the three he built).
- Opportunity remains, with Cluely as an example. Cluely is a hidden laptop tool for cheating on interviews, built by his friend Roy Lee. It is more complex than his own apps and still very viral.
- "The alpha is becoming harder to capture."
- In a period of rapid AI progress, he says the most important skill is picking the right things to work on.
- He thinks it would be a bad idea to wear "complete blinders" on one project for years if it ignores new AI developments. He moves on quickly to stay at the frontier.

## Influencer and Organic Marketing: Volume, Comment Sections and Briefs

- His influencer approach is high volume. He says to DM hundreds of influencers per day and not fixate on a few dream names. "If you're not DMing hundreds of influencers per day... you're not taking enough shots."
- He pays flat fees with minimum-view clauses. His example is $10,000 with the expectation of at least 2 million views.
- Costs vary by niche. A target CPM might be $5 per 1,000 views, but some creators quote $20 per 1,000.
- Edtech is expensive. Fintech and finance creators are the most expensive, with CPMs of $50 or more.
- He also examines view quality, not just view count.
- If an app isn't inherently viral, he says influencer and organic marketing is "an uphill battle", because it relies on organic recommendation engines.
- Organic is harder to track than platform ads. His team uses automated spreadsheets with video links, views, likes and engagement over time, mapped to downloads.
- Beginners don't need that. Compare each video's views over time against downloads and conversions, and build intuition for which hooks and creators convert.
- He says strong organic marketing "comes down to a deep understanding of statistics and psychology". Psychology covers hooks, messaging and negotiating with creators. Statistics covers inferring what converts, then leaning into those creators and hooks.
- His main creator-selection rule is engaged comment sections. Follower count mattered in the 2010s, then views, and now content quality.
- A video can get hundreds of thousands of views in 6 seconds and still not convert.
- An engaged comment section implies either a deeply trusting fanbase or content engaging enough that people leave thoughtful replies.
- He wants comments with insights or applause, not a stream of fire emojis. Top comments should have good like counts, and there should be sub-reply conversations. A "community" should exist in the comments.
- This is not a perfect one-to-one mapping, but he calls it a very underrated metric. People unwilling to engage in comments are unlikely to click through to the product.
- The team computes median views, average likes and average comments with basic functions, but evaluation is mostly intuitive.
- He uses a content brief PDF for every creator. It includes ways to integrate the product, example videos that did well, and basic rules such as showing the product for at least two seconds and before the 15-second mark, and tagging the app.
- Creators send videos for approval before posting. He may drop that step as he comes to trust certain creators.
- The brief "saves both parties time" and signals professionalism.

## Pricing and Distribution Fit

- He reverse-engineers distribution when evaluating ideas. It helps to know the distribution strategy before building.
- Engineers tend to think only about technical feasibility. Growth people tend to think only about whether the channel exists and whether customers can be acquired for less than they earn.
- He advises understanding both marketing and engineering well enough to see the playing field, without being extremely technical or a deep TikTok expert.
- Distribution informs pricing. Paid ads suit a high-LTV product with a higher price point, such as $20 per month, or a free trial then $100 per year.
- Organic is more conducive to lower price points, such as $30 per year or $4 per week. He says he has used these pricing strategies.
- Product and distribution form a positive feedback loop.
- His shorthand is to study Silicon Valley for product and dropshipping for marketing. Dropshipping has minimal product differentiation and competes on generating cheap views. Silicon Valley emphasizes product over distribution.
- He thinks there is a lot of value at the intersection of the two.

## Opportunity Ideas: App-to-Web, Services and AI Content

- He calls helping apps optimize app-to-web payments one of the greatest current opportunities. A recent US court ruling lets mobile apps offer web payments.
- Apple takes 30% of revenue for apps earning over $1 million a year, and 15% under that.
- He and others he knows have started testing it. He expects at least a 20% increase in proceeds for apps above the $1 million mark, and also over 20% for smaller apps. He says the real figure is "well over 20%" but won't give it.
- His team has about 10 different strategies to test, and he calls it an unsolved problem space.
- He suggests an agency that offers to do it free for a reference client, then uses that name to win paying clients on a performance basis. He thinks such an agency could scale past $50,000 a month in profit.
- For zero to $10,000 a month, he says the most important skill is becoming very skilled with AI tools.
- As an example, a designer he hired on Upwork produced far more in a 2-hour paid design challenge than nine others by using an AI tool to generate icons.
- He would apply AI skill to content creation: generating AI content and distributing it across platforms. Generating a few million views a month for a company could earn $10,000 or more a month. He says few people do AI content really well.
- Another option he gives is a UGC agency working with app founders.
- He cites the "sell shovels in a gold rush" idea. Building AI apps has a lower probability of success than servicing the people building them.
- Even so, more people overall will make a lot of money from AI apps than from servicing them.
- Servicing is generally easier, less grindy and has more upside if it works. His examples of big service-side companies are Cursor and Lovable.
- He personally enjoys building consumer products, and he advises doing things you find interesting and enjoyable because that is where you will put in the most hours and creativity.

## Co-founders, Equity and Expectations

- He is a big advocate of co-founders. His apps have all been built with partners.
- Rizz GPT had three founders: Blake did the engineering, Alex the design and Evan the marketing. Together they reached $250,000 a month within three months.
- He and one co-founder disagreed about the product's direction. They concluded one of them had to leave, and he left but kept his equity. They have since reconnected.
- For UMax he brought in his older brother, who did data analytics work and helped with backend engineering, and Sam Zia, an influencer in the looksmaxing niche. Both were half-partners and part-time until the third month, when they went full-time.
- Cali has four co-founders. Zach and Henry approached him after seeing his success. Zach gave him a list of ideas and Blake chose the AI calorie tracker.
- Blake acted as coach, supplying marketing strategy and capital. Henry and Zach ran the day to day. Jake Castillo joined as the fourth co-founder and CMO after it started succeeding.
- Zach, Henry and Blake split equity three ways equally at the start.
- He says Cali is on track for over $30 million in revenue in 2025.
- His reasoning: equal splits keep everyone incentivized, and unequal splits could have led people to leave after it succeeded.
- His line is "it's a lot better to have 50% of a watermelon than 100% of a grape". The question isn't your share of the upside but whether you can capture it at all.
- Alone, he thinks Rizz GPT might have made $10,000 to $20,000 a month, or never launched.
- Co-founders can go badly, and he has experienced that.
- He says the biggest issues come from unclear expectations. Examples are one founder wanting a billion-dollar business while another wants $50,000 a month, or someone failing to deliver engineering. Define responsibilities clearly from the start.
- Good co-founders are usually found organically through relationships, not by searching urgently.

## Choosing Projects, Persistence and Taking Advice

- He has worked full-time on five projects in two years: Rizz GPT, UMax, Cali, Apex and 10X. He still does some work on UMax, a good amount on Cali, and has moved on from Apex and 10X.
- On when to quit, he says many people work on the wrong projects for too long, while others don't give a project time to mature. The right call depends on the person and the product.
- He tries to ask whether it's really time to move on or he just wants new excitement.
- He thinks building 20 products a year is probably too fast.
- A universal principle for him is to optimize for learning. Even if a project fails, he wants to come out with transferable skills. He says day trading fails this test, because failing at it teaches little transferable knowledge.
- The counter-example is Speechify, which took years before succeeding. He says Sensor Tower implies at least $50 million a year in revenue, and the real number is higher. He therefore avoids bold claims where there are many counterfactuals.
- He warns against mimetic desire, doing a thing because many others are doing it. This tends to give the same results as everyone else. He prefers reading between the lines and seeing opportunity others miss.
- He chose AI apps over TikTok Shop despite friends succeeding there, because he wanted learning and AI's upward trajectory.
- He currently explores AI hardware with his brother, aiming at local inference: an open-source LLM on a small processor with no cloud connection.
- Their first build is an AI pocket recorder using an ESP32 board, a microphone, an SD card, Bluetooth and a lithium-ion battery. Audio is uploaded to a computer, transcribed and summarized.
- He cites Plaud as proof of market. Plaud sells the recorder for $160 against roughly $25 cost of goods and a $250 per year subscription, where inference cost is pennies. He says it makes multiple millions of dollars a month.
- Next he wants an AI smart speaker running a local model on a Jetson or Nano-class processor, with about 12 hours of battery.
- He expects powerful LLMs to run on a watch within 18 to 24 months, citing a Moore's-law-like trend in LLM performance by model size.
- The hardware projects are low-probability for a business but good for learning. He recommends having fun intellectual hobbies, since they may help professionally later.
- On advice, he says people are complex systems, so universal rules rarely apply and his own advice should be taken "with a grain of salt".
- He thinks the best advice comes from testing ideas yourself and from close people who know you well. He cites Peter Thiel as famously not giving advice.
- He turned off Twitter DMs, avoids networking and doesn't take advisory requests.
