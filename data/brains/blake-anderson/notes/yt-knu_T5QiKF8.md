## Distribution through a viral hook, with a bigger product underneath

- Blake and Roy Lee discuss Cluely (Roy's company) and its "cheat on everything" positioning. Blake says Roy's cheating angle works like UMAX's face-rating hook: a viral concept that funnels people into a less viral but more sustainable product.
- Blake says Roy did this before with Interview Coder: start with a narrow "cheat on" use case, then widen it.
- He argues a "Trojan horse" approach is the way to build AI wrappers. Package several single-function tools into one app, but market each niche separately.
  - In astrology, it is "your AI horoscope report."
  - In fitness, it is "an analysis of the workouts you need."
  - In education, it is a cheating tool.
  - Each audience hears about a different feature, and the company underneath is the same.
- He says he wished he had done with UMAX what Roy did: use the hook to get the download, then push people to the bigger product.

## Where AI application companies win: UX and non-obvious use cases

- Blake thinks the frontier LLMs are roughly the same. In mass consumer products the difference is the UX and how smooth the application feels.
- He cites Perplexity as an example. The labs compete on training foundation models, and Perplexity competed on UX.
- He says memory handling and fast, smooth search are what make ChatGPT impressive. He is not impressed by the idea of a desktop app that just connects to a chat API.
- He says most people do not know what ChatGPT can do or how to prompt it. A blank canvas does not help because people are not that creative. Packaging a specific vertical for them is worth real money.
- He believes the consumer value is context and memory, since an assistant with perfect context becomes more effective as you use it.
- He also believes the key bottleneck today is that you must still explain what you need done. He built a prototype wearable that recorded everything he said and found it very effective.
- He backs the idea of a personal "computing puck" (a Satoshi-style local device). It would hold your personal LLM and memory and connect to the watch, glasses, phone and laptop.
- He is bullish on smart glasses and thinks they could replace the phone.
- He thinks the AI winners in consumer will be the distribution and UX players, not the labs. He sees OpenAI as strongest on the consumer side.

## Personal branding and short-form distribution

- Blake and Roy agree that the internet forgets. Their examples are Andrew Tate and Donald Trump (who launched a memecoin right after becoming president).
- Blake agrees that attention is the real currency, and that the worst thing you can be is irrelevant.
- Blake says that short form rewards outrageous things, and that distribution is becoming more important.
- He argues Gen Z founders understand distribution and fragmented social platforms in a way older founders do not, because they see the different niches through their friends.
- Blake told a story about xAI. They came to him wanting influencer marketing and short-form virality, and he offered to run it for a few hundred thousand dollars a month with no retainer. They declined and said they would keep prioritizing X because Elon is the most viral person on the platform.
  - His takeaway: engineers do not understand how fragmented social media is. There are separate pockets of the internet, and a post that goes viral in one is invisible in the others.
  - He said back in 2015 something viral was seen by everybody, and that is no longer true.
- He says older founders (over 30) will get "steamrolled by Gen Z" in distribution, while AI labs will keep doing deep tech.

## Risk-taking, compounding wins and the "winners effect"

- Blake agrees with Roy that risk-taking is positive expected value. His framing: if four in five risks fail at -1, but one in five pays 10x, and you keep compounding, then people see "luck" at the big moment.
- He says most people think risk is bad because they only see the downside of a couple of failures. Meanwhile the people who took none stay at baseline.
- He talks about "winners effect": try 20 pieces of content and all flop, then one hits and the next 10 hit as well. It feels like flow state, and top performers in every field recognize it.
- He says the cause is not luck but a repeating cycle of understanding what works and doubling down.
- Blake's view of success has table stakes: you need to be smart enough and charismatic enough. Past that, it is about choices: taking big risks, making sacrifices, working hard.
- He observes that most people never take big career risks because the downside is a lower position in the social hierarchy.
- He notes that most people are lazy, so working at moderate intensity, sustained, is enough to stand out.
- He says that meeting people far above him in IQ has shown him there is a tradeoff, because some cannot communicate. How far you get is mostly up to how much you push.

## Raising money and building ambitious startups

- Blake's view of VC: it is a massive lever, and if you are in a competitive space and want to move as fast as possible there is no other option. He has not raised so far because his projects have not been that large, and he likes the freedom.
- He says he will certainly raise in the future.
- He describes why bootstrapping caps the upside. When the money comes out of your own pocket, you cut corners and do not make big bets like hiring someone at $500,000 a year in cash compensation.
- He describes a dynamic where VCs are somewhat predatory to the typical founder, but a founder with leverage (momentum, a hot company) sits in the center and has most of the power.
- He and his partner Zach do not want to work on projects that are not notable or interesting. Some B2B SaaS founders are worth billions and nobody cares about them, and he never wants to be that person.
- He suggests a hiring pitch for a viral company: being a cool place to work, with people around, can beat a bigger paycheck at a large tech company.

## Why people build: biology, status and ambition

- Blake says his thesis behind every app he has built is that the biological imperative makes the world go around. He and Roy agree that most ambition traces back to primal drives like status and attracting a mate.
- He observes that the people who take big risks are often those who want more control over who they can attract, while others stay comfortable at their position.
- Blake's view of how he builds: "pretty much everything I do is for the sake of women," meaning the products and brands are aimed at that drive.
- He recommends Cartier over a Rolex or Audemars Piguet if you want to attract women, because women recognize it and men do not. He also mentions Van Cleef, and says the Internet Money guys do "female drip" well.

## Mindset: ignore society, know what you want, work hard

- Blake agrees with Roy's closing point that you can have almost anything if you know exactly what you want, can ignore the voice of society, and work hard. His version: you only internalize it once you are on the other side of the risks (gym effort, hard work, getting yourself blacklisted from the big tech world).
- He says you see what lies on the other side only after you take the risk, and compares it to the allegory of the cave.
- He mentions a mini-sabbatical he is on now, and says that he has been working less hard recently.
