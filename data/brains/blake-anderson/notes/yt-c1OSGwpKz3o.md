## Core principle: a viral idea minimizes friction from inception to millions of users

- Blake defines a viral idea as "one that minimizes the amount of friction from inception to millions of users."
- He focuses on single-player utility apps: no complex backend, no social or fun functionality required.
- The idea sets the foundation for design, development and distribution. A bad idea wastes time on everything downstream.
- Example: Blake says the execution on Rizz GPT "was not great at all," but it had a viral idea, and that made everything else "10 times easier."
- His track record: three apps (Rizz GPT, Umax, Cal AI) that each generated millions of downloads and millions in profit in one year.

## Principle 1: solve big problems

- People want solutions to big problems they actually have. He cites Maslow's hierarchy of needs: nobody cares about feeling part of a group if there is no food on the table.
- Solve the most fundamental problems. His apps map to them: Rizz GPT helps you get more girls, Umax helps you become more attractive, Cal AI helps you reach fitness goals.
- Biological imperative: life exists to continue living and to reproduce, so help people with those things.
- The app does not need to be the entire solution. Framing it as one solution to a big problem is significantly more viral than framing it as the full solution to a relatively minor problem.

## Principle 2: more complicated means less viral

- Virality is contingent on simplicity. Even if the app is framed as the answer to getting more girls, a 25-step path to get there kills interest.
- Test: you should be able to describe the app's unique nature in three words.
  - Rizz GPT: "respond to girls"
  - Umax: "face analysis"
  - Cal AI: "photo calorie counter"
- Simplicity affects every part of growth:
  - How easily people understand the app.
  - How likely they are to tell friends.
  - How quickly it can be demonstrated in content.
- On TikTok you have about 3 seconds to capture attention. If the app is too complicated to show in that window, "you're fighting an uphill battle."
- Formula: simple concepts, simple execution, massive growth.

## Principle 3: the "did you hear about..." test (make it remarkable)

- The app must be something people naturally want to tell friends about. If you can't fill in "Did you hear about the app that ___?" naturally, growth will be a struggle.
  - "...the AI that can text girls for you?"
  - "...the app that rates how attractive you are?"
  - "...the app that lets you count calories just with pictures?"
- How his apps spread:
  - Rizz GPT: screenshots posted in TikTok content.
  - Umax: people sharing transformations and before/after ratings.
  - Cal AI: people showing off their meals and macros.
- The app needs to create moments worth sharing. Gut-checks:
  - Would someone bring it up at a party?
  - Would someone text their mom about it?
  - Would someone send it to a group chat?
- It should make people feel something (surprise, excitement, anger), "anything but indifference." When Blake first built Umax and pulled it out at a party, everybody wanted to find out what they were rated.
- If the app doesn't create these moments, keep iterating on the idea until it does. "The most viral products aren't just useful, they are remarkable."

## Method 1: scan social media (non-negotiable)

- Social media is "a manifestation of the collective conscious": what people are interested in and talking about.
- He says there is no magic bullet for spotting new trends, but he:
  - Uses Google Trends to see what's popping off.
  - Explores Reddit communities.
  - Reads comment sections on Instagram, TikTok and other platforms.
- Next level: create new accounts and consume content you would never normally engage with. Example: a 25-year-old guy who watches weightlifting and programming content makes an account for what a 35-year-old mom would watch (family cooking, interior design, pickleball). "You'll be amazed with parts of the internet that you never knew existed."
- How this led to Umax: he saw one thing about looksmaxing that piqued his interest, then made accounts dedicated entirely to looksmaxing content. He figured out which forums they lived on and what products they used, and eventually learned exactly what problems these people had, which let him build a product around it.
- Key point: break out of your bubble. Your feed is an echo chamber of what you already like. New perspectives mean new opportunities and new viral ideas.

## Method 2: research the App Store

- Don't just browse; discover what works and what doesn't.
- Process:
  - Download a new app every day.
  - Watch the content the app posts on social media and work out why it is or isn't succeeding.
  - Look up the app on Sensor Tower for monthly downloads and monthly revenue, because your first guess about whether an app is succeeding isn't necessarily right.
- Example: before building Cal AI, he downloaded every calorie tracker he could find and asked whether there was really opportunity, looking for shortcomings and room for improvement.
- Avoid graveyard spaces: if an idea has been tried 100 times with no successes, stay away. Examples: social weightlifting apps, and a college marketplace (the first app he ever tried, which fell victim to this).
- The goal is not to copy but to find market gaps and inefficiencies. A problem across the industry that nobody has solved may be an opportunity.

## Method 3: disconnect to let ideas form

- The best ideas don't come when forced; they come in moments of clarity. He recommends long walks with no phone, no music, nothing.
- Where his ideas came from: Rizz GPT on a walk home, Umax on a hike.
- Social media and app research are inputs. The mind needs time to produce outputs, like a computer clearing memory.
- Most founders consume content 24/7 (X, tech articles, TikTok), leaving no space for original thought. That is why everybody builds the same things: everyone saw ChatGPT and built AI chatbots; everyone saw Stable Diffusion and built AI avatar generators.
- Real viral opportunities require synthesis, connecting dots others haven't:
  - Male dating frustration + AI led to Rizz GPT.
  - Looksmaxing community problems + AI vision led to Umax.
  - Calorie-counting friction + computer vision led to Cal AI.
- These connections happen in the shower, on walks and during workouts. "The greatest founders aren't necessarily the smartest, they're the ones that dare to think differently from the rest."
