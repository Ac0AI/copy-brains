## Launching a subscription alarm product and chasing profitability

Zach is building Flow, an alarm clock with a dock. The user must physically stand up and tap their phone to the dock to turn off the alarm, and that then blocks their most addictive apps for the start of the day. His goal in this episode is profitability so ads can scale again. His wider target is "$100 million in sales", which he says means selling about 1.3 million units, plus $1 million in a month on a goal he set (34 days to go at the time).

- He launched subscriptions (Flow Plus) a week before the episode, and the early data wasn't good.
- Day-level profit on his new dashboard looked like roughly -$1.5k, then -$300. He first suspected the data was wrong, then found an experiment was the cause.
- That experiment had a variant with no subscription, and that 20% of traffic was "very unprofitable".
- Another 20% got $10 a month, which "was just not making that much money compared to the yearlies". He turned both off.
- That left a three-way split test: $20 a month, $60 a year, $99 a year.
- Refunds were averaging about $1,000 a day, but he reached profitability after setting $99 a year live.

## Fixing confusion about subscription requirements

Zach found that customers thought they needed the subscription to use the product. He treated this as a product and flow problem, not just a support one.

- Unboxing test: his girlfriend set up Flow for the first time while he watched without helping. She got stuck on the select-sound screen. She hit the back arrow instead of "select sound", so the alarm never set. This is probably why some people think a subscription is needed to set an alarm.
- Planned fix: add a screen that says "your dock is there".
- Customers saw a subscription email, or only noticed the charge after paying, because it wasn't visible in the cart.
- The App Store rating was 3.9. One-star reviews said the subscription was "required". Getting the creators to leave ratings wouldn't fix it long term.
- The Flow Plus code screen wasn't obvious enough. It appears if the app can't match your email to a customer, and he asked for it to be made more obvious.
- Support approach: prioritize customer support and give refunds to anyone who wants one. If someone wants to cancel, they help and process the refund.
- Perspective on rough launches: with Cal AI the first app version was something they were "not proud of". He cites "if you're not embarrassed of what you launch with, you shipped too late", and says the goal is to have all the flows dialed in within four or five months.

## Metrics and internal dashboards

Zach says the team is "flying an airplane semi-blind" and assigns Alex a complex dashboard in the Flow admin panel.

- He wants metrics for people who purchase: do they download the app right away?
- He wants the number of Flow Plus codes out there and how many have been activated, plus the time to activation, e.g. how many activate within the first day. He flags this as especially important.
- He built an internal profit dashboard called "Triple Whale Shark", modelled on Triple Whale, the real ad-analytics platform many people use. You can click into it to see the formulas behind each calculation, and it serves as a head start for Alex.

## Learnings from a funnel and ad audit

The team did a two-hour audit and Zach expected "two or three tips" such as one tweak to lower Meta CAC.

- Pricing: test more monthly prices, such as $20 a month. He heard the industry standard is about 25% still subscribed at the end of the year. At $20 a month, LTV would be about $150 for the year, which he says would let them scale.
- Funnel messaging: emphasize "free shipping" and build more social proof.
- Ads: entertaining users works, e.g. Gabe doing stunts, but the messaging needs hammering. He wants claims like "if you wake up after 7:00 a.m., you're 50% less likely to be successful in life".
- Offer testing: try specific odd numbers, e.g. "63% off" instead of "25% off". He wants to read *Influence* by Robert Cialdini.
- A friend said a web funnel (ad to a questionnaire) works well. He built one asking age, gender, phone usage and how often you're late to events, with an interstitial like "X amount of hours a year".
- He recalls Cal AI used about 20 questions, which "reinforce the fact that they have a problem, we have a solution", and had the highest conversion in the ad game. Flow's version: "you're operating at 50% of your potential, if you get it to 80%...".
- Results: going to the funnel before the product page converted about 20% better than going straight to the product page.

## Test results: bundle offer failed, funnel won

- Test: an offer page led with the two-dock bundle plus free gifts, to raise average order value.
- Result: overall revenue per visitor was down about 5%.
- He notes it works well for some of his other websites (not just supplements), but doesn't know why it didn't work here. Lesson: you have to test it.

## Creative and belief from advisors

The team flew to North Carolina to meet advisors for a roughly four-hour creative session and filmed an ad with them.

- Biggest value was "just meeting them, just seeing them in person" because it builds belief that "if they can do it, I can do it".
- It showed there is more room to improve creatives, with regular creative meetings and many "micro things".
- Angles to try: ads about the alarm sounds, and ads that start with the alarm ringing and then show all the sounds.
- On the offer, he set $99 a year live straight after the meeting.
- The advisors told them they can do $100 million in the next 12 months, with the only possible bottleneck being shipping.
- After the trip he works through a list of every funnel, ad and offer improvement and sets the web funnel experiment live around 1 a.m.

## Choosing ethics over profit on the subscription

After $99 a year went live, Zach was profitable, but he got many calls from people who didn't know they were subscribing.

- Lower tiers hid the problem because people cared less; at about $100 a year it became clear.
- A customer was charged $40 for the alarm plus a $99 one-year subscription they didn't intend to select.
- He said they could scale as is with "healthy margins", but "we don't want to run our business this way". It would hurt the brand and is "ethically wrong". He plans many Flow products, and a bad first experience would lose those future customers.
- Decision: switch to a one-time purchase with no subscription model, even though it makes them unprofitable again for now. "Got to do it. This is business."
- Takeaway from one call: a customer was open to paying monthly, so he wonders if more would be.
