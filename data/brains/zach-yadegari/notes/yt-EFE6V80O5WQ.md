## Building AI apps: what changed and why Zach's team beat funded incumbents

- AI lets software make human-like judgment calls instead of binary "if statements." Zach's example: the old version is "if the color I detect is red, turn on the light." With AI, a light can respond to time of day or mood, which are factors that are hard to quantify.
- Public AI APIs now cost "just a few cents for thousands of AI calls," so anyone can build things that were previously impossible.
- Zach started coding at 7, put his first app on the App Store at 12 (no AI; custom models were needed back then), then built an unblocked gaming website that reached 5 million users and sold it. That taught him distribution.
- Cal AI lets users photograph food and get calories and macros.
- When Cal AI launched, a YC-backed company with a similar product had raised millions two or three years earlier and gone nowhere. In roughly Cal AI's first month after launch, it "generated more revenue in a month than they probably did that year."
- His takeaway: you don't need sophisticated in-house models if you expect the models everyone can access to keep improving. Staying at the frontier of new technology matters more than traditional expertise.

## Where AI app opportunity lies

- His framework: "anything that's monotonous can be and will be replaced."
- Almost any product that doesn't use AI yet can be improved with it. Examples he gives:
  - Calendars (Motion schedules around your work week).
  - Note-taking (voice notes transcribed by AI).
- These examples commoditize a personal assistant for people who can't afford one.
- Cal AI follows a second pattern: take an action consumers already do (scan a barcode, type in food) and make it more efficient (take a picture).

## Glasses and AI hardware as the next software wave

- Zach has been using Meta Ray-Bans instead of AirPods and sees the vision, though they're audio-only for now. He expects graphics overlays to make them much more compelling.
- Glasses, unlike watches or pendants, could replace the phone, so he sees them as the next primary form factor.
- Most people can't compete with Meta, Google or Snap on the hardware, but there will be "an insane new software wave."
- Platform owners won't build everything themselves and will open app stores.
- Early App Store apps were rudimentary, and the AI wave was a "mini wild west." Glasses would be different:
  - Builders already understand consumer software use cases.
  - There are no major incumbents.
  - The territory is largely unexplored.
- He notes that most of the top App Store apps were built before 2016. New-app traction dipped, then spiked again around 2022-2023 with AI, because every single-player idea could be reinvented.
- He sees social as the open question for AI. Single-player AI apps arrived first, and he expects new AI UX patterns to eventually enable novel social and messaging experiences.
- He is bearish on virtual reality (you lose real stimulus) and bullish on augmented reality and brain chips.

## Job displacement, UBI and creating versus consuming

- He expects power laws to keep scaling and many people's skills to be displaced by AI. In his view anyone fully replaceable was already "plug and play."
- His techno-optimist view is that this frees time for art, friends, family, nature and health, and that vibe coding lets everyone build tools for themselves, such as a personal CRM.
- He thinks UBI becomes necessary once the highest-leverage work of the average person is commoditized. His concern is that it lets people default into consumption and sloth. He says anyone who pushes people toward creation over consumption will do the world a major service, though he doesn't know how to capture value from that.
- He argues experience (a walk in nature) is closer to creation than to consumption.
- He cites Man's Search for Meaning: meaning comes from creation, experience, and virtue through suffering.
- On inducing bliss with brain chemicals: "there is no biological free lunch." He contrasts dopamine from cocaine (lowers future baseline) with an ice bath or working out (raises it, at an energy cost).
- He mentions AI-powered education, citing Alpha School, whose SAT/ACT scores are very high, while noting selection bias. He says a high-intelligence student spends 80%+ of K-12 time daydreaming.

## Making macro life decisions

- He used to think the way to decide was to define one purpose and orient everything around it. He now thinks purpose is fluid and humans are too complex to optimize on a single dimension.
- His current approach:
  - Ask what he wants to have done by about 80.
  - Pick a handful (3-5) of fundamental values.
  - Follow curiosity and flow state, while staying wary of shiny object syndrome. His summary: "Follow your deep curiosities, ignore everything else."
- His own list includes:
  - Building a product that billions of people use.
  - A song that hits the Spotify top charts.
  - A Steve Jobs-style keynote.
  - A documentary (which he says is probably ego).
- His plan is to stay on the entrepreneurial path to a billion users, then after an acquisition spend a year on music.
- He says success holistically becomes less about specializing in one field and more about being a high performer across the board.
- His advice for beginners: "whatever it takes, get that first win." Before it, all feedback is negative and everything is a black box. After it, you see what works, learn your propensities and gain connections.

## Learning from founders and old playbooks

- He thinks mid-tier founders in the trenches teach more than billionaires, who are far from the early-stage struggle and can't easily recall what it's like to get a first win.
- He believes billionaire operators like Zuckerberg would still succeed at building an AI consumer app if it were their sole focus, because they study a space, replicate and make deals. They lack intuition for viral TikToks.
- He agrees with Gary Tan that you can't apply past frameworks to this era: a solo builder can now spin up a venture in three months with tools like Figma and services that solve engineering and distribution.

## Playbook: turning $5,000 into $100,000 in apps

**Zach's own app strategy (what he'd do himself):**
1. Browse the App Store top charts (health and fitness, entertainment, lifestyle) for apps ranking high, or new up-and-coming ones.
2. Prefer apps that don't use AI or are built poorly, but have clear virality and a use case people want.
3. Innovate on the validated idea by adding a viral hook, a new feature to feature in all TikTok promotional material. His example: an IQ test as the hook in a learning app.
4. Make the first videos yourself.
5. After validating further, use the capital to pay influencers in the niche.

**Development-agency/influencer-partnership variant (what he'd do without a personal brand):**
1. Spin up a development agency with a few "really cracked" devs, giving them equity.
2. Build reusable templates: onboarding templates, paywall strategy, and the ability to design viral AI features quickly.
3. Partner with 10-20 influencers who each have 1M+ followers across platforms.
4. Give each influencer 60% of profits and keep 40% and general control, so they feel it's their product.
5. Ship roughly 20 apps in about four months. They need to be good, "but we don't have to make them perfect."

- He calls this pretty much zero risk. The hard part is social engineering credibility with influencers if you've had no success yet; solve that and "it's game over."
- He says a content agency (UGC, influencer marketing) was his earlier answer but is starting to become saturated.
