## Building Cal AI: origin, growth engine and economics

Zach Yadegari is co-founder and CEO of Cal AI, an app where users photograph their food and AI calculates calories and macros. He says it brings in about $1.4 million a month after the app stores' cut. He was 18 when he said this.

## The problem and the AI timing that created the product

- Zach started working out as a skinny kid. He downloaded one of the most popular calorie trackers at the time and quit 3 days in.
- The reason was friction: the app made him weigh food on a scale and type in everything he ate. He says it was "unsustainable for my lifestyle."
- A year or two later, ChatGPT and other AI technologies launched. He and his co-founders hypothesized they could build an app where "you could just take a picture and AI will do all the work for you."
- They launched and the response was immediately very positive. That told them they had something and they decided to go all in.
- He says the competing apps "are the reason that we started Cali. It's because they suck."

## Going all in: the San Francisco summer

- With his CTO Henry, he decided to spend the summer doing everything possible to make the app succeed.
- They moved to San Francisco for July, lived in a hacker house and "basically slept in the office."
- He calls that month "the real turning point." They made their first real hires, built up a team and found a rhythm that carried through the next year.
- Cal AI has three co-founders.
- Alongside high school he put in about 40 hours a week on Cal AI, often working during school. Earlier projects had been side projects behind schoolwork; Cal AI was the first he prioritized.

## Influencer marketing is the special sauce

- He says what Cal AI "really" does is influencer marketing, and "a far majority" of its spending goes there.
- That is the core distribution engine, ahead of any other channel.

## Managing user expectations and refunds

- Some users expect "X-ray vision," for example that the AI will detect food hidden at the bottom of a bowl. It won't. Every ingredient must be clearly in frame.
- The team teaches correct scanner use early, in the app's tutorial.
- On refunds, Apple processes payments, not Cal AI, so users must request refunds from Apple directly. He calls the belief that the app handles card info a common misconception.

## Cash flow: scaling faster than Apple pays

- Building the app was cheap because he and Henry programmed it. Money was spent when they launched publicly and scaled.
- Scaling took everything he had made from his earlier site, Totally Science. His co-founder Blake then doubled that amount on top.
- Apple pays about 45 days after a sale. Cal AI was scaling faster than Apple paid, so for months they put money in without seeing a proportional amount come out.
- It took about six months to get ahead of Apple's payout schedule and earn more than they paid out.
- He stresses they were "very profitable" during that period; the gap was a timing problem, not a profitability problem.

## Reinvestment, dividends and founder mindset

- Most revenue is reinvested into the app. When enough cash is left over, the founders pay themselves dividends. His most recent personal dividend was $100,000.
- He spent much of his earnings, including on a Lamborghini, and says he plans to start saving since he has little else he wants to buy.
- His earlier projects included an unblocked gaming website he built in freshman year of high school, Totally Science, and he started coding at age 7 after begging his mom for a game-making summer camp.
- He rejects age as a self-limiting belief: "When I've wanted to do something, I will just go and do it."
- His goal for Cal AI is to become the biggest calorie tracking app; he considers the job complete when the app is helping people "on autopilot." He plans to build "a generational company" afterward.
- On age: "You're either good or not good at what you do. And then the market will decide the rest."
