## Self-belief and the "when, not if" mindset

- Zach built self-belief by wanting the outcome so badly he "needed it to be true." As a young teen he filmed a kitchen video saying he would make a million dollars before graduating high school, and he always believed in his heart he would do it.
- He deliberately shifted his language from "if" to "when." As a kid he told his mom "if I'm rich when I'm older," then corrected himself to "when I'm rich."
- He still had doubts ("can I actually do this?"), but he keeps self-belief as the thing that got him past obstacles like "I have to go to college / get a degree first."
- In the year before he started Cal AI, he curated his TikTok For You page to show only motivational speakers (his feed was "David Goggins yelling at me"). He still consumes motivational content, just less than before.

## Finding app ideas by watching what commenters ask for

- His first app, Grind Clock, was a motivational alarm clock where users pick a motivational speaker's voice as their alarm sound.
- The idea came from watching those motivational TikToks: commenters kept saying the clip pumped them up and asking how to make it their alarm, and there was no easy way to do it.
- Test he used: he wanted it for himself, and the comment sections showed clear unmet demand from others.

## Skill stacking over being a genius

- Zach agrees that you don't need to be a prodigy. Being good enough at building plus strong at marketing and entrepreneurship is the valuable combination.
- Coding is getting easier with AI tools, and his coding ability mainly helped him learn to lead a team.
- He applies the Pareto principle: learn the 20% that gets 80% of the results. That is enough to hire people smarter than you and manage them well.
- He did this across multiple areas, which let him build strong people around him and put systems in place.

## Running an M&A process for his first sale (Cal AI)

- Timeline: the process began in May 2025, a few months before college, because he wanted a normal freshman experience without running a company. The sale closed in December, was announced in March 2026, and the wire hit the same day it closed.
- He ran it like a sales cycle: he listed the 10 companies that made the most sense as buyers.
- Warm intros over cold outreach: he had been told you lose leverage by cold outreaching, so he had people he knew introduce him, framed as a cool company worth meeting rather than him asking.
- Several CEOs shut it down right away, some even saying "cool company, not interested." A few took calls and met the team, and offers came quickly after a couple of meetings.
- Timing: he and the buyers avoided influencing anything during peak season (January and February), which is part of why the announcement waited.
- Being undervalued: he felt Cal AI was extremely undervalued relative to the first offers.

## What happened after the low first offers

- The first offers were roughly one year of profit, less than the company would make in a year. He politely turned them down.
- He then wondered whether the company was simply unsellable and began accepting it might be a cash-flowing business forever. He started looking for a CEO to bring on so he could step away.
- The turning point: another founder who had sold told him how M&A bankers work. They build lists of companies you've never heard of, including ones about to go public that want to roll up smaller companies and will pay fair market value.
- He agrees with the pattern that the second round of interest comes when you care less and the company is stronger because you committed to building it to last.
- He frames this as the "no one's coming to save us" moment, which strengthened his resolve.

## Making Cal AI premium and how the real deal started

- While committed to building a lasting business, he reasoned that any big, decade-old app he could think of was premium, so he began working toward making Cal AI premium.
- He tried to get free guidance from the MyFitnessPal CEO, who is strong at freemium, framing the call as catching up. The CEO answered none of his freemium questions, which Zach says makes sense since it wasn't in their interest.
- Instead, that conversation pivoted to a possible partnership, and that is how the actual acquisition talks began.

## Negotiating advice for first-time sellers

- Play a little hard to get, a push and pull. Show interest without seeming too interested, and signal that you're going places regardless of whether they get on board.
- Find advisors who have been through acquisitions and keep them on the phone throughout the process. He did this constantly. Acquisitions across companies look similar, so they told him the right things to say as situations came up.
- It was his first time and the buyer was owned by private equity, so they were seasoned pros, which made experienced advisors more valuable.
- He felt the excitement when the offer first came by phone, but made himself quell it until anything was signed, and it never felt as good again afterward.

## The Lamborghini course rage-bait launch

- He lived in a Miami house with entrepreneur friends. The original plan was to build companies side by side and share learnings, then record content, which required paying videographers and editors.
- To at least break even, they launched a course on making apps. He says they are "business people at heart," so they wanted it to cover its costs.
- They deliberately chose a controversial, Andrew Tate-style angle: "sell the dream, sell the lifestyle instead of sell the product." Videos featured a Lamborghini and money in the air. He says it was intentional rage bait and not a real representation of their characters.
- It took over the Twitter timeline but started beefs that weren't great. His assessment: it makes the story more interesting, but he would take a different angle next time.
- Launching on Twitter was also a mistake in targeting, since Twitter users weren't the audience the course was meant for. It was just their native platform.
