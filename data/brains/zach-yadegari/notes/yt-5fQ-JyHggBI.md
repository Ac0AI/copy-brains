## Selling Cal AI to MyFitnessPal: the deal and the numbers

- Zach sold Cal AI to MyFitnessPal (his biggest competitor) at 18, about a year and a half after launch. The two had talked for a while as the relationship built.
- MyFitnessPal bought 100% of the company. Cal AI and MyFitnessPal stay as separate apps. Zach stays through a transition and holds no equity.
- Deal price and cash-versus-stock terms are undisclosed.
- 2025 revenue was $30M. January 2026 revenue was $5.7M, so ARR is now about $50M. A year earlier it was around $1M per month, then over $3M per month.
- His reason for selling: combining resources would help the most people become healthier. The two apps have different core audiences, with overlap where they competed head-to-head.
- Team at sale: about 30 full-time people running app, marketing and design, plus a separate team of virtual assistants.
- He plans to drop out of college, start something new, and says the next one would shoot for a billion.

## Growth channel progression: influencers, then paid ads

- Stage 1: fitness influencers posting UGC drove growth to roughly $2M/month.
- Saturation: the big fitness influencers share audiences, so each additional one added less. Cal AI then worked with non-fitness influencers, which also worked.
- The big unlock was performance marketing on TikTok, Instagram and Facebook, which took revenue from about $3M/month to $5.7M.
- Influencers still account for "a few million every month" and never died down. Paid ads sit on top of them.
- The mix compounds. Having already been everywhere on social, Cal AI now runs direct-response ads to warmed-up people.
- Organic and influencer awareness make paid ads perform better. Paid ads are where you "liquidate" that awareness.
- Mr. Beast sponsorship:
  - It cost $500K.
  - Zach flew to North Carolina, was in the video, and a friend on the Beast team made the connection.
  - The team never sent an invoice. Zach reached out months later to pay.
  - The ad itself was slightly unprofitable on conversions.
  - The brand authority helped sign more deals later, which likely made it profitable overall.

## Running paid ads: structure, creative and the agency mistake

- Zach started with an agency to run the influencer content as ads. They capped at about $5K/day in spend. He dislikes agencies because "they're never going to care as much as someone that's actually on the team." He took ads in-house, cycled through a few hires, and built a solid team, which took time and a lot of money.
- Campaign structure:
  - Keep it simple. Over-complicating the many Facebook settings hurts.
  - Cal AI runs mainly one scale campaign (CBO) and one testing campaign.
  - No interest targeting or lookalikes. "The algorithm is just so good at this point that you target with creative."
  - Retargeting is done in the creative itself, e.g. "you've probably seen our ads before."
- Creative is the whole strategy: "You just set it up once and then it's all about the creative." Meta's Andromeda update surfaces ad combinations (ad A then ad B converts), so you need many creatives.
- Optimization event: Cal AI optimizes for starting a free trial (3-day trial, $30/year price).
  - Apple only gives high-quality signal in the first 24 hours, so a purchase 3 days later doesn't reach Facebook in time.
  - The team models whether a given cost per trial start is profitable.
- Retention: Zach says annual health and fitness subscriptions average roughly 30% retention.
- Tools: AppsFlyer as the mobile measurement partner. It never gives fully clear data, so a team member keeps a model of true revenue by source.
- Influencer-style content plateaued as ads at about $10-15K/day. Direct, dedicated videos scaled to about $40K/day. Those say "this is an app I use to track calories, I just take a picture" within the first 5 seconds.
- Many winning creatives have no talking. Examples are gym flexing with music, scanning food, and a caption like how he cut a number of pounds in a month.
- Zach left creative direction to creators through an affiliate program (run on Tribe, coordinated via WhatsApp). Creators make videos exclusively as ads for a percentage of revenue, and some make a lot.

## Attribution tricks for apps

- App Store custom product pages: create a separate page for paid traffic and link each ad to it. The App Store shows revenue per page, so you see the minimum revenue an ad drove.
  - Inflate by about 30%, since some viewers search the app name themselves.
  - It works well early. It gets hard to manage with many ads.
- Separating influencer from paid revenue: send all paid ads to a custom product page and compare against organic search traffic on the main page.
- Modeling big sponsorships (e.g. the Mr. Beast video):
  - Clicks in the description times a multiplier, assuming maybe 1 in 10 click.
  - Promo code usage, assuming maybe 1 in 8 use codes.
  - The revenue spike on posting day versus an average day.
  - If the methods point to a similar number, treat that as the answer.
- Attribution is "the hardest thing" because of Apple privacy rules. His approach: look at spikes, run incrementality tests (turn a channel off for a few days, then on), and hire gritty people who can separate noise.

## App design built for ads

- Cal AI has a creator version of the app for filming.
  - It shows a big Cal AI logo on every page.
  - It shows a perfect multi-week streak.
  - Viewers of the recording see the brand name and a flawless-looking app.
- He compares it to burger ads where the burger is sprayed to look perfect.
- Zach knew his marketing strategy from launch: "I always knew from the start, as soon as I launched, what marketing strategies I would take."

## Team, hiring and co-founders

- Co-founders and roles:
  - Henry Langmack, CTO, met at a coding camp at age 10.
  - Blake Anderson, hands-on advisor who put in early money, advised on influencer marketing and early product design.
  - Jake Castillo, CMO/COO, built the systems to scale influencer marketing and kept things organized.
  - Zach, CEO.
- Early on, founders carried everything. The team used geographic arbitrage with roughly $1K/month developers in India, and he says the work quality showed.
- Investing in real talent changed things. The company started growing on its own, and recent months of his time went to talent, team, vision and hiring.
- When he stepped away for a month at the start of college, morale and numbers stagnated, then recovered when he returned. His takeaway is that someone must set the vision and remind people why they're doing it, which is "extremely important" once you have product-market fit.
- Co-founder advice:
  - Complementary skills matter: an operator, a creative marketer and a technical builder.
  - Friends with the same skill set can build alongside each other rather than on the same product.
  - Define zones of genius and a final decision-maker per area, using the "disagree and commit" approach.

## Starting an app today: ideas, tools and marketing

- It's "the best time ever to build." Zach points to a high-school friend who built Wrestle AI / Fight AI on Roar, never coded, learned organic TikTok marketing, and now makes about $30K/month.
- Claude Code is the best build tool. Vibe-coding tools are good enough to reach tens of thousands per month, but weaker.
- Don't build unless you know how you'll market it. Solving a problem in your own life usually works.
- Channel strategy:
  - Performance ads (TikTok, Facebook) take a subscription or pay-up-front app the furthest.
  - Another channel may be more profitable at first.
  - Social apps need unique growth strategies.
- With no budget, make videos yourself (maybe 1 in 10 goes viral). Reinvest early revenue into UGC creators, then ads take you from roughly $10K to $100K or $1M to $5M.
- Marketing beats building: "If you had the worst app but amazing marketing, you would still get downloads." Taste and design matter, and the creative person has the advantage over the pure developer.
- To learn paid ads: talk to founder friends and watch free content. Spend roughly $50-100/day on experiments. Learn the 20% that explains 80%, then hire someone who knows the rest.

## Speed, self-belief and mindset

- Speed was the top core value at Cal AI. He told every new hire on their first call that "speed is our number one core value" and "you should never be the bottleneck." If a call stalled on someone, he got them into the call and had it done immediately.
- Self-belief came from early wins, such as winning a hackathon at 12 against older students. He believes "I can always execute": given a roadmap, he can take it and execute it flawlessly.
- High standards: he kept iterating Cal AI's design until it met the standard he demanded.
- Language: he points to Tony Robbins ("words become thoughts, thoughts become beliefs"). He spoke in "when I'm rich" terms even as a kid, and says to speak of success as already happening.
- Meditation and journaling did not drive his success. He did them before Cal AI, stopped, then started Cal AI.
- Uncertainty: when stuck, he and his co-founders ask what the maximum downside and upside are, make the best decision with what they know, and trust they'll figure it out together.
- His YouTube: raw, unedited rants to document the real founder journey, including that "it still feels like the ship is sinking every day."

## The App Mafia course and lessons on education content

- Zach and fellow founders lived in a Miami mansion and made content. They launched a paid course, then made it free after about 3 days, and the community add-on earned a few hundred thousand dollars that they spent within a month.
- The larger vision was a "Shopify for apps." It wasn't sustainable because everyone ran other companies and wasn't full-time. Their lives were also at different points.
- Lesson: they deliberately went controversial ("internet money gurus"). He now thinks that was unnecessary.
- He built the course for his younger self, who wanted to start a company but had no idea how.
- College view:
  - He went for the social life and doesn't regret it.
  - He doesn't think college is needed for founders. He'd want a chief innovation officer to keep curricula current, plus Claude Code in classrooms, first-principles problem solving instead of memorization, and AI integrated everywhere.
