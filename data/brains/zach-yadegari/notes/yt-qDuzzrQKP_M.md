## How Zach found and built Cal AI: the "aha moment" product framework

- Zach starts from marketing, not code: "I always like to think about things from a marketing first perspective." He looks for an eye-catching, captivating, useful idea, then builds a full product around it.
- Cal AI's single hook is "take a picture of your food, it tells you the calories and macros." The rest (food database, barcode scanning) copies what existing calorie trackers do, but only the photo feature is marketed.
- Other examples he endorses: TurboLearn (record a lecture, get notes and quizzes) and Quitter (a panic button for quitting porn, wrapped in streaks and milestones).
- To find an idea, he scrolls the top 200 of app store categories (education, health and fitness, lifestyle, games). He looks either for something nobody has innovated on in years, like MyFitnessPal, or for something new built on a new AI model.
- He suggests packaging new model capabilities into their own apps, for example a virtual try-on "Closet AI" built on an image model. The app should improve as the technology does.
- He prefers utility apps to social apps. Social apps have a cold-start problem, since they need many users before anyone gets value. Hybrids (a useful solo app plus social features, like the calendar app Saturn) are easier.
- Cal AI was roughly his fifth app. Before it came Grind Clock (a motivational alarm clock, 20,000+ downloads in two weeks), Flex (a social gym app), a "Linktree for payments", a motivational wallpaper app and a breathing app.
- His view on luck: "if we keep launching apps, inevitably one will succeed." He says they weren't going to stop until something worked.

## Building the MVP and validating it fast

- The first Cal AI version took about three weeks to build, plus a week for App Store review. It had two screens (settings and calories left for the day) and one button to take a picture of food.
- He doesn't spend more than a month on an MVP. It should be the main feature plus the minimum surrounding components for a decent user experience.
- He advises against outsourcing the first product. You can't manage a developer if you can't do the work yourself, and incentives are misaligned. Project-based pay rewards fast, unscalable code. Hourly pay rewards over-reporting hours. Equity co-founders are the aligned option.
- His claim: with AI tools such as Cursor, ChatGPT and Figma for design, a beginner who puts in about four hours a day can launch an MVP within a month. Keep the MVP to few buttons.
- On the "market the design before building" approach: a friend posted Figma-based TikToks that got millions of views, then took months to ship through an outsourced build. By launch his posts wouldn't break 1,000 views, and competitors had released first. If you test this way, be ready to build very fast.
- Validation: pick the one dream influencer closest to the product's audience. If it doesn't work with them, "it's not going to work with anyone." He counts it as working if a promotion is profitable, for example $100 paid generating $1,000 in revenue. Then he expands to more influencers.

## Influencer marketing playbook and ROI math

- Zach calls influencer marketing the engine behind Cal AI. Blake Anderson, a co-founder who had grown UMAX and RZGPT to hundreds of thousands of dollars a month, supplied the strategy.
- Cal AI worked with about 250 influencers, with six-figure monthly spend on them. Paid ads were about $3,000 a day when he spoke (down from $5,000 after switching to a free trial). Paid ads are "by far the least" of the marketing effort.
- Zach says brands fail by not predicting influencer ROI ahead of time. They overspend and file it under "brand awareness." Pure CPM or affiliate deals fail because influencers can take guaranteed upfront pay elsewhere. So you need to know a safe price per influencer upfront.
- He judges by revenue per thousand views (RPM) versus cost per thousand views, not CAC. Cal AI's RPM varied by influencer, roughly $5 to $20.
- Evaluation rules:
  - Followers don't matter, only views.
  - Not all views are equal. He assesses community strength, niche, gender and demographics.
  - Cal AI built internal tools for this.
  - He'll work with any size, including influencers with zero followers, if the deal is profitable.
- Creative control: creators decide the content. The ask is to expect the same views as their normal videos, with Cal AI sneaked in subtly (for example, a gym vlog with a Cal AI screen). An obvious ad ("sponsored by Cali, everyone go download") "is going to get two views."
- Bigger influencers can be cheaper than small ones, because 10K-follower accounts sometimes charge absurd rates (like $10K for a video that gets 1,000 views).
- Audience: most users are men aged 15 to 25, mirroring the fitness and bodybuilding influencers they hire. He wants to reach more women but says there are fewer female fitness influencers.
- Exclusivity: he calls the exclusivity deals with the influencers Cal AI's biggest moat.
- Outreach: early on he sent about 100 DMs a day with no replies for the first few weeks. DMs get lower response rates than email but better rates, since emails often reach managers who add their cut. At one point he also paid to use a large influencer's account for outreach.
- Single-TikTok value: one TikTok has made upwards of $50,000 in revenue.
- Theme and meme pages: Cal AI has run meme pages (for example, scanning a blended Thanksgiving smoothie).

## Pricing, paywall and subscription strategy

- Cal AI charges $30 a year or $10 a month, with a three-day free trial. About 95% of subscriptions are yearly, so he said churn couldn't be measured yet.
- They A/B tested many prices and paywalls. The $30 yearly plan beat a $70 yearly plan on revenue per download, because many more people subscribed even though each paid less.
- Android is under a tenth of revenue.
- Apple pays out about two months after revenue is earned, so Cal AI scales on money earned two months earlier. Cash reinvested into growth means almost no profit taken out. He considered payout-advance services but said scaling faster would be less efficient.
- Ideas for upsells: an experiment launching within two weeks (software, not a scale). He also floated shipping a shirt after a user loses 10 pounds and selling a physical scale.
- Retention tactic: users who turned off auto-renew now see a banner warning they'll lose access after a set number of days. A new hire focuses on LTV.
- His three company levers: distribution, onboarding flow and paywall conversion, and retention/LTV.
- Numbers at the time of recording: just over $2 million revenue in the previous month; the projection was $24 million by year end; he gave a 12-month figure of about $15 million. Zach estimated the app's value at $40 to $120 million, noting that valuations are subjective. A million downloads came in about three to four months, before paid ads started.

## Viral content, hooks and distribution tactics

- Totally Science: Zach made unblocked-games TikToks by recording his screen in class with text-to-speech, saying "I was bored in school, so I made this unblocked games website. Comment what game you want me to add next."
- The comment prompt created a loop: engagement made TikTok push the video to more people, who commented too. The first video got 60,000 views and the second over a million. The site reached 5 million users in about two years and sold for $100,000 at 16.
- He monetized with Google AdSense only after 300,000 monthly users, and made over $100 on the first day with ads. He says he left thousands on the table by waiting. If he did it again, he'd partner with a game publisher like Voodoo and take a per-download payout.
- Grind Clock: motivational videos (like David Goggins) had hundreds of comments asking how to set them as an alarm. He replied to thousands of them with "just download Grind Clock." It wasn't scalable but it worked.
- Going viral on TikTok: "there is no one formula." Scroll the For You page for hours, find hooks that are working, replicate one, and keep remaking it with small changes until it stops. Then find the next hook. The first three seconds are the most important piece.
- Copycat dynamic: clones of Cal AI that look the same get influencer promotion, and viewers comment "what app is that?" and others answer "Cali." So their marketing helps Cal AI. He credits first-mover advantage and speed with getting so many fitness influencers.
- Defensibility: he says patents are hard for consumer software, so they're pursuing a trademark. The moat is the influencer network. He also argues for being the best single-purpose tool, since MyFitnessPal is bloated with sleep and heart trackers.
- Product: Cal AI runs the photo through a pipeline of several LLMs, a fine-tuned model and a food database RAG model, with different pathways per food type. Against "just use ChatGPT," he says Cal AI gives the dashboard and tracking.
- Testing: they now A/B test features and check whether users with a feature stay longer. In early months, features were based on his own intuition as a user.

## Team structure, equity and how Zach operates

- There were four co-founders. The equity split was 30/30/30/10, with the last 10% going to Jake, who joined a couple of months after launch and leads marketing. Henry is CTO. Blake gave strategy and now works on other projects.
- Zach's argument for equity: people who can push a team need real incentive. "Without it, he would not have been willing to share as much."
- Zach's own role changed from programmer to lead marketer to lead product person. Around the ten-month mark he moved to working on the business: recruiting, talking to other founders and looking for levers to 10x the company. The team was nearly 20 people.
- His decision filter for product: if something confuses him for a second, he multiplies that time and effort by five to ten to estimate how an average user will feel.
- Prioritization: if forced to pick one lever, marketing, because a bad product with great marketing still gets downloads while a great product with none gets zero. He treats programming and product as different, and says product intuition is rarer than coding. He says marketing is the most in-demand skill.
- Mindset: "Brute force it like Thomas Edison", meaning start building and test instead of theorizing. He gives the Boring Company as an example.
- Advice: on seeking opinions, he cites Naval: "If you want to make the wrong decision, ask everyone their opinion." His other rule is that there are no rules, and any heuristic can be wrong in another industry.
- Reason for success: "giving up too early." He started tutoring kids in coding at 10 for $30 an hour, and says anyone can do what he does with enough reps.
- Ambitions: hardware is next (likely glasses, later brain chips). He says he wants to be at the wheel as founder, valuing freedom over a salary, and sees top-10 wealth as a side effect of impact.
- Ideas he floated for virality: a celebrity FaceTime AI as a hook, with the app built around paid features like celebrity voices and videos. He thinks the roast-from-a-photo feature alone wouldn't be paid for. He also sees AI chat apps growing from loneliness.
