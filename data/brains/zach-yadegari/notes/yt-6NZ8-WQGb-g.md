## How Zach Yadegari built Cal AI to $2M/month in about 10 months

- Cal AI is a calorie-tracking app: take a photo of a meal and get calories, protein, fat and carbs. Zach says the scan is about 90% accurate, and notes that FDA nutrition labels can be up to 20% inaccurate.
- He started it at 17 with three co-founders: Henry Langmack (CTO, a friend from a coding camp they attended at age 10), Blake Anderson (found on Twitter, had two earlier apps with a few million downloads) and Jake Castillo (COO, handles organization).
- Launched in May. The first month was slow. The second month did about $30,000. June was the first six-figure month. By October the app did its first $2 million month (about a $24 million annual pace).
- Revenue in the first 12 months would exceed $10 million. The company is fully bootstrapped, has 15 full-time employees, and is profitable at more than 30% margins. The founders pay themselves nothing and reinvest everything.
- Over 95% of subscriptions are annual, so churn data does not exist yet. The app had about 65,000 reviews at 4.8 stars.
- He values the company at roughly $100 million, which puts his stake in the tens of millions on paper.
- Bet behind the product: there is a middle ground between hardcore trackers who weigh every gram and people who track nothing. Cal AI targets that middle.

## Start as an AI wrapper, then build the real product

- Zach compares the path to dropshipping in ecommerce: the wrapper is the proof of concept, and you manufacture it yourself once you find traction.
- The first version was a thin wrapper over ChatGPT and other AI tools. It had one feature: photo in, calories out, plus a daily breakdown.
- Only after seeing people like it did the team add more features.
- He dismisses the "just an AI wrapper" criticism: "we started as an AI rapper and I think that that's something all apps should do."
- Early validation came from influencer posts, not from family and friends. He discards feedback from family and friends as biased.
- He later pushes the same logic for new ideas: take any existing tool category (calendar, notes, calorie trackers) and ask what the AI version looks like.

## Influencer marketing as the only growth channel

- All growth came from influencer marketing. Nobody on the team makes videos.
- He says he did make the videos himself for his earlier site, Totally Science.
- Targets are fitness influencers on Instagram and TikTok. Cold outreach mostly goes unanswered; the first response took over two weeks.
- DM format, A/B tested: lead with "paid promo?" because influencers only see the first line or two in their inbox preview. Then: the app lets you track calories just by taking a picture of your food, we think it fits your audience, happy to hop on a quick call.
- Pricing is paid up front, not CPM. CPM would guarantee profitability, but influencers will not accept it because other brands pay in advance.
- This means predicting ROI before any video exists. He looks at the influencer's previous videos and also "not all views are worth the same": read the comment section to gauge how strong their community is, then weigh what to pay. He says they track "a ton of factors and variables."
- Beyond pay, he gives influencers video ideas, using lessons from his earlier TikTok work, but mostly lets them run: "they're the professionals they know what goes viral."
- Start small: the first feedback came from just two influencers posting on their stories.

## Funding it: the unblocked-games site

- Before Cal AI, in freshman year, he built Totally Science, an unblocked-games website for school laptops. It got around school web filters and was categorized as educational.
- It had a hide key: pressing the Tilde key redirected to Google Classroom if a teacher approached. His dad made him add terms of service banning use during class.
- Growth: about 5 million users via TikTok. Zach recorded his own screen in class and told viewers to go to the site, with a caption "comment what game to add next" to farm comments and push virality.
- Money came from ads on the site. It made about $60,000 over two years, and he sold it for $100,000 at 16. That money funded Cal AI.
- He reinvested nearly all of it into Cal AI; the only thing he spent on himself was a trip for friends to Miami for his 18th birthday.
- Earlier, he taught coding lessons to kids for money from a young age. He says he has coded since age seven.

## Idea frameworks: how he would find the next app

- **Work backward from the "aha moment."** Find a magic AI trick that works as marketing material, then wrap an app around it so people stay. Cal AI: photo of food, get calories, then tracking and charts keep users. He cites Fitness AI as a parallel (body scanner shows body fat percentage as the hook, workout tracking as the retention).
- **Take an existing popular app category and ask what the AI version is.** Examples he gives: Motion (AI calendar), Turbo Learn AI (record a lecture, get generated notes), AI calorie tracking.
- **Ideas he offers:**
  - An AI journal app that periodically sends insights from your entries, e.g. "on Monday and Tuesday you hung out with Sally and had a bad day." He thinks AI therapists are an open space; existing ones fail to make you feel heard, and voice mode could help.
  - A tool that converts a native iOS app to Android (or the reverse). Cal AI built in Swift for a smoother iPhone experience; the Android port cost tens of thousands of dollars and pulled engineers off iOS, and every feature must be built twice. He thinks AI handles about 90% already, so it could start as an AI-powered agency with human finishing.
  - Remotely configurable onboarding flows with A/B testing. Every consumer app needs an onboarding survey that primes users before the paywall, and changing it currently requires an App Store update that takes days. A system to swap questions and screens remotely and measure conversion, completion and drop-off would be large.
- Good startup ideas often come from papercuts you hit while building. He says working at a startup is a great way to find ideas.

## Focus: why they dropped the app-studio plan

- For a few months they briefly considered becoming an app studio, building many AI apps and applying the same marketing, because their real edge is marketing, not app development.
- They dropped it because at Cal AI's scale and growth rate, staying full-time on it paid off more. The time to take another app to six figures could instead add seven figures to Cal AI.
- Reason: improvements compound. Higher retention raises LTV, which allows spending more on the funnel, so "one plus one can equal three instead of two."
- Cash note: app store payouts arrive two months after earnings, so each month's revenue from two months back goes straight into marketing. He expects a surplus that cannot be spent on growth within a few months.

## Running a young company and mindset

- Managing older employees is hard. He has had to fire someone with kids and admits imposter syndrome, but acts the way he knows he should anyway.
- He reads "The Great CEO Within," keeps it on his desk, and tries to lead by inspiring people rather than ordering them.
- He spent July in a San Francisco hacker house with his CTO, working from a co-working space. It was productive but lonely, since everyone was years older.
- Long-term he sees Cal AI as a stepping stone. It could broaden into nutrition tracking linked to health. Otherwise he wants it to make him financially free so he can start a company driven by impact. His goal is to build something as ubiquitous as the iPhone.
- On sharing his playbook publicly: people usually know what it takes but want an easier way. Only a select few will put in the hours, and he wants to help them.
- He prefers learning from someone ten steps ahead rather than a hundred.
- Influences: The Social Network, David Goggins, MrBeast, Elon Musk, Blake Anderson, and Founders podcast-style stories of dead founders.
- Plan: finish high school (4.0 GPA, 34 on the ACT), attend college mainly for friendships, then drop out after a semester or two.
