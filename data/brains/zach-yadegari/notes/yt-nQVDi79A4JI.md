## Starting Flow with a physical product and a thin cash runway

Zach started Flow after selling Cal AI. He wanted something physical with a big TAM, and he says "the things with the biggest TAMs are really the things that everyone uses on a day-to-day basis." He also noted that many big companies aren't innovating on technology people use daily.

- He looked at many companies and niches, and found that manufacturing time for hardware is long, which clashes with his software habit ("I have a lot of ADHD. I want things done instantly").
- He landed on a sleep device similar to Brick, which blocks the phone for screen time but has no alarm function. He asked what other use cases a Brick-style device could have, and arrived at an alarm.
- Flow is a bigger brand meant to hold multiple products. The first is the Flow alarm clock: the alarm rings on your phone and you can't stop it until you tap the phone to the Flow dock, so you have to get out of bed.
- Zach says he can't take long breaks from work and got bored fast after selling Cal AI.

## Assembling the team around specific gaps

Zach picked people for specific roles in the vision.

- **Face of the brand:** he wanted someone charismatic, modeled on the guy who runs Carp's ads (Carp was a brand pumping out content). He met Gabe when Gabe approached him on the street and bombarded him with questions. He checked Gabe's Instagram content, had lunch, and pitched him, knowing he'd be "down for the craziest stuff" with high energy.
- **Website and app builder:** a "grinder" who works quickly and is also strong at product design.
- **Media buyer (Nick):** someone Zach already knew, who runs Meta ads. Zach's view of good media buying: understand the core fundamentals, turn off the extra things Meta tries to turn on for you, and "keep it simple stupid."
- He also works with a designer for the dock and app UI.

## Pre-selling to get past a long manufacturing lead time

They started in late February by getting the Flow Dock manufactured, calling manufacturers on Alibaba.

- The chosen manufacturer needed 3 months because Chinese New Year was approaching.
- Rather than wait, they launched a pre-sale anyway.
- They made it feel scarce but were clear that orders wouldn't ship for a while: get it now or miss out.
- Side effect: with customers still in pre-sale and not yet holding docks, they couldn't launch a subscription at first.

## Cash discipline: injecting capital and scaling ads to profitability

A month and a half after launch, the last 30 days were slightly under break-even, and CAC had risen quickly.

- Zach injected $200K at the start, spent $150K, and had $10K left. He spent $7.5K of that paying the ad bill, then injected another $100K.
- His rule for the new money: "We should all be operating under the assumption that like this is all we have." The team had about $105K and needed to achieve profitability off it.
- He scaled ads down despite losing momentum: "sometimes you have to take one step back in order to take two steps forward."
- At a later point, CPAs were $21 against a $29 break-even target (the days before were about $45 and $50), after lowering budgets. They could run about $300K a month profitably but wanted to be "in the millions," which he said would come from raising LTV via subscriptions.
- He notes the uncertainty of tweaking variables: you change something, it hurts, you turn the dial back, but "it's too late and you never get as good as a result that you once had."

## Subscription attach-rate tests and the LTV logic

Zach and Nick tested a "Flow Plus" subscription offered at checkout with a 3-day free trial.

- First test: opt-in box under the buy button gave about 50% opt-in, which Zach worried was deceptive since it sat below the fold. A bug later turned out to be that the subscription was auto-added to the cart in one test arm.
- Price tests: he cut the initial price from $20 a month to $10 a month to A/B test. Corrected numbers: $40 a year had 77% opt-in versus 45% for $9.99 a month, and the monthly option dropped overall conversion about 30% while $40 a year dropped it only 6%.
- Moving the checkbox above the buy button: attach rates held (about 69% and 63% before, 67% and 69% after), so placement wasn't deception. Overall conversion dipped slightly.
- Retention: free trials tied to a $40 dock purchase mostly converted, because the card is verified by the dock payment. Free trials with no charge see many declines.
- A four-way price split followed: $40 a year, $100 a year, $5 a week, and $10 a month, with a weekly price noted as common in apps but rare in ecommerce.
- Strategy borrowed from Cal AI: don't aim for profit on day one. Get as many subscriptions as possible, break even up front, and make money on the second renewal.

## The unit-economics math that justifies spending more on CAC

Zach walks through his reasoning for raising the allowable CAC.

- Average order value about $44, fulfillment about $15 per order, leaving about $29 that can be spent to acquire a customer before it's unprofitable.
- Adding the subscription conservatively: $40 a year, at least 50% keeping the box checked ($20), at least 60% of trials converting ($12). That adds $12, taking the allowable spend to about $41.
- His contrast: "An average business person would be like, 'Okay, we have $41... let's still stay under the 41.' A smart business person would recognize that this $41 is a recurring subscription" and would spend right up to that number, making profit from renewals a year later. He calls the numbers super conservative.
- He says it would take one to two months to know monthly renewal rates.

## Doing the work himself and verifying the data

Zach stays hands-on across product and operations.

- He recoded app UI changes with Codex after redesigning screens in Figma, simplified the alarm-setting flow, and built an AI customer-support dashboard: emails enter a queue, the AI uses guideline documents, responds, and escalates to a human when needed. He also wired up cancel-and-refund for users.
- On coding with AI: "it doesn't feel like coding"; non-technical people "have no excuse" for not building million-dollar apps.
- On email, he notes ramping from zero to thousands of sends needs a warm-up period with Klaviyo and inbox providers.
- He calls customers who abandoned checkout, asking why they didn't buy and whether the subscription option was clear. One said she hadn't looked closely and wasn't sure she could use the product without the subscription.
- He found Triple Whale data inaccurate and that the dock alone, without subscriptions, was losing money. Attach rate on the plans was uncertain, and CAC spiked after adding the subscription to every product.
- Ideas raised: a money-back guarantee if Flow doesn't get you out of bed, and unchecking the subscription by default or making clear in the funnel that the core features work without it. Zach also considered whether Brick, which sells at $60, shows pricing headroom.
- A recurring theme: he thought the subscriptions had it "golden" and found things "way worse than I thought."

Zach saw an octo-pouch product on Instagram that he is sure would be a winning ecommerce product, since his girlfriend and her friends use it. He had not decided whether to stay in the tight productivity niche under Flow or run ads for any winning product.
