## Starting from zero: the first 1,000 users

- Zach's first ~100 Cal AI users came from TikToks he made himself. The first 1,000 required working with health and fitness influencers.
- For Cal AI the niche was obvious: bodybuilders and anyone interested in diets. For other apps he says the niche can be harder to pin down.
- Early influencers posted via Stories, not full videos. Stories are cheaper, usually reach the target audience, and allow a direct app link, so he could test many influencers and niches faster.
- Each creator got a custom link through App Store Connect. He could only tell which creator converted best when they posted on separate days, so he scheduled them that way. Early on it is hard to get replies at all, so he took whoever was available.
- Cold outreach is slow at first without social proof on the sending account. Email worked well early. Over time, influencers tagging the brand account brought followers, and creators who saw the brand on other pages started reaching out themselves.

## Influencer outreach system (DMs at scale)

- In month one the outreach was bulk DMs and emails, by hand. After roughly a month he had 10 influencers on fixed contracts posting four times a month each, then kept increasing the number every month.
- He moved DMs to virtual assistants (VAs), who send around the clock from multiple accounts. Instagram caps at about 50 DMs per hour (beyond that you are blocked for a few hours), so they use several accounts and stay under the limit. VAs switch between TikTok and Instagram. This still runs today.
- He gives VAs the full script. The key is getting the influencer to open the message and want to reply. Every DM includes "Paid promotion?", which sits in the message preview next to "I'm your biggest fan" style messages and gets clicked because creators want to be paid.
- The rest of the message briefly says what the app is, then something like "let us know if you're interested" and more details come later. He avoids long essays about why they are reaching out or how much will be paid.
- Messages must sound like a real person. If it feels like ChatGPT or a bot, there is no reply. He also says to respect the creator's time, since nobody reads a long message.
- Targeting: for each app he picks a niche, makes a fresh TikTok or Instagram account, and engages only with that niche's videos so the For You page fills with it. VAs then browse that feed and DM everyone who appears, with a few criteria added.

## Content strategy: make the app's "wow moment" visible

- For Cal AI the hook is scanning food. Creators don't name the app in speech. They post their normal content (for example "day in my life") and scan their food as part of eating.
- The app name is placed as text prominently at the top of the screen. Viewers see "Cal AI" as text and know to download it.
- His first TikTok failed. The fault was theirs: they were poor at approving videos, and didn't yet know what makes viewers see the app and then download it. It took several attempts to settle on product placement and find the app's "aha" moment. Some creators' views are worthless and convert nothing.
- Now there is a firm system with the key points to show in every video. He argues apps need a visual hook; text-only apps are harder to promote because creators' posts look like blunt paid promotion.
- Team members tested user-generated content (UGC) accounts, where creators run branded accounts. After about three weeks no video had gone viral, and they were still working out the right content strategy.

## Tracking and attribution

- At the start, after each post he watched the App Store Connect feed for 24 hours, noted spikes, and worked out whether a given post or a different one caused each spike.
- He analyzed each winner by creator, niche, gender and video type, and from that built a mental model that still drives growth and attribution. They now post about 30 videos a day, so they can't watch every spike.

## Paid ads and the three pillars

- They take about 400 new influencer videos a month and test them as Meta ads. Paid is currently a small share of revenue; influencer marketing has been the core throughout, and meaningful ad spend only began about a month or two before the interview.
- Adding free trials beat no trial in an A/B test, but the switch broke their Meta ad optimization, so they restarted those campaigns from scratch. They are re-scaling with a focus on optimizing for free trials rather than paid subscriptions. He calls this a minor setback.
- They use an agency for Meta media buying, but he says it makes sense to outsource only that. He names three pillars of a consumer business: onboarding flow, paywall (conversion rate), and the in-app experience, plus the marketing funnel. In his words, you shouldn't outsource a core growth lever: "If you could have massive success by outsourcing it, everybody would do it." He is learning paid ads himself.

## Onboarding and paywall tactics

- Onboarding for Cal AI asks for height, weight and goal weight. His main advice is A/B testing many theories.
- A recent test added animations between screens, which maybe doubled onboarding length and still lifted final conversion by 10%. His explanation: the user invests more time, so they want to try the app and not waste it.
- He recommends replaying the answers users gave in onboarding at the paywall (for example, why they want to lose weight). He also suggests asking a series of yes/no questions (healthier? lose weight? reduce fat?) so users are primed to say "yes" at purchase. He admits he hasn't A/B tested these psychology tactics as much as he should.
- Paywall: in his experience, showing multiple price options converts higher and earns more revenue per viewer.
- Pricing test: $70/year instead of the current $30/year would have given 10% more revenue but about 30% fewer users. They chose to keep $30 because the extra users spread the app by word of mouth and growth will outweigh the 10%. He stresses deciding up front whether you optimize for revenue or user count.
- No free tier: there is a free trial but no free usage. They didn't test freemium, and like being "the premium calorie tracking app" for people who want a better experience than weaker free alternatives.

## Product, team and scaling

- He built Cal AI partly for himself after trying other calorie trackers that didn't suit him, so he knew what he wanted as a consumer. Eight months in, he leans on "no-brainer" features, user emails, and A/B tests to find the retention levers.
- Things moved faster than planned. He had expected to bring on co-founder Jake Castillo to run influencers in a month or two, but hired him a week later because inbound influencers overwhelmed him. The same happened with development, design, VAs and DMs. His advice: expect rapid change and be ready to adapt.
- The team is 12 people (four at the start): about 4 VAs, 4 developers, 1 designer, plus CTO, CEO, COO and head of marketing.
- Leadership: a quote from "Beyond Entrepreneurship" that leading is not pushing people to work but inspiring them to want to. Workers heads-down in code or DMs rarely ask why, so he keeps reminding them the work helps improve people's lives and fights health problems. For remote teams he says to repeat that message more, since people close their laptops and stop thinking about the company.
- He treats hard conversations as a muscle, including firing someone older with kids. He pushes into them even when uncomfortable and says he is improving.
- He says the founder should be the hardest worker on the team.

## Revenue, timing and goals

- Revenue is $1.1M a month (revenue, not profit). The app launched about eight months before the interview.
- Timing view: the tech didn't exist earlier, and waiting three years would have let someone else win. Launching a year earlier would probably have worked too, perhaps with different marketing.
- Niche creators with 20-100k followers matter: algorithms now trust niche accounts, and he argues you pay for their ability to produce viral videos, not their follower count.
- Goal: in a year, be the #1 and most-used calorie tracking app in the store, with $5M a month instead of $1M and possibly overtaking MyFitnessPal. He doesn't want to lose momentum, sell too early, or slow down from complacency.
