## Starting young: Totally Science and the first exit

Zach built his first business, Totally Science, in 2022 right after quarantine, when schools handed out Chromebooks and students played games in class.
- He noticed students kept five tabs open, one unblocked game per tab, because the games were scattered across obscure sites. He aggregated them into one site and built the first version in about a week.
- He named it "totally science" to get past school blocking filters. A name like "games hub" would have been blocked at once. He also added a Tilde-key shortcut that redirected to Google Classroom when a teacher walked by.
- Growth came from word of mouth (K-factor): one person shares it with one or more people. A TikTok video got 100,000 views, the next got 1 million, and the site grew to about 5 million users.
- Most of the games were not his own. They were scraped from other sites, which he calls legally questionable.
- After about 2 years he listed the site on Flippa. After a few weak offers he took one just under $100,000, and asked for an extra $10K by text to close immediately. Selling extra domains afterwards brought the total to about $100,000.
- He admits he negotiated less than he could have because he wanted to sell fast.

## Learning to code and what pushed him to move on

- He learned to code by binge-watching YouTube, mostly Unity/C# game tutorials (he names the channel Brackeys). He often didn't follow along and just consumed huge volumes of content every day, until he could implement the mechanics himself.
- His goal was to beat Cool Math Games. After a phone call with its CEO he realized "they are just people and people can be beat."
- A call with a 25-year-old founder changed his course. That founder had built a chat app with a few million users and a few thousand dollars a month, never went to college, and had stagnated. Zach didn't want to be a one-hit wonder, so he "burned the boats," dropped the Cool Math goal and sold.
- Reading about Nikita Bier's apps (TBH, then Gas, a rebuild of the same idea sold both times) inspired him to move into consumer apps. He started reaching out to app founders on X.

## Speed as the core value

- Speed is "the core value that I always hold in mind for everything I'm doing."
- He says people wait for permission, such as taking a class in college to learn coding, when YouTube is free right now. Without "a fire under you," you won't make it.
- Totally Science v1 went up in a week. Cal AI's first prototype took 3 weeks to build, launch and get feedback.
- "If you don't move with speed someone will beat you," especially in consumer, which is very competitive.
- He says he has never met a successful founder who doesn't operate with speed.

## Cal AI: idea, product and results

- Cal AI is an app where you photograph food and it estimates calories, protein, carbs and fat.
- The idea came from his own bulking. He quit MyFitnessPal after 3 days because typing in every meal and weighing food on a scale was too much friction. The idea existed about a year before AI models were good enough to make it feasible.
- Accuracy comes from a pipeline of several models that gather as much context about the food as possible before a final model makes the estimate. He won't share details because of competitors. He cites about 90% accuracy when the photo is taken at a natural angle, which he says beats the FDA's approved margin of error for nutrition labels.
- He says the app went from zero to 9th in the App Store's health and fitness category in 6 months, and reached $300,000 a month in under 100 days. At recording it was at about $1M a month.
- He had set a goal of $50,000 MRR by the end of summer and far exceeded it. They reinvested everything, and he says he hadn't taken cash out himself.

## Influencer marketing system

- Zach says the way they work with influencers lets them scale in 6 months what usually takes 3 years.
- Start scrappy, as a numbers game: reply to 100 influencers and maybe one answers. Doing this long enough builds a base that compounds, because more influencers give you credibility with new ones and existing influencers refer others.
- Scale by keeping influencers on retainer with deal structures that ensure profitability:
  - Estimate each creator's average views per video.
  - Negotiate a CPM (cost per thousand views) based on that average.
  - Make sure your RPM (revenue per thousand views) exceeds the CPM.
- The hard part is attribution. You can't have clickable links in the video, so you can't tell which creator drove a user, especially with several posts running at once.
- Finding creators is easier for health and fitness, where there are many. For a niche like a fashion app you have to be creative, for example "get ready with me" creators. It takes testing.
- He says big brands mostly buy mass brand-awareness campaigns that are hard to measure and unprofitable. Applying the Cal AI tactics at micro level and then scaling should find profitability.

## Distribution, validation and funding

- "Distribution 100% is where the advantage lies." He says the bottleneck for consumer companies is distribution, not building. Developers are easy to find if you pay.
- He says he has never met a head of growth who actually led growth effectively, otherwise they would have founded their own company.
- On raising capital: for consumer, he advises against it. Outside capital removes urgency because it isn't your money. If an app isn't working quickly it probably never will, so about $1,000 is enough to test feasibility. Raising only makes sense at scale, such as an Instagram-like ad model that needs millions of users.
- Validate before building. Don't spend a year building before launching. A first version doesn't need to be good.
- He recommends *The Million Dollar Weekend* by Noah Kagan, which teaches validating ideas and getting revenue within 48 hours. His example: asking friends to pay $500 upfront for a planned service.
- He has not raised money for Cal AI or most of his other businesses.

## Failures, new products and selling shovels

- Grind Clock: a motivational alarm app where a figure like David Goggins screams to wake you. It got 20K downloads in 14 days and now makes about $2,000 MRR. They hadn't yet figured out influencer systems when it launched. He plans to relaunch a bigger version called Locked In, a motivation app with content as well as alarms.
- Screenshop: screenshot an item from Instagram and find where to buy it. Influencer promos weren't profitable because intent to pay was low. His lesson: assess the immediate intent to solve a problem. People won't pay for a problem they might have in a week. He plans to use this as a framework to validate ideas before shipping.
- He is launching Viral Tech, a B2B platform packaging the internal influencer-marketing tools they built, for early-stage startups, indie hackers and late-stage companies. His reasoning: "sell the shovels during a gold rush," and more people will learn influencer marketing soon anyway.
- He says he has no great failure yet and expects one to come.

- He doesn't drink or use drugs, wanting to stay in control of himself.
