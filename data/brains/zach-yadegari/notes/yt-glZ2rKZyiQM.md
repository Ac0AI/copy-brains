## Cal AI's accuracy perception as the growth bottleneck

- Zach says May was Cal AI's best month ever, over $3 million in revenue.
- Early in the month he had $160,000 revenue days, driven mainly by appearances on Fox News, TMZ and other live national platforms.
- Later in the month revenue settled to roughly $80k a day on average. He expected it to fall back toward $100k a day, not hold the spike.
- After analyzing recent social posts, he concludes the biggest bottleneck is that many people still don't believe Cali is accurate.
- Those people think the app is only a scanner. In fact it also has a food database (track food like on MyFitnessPal) and barcode scanning.
- He describes it as the company versus many influencers making attack videos plus a comment community. Comments like "finally someone's coming out and saying this" get thousands of likes (he cites one with 5,000).
- His main goal at that point is to push the narrative that Cali is accurate.

## Market saturation and the share of customers lost to skepticism

Zach gives rough estimates, not measured numbers:
- Almost 100% of the fitness niche has heard of Cali.
- Some portion of that niche is potential customers. He guesses maybe 25% (up to about 50%) of potential customers have started the app.
- There is still room to grow.
- He believes at least 50% of potential customers won't give the app a chance because of the "not accurate" claims.
- He gives skeptics some credit: the scanning claim is hard to believe.
- Marketing mostly shows the scan feature because that is what goes viral. As a result most people don't know the database and barcode features exist.

## The lesson: pre-empt the credibility objection early

- His biggest takeaway for future projects: he should have handled the accuracy objection earlier.
- If, from the start of Cali, they had published material on why it's accurate while scaling, "that would have actually helped a lot."
- By now it is "kind of out of hand" and "going to be a big uphill battle."
- General rule he draws: when the viral hook creates an obvious skepticism, answer it in your own content before it hardens into community consensus.

## Tactics being considered to fix the narrative

- Have influencers talk to camera about accuracy, then "spark ad" (promote) those videos to put engagement behind them.
- Make high-quality videos in-house and post them. His concern is that these may not reach the skeptical audience that influencers' followers represent.
- Short influencer videos first, then longer videos that break everything down.
- Plan: start with the short version, assess how people take it, then double down.
- He is writing influencer scripts himself, going through the attack videos and their comments, which he finds overwhelming because there are so many points to rebut.
- He frames it as a step back to take two steps forward, sometimes necessary, while still pursuing other initiatives and going broader.

## The influencer script he wrote (accuracy messaging)

Key points of the script:
- Be honest about limits: "Cali does not have X-ray vision. It can only track what it sees." It won't know how much oil you use, so it estimates a typical portion.
- Quantify: the scanner is "closer to 90%," not 100% accurate.
- Borrow credibility: Cali uses the same database and barcode technology as MyFitnessPal.
- Give the strict option: for 100% accuracy, weigh your food.
- Position the scanner as the best tool for tracking calories when eating out, as long as everything is visible in the shot.
- Add product praise: the UI is the best he's seen and very beginner friendly.
- Close on mindset: weight loss comes down to consistency. "Stop overthinking it. Just start."
