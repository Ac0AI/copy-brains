## How Zach sold Cal AI to MyFitnessPal

Cal AI is a calorie-tracking app where users photograph a meal and AI logs the calories. Zach finished 2025 at $30 million in revenue, and January 2026 alone was $5.7 million. He sold the company to MyFitnessPal, which is owned by private equity. The sale closed in December 2025 and was announced in March 2026. He can't disclose the price. He stays on as Cal AI's CEO for a transition period.

- **May 2025 outreach:** He listed the 10 companies that made the most sense as buyers. He didn't cold-email them. He asked people he knew to make warm intros, framed as "you two should meet" rather than "he wants to sell". Leverage is lost if you're the one cold-outreaching.
- **Result of round one:** Many CEOs shut it down immediately. A few took calls and met the team. Offers came after only a couple of meetings. The first offers were low, about one year of profit.
- **After the lowball:** He politely declined and began to believe Cal AI might just be a cash-flowing business forever. He started looking for a CEO to bring in so he could step away.
- **What changed:** A founder who had sold his company explained how M&A bankers work. They find companies about to go public that want to roll up smaller ones and will pay fair market value.
- **The MyFitnessPal path:** Zach wanted to make Cal AI premium, since long-running big apps all are. He called MyFitnessPal's CEO to ask about freemium. The CEO answered none of it and steered the conversation toward a partnership, which became the acquisition.
- **Posture:** Play hard to get. Show interest, but make clear you're going somewhere either way.
- **Advisors:** He talked constantly to people who had already been through an acquisition. Their deals looked similar to his, so they knew the right things to say.
- **Feelings:** The excitement peaked when the offer came by phone, and the wire itself felt less exciting. He kept the excitement in check until it was signed.
- **Expected value:** His co-founder Blake Anderson taught him EV (chance of winning times the prize) and he used it to decide what to sell for.

## Growth: influencers first, then paid ads

- **Influencers:** Influencer marketing started the growth and took Cal AI to about $2 million a month.
- **The plateau:** Growth flattened at that level.
- **Performance ads:** Running ads on Instagram, TikTok and Facebook got it over the hump. He says ads now fuel most of the growth.
- **Spend:** Ad spend scaled over time. In January 2026 it was a little over $1 million.

## The MrBeast sponsorship

- **The deal:** Cal AI paid MrBeast about $500,000 to feature the app in a follow-up video, on his second channel. The video was about whether a contestant kept his weight off after being locked in a gym. Zach knew someone on the team, flew out to the set, and recorded one of the takes about the app himself.
- **Attribution:** Attribution is hard because most viewers go straight to the App Store instead of clicking a link. He used the time overlap with the video, promo code usage and other metrics.
- **Payback:** The estimate was $350,000 to $400,000 from the promo. He calls it a little unprofitable at first, but profitable long term.
- **Why it paid off:** The MrBeast name gave the brand credibility and let him close many more deals afterward. He says the profit from those later deals exceeds the total cost.

## Validating app ideas

- **Skill to build:** Learning to filter ideas matters more than learning to generate them.
- **Personal fit:** Filtering is personal. He looks for ideas he can market, that could go viral, and that he or people he knows have experienced firsthand.
- **Existing competition:** Competitors already earning revenue are proof, not a deterrent. He saw another calorie app making $200 million a year, which set the minimum ceiling in his mind. "If a company is still growing, that means that there's still a gap."
- **Market sizing:** He mostly trusts his gut instead of formal TAM research.
- **Adjacent comps:** He compares an idea to adjacent companies' revenue to judge how far it could go.
- **Example apps:** He cites Quitter (an app to quit porn, streaks, roadmaps and meditation guides, over $5 million a year) and Claim (class-action lawsuit sign-ups, about $1 million in its first 30 days).

## Mindset and building skills

- **Self-belief:** He made a video at about 13 or 14 saying he would make a million dollars before graduating high school. He says he shifted his language from "if" to "when".
- **Motivational origin:** He curated his TikTok feed with only motivational speakers like David Goggins. He saw commenters wanting those clips as alarms, and built Grind Clock, a motivational alarm clock app.
- **Skill stacking:** He applies the Pareto principle: learn the 20% that gets 80% of results, enough to hire people smarter than you and manage them. Coding helped him lead a team.
- **Early business:** At 16 he sold his unblocked-games site (5 million users, $60,000 a year from banner ads) for $100,000.

## Money and next steps

- **Portfolio:** He holds roughly 70-75% in the S&P 500, about 25% in a money market or bonds, and small amounts in Bitcoin and gold. He prefers investing in himself over other people, and plans to move money into his own ventures.
- **Next company:** He will likely raise money for the next venture and keep it more stealthy than Cal AI. He's aiming for a billion-dollar company and wants a hardware component.
- **Idea he tweeted:** A semantic search over your Instagram or X followers, with alerts when someone interesting follows you.
- **AI and the future:** He mentions Steve Cohen's advice to just survive the next three years, since no one knows where AI is heading. He notes humanoid robot hardware is a separate hard problem from AI software.
