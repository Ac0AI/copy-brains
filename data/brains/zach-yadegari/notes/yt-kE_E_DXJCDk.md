## Using Mr. Beast sponsorship as a growth and credibility play

- Zach and Cal AI paid MrBeast "half a million dollars" to be placed in one of his videos. It was a follow-up on his second channel, on whether a contestant kept the weight off after being locked in a gym for 100+ days.
- A contact on MrBeast's team helped Zach get the sponsorship. Zach was flown out to watch it happen and filmed one take himself talking about the app, which the video used.
- Attribution is imprecise because most viewers go straight to the App Store instead of clicking a link. Zach estimated results from time overlap, promo code usage and a few other metrics.
- On those estimates, the $500,000 spend brought back roughly $350,000 to $400,000 directly, so it was "a little bit unprofitable" right away.
- He believes it was profitable long term, or will be. Being able to say the app was in a MrBeast video gave Cal AI "way more credibility" and let it do many more deals afterward.
- The profit from those follow-on deals has exceeded the total amount spent on the MrBeast placement.
- At the time, the Cal AI team was about 30 people, a bit more than that.

## Making decisions with expected value (EV)

- Zach learned decision frameworks from his co-founder Blake Anderson, who taught him EV and related principles.
- EV is the chance of winning times the prize. Compare a certain outcome against the chance of losing it all against the possible upside, and generally pick whichever is greater.
- He used EV to think through selling the company. He asked what the chance was of getting to a given outcome, what the chance was of it falling apart, and what the sale was worth against those.
- Worked example (from the discussion, which Zach confirmed was "a perfect example"): a $100 sure offer has an EV of $100. Holding out for $500 at a 10% chance is worth $50, so taking the offer wins. You also have to factor in ongoing risk the business falls apart if you keep running it.
- The point is an objective way to decide instead of acting on emotion, mood, fear or greed.

## Validating startup ideas instead of just generating them

- Zach says coming up with ideas is a skill, but the more important skill is a framework for filtering good ideas from bad ones, so you can sift through them quickly.
- He says the framework should differ per person, because it depends on your skill set. His own filter is ideas that are good for him: things he's interested in and thinks he could "kill" and blow up.
- He looks for ideas that are very marketable and can go super viral.
- He looks for problems he has experienced firsthand, or that people he knows are experiencing, so he knows exactly who he's building for.
- He benchmarks against adjacent companies. For Cal AI he saw another app making $200 million a year, which set "the lowest floor of what we could get to."
- Before you have that intuition, you can analyze TAM, the competitive landscape and a few other objective metrics. Zach himself mostly goes by gut on TAM and didn't research the calorie-tracking TAM before starting Cal AI.

## Competition as proof the market works

- Zach says many people are deterred when they see that someone else already built their idea. He thinks they shouldn't be.
- Existing growing competitors are proof the idea works. If a company is still growing, there is still a gap and people who are underserved.
- Markets can be big enough for many apps to succeed. He noted that at his entrepreneurship class, everyone pitching responded with "that already exists" and was discouraged by it.

## Why apps are a good starting point, and app ideas he points to

- For someone starting out, with one of their first projects and aiming for a reasonable goal like a million-dollar company or a few million dollars, Zach thinks apps are a great space right now. This is true especially with AI, but also without it.
- Quitter, built by a friend, helps guys quit porn. It doesn't really use AI. It gamifies the process with a streak, a roadmap, rewards and meditation guides, and it now makes over $5 million a year.
- Claim (spelled "CLAIM") lets people sign up for class action settlements, such as a Pepsi suit, to get $10 to $15 when it settles. Zach says it made about a million dollars in its first 30 days, but was taken off the App Store.
- Zach says an easy "few million dollar idea" is semantic search over your Instagram or X followers. It would let you find, for example, entrepreneurs or people you'd want to date among your followers, and alert you when someone interesting follows you. He tweeted about it and it blew up.
- For his own next company he says he's "shooting for like a billion dollar company," so smaller million-dollar app ideas aren't worth his time now. He plans to be stealthier about the next one than he was with Cal AI.

## Staying consumer-focused and hedging against AI uncertainty

- Zach says he loves building products for consumers. Hearing that someone loves something he built is the biggest compliment. He wants to keep doing that and would like a future company with a hardware component, possibly alongside software.
- On AI, he says he sees a world possibly changing completely soon if AI labs reach their goals. That is scary but exciting.
- He relayed advice from Steve Cohen at a dinner: don't try to predict the end state. Work out how to extend your companies' timelines about 3 years and survive the intermediate period, then figure out what's next.
- He argues people overweight software when thinking about AGI. Humanoid robots and hardware are a separate problem that may be just as hard, and companies like Physical Intelligence are working on it.
