## Getting the first customers: one influencer story

- Cal AI's first customers came from a single paid influencer story. Zach found someone documenting a weight-loss journey with over a million loyal followers and paid him to post a story of himself scanning a meal with Cal AI.
- Cost was "probably 2000 dollars" (he wasn't certain of the exact figure).
- The story drove about 1,000 downloads in two days, roughly 100 of them paying users.
- Zach says it worked because the fit was ideal: followers were on their own weight-loss journeys, saw the app working for someone they trusted, and thought "that looks easy, I'll download it too."
- After that result, "we realized this could actually work," so the team hired as many influencers as fast as possible.
- Starting capital was $5,000, which was enough to reach profitability. After that, growth was funded by revenue from profitable spend. He had more cash from selling a games site for $100K, but did not need it.
- Advice by budget: with $0, make organic TikTok content yourself; with a few thousand dollars, pay influencers to post for you.

## The four growth levers and why Cal AI relied on influencers

Zach lists a few ways products grow:
- **K-factor above 1:** each user brings in at least one more, giving exponential growth. It is very rare (his example: NGL, where the whole experience is shareable). Cal AI never achieved this.
- **Word of mouth from a technological leap:** ChatGPT spread this way. He showed it to about 10 people, who each showed 10 more.
- **UGC (user-generated content):** 10, 20 or up to 100 people create accounts and post daily for your brand. Most posts get a few hundred or thousand views, but about 1 in 10 gets millions. With enough posters, you are nearly guaranteed a viral post every day. This was not Cal AI's main driver.
- **Influencer marketing:** a more "sniper" approach. You pay, they post, you know roughly how many views you'll get, so it is lower risk, and views convert into downloads.
- **Paid ads** (Facebook, TikTok) were the fifth channel. For Cal AI, influencer marketing was what drove success.

He tried making TikTok content himself early on. It brought a little usage but wasn't the reason for the big growth, so he went back to focusing on the product.

## Influencer marketing as a predictable, near-linear machine

- Each influencer post earns a fairly predictable number of views, which convert at a fairly predictable rate. The equation was clear and roughly linear, but fast because the team kept adding influencers at an accelerating pace for the first year and a half.
- Revenue trajectory: month 1 about $30K, month 2 over $100K, month 3 over $300K, month 4 $500K, month 6 $1M.
- Outlier posts reached 10M views, and some days brought $100K. The core was steady, predictable growth where "every influencer we added would bring us a profitable return."
- Influencer revenue got to about $2M per month and then plateaued for a few months.

**How he picked influencers**
- Look for tight-knit communities and engaged audiences. Not every view is equal.
- Prefer creators whose followers aspire to be like them and want to use what they use, "almost cult-like" loyalty.
- Minimum around 50,000 followers, but views mattered more than follower count.

**Lessons from cofounder Blake Anderson**
- Blake previously built Rizz GPT and Umax, both viral with millions of downloads. Zach credits him as a pioneer of paying influencers to promote apps.
- Key skill: integrating the ad seamlessly into the content so it stays shareable and the video doesn't flop by looking like an ad.
- He also taught how to approach creators and structure payment so the deal is profitable.

## Negotiating influencer pay

- Small creators (about 50K views per video) often ask for thousands of dollars per video, which would be unprofitable for 99% of brands.
- Insight: they don't care how many posts they have to deliver. They want the cash, and are often broke college students.
- Tactic: take the price they quote and attach conditions. Examples: "we'll pay this, but you have to hit X views and keep posting until you do," or "we'll pay this, but you give us four videos instead."
- This brought the cost per video far down and made the deals profitable at scale.

## Scaling with paid ads (and why organic creative failed there)

- Year one was all influencer content, reaching $2M/month in revenue. Then it plateaued.
- Paid ads took Cal AI from $2M to about $4M per month, a doubling.
- First mistake: reusing thousands of influencer videos as ads. That stalled at very low profitable Facebook spend, around $5,000 per day.
- Lesson: content that wins organically with the algorithm differs a lot from content that performs in paid ads.
- Fix: hire an army of creators to make content built specifically for paid ads.
- Paid creative is trial and error: find pain points, address them for your brand, and test which hooks work. It is highly iterative.
- Viral organic content did not carry over to paid. Sales-focused ads won: direct ads showing the app and the value it delivers, rather than a funny viral skit with the app dropped in at the end.

## Retention is a product problem

- Consumer churn is very high, especially when the product demands daily discipline, as a calorie tracker does.
- Zach did not run retention-specific marketing. They did email campaigns but nothing very specific.
- His view: "retention is a product problem," so improving the product naturally improves retention.
- What worked: they kept an evaluation set for the AI food scanner (photo to calories and macros). They saw a fairly direct correlation: as scan accuracy rose over time, retention rose too. So accuracy became a major focus and investment area.

## Pricing and monetization lessons from earlier apps

- Before Cal AI, he and cofounder Henry spent a year launching roughly one app per month and doubling down on the most promising. They launched about six that year; Cal AI was the one they focused on. Others were all fitness-related, including a social gym-check-in app (friends get notified when you go to the gym) and a Linktree-style payments product.
- The social app failed because social apps are hard: they have no value unless many people use them. Cal AI is useful even if used alone.
- Grind Clock (a motivational alarm app where a speaker yells at you to get up) taught the main pricing lesson. It launched free with ads. He was about 16 and didn't believe people paid for subscriptions.
- Findings: in-app ads earn little, and people will pay for apps. Making it fully paid multiplied revenue by 10x.
- He applied this to Cal AI: free trial, but you must pay to keep using it. He says it would not have gotten as far if revenue had depended on ads.
- Freemium question from an attendee: a hard paywall earns more in the short term. Freemium is a riskier bet with a longer feedback loop, because you must keep funding growth until monetization works. Weigh the trade-offs against your risk tolerance.

## Persona strategy, funding and competing in a crowded market

- Persona raised $10M. It is an AI personal assistant reachable anywhere (iMessage, app, email, phone call), with a hardware ecosystem planned: a wristband first (prototype arriving in about two weeks from China), then smart-home hubs.
- Early beta users (a few thousand) came only from his tweets and Instagram posts. Product was the focus first; he said he was shifting to heavy focus on growth and would reuse many Cal AI strategies.
- Why VC money here: bootstrapping suits someone who wants a few million in cash flow. VC money suits a big bet with a high chance of failure but astronomical upside, which is what he wants for Persona ("a generational company").
- Mindset: keep iterating and pivoting with a strong team until it works. Same approach as Cal AI.
- Competing against giants: advice to an attendee building a personal AI system was to pick a niche (for example, AI assistant for parents) and build the whole experience around it.
- Zach's own view: Persona isn't niching now, but may later. He doesn't see this market as winner-take-all like social networks (Friendster vs. Facebook), but more like smartphones, where Apple, Samsung and Xiaomi each hold around 20%. So multiple winners.
