## Zach's definition of a business and the LTV/CAC rule

- Zach says a business is "a product to sell" plus a way to sell it. The product can be physical (a seltzer), digital (software, an app), information (a course) or time (tutoring).
- Selling means generating demand and making people want to buy. Channels include walking up to people in the street, social ads, paid influencers, organic posts or a blimp. He doesn't go deep on any one channel and tells people to research the one they pick.
- What separates a good business from a bad one is profit: LTV (lifetime value of a customer) must be greater than CAC (customer acquisition cost) plus cost of goods.
- Worked example: a customer buys 10 seltzers a year at $10 each, so one-year LTV is $100. If acquiring them costs $80 and the 10 bottles cost $10 to make, total cost is $90 against $100 of revenue, which is profitable.
- LTV can be measured over different horizons (a few months, a year, three years).
- You always pay for customers, whether to ad algorithms, to influencers, or with your own time.
- He notes that companies like Snapchat and Spotify run unprofitably to take a whole market and raise prices or add products later. He doesn't teach that route and focuses on being profitable from the start, with companies making $10K to $1M or more a month.
- Background he gives: at 19, he built an unblocked gaming site in freshman year (5 million users, about $60K a year, sold for $100K at 16), then built Cali, a calorie-tracking app. Cali did about $5.7M in January 2026, its best month. He puts total revenue at roughly $30-50M in 2 years and has never raised money.

## Scalability of the product and the marketing channel

- After profitability, Zach checks that the business is scalable. Selling your own time (one-on-one tutoring) is profitable but not scalable. Hiring others to teach makes the model more scalable.
- Software is very scalable. Physical stores are harder because chains are a logistical nightmare.
- Channels have different scale ceilings:
  - Walking up to people in New York City doesn't scale.
  - Influencers scale only as far as the number of influencers in your niche.
  - Paid social ads scale most, since the algorithms "literally go out and find each customer."
- For people with no money, he recommends posting organic content: it costs $0, can be scaled later with a team, and going viral organically is "a very learnable skill, but it is a skill."
- Channel ROI decays. Early on, Cali's influencers (especially bodybuilding niche) were very profitable because no similar app existed and the audience was close to calorie tracking. Returns fell as the Cali name spread and the same audiences saw it repeatedly.

## Hiring employees so the business works for you

- Zach says the point of a business is that "you don't want to work for the business. You want the business to work for you," otherwise "you just have a job." The way to get there is employees, and a CEO's time becomes mostly interviewing and maintaining culture.
- Method: look at where you spend most of your time, then hire someone and delegate it.
- Cali sequence: he and co-founder Henry coded the app at first. He hired developers (Henry stayed CTO), then spent his time on influencer outreach, deal negotiation and post review, then delegated that, then focused on product and features, then hired a product person.
- Where he finds people:
  - Referrals from your network.
  - Upwork, where there are "diamonds in the rough." Cali's designer came out of roughly 100-200 applicants who submitted trial designs, and he credits her for the app's clean UX.
  - Headhunters, who often take about 20% of base salary. He says his best hire came through one.
  - Employees referring their friends.
  - Cold outreach.
- Interviews: he goes on vibes with no set structure, asks about experience, then follows his curiosity to judge who actually knows their skill and who is intelligent.
- He deliberately tries to scare candidates off: "This is a startup. We are staying up all night. Sometimes you might get a call from me at 3:00 a.m." He wants a culture of hustlers and grinders and wants candidates who answer "I was born for this."
- Traits he wants: smart, takes initiative (pitches ideas and spots improvements beyond their role), and smarter than him. He says don't be intimidated into avoiding that.
- Interview question borrowed from the founder of Boom Supersonic: "Teach me something" about your field, which shows how well they understand it.

## Aligning incentives: equity, bonuses, KPIs

- Zach calls aligned incentives crucial. He has heard "you can't pay an employee to care," but says the right incentives make people work much harder.
- Equity: a common structure is a roughly 5% pool split among employees, with individual grants such as 0.1% to 0.5%. Cali gave no equity at first, and he says people didn't care and the company underpaid for talent. After equity came, people worked weekends unasked, pitched features and cared about success. He thinks equity is the best tool because it ties people to the whole company, not just one metric.
- Bonuses: tie them to the employee's work. Salespeople on commission are easy to hire, because those who don't produce weed themselves out.
- Every hire should make more than they cost, even though attributing revenue to a role is hard.
- Every employee should have a KPI (one to three metrics) on a dashboard. Missing KPIs repeatedly is a reason to let them go, and crushing them deserves a bonus.
- Pay: he says you can't overpay for real talent. With AI tools, one person can do the work of 10, so one strong hire can be worth several salaries.
- Without aligned incentives, employees do enough to avoid being fired, stay "one foot out the door" and can be poached.
- He strongly prefers in-house employees to agencies. An agency takes on as many clients as possible, does enough to keep them quiet, and its account manager gets little of the upside. You can enforce speed, bias to action and culture on employees, not on agencies.

## Rules for success: speed, action, focus, and doubling down

- Speed: it was the number one core value at Cali. Don't delay communication. If a teammate needs to weigh in, he adds them to the call on the spot. "The world moves too fast. People will forget about you. Your idea will be taken by a competitor."
- Legal: get the company "somewhat papered correctly early on," but don't spend a day on contracts for a $100 payment. Handshake deals are fine early, since the time and money saved outweigh occasional losses. Get legal after you get rich.
- Sharing ideas: don't hide your idea. A busy successful founder won't steal it, and you lose valuable advice. "Ideas are worthless" and "execution is everything." Many ideas he thought were billion-dollar ideas weren't.
- Bias to action: he has seen companies fail from deliberation and from too many decision makers. He runs "monarchies" where he's the shot caller. Some decisions will be wrong, and the team pivots and fixes them.
- Focus: for an app he reduces everything to three drivers: traffic (downloads), conversion rate (the paywall), and retention leading to LTV. A new feature affects retention, a new channel affects traffic, and a paywall or price change affects conversion.
- Double down: he met a founder whose Facebook ads drove about 90% of the business while the team spent about 90% of its time on other initiatives. His advice was to scale paid ads: better creatives, more spend, a better account structure, and moving in-house from an agency.

## Pricing and A/B testing

- Pricing method: if competitors exist, start at their price, then A/B test up and down and try different pricing structures. With no competitor, pick something intuitive and test.
- A/B test means showing one price to 50% of users and another to the other 50%, and seeing which makes more money.
- He says nobody can answer "what will perform better" on the spot: "I'm not a computer. No one is." Don't ask people or guess.
- The same applies to paywalls, features, marketing hooks and ad captions. Test video hooks against each other.
- At scale he A/B tests almost every change, because untested changes can quietly hurt the user experience and LTV. Early on, don't let testing slow you down.

## Coming up with and validating ideas

- Zach says founders' origin stories are mostly "for show." A founder he knows built a billion-dollar custom shampoo company without caring about hair; he just saw a market gap.
- He agrees with Thomas Edison that "ideas are in the air" and free-flowing.
- His approach is to first build the skill of recognizing a good idea. Considerations: size of the market (TAM), the competitive landscape, and any advantages you have. He also uses pattern matching ("this worked in that space, so it should work here"), and he checks whether it solves a problem he has himself.
- Niche problems: if only 1 in 100 people has your problem, that's still about 80 million people out of 8 billion. Don't dismiss an idea because your friends don't relate. Weigh a successful founder's view over friends, who "just want you to play video games with [them] on the weekends."
- Sources of ideas: meeting founders and hearing theirs, problems hit while building another company, and ads for a good idea poorly executed.
- He treats idea generation as a skill, like Jerry Seinfeld writing a joke daily: come up with one new idea a day, since most are bad but quality improves.

## Co-founders, product-market fit, and the mindset for the hard parts

- Co-founders: pick people with complementary skills (one business/marketing, one product/engineering) and avoid co-CEOs. Set clear ownership, e.g. one owns marketing and the other owns product, so each is accountable.
- Finding them: friends, X, or Y Combinator co-founder matching, which he says is mostly low quality with some diamonds. Don't rush. If it's your idea, vest co-founders with a one-year cliff so you can remove them in the first year with zero equity.
- Product-market fit: he says he has never understood it. His mindset is to build a profitable business from the start and "if you're not profitable, pivot until you are profitable." Sometimes the problem is just the wrong marketing channel, not the idea.
- What makes business hard is the uncertainty, not the steps: random lawsuits, law changes, Apple or Google blocking an app upload, Facebook or TikTok banning ad accounts (both happened to Cali), competitors appearing. Treat every punch as one competitors take too, and keep getting up.
- "You genuinely can't lose unless you give up." He says he hasn't seen anyone keep trying for years and never succeed, and failures teach you until a success takes off.
- Take founder advice with a grain of salt: survivorship and probability apply, and "TikTok ads suck" may be true for one company only. Don't over-optimize either: he worked about 4 hours a day from his bed in Cali's early days, before it hit $1M a month.
