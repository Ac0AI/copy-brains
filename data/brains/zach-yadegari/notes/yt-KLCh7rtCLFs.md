## Why Zach started App Mafia and what the course is

- Zach co-founded App Mafia with Blake, Alex, and Connor. The four were in Vegas for a mobile app convention, then decided to live together in Miami. Zach and Blake had first been thinking about a personal-brand course.
- App Mafia starts with a course on building apps. Zach says they will take backlash because many people think course sellers are bad. His counter is that "for every nine bad courses, there is one that actually provides a lot of value."
- Their differentiator is that everything is public. The apps they built can be verified, and so can the revenue, which he says is "different from pretty much every course seller."
- He says they have seen more than 30 apps doing over $100,000 a month from people who came across their content. Two people he worked with now run companies doing over $1M a month by applying the playbook.
- Their reason for structuring it: they had no incentive to organize the knowledge until they decided to do it together.
- He argues drop shipping, day trading, meme coins, copywriting, and social media marketing are the popular forms of entrepreneurship. These tend to be zero-sum, low-margin, and crowded, and their sellers make money selling courses. App Mafia's pitch is that software is now accessible: AI plus tools like Firebase and Supabase mean you don't need a CS degree or a team of developers.

## Direct response marketing and when to make big brand bets

- Zach says most of their scaling is direct response, not brand awareness. They know what an influencer post costs and what it returns, and they run it only if it is profitable. The same logic applies to paid ads, UGC, and organic content. Each channel has to be profitable, then they scale the number of posts.
- Cal AI has scaled mostly through influencers and only recently started paid ads (Facebook pay-per-click).
- Recently they have taken bigger swings. They spent $500,000 on a MrBeast video promo, their most expensive ever. By his own view, the view-to-dollar math alone would not have justified it for another influencer.
- His reasons for doing it anyway: second-degree effects. Working with MrBeast makes other brands trust them, people talk about it and share it, and it builds mind share. He says "mind share" gets to a point where you have enough that growth goes hyper, and he doesn't think they are there yet.
- Risk framing at Cal AI: roughly $100,000+ a day in revenue at the time, so $500k is about five days of revenue. Apple payouts are delayed about 45 days, and revenue is not profit, so the real exposure is closer to 15 days of profit. The money was either spent on the video or paid out to themselves, and they decided delaying their own pay was fine.

## Risk tolerance, power-law bets, and bootstrapping

- Zach's view is that when an opportunity has a clear floor and a large upside, you take it every time. The team's framing for the MrBeast spend: they expected at least about $300,000 back from the $500k, with upside in the millions.
- The earlier example was Alex Eubank. They spent $100,000 a month on a deal when Cal AI was at about $500k a month in revenue. Within a few months it was over $2M a month, which they credit largely to him and the influencers he helped them get. Zach says it took them a while to learn to take these opportunities every time.
- Quitter: Alex built an app, and Zach pitched an idea to help men quit porn. They started with Reddit and X marketing. Zach put $3,000 into influencers and, paying 20% up front on deals, leveraged it into about $15,000 of placements with religious creators. That got Quitter to about $1,000 a day. He says it could have failed and he trusted his gut.
- On cheap, small bets in the same spirit: a 20k deal with a large Christian creator (Bryce Crawford) was justified by Zach even if only $10-15k came back, because of word of mouth, easier access to more creators, and larger later deals. Their biggest competitor markets only through churches, so it targets that audience on the social media channels they are good at.
- Bootstrapping downside: every dollar comes out of your pocket, so it is harder to place huge bets than with VC money. Zach says his team would "yolo everything" into a play they believed in.
- Worst-case thinking: if everything burned, they could rebuild in a month or two, so he treats flights or small outlays as non-risks.
- He says the VC path shouldn't be the default. Raising money carries an implicit expectation of chasing a huge outcome, and bootstrapping lets you work on progressively harder things as a stepping stone, like Elon (Zip2, PayPal before Tesla/SpaceX).

## Controversy, social capital, and algorithmic reach

- Zach argues people are too scared of controversy. His view is that cult-like followings come from having a core of fans and a core of haters, and that people who change how things are done are the ones remembered.
- Their approach: they built apps by seeding the algorithm on TikTok and Instagram. For App Mafia, they plan to sacrifice some social capital (dropping a course) for controversy and then use YouTube vlogs of their lives.
- On hiring: the more algorithmic reach you have, the higher-quality talent you attract. He uses Cluely and Roy Lee as examples. Inbound from sharp, hardworking people is a payoff of posting publicly.
- Longer-term content plan: systems built so that their lifestyles are the content, with a team filming and them helping on ideation.
- Brand idea for Quitter: move from direct response to seeding the algorithm so the culture shifts (porn becomes uncool, Quitter becomes cool), taking a cue from products that turned into movements like Whoop and Strava.

## Team, hiring, speed, and prioritization

- Cal AI has 30+ employees; about half are virtual assistants and the rest are specialized roles. Zach says people assume it's a solo founder, which is not true.
- His view on solo "one-person billion-dollar company" talk, based on Blake's experience: hiring a full-time employee made output jump immediately. Find cracked people and get out of their way (the Richard Branson idea).
- Hiring advice: the founders can all code, design, and market well enough to judge quality. He notes people with no technical knowledge keep hiring poor engineers because they can't tell the difference.
- Reducing cognitive load: a private chef saves hours and improves energy, which helps the business.
- Prioritize the core: for consumer apps with hard paywalls, three things matter: getting people to download, converting downloads, and getting them to stay (marketing, conversion, retention). Spend roughly 80% of time there and 20% on the rest, and let small fires burn.
- Speed is a core principle at Cal AI, Quitter, and App Mafia: if something is not done quickly you "slowly fall behind" and don't hit escape velocity. Their approach was to set deadlines after early weeks in Miami drifted.
- They killed a launch video that cost about $25-35k because it wasn't good enough, and made another. His rule: if it isn't much better than anything they've done, kill it regardless of cost.
- Confidence and many decisions build intuition. Taking life less seriously, shipping, and accepting embarrassment gives more data.

## Goals, advice, and mindset

- Zach set a goal at 14 to make a million dollars before graduating high school (there is a YouTube video of it). He did, but said it didn't feel like a finish line and that goals should keep shifting. He thinks lacking goals to chase is where depression comes from.
- He kept a 4.0 GPA in high school while building Cal AI and argues people have time (average screen time is hours a day).
- Advice that nothing is universal: for every story of what worked, there is a counterexample. He says only the laws of physics are universal, so lean into your own attributes.
- Companies on the brink of death sometimes survive and sometimes die for reasons beyond their decisions, so survivorship stories overstate what "it takes."
- Hardest thing he's overcome: being jumped at a party (broke his nose, needed surgery) while the team was taking acquisition calls, which he describes as the hardest week or two of his life.
- Future plans: no near-term need to build a billion-dollar company, preferring to enjoy being young; the team could do it but wants to be YouTubers for a while. He mentions long-term interest in AI hardware like a Whoop competitor with local inference, and AR/glasses software.
