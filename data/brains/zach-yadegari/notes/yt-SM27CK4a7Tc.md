## Starting with games: Scratch, Unity and an App Store release at 12

Zach got into building through a love of video games. As a kid he sketched game ideas and screens on paper without knowing what coding was, then signed up for a summer camp (Kidoyo) that got him into it.

- His first project was a maze game in Scratch: go through the maze, hit a wall and you restart.
- He picked up Python basics, then jumped from Scratch to C# so he could build in Unity, the engine many professional game studios use.
- He put his first Unity game on the iPhone App Store when he was 12.
- He says Scratch is great for beginners because of its simplicity.

## Totally Science: spotting an opportunity and marketing it with social video

Totally Science is an unblocked games website that plays in school and uses a few techniques to bypass school blocking systems. The idea came from his 8th-grade classroom right after COVID, when every student had a school Chromebook and was playing games.

- He noticed classmates keeping multiple tabs open, one website per game. His idea was to combine all the games from different sites into a single hub.
- His marketing was social media content he made himself: recording himself playing the site in class. Those videos reached a few million views and drove five million users to the site over two years.
- The first version "looked awful," yet it still pulled hundreds of thousands of visitors.
- It started on Kidoyo's hosting and website editor. He later moved to Visual Studio, GitHub and a virtual private server.
- After moving it onto GitHub and self-hosting, Google Analytics showed about 350,000 monthly users. His earlier free JavaScript view counter had capped out around 3,000 unique users.
- He monetized it through Google AdSense.

## Totally Science: product improvements and the exit

His biggest improvements were a much better design and automating the game pages.

- Originally every game was its own static HTML file (game1.html, game2.html, and so on).
- He replaced these with a single game.html that loads the right game from a URL parameter, so one page serves every game. A former Kidoyo student, Henry, contributed this change.
- By the second year he was getting sick of the site. He tried a homework-help Discord community platform in the same niche, but it did not work out.
- Towards the end of year two he started building iOS apps and stopped focusing on Totally Science, which is why he decided to sell.
- He listed it for sale on Flippa. The buyer already owned another gaming website and planned to grow it through SEO, which Zach says he never planned to do and would not have taken it to those heights.

## Grind Clock and the lesson in revisiting a working app

Grind Clock was his first mobile app: a motivational alarm clock with a library of sounds from motivational speakers such as David Goggins and Joe Rogan, plus Andrew Tate.

- Marketing was influencer-based, with a few motivational pages on TikTok promoting it. It reached about 15,000 users in the first two weeks, after which they stopped marketing and moved on.
- With no marketing, it still got around 30 downloads a day. He took that as clear proof people liked the app, partly from lingering TikTok views and partly from people sharing it with friends.
- So they went back to it. The app "looked horrible," and applying what they learned building Flex, they revamped the whole app in two days of late nights, staying up until 4 AM.
- They changed the monetization from ad-based to subscription-based.
- He says the months of building apps taught him a lot about the best business strategies for apps, and about design and user experience.

## Flex, and why he avoids raising capital

Flex was a competitive gym app built with Henry. Friends join a group, take a picture at the gym and log a workout, which adds one to their streak, like a Snapchat streak. The peer pressure of seeing friends go holds you accountable.

- They got sidetracked by a new idea before seeing the app's potential. He still believes in it and wants to revisit it.
- It is a social-media-style app with no obvious way to use subscriptions, so if they revisited it he would probably go the venture capital route.
- On funding in general, he has never raised capital and has no plans to. He says "a lot of people think the only way to make money or start a business is to raise capital," but having none puts "a fire under you."
- Without funding, he says, the business must be profitable from the start, and you cannot be lazy or waste days.
- Most of his apps and app ideas he does not plan to fund externally. Flex is the exception because it likely would not be profitable except at massive scale.

## Cal AI: AI calorie tracking, traction and accuracy

Cal AI lets you take a picture of your food and estimates the calories, protein, fats and carbs, far faster than typing entries into an app like MyFitnessPal.

- Marketing is organic content on TikTok. At the time of the interview it had just passed 100,000 downloads.
- The team is small, but they had just hired two developers to move faster.
- Accuracy: they keep a test bench of many different foods and test the AI against all of them to improve it. It sat at about 90% accuracy.
- He cites a paper comparing GPT Vision with a nutritionist, which found GPT Vision on average 40% more accurate at estimating calories.
- His pitch: weighing food on a scale takes too long, and at restaurants you do not know the portions. Many people would rather have 90% accurate results and save the time.
- Free trial: they were A/B testing it and did not have one at the time.

## Cal AI roadmap and competing with an entrenched app

His approach to competing with MyFitnessPal, which he says has led for years with many features, some of them outdated and hard to use:

- First they took all the necessary features and made them easier with AI. Next they take all the useful features and make those easier with AI too.
- Next feature: logging workouts, which adds the calories burned to that day's calorie balance. MyFitnessPal has an exercise feature, but its flow for adding an exercise is much more complicated. Theirs would have the AI do the calculations and make assumptions so the user gives no input.
- The team also wants to build in-house tools for extensive testing of different flows in the app. These give faster, data-driven decisions and can be reused across future apps, making it easy to get a foot in the door of a new industry.
- He points to a competing app backed by an app studio with apps valued in the millions, which clearly has such in-house tools and moves fast, and says they want to keep up with studios like that.
- Cal AI is a wrapper app built on OpenAI and other AI models. Building their own food-recognition models is far off and not in the plan.
- He was heading to San Francisco for all of July to work full-time on Cal AI. He had been to a program called On Deck Founders there.

## Views on AI agents and what comes next

- At On Deck Founders many people were building AI agents for monotonous tasks: messaging lots of people, or sorting unstructured data. He expects office work like going through spreadsheets and checking off boxes to be replaced soon by agents using large language models.
- He expects people to rarely search the internet themselves. You would ask your personal AI agent and it retrieves the answer. Messaging could work the same way: your agent talks to your friend's agent, which relays the information.
- He sees one AI you interact with that delegates tasks to AI models specific to each job.
- He is unsure whether that interface would be an app, hardware or a website. He notes a counterpoint he heard from someone smart: information understood through conversation is linear, while apps and websites let us organize and go through information exponentially.
- His stated stage: he is still learning, and what he does now is meant to lead to a bigger thing. He had been quiet on X on purpose, to come out of nowhere with the numbers.
