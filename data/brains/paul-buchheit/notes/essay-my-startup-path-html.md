## Learning by moving: "You don't need to have it all figured out right now"

Paul has always wanted to start an important company or build something everyone will use. In college (Ohio) he got interested in founding or joining a software startup but had no idea how to do either. His only plan was to move to Silicon Valley, since that's where the cool startups seemed to be.

- He joined Intel in Santa Clara in 1998, after a friend (Chad) took a job there.
- His core advice: "You don't need to have it all figured out right now. The important thing is to keep moving forward."
- He urges seeking "opportunities to learn or try something new, something with uncertain outcomes."

## Finding a startup with almost no research

After almost a year in Intel's cubicles ("two shades of gray"), Paul wanted something new. He didn't know much about startups, and the ones in "Red Herring" magazine all sounded boring.

- He skipped intelligent research. He read Slashdot at Intel and sent his resume to the handful of local startups that sounded technically interesting.
- Most never replied. One offered him a job: Google.
- At the time Google was a small team in a little office on University Ave in Palo Alto.
- What drew him: he liked the product, the people seemed smart, the work sounded interesting, and the office felt bright and high energy.

## Joining Google while doubting it would win

Paul says Google's "business model" seemed pretty unlikely to him, and he assumed it probably wouldn't make it.

- His reasoning: Altavista had more users, money, engineers, and "more everything." If Google took off, Altavista could simply match the product and kill it.
- He took the job anyway as "a fun job and a great opportunity to meet new people and learn more about startups," planning to find a better startup next time.
- He was "only half-right": the "Altavista crushes Google" outcome never happened, but Google was a great opportunity to meet people and learn about startups, technology, and products.
- Takeaway: his predictions about which startup would win were wrong, so the choice was driven by learning and people, not a confident forecast.

## Advice: go join a startup, treat it as education

If you're interested in startups, Paul says don't wait for the perfect opportunity. Find one that sounds interesting and get a job there.

- "The startup will probably fail, but you will succeed because you have learned a lot more than you otherwise would have."
- Big companies teach things too, but he jokes that you can learn "how to be a worker bee."
- Seek uncertain outcomes on purpose; the learning is the payoff, not the startup's survival.

## Defending "just find one that sounds interesting" against the "right thing" objection

A commenter (Nivi) argued people should focus on doing the "right things" rather than doing things right, and warned against joining companies casually. Paul's reply:

- The future is very difficult to predict. In 1999 many people thought the world didn't need another search engine and that Google was the "wrong thing."
- If there's a business you really believe in, join that.
- Otherwise, "look at it more as a learning experience, a part of your education, and find someplace where you can learn a lot."

## Leaving a comfortable job for uncertainty

Paul quit Google in 2006 because "life at Google had become too predictable, and too typical." He chose to move toward "something new, something uncertain."

- The same principle applies at every stage: when a role stops stretching you, leave and take on something with an uncertain outcome.
