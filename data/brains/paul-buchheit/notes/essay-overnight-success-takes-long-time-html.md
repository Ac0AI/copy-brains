## Gmail's long road: why "overnight success" is misleading

Paul uses Gmail's history to argue that big products take years, and that early disbelief is normal.

- Work on Gmail began in August or September 2001. It launched on April 1, 2004, about two and a half years later.
- For a long time almost everyone disliked it. Some people used it anyway because of the search, but "they had endless complaints."
- Quite a few people wanted to kill it or "reboot" it as an enterprise product with native client software, "not this crazy Javascript stuff."
- Even at launch, many people inside Google predicted doom: the product was too weird and nobody wants to change email services. He was told Gmail would never get a million users.
- After launch it was still called "niche" and "not used by real people outside of silicon valley."
- Almost 7.5 years after starting, a Financial Times piece (cited by Paul) put Gmail at close to 100m users worldwide, versus more than 250m each for Yahoo and Microsoft webmail (comScore). In the US, Gmail users grew more than 40% in the past year, versus 2% for Yahoo and a 7% fall for Microsoft.
- He notes that count likely excludes "Apps for your domain" users, and that he "still [has] a huge list of complaints about Gmail."

## Big success takes years, with few counter-examples

- Paul's expectation: "big success takes years, and there aren't many counter-examples."
- YouTube is the one fast exception he names, and it hadn't yet reached piles of money.
- Facebook grew very fast but was almost 5 years old at the time.
- Larry and Sergey started Google in 1996. When Paul joined in 1999, few people had heard of it.
- He calls the notion of overnight success "very misleading, and rather harmful."

## What to do if you're starting something new

- Expect a long journey.
- That is no excuse to move slow. "You must move very fast, otherwise you will never arrive, because it's a long journey!"
- Be frugal, so you don't "starve to death half the way up the mountain."

## Don't overgeneralize from your own success

- Paul warns against assuming that because Gmail took off, FriendFeed will too. The products are very different, and "maybe we just got lucky with Gmail."
- The Gmail story only gives perspective: creating an important new product generally takes time, and the product has to keep changing and improving. He points to old Gmail screenshots as proof of how rough it once was.
- FriendFeed (at the time) "shows a lot of promise, but it's still a 'work in progress.'"

## Complaints mean people care; launch early to get them

- Paul is always concerned by complaints about a service, but also encouraged: people write long blog posts about what to change because they care about the product.
- That attention suggests the product is "at least headed in roughly the right direction."
- Silence would worry him more: it would mean nobody cares and the team is "off in the weeds."
- He calls this feedback "one of the major benefits of launching early."
- He expects the product to need to evolve, since "our product isn't quite right."

## Asking users to describe their ideal product

- Paul invites users to write up their vision of "the perfect FriendFeed" for themselves, on their own blog (or via a Posterous email address if they have none).
- They are free to drop or change features.
- He admits they are "technically... doing my work for me," but frames it as mutually beneficial: the team will try to build something users like, and even if not, others can use the concepts since they are public.
