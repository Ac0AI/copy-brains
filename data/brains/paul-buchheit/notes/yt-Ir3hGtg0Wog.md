## Starting in the 90s: Linux, Google, and what drew Paul to startups

- Paul wanted to do a startup from childhood. He disliked the idea of working for someone else and liked the idea of inventing things and making money from them.
- He graduated college in 1998 with no startup connections and no resources. The web was tiny, and he assumed startups were in California because of "eBay, Netscape, Yahoo".
- He took a job at Intel hoping to find a startup from there. He found the retirement logic ("your age plus your number of years of service") a signal to leave: "why don't I just take the next 30 years of my life and stick it in a box and bury it".
- He picked startups using one criterion: technically interesting Linux work. He built a list of six from Slashdot and emailed his resume to all of them.
- Google's mail server was misconfigured, so the first resume bounced. He ended up interviewing at only Google and one other company. He joined Google in 1999 as employee #23, when it was above a shop on University Ave in Palo Alto.
- He judged startups by their interview vibe. Google asked "really smart questions" and the other company's office felt flat, with people not excited to be there.
- He asked for more equity a week after starting and was told to negotiate before starting. He says he wishes he had negotiated for more.
- Early Google felt like it had "a buzz of productivity". He was eager to go to work each morning because of the products being built.

## Why he believed in Google, and the role of vision

- Paul says there is "kind of like an irrational belief" in a great early company. Google was objectively tiny, but Larry Page was always thinking years ahead. Example: Larry said not to bother with disk, since everything would be stored in memory, when there wasn't enough RAM for the data.
- He argues you need a big-picture vision but not a detailed step-by-step plan. Some startups present five-step plans where only the fifth step is a product anyone would want.
- You need a good product today, a good one tomorrow, and so on, every day.
- He wants "a certain amount of vagueness" in the vision, since too much detail is wasted effort that will turn out wrong.
- He compares it to the Lewis and Clark expedition: know the direction (west), but you can't know every river and mountain, so stay focused in the moment "so that you don't get eaten by a bear".
- Two things must coexist: intense attention to current customers, and a take-over-the-world vision.
- Google felt inevitable once it landed the Yahoo deal and started crushing other search engines, because competitors didn't know what they were doing.

## How Gmail was built: ship in a day, iterate with users

- Paul had been interested in email since college. People then downloaded mail via POP3 to one computer, and "information shouldn't be stuck in a box". He wanted everything online and accessible anywhere.
- In summer 1996 he tried to build web-based email himself. He designed a detailed, intricate system and implemented it piece by piece, then got bored and gave up. He concluded that big detailed design doesn't work for him: "figure out what you can launch today".
- In 2001 Google reorganized, removing managers and giving engineers free rein. Paul was told to do something in email.
- He wrote the very first version of Gmail in a day. He reused code from Google Groups (a Usenet search) and loaded his own email into the indexing engine, making email search.
- He emailed it to the engineering team. Feedback: it would be better with their email than his. Version two indexed other people's email. Next request: reply to messages. The whole process was iterating step by step to make people happy.
- Search was a big deal then because email clients mostly let you only find and click a message.

## Gmail's launch gate: 100 happy users

- The internal launch gate was 100 happy users inside Google, not yet the world.
- He embedded a pop-up in the interface asking one question: are you happy with Gmail (then called Caribou), yes or no.
- He read the list of who said no and went to each person asking "what's it gonna take to make you a happy user", then fixed things one user at a time.
- Some wanted a clone of Outlook and he told them they would never be happy. Some Outlook users became Gmail users anyway: Outlook corrupted its database at 2 GB, he held the only copy of their email, so they moved to Gmail, "not always happy" but users.
- Other users needed just one feature or one bug fixed.

## Narrow and deep beats broad and mediocre

- Paul says email was about 30 years old when Gmail started, with strong opinions on how it should work. People angrily told him he was doing it wrong, for example for putting replies on top instead of the bottom.
- It is impossible to appeal to everyone in an established category. Trying yields "a mediocre product that nobody really loves".
- His philosophy: build something with deep appeal, even to a tiny fraction of people. If a small group obsessively loves it, you can grow it, since there are always people at the margin who join if you improve it slightly.
- It's easier to start with deep but narrow appeal and broaden than to start broad and convert people to love it.
- He applies this to retention: a hundred people who never leave is a great start.

## Focus, frugality, and the danger of too much money

- Paul endorses "focus, frugality, obsession and love" as what a company needs.
- Focus is a startup's most powerful weapon. Big companies do a thousand things and a startup is underpowered in every way except it can be 100% focused on one thing. Google won at search by focusing on only that, while others wanted to be portals.
- Frugality: the best indicator is the input to output ratio. Most startups "do less with more", while you want more output with minimum input. His example is turning a dollar into ten dollars of value.
- Minimal launching ("if you're not embarrassed of your product you've waited too long to launch") saves time and money and avoids running too long on the wrong thing. Building too much email upfront would have meant not learning what to build.
- Too much money is a frequent cause of death. He has seen startups die because a huge round arrived before product-market fit.
- Example: Juicero raised $120 million before talking to customers, for a $700 juice bag squeezer. Under YC they would have gotten $120,000 and had to talk to customers. He says the problem was it already strained juice that had been macerated, so it was no better than buying juice, and it was built on flawed premises.
- He explains the mechanism. Facing a lack of product-market fit is painful, so founders instead tell themselves stories while hiring a team, getting an office, speaking at conferences and being in the press. They become detached from reality because they aren't selling something people want.
- The lesson: whatever you raise, you must create proportionally more value, and you must stay close to customers by selling.

## Facebook and what network businesses require

- After leaving Google in about 2004-2005, Paul started FriendFeed. Its goal was to build a place awesome to work at, recreating the early Google experience without the frustrating parts, with mostly ex-Google people.
- FriendFeed created the "like" button, which he says appeared on Facebook months later.
- He says social is one of the hardest product categories to engineer success in. Google, Twitter and many YC companies have struggled in it.
- He kept running into Facebook, which "actually knew what they were doing". He concluded they were unstoppable, so he sold FriendFeed to Facebook, hoping to learn how another Google-sized company works.
- A key insight from inside: Facebook understood the most important feature of a social network is that your friends are there. When individual and network interests conflicted, they chose the network. In his words, it's "not the end user who you have to please, it's the network".
- Generalizing: identify whose happiness makes you successful. For Google it's search users, for enterprise it's the buyer (which is why enterprise software is often unpleasant for the actual user). He rejects the "if you're not paying you're the product" meme as too simplistic.
- On Zuckerberg's conviction: Paul says he'd bet the whole world could tell him he's wrong and he would say no. Examples he cites: turning down Yahoo's roughly $1 billion offer when everyone around him urged selling (after which he replaced management who wanted to sell), and launching the News Feed when something like 8 of 10 million users joined a protest group, believing people would come around.
- Paul adds that if you are offered a billion dollars, you should take the deal in general. The Zuckerberg story is about extreme conviction, not advice for most founders.
- Why Google never won social: it wasn't in the DNA. Orkut was growing fast, then became unresponsive, and inside Google it was seen as a distraction from interesting computer-science problems. Twitter's founders were Google employees, and a Twitter-like product would have been dismissed as technically uninteresting. Google only tried later to compete with Facebook, when Facebook was far ahead. "If you're behind and not as good you're guaranteed to lose."
- Company energy as an indicator: walking into an office, you can feel whether people think the company is going somewhere, since employees know what's really going on.

## The path to $100B: ride an exponential change

- Paul's view: the biggest companies (Google, Netflix, Amazon, Microsoft, Intel, Apple) weren't necessarily smarter or harder working. They sat on top of a large exponential change in the world.
- Examples: Microsoft rode the rise of microcomputers and owned the operating system. Google rode the exponential growth of online information, hence its mission "to organize and make useful all the world's information".
- His $100B office-hour question has two parts:
  1. Imagine a time machine ten years ahead (2028 from 2018). What is fundamentally different about the world, as it pertains to your startup but factoring out your startup?
  2. How will you be the one who captures it?
- The point is to identify the inevitable change. Even if Facebook hadn't existed, someone would have built a giant social network, so the position is inevitable and the question is who takes it.
- Founders of the biggest companies show obsessive focus and some irrationality. His example is Elon Musk, who in a Google tech talk around 2003 said he had enough money for three SpaceX launches and hoped one would succeed. The first three failed, and he scraped together money for a fourth. Paul calls that level of irrationality "wonderful".

## Finding the tipping point and the right customer

- On identifying a market tipping point: don't overthink it. If people pay $40 for a cup of coffee, start selling $40 coffee. The tipping point is "when you can't keep up with demand". You find out by talking to the market and having them give you dollars. At Google the sign was not being able to build data centers fast enough.
- On enterprise: the danger is solving a problem that isn't urgent. Customers say a solution would be great, you build for years, then they say it isn't in this year's budget and to talk again in nine months. That is deadly for a startup.
- Find a customer with a really urgent problem. His analogy: your arm is pinned under a boulder and your choices are to gnaw it off or sign an LOI with a promising young startup. You want the desperate customer.
- Desperate customers will tolerate a poor product and spend time working with you.
- Founders working inside the customer's organization works well. Early Stripe, Patrick Collison would show up and integrate Stripe into your code.
- If a prospect isn't eager for you to integrate into their systems or talk to their people, they aren't in enough pain. Find a different customer in more pain.
- Paul says he doesn't believe in using personas to represent customers.
