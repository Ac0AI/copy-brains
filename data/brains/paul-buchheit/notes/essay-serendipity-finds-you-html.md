## Allowing serendipity instead of seeking or planning it

Paul's theory: good things in his life (Google, his wife) came from low-key, unplanned actions that he let play out. He thinks in terms of "allowing" serendipity, not "seeking" or "creating" it. "Opportunity is all around us, but we have beliefs and habits that block it."

- His 11th-grade English teacher stopped for two soaked cross-country cyclists, took them home for dinner and heard their stories. Paul loved it because "none of it was planned -- they simply allowed it to happen." It felt like "the **fun** way to live."
- Google (1999): he sent his resume to a few companies he'd seen mentioned on Slashdot (he calls it a lazy job search, interested in startups and Linux). Most never responded; only Google offered a job. He was skeptical of its business and didn't expect it to last, but it "seemed like it could be fun and educational," so he accepted.
- His wife: soon after moving to California he signed up for match.com, read profiles, emailed three people, and one replied. He was not looking to marry.
- He notes most good things in his life "follow that general pattern, and aren't part of any 'plan'", and his plans rarely work unless they are "boringly simple."

## The two blocks to serendipity: ego-fear and "other plans"

Paul names two main blockers of serendipity:

- **Ego-fear:** fear that goes beyond rational concern, driven by embarrassment, humiliation, rejection or criticism. It keeps people from public speaking, talking to strangers, interviewing for jobs.
- **"The plan":** attachment to one fixed plan.
- Healthy fear (not running onto a freeway) is different from ego-fear, but ego-fear "will rationalize itself as healthy fear," e.g. "I don't want to talk to that stranger because they could attack me, or waste my time."

## Ego-fear: how it costs opportunities and how to remove it

- Example he cites: a Hacker News commenter who liked Google in 1999, applied for a sys admin/data center job, was called twice, and never called back because "they would not have hired me anyways." Paul calls that fear of rejection and humiliation, and says it probably blocks many great opportunities.
- Thinking your way out of it "only makes the problem worse by generating a more complex tangle of rationalizations." "Fear is defeated by confrontation -- avoidance only makes it stronger."
- He endorses Eleanor Roosevelt: "You gain strength, courage, and confidence by every experience in which you really stop to look fear in the face. You must do the thing which you think you cannot do."
- His program: "seek ego-fear. Hunt it down and soak in it. Steal its energy." It is scary by definition, "That's good."

## Treat unexpected events with gratitude, not as obstacles

- Serendipity and luck are unpredictable, so they don't fit any good plan. When something unexpected happens, people see a distraction or frustrating obstacle.
- "The difference between a life full of frustrating obstacles, and a life full of serendipity, is largely a matter of interpretation."
- His recommended response is gratitude: "Accept what is given." Even seemingly adverse events can lead to something great. He points to the film *Yes Man* as a cute caricature of this mindset.

## Have a thousand plans, not one

- He quotes Eisenhower: "Plans are worthless. Planning is essential." Planning isn't bad; obsessively sticking to a single plan leaves little room for serendipity.
- "The world is very complicated, and we humans are very stupid, so it's good to be flexible and open minded." His advice: "Instead of having one plan, have one thousand plans, and revise them as necessary."
- To avoid analysis paralysis (pouring energy into the perfect plan and never acting): pick a plan knowing it's flawed, set it in action, then adapt, revise or switch as the world unfolds.
- He calls plans "ideas" or "rough sketches" instead. He tries to avoid having plans for his life but has many ideas, and which happen "will be a surprise to me. It's more fun that way."

## Why predictability kills innovation

- Paul suspects the desire for a definite plan is partly rooted in fear: uncertainty is scary, and a plan creates "the illusion of predictability in a very unpredictable world."
- Reducing risk and unpredictability also reduces serendipity.
- He sees this as a reason large organizations struggle to produce innovation: "they want it to be planned and scheduled, but that just kills it."
