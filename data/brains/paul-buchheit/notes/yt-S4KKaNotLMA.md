## YC's early hard tech bets worked, against wide ridicule

- Paul remembers it as "a little bit crazy" at the time, because YC was long known only for things you could build with a website.
- Sam recruited hard tech companies including Ginkgo Bioworks, Oklo, Rigetti and Helion. Paul was a YC partner to Oklo, Rigetti and Helion, which were all in one batch.
- His main lesson in hindsight: "it worked". Several of those companies are now public.
- It showed YC could fund more than websites. It also showed the startup world that you can build just about anything, and that new technology can come to market.
- Example: Helion is building nuclear fusion, long science fiction. Paul says it is a couple of years from delivering power to customers.

## Why hard tech worked: great founders, YC supplies company-building

- Paul's belief is that a lot comes down to the right people, with real ambition, who are real builders.
- YC never brings the technical expertise. "If we have to tell you how to build the fusion reactor, it's not going to happen."
- What YC does help with is everything else: building a company and raising capital. Paul says that goes well beyond the details of the technology.
- Billion to One is the example. It is his pick for YC's most successful bio company, and it IPO'd at the end of last year. Its investor updates laid out the plan years ahead. The one from around 2020 said it would IPO in 2025, and it hit that.

## Harder startups can improve your odds

- Paul says "counterintuitively", harder startups can give you a better chance. They are more inspiring and exciting, and they face less competition.
- A low-ambition idea attracts many other low-ambition people competing on the same thing. Outrageous ideas are avoided by most people.
- SpaceX is the extreme example. 20 years ago, trying it was "pure insanity".

## OpenAI as a hard tech bet

- Paul points to OpenAI, started at YC, as another example of hard tech ambition that looked crazy at the time.
- The idea that a startup would develop AGI was "pretty laughable".
- It was up against Google, which had all the data, money, researchers and computers. OpenAI was a small nonprofit research lab without a product.

## How this shapes what Standard Capital funds

- Paul applies the same rules as at YC: great founders with a lot of ambition and a path from here to there.
- He wants a clear route: deliver a product today, scale, raise much more money, and prove yourself as you go.
- He does not want "we're going to do this thing and we don't know how".
- A second benefit is community. Bringing credible hard tech founders together lets them compare notes. Few peers have built a late-stage hard tech company.
- His framing: you are the average of the five people around you, so Standard wants to surround founders with impressive peers who raise each other up.

## Hard tech is a chain of miracles, financed in stages

- Paul calls the "series of miracles" frame a great one. A hard tech company succeeds by proving it is a miracle worker.
- Tesla is his example: the Roadster, Model S, Model 3 and self-driving were each a miracle. The company chains impossible things on top of each other, and each one increases its ability to raise capital. SpaceX works the same way, with Starship as the new miracle.
- Investors are more excited to back a proven miracle worker than a fresh one.
- The practical lesson for founders: don't expect investors to see the brilliance of your idea and fund the whole end state at once. Keep succeeding, and at each stage raise enough money to reach the next level.
