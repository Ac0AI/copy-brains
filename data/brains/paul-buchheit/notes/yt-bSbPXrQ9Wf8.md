## Obvious ideas don't exist, so good startups look bad

- Paul's view: if an idea were obviously good, big companies would already be doing it. "Big companies are not that dumb," so a real opportunity has to look stupid or unimportant from inside one.
- Google example: Larry Page and Sergey Brin tried to sell Google (then "backrub") to Yahoo, Excite and Infoseek for a million dollars. Everyone said no because they thought search was an unimportant commodity.
- He says he doesn't limit himself to good ideas. A lot of his own ideas are not good, and many good startup ideas look bad at first.
- He likes ideas where the outcome is unpredictable. If he already knows the outcome, there's no point getting involved.
- Ideas that look like toys, or like impossible moonshots, are often the best. Mid-sized, sensible ideas tend to already have a big company or a hundred startups working on them. He wants to see something that sounds impossible (a supersonic jet) or plain silly (a camera on Justin's head).

## Luck, being a user, and how he ended up at Google and YC

- Paul stresses "being extremely lucky." He compares being a startup employee to being an investor, except you can only do one at a time.
- He joined Google because he used the product early (they made something he wanted) and because he loved Linux. His only criterion for a startup was cool Linux work. He liked that the interviewers were smart and asked good questions, unlike another startup where they asked dumb ones.
- He didn't expect Google to be a good business. He thought Alta Vista might squash it, and joined to learn about startups, not to make money.
- He got involved with Y Combinator in 2005 because he'd read Paul Graham's essays and thought PG was smart with good insights. He cold-emailed PG offering to help.
- YC's first batch was pitched as a replacement for a summer job. Original lawyers refused to do the work, and investors thought it was a joke. Paul found it intriguing and interesting rather than obviously good.
- He has invested in every YC batch since, about 12 years, and has invested in hundreds of companies and interviewed thousands of founders.
- He did this talk to stop and work out which patterns led to good and bad outcomes for him, after years of "somewhat haphazard decisions."

## Early bets that paid off: Wufoo and Justin.tv

- **Wufoo:** his first angel investment, in a YC company. The internet mocked it ("anyone keeping a list of all these new Y Combinator startups... entertaining a year from now"). It never IPO'd, but SurveyMonkey bought it and his investment returned about 44x. Founder Kevin Hale later became a YC partner, which gave a long-term return too.
- **Justin.tv:** invested the following year. The idea was Justin putting a camera on his head and live-streaming his life 24/7. Paul's reaction: "are you joking... awesome," because the outcome was unpredictable and it's "not the idea that would come out of Google."
- Why he backed them: they told a simple story and weren't going to plan for ages. They bought a camera, stuck it on Justin's head and hacked together streaming, so they could test and iterate fast.
- The company struggled for years. They even tried an acqui-hire but no one would take them, because acquirers thought there was no talent. Paul's answer to a question about companies going sideways: don't kick founders to get your money back.
- They eventually pivoted to Twitch (video game streaming, something he'd never have thought was a business). Amazon bought it for a billion dollars, and Paul says it's probably worth far more (he mentions an estimate of about twenty billion).
- The four founders went on to: Emmett Shear (CEO of Twitch), Justin Kan (Atrium, and a YC partner), Michael Seibel (Socialcam sold to Autodesk for 60 million, then YC CEO), and Kyle Vogt (Cruise, sold to GM for a billion).

## Find your betters: backing founders smarter than you

- FriendFeed: Paul's own company. He had many ideas, most not good, but was right that the most important thing was a great team. He convinced Bret Taylor to co-found it.
- Bret story: as a PM (maybe APM) on Google Maps, frustrated that the product was slow and clunky before launch, he rewrote the entire JavaScript front end over a weekend. It came out a third the size and ten times as fast as what a whole team had spent a long time on. "This is the kind of person you want to invest in."
- FriendFeed got buzz but not growth, partly because it competed with Facebook. Paul met Zuckerberg, found him impressive, and came to believe Facebook was unbeatable, so they sold to Facebook for about half a percent of Facebook's equity, "a pretty good deal."
- Core lesson: "find your betters." Look for people smarter and more insightful than you. If he thinks he could do the founder's job better, he shouldn't fund them.
- Idea and market still matter (you can't build a big company in a tiny market). But if the founders are better than him, they'll have better insights, so he can "outsource that part of the thinking to the founder" and just decide whether the founder believes it.
- Use your own knowledge to probe and ask intelligent questions, not to decide. Example of the wrong approach: "I'm an expert on air mattresses, I know there's no business here."
- He says he honestly doesn't know much about almost anything, which is why he invests in people better than him. This is also how he handles hard tech: he gets excited about a supersonic jet more than obscure enterprise software, and relies on the founder's expertise.
- Don't skip non-software. Paul says many YC bio companies will have giant exits in about 10 years, and at demo day he sees at least half the investors cross bio off on principle.

## Founder traits he looks for, with examples

- **Clear, concise communication:** not slick or salesy, but real depth and clarity. Paul Graham's essays are the model (still a top source of YC applicants). If Paul is doing the work to understand, or talks until he sells himself on his own story, that's a bad sign. A third of YC interviewees can't explain what they do after 10 minutes, and that's an easy no. Blake Scholl (Boom) explained clearly the three things wrong with Concorde he was fixing and the economics, and sends the best investor updates Paul has seen.
- **Moves fast:** Bret rewriting Maps in a weekend; Kyle building a media server. "Founders will come up with infinite reasons to move slow and that's fine, I'm just not going to invest." You can almost predict success from how quickly a company iterates; great ones iterate quickly, "the ones who do a release every two years fail."
- **Accomplishes a lot with a little:** Meraki (2006). Paul visited their small Mountain View office, saw mesh Wi-Fi hardware they were already selling, and asked how much they'd raised. "We don't raise any." They'd strung it together for $0, "exactly what I like to see." Cisco bought it for 1.2 billion (a 73x return for him), and he thinks they sold too early.
- **Implausibly ambitious or frivolous ideas:** the opposite extreme from the sensible middle. Examples: Boom (supersonic jets, founder a software guy who taught himself aeronautics) and Cruise (self-driving cars four years earlier looked absurd for a startup against Google's huge team).
- **Talent magnet:** great founders attract other great people. Paul says that's the secret of YC too: it's a top-talent magnet because the best people want to be where the best people are. Stripe is an example.
- **Make something people want:** the YC slogan. Google worked for Paul because he was a user. DoorDash example: when they interviewed at YC he asked if they'd deliver to his house, since there was no food delivery where he lived. They said yes, and he mainly funded them because he wanted the food himself.
- **Determination:** Paul says PG calls it the most important founder trait, because startups are hard and unpleasant and people who aren't determined quit.

## How he probes for speed and determination

- **Speed:** ask what they've accomplished in the past month, and what their schedule looks like. Justin.tv's story was "we're launching this next week," not next year.
- He recommends a YC blog post titled something like "a little bit of slope is worth a lot of y-intercept": favor a tiny thing with an upward slope over something big that has flatlined.
- Ask founders why they aren't doing something faster, and see whether they give excuses or a legitimate reason. After funding, push them to move even faster and watch how they respond.
- Kyle story: Justin.tv was burning money on expensive streaming CDNs and licensed software. Paul told Kyle to write their own streaming media server, saying he should do it "in an evening... build this tonight, launch it tomorrow." It took maybe a weekend, and it's why Justin.tv survived at all. "99% of engineers" would have told him he was smoking crack.
- **Determination:** look for hedging and an easy Plan B. He sees this most in founders on leave from med school, who think "this is hard, I'm scared, I'm going back." Also watch for conditions like "if I raise enough to pay myself XYZ, I'll quit my job." Ideally you want people willing to "charge straight into a brick wall."
- Look at the past: has the person made mistakes and failed? He's nervous about people with a perfect resume, always perfect grades and the right clubs. Founders fail day after day, and people scared of failure take an early exit, then put "started a YC startup" on their resume and go to a safe job like McKinsey.
- The YC application asks about the founder's past partly to find people who got punched in the face and got back up.
- Airbnb example: they worked on it for a long time, pitched many investors who said no, and PG tried to talk them out of the idea in the YC interview. They said no, and he points out that if PG had talked them out of it, it probably would have meant it was a bad idea.
- YC funds from a 10-minute interview. Paul says you can get a lot out of people in 10 minutes. As an investor you can't do months of private-equity-style diligence, because slow investors face adverse selection.

## Mistakes: missing Dropbox and Airbnb, and moving slowly

- **Dropbox:** he went to Boston to meet the summer 2007 batch (only 19 companies). Drew Houston seemed one of the strongest founders. Paul had questions about competition from Google Drive, scheduled a meeting back in San Francisco, then cancelled when a previous meeting ran long. He says that was "really shitty investor behavior" because it wastes the founder's time, and he missed what would have been a thousand-fold return.
- **Airbnb:** Michael Seibel introduced him to "air bed and breakfast" on June 26, 2008. He replied years later (he says three or four years). He had his own startup at the time and says founders should give their own company absolute priority over angel investing.
- Later, in YC, PG constantly hyped the Airbnb founders. Paul decided to invest and planned to find them at angel day (what demo day used to be called), but they weren't there. He emailed Brian, who said they'd just signed a term sheet with Sequoia. He later got into a round at a much higher price.
- Lesson: if you move slow, you get adverse selection. Companies you can string along for months aren't the best ones. "Do or do not." When you've identified a company as promising, you can spend another week thinking or pull the trigger and get Airbnb.

## Mistakes: price, pity, cynicism, numbers

- **A "really good price":** he's been duped by bargains many times, saying "I've never gotten any return" from something he invested in because it was a good deal. There's "no such thing as value investing in startups."
- **Not investing because of price:** he's never regretted paying, but has always regretted passing because the price was high. His rule now is the reverse: if he's disappointed by the price, he should probably invest more and write a larger check.
- **Investing out of pity:** he gets emotionally attached to founders he works with and wants to help. He's never made money on pity investments. The truly great founders are "a force of nature." The ones with a sob story ("no one wants to invest, maybe if you write a check they will") don't work out.
- **Cynical ideas:** ideas he wasn't excited about for the future but thought could make money. He's never made money on them. His principle: "invest in optimism, invest in the future you want to see, not the future you fear." He once considered investing in anything that wouldn't be out of place in the movie Idiocracy, and those investments didn't work.
- **Impressive numbers alone:** good numbers can blind you to a weak founder or product. He's never made money when the numbers were the main reason. Ask whether the company is actually creating value or just exploiting a temporary inefficiency (e.g. a Facebook algorithm that could change and kill it).

## Investing rules and answers to questions

- Don't over-learn from past mistakes. It hurts to lose money, but seed investing involves "a great deal of luck." If you over-analyze failures, you talk yourself out of good deals. He notes you could find a hundred reasons Justin.tv wouldn't work after five minutes' thought. Even failures can be random, like whether a Google person flips a bit on an acqui-hire.
- Yet he says it's a "slightly contradictory" game: learn from the past and ignore it at the same time.
- Experts can over-trust their own knowledge. On email startups, he's a bit burnt out so the bar is higher, but he uses his knowledge to probe. If a founder can't explain why their product isn't just a Gmail feature, that's not a good answer. He did back Front (a recent big Sequoia round) because founder Mathilde told a clear story of why it wasn't a feature of Gmail.
- He ran one of the first-ever personal services and says refusing every one afterwards would have been a bad mistake.
- **Bio and making something people want:** for cure-type companies demand is obvious ("I want a cure for cancer"). The risk is whether they can actually make the product.
- **ICOs:** he sees something interesting in crypto. If you were watching what nerdy 21-year-olds were excited about five or six years ago you'd have invested in Coinbase and Bitcoin. But he thinks most ICOs are scams or companies that could raise maybe half a million from this room and instead raise a hundred million, and says they're going to zero.
- **Too much money is a liability:** a mountain of money lets founders insulate themselves from reality. Juicero spent about a hundred million on a juice-bag squeezer without ever talking to customers or trying to sell a bag of juice. With only $100K they'd have had to sell juice bags. The opposite is Meraki.
- His quote on morality for products: "the best kind of morality is a product that people like so much that they just want to tell people about it."
