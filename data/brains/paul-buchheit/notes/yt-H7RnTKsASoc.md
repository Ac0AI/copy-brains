## Hire only after product-market fit, when work exceeds capacity

- Paul says to start hiring once you have product-market fit and are ready to grow the company, and not before.
- Hiring before product-market fit makes it harder to find product-market fit: "the larger the team is, the slower you can change."
- Employees get nervous when the founders change direction. They have to be managed and have their feelings handled, and they have to be told why the company pivoted from thing A to thing B.
- Once there is product-market fit, he wants hiring to accelerate growth.
- His trigger for flipping to hiring is having "more work than you can possibly handle" because the company is growing so fast.
- Paul and the other speaker describe YC's internal startup guide, which listed "don't hire" as one of its first rules. Founders who had just raised money often said "Great, we raised money. It's time to start hiring."
- Paul says there was "true wisdom" in the rule. Hiring someone good slows you down immensely, and a hire onto a startup that hasn't figured it out leaves you with a person to manage and reassure.

## Why hiring too early creates morale problems

- A new employee joining a pre-product-market-fit startup has made a big bet on it. Good employees have many other options.
- If you recruit them, you have to sell them on the vision and on the reality of the company growing fast and becoming big.
- Paul recalls many office hours where founders said their employees wanted to quit or thought the founders were doing a bad job, and asked what to tell them.
- His answer was to ask whether the founders were doing a good job: "the best story is the true story."
- When the company wasn't taking off, those founders had constant employee morale issues.

## Do the job yourself before hiring for it

- Paul says founders often hire for something they cannot do themselves.
- Founders in that position don't know how to interview for the role or how to manage the person.
- A common result is that they hire someone from a big company with an impressive resume, who then spends 6 to 12 months failing. The founder is frustrated and can't tell whether the person is wrong or the job is simply hard.
- His advice: the founder should first do the job themselves, then find someone who can do it better.

## Hiring at early Google

- Paul was employee 23 at Google and found the job on Slashdot, a site he describes as "news for nerds, stuff that matters" for Linux enthusiasts.
- He was a Linux enthusiast looking for a company building on Linux, which was rare in the 90s. He found about six such companies, applied to all of them, and got one job offer.
- He says he didn't appreciate at the time how special Google was. He credits the company's success partly to hiring super talented people.
- Google's interviews were technical: coding on a whiteboard, algorithm questions and similar.
- Paul's own habit was to ask candidates about one of the hardest problems he was working on, one he didn't know the answer to.

## Interviewing with a problem you haven't solved: the "did you mean" story

- In 2000 Paul built Google's "did you mean" spell corrector. He is not good at spelling, and the logs showed he wasn't alone: he estimates probably half of queries were misspelled. Fixing spelling was one of the easiest ways to improve quality.
- His first version used a spell correction library and "was not brilliant." He believed a statistical model could work out what the user meant.
- After several weeks his own version was "okay but not brilliant." He started using the problem as an interview question, hoping to find someone better.
- Most answers from the many engineers he asked ranged from mediocre to terrible.
- One candidate, Noam Shazeer, was already ahead of Paul's thinking during the interview. Paul told the team they had to hire him.
- In his first two weeks Noam built the "did you mean" corrector that Google still uses, which Paul calls "basically the greatest spell corrector that has ever existed."
- About 17 years later Noam was a key author of "Attention Is All You Need" (the transformer paper). Paul notes his interview question was, in hindsight, to build a small language model.
- Paul says to pose the hardest questions you don't know the answer to, and to look for people who are smarter than you.

## Hire people better than you

- Paul cites the saying: "A's hire A's and B's hire C's."
- His reasoning: people who aren't that good often know it, and find smarter people threatening, so they hire people who are less good.
- Someone who is smart and ambitious wants to be surrounded by people who inspire them and from whom they can learn.
- In any interview he asks himself whether it is a person he can learn a lot from.
- His basic end-of-interview test: ask "what did I learn?" If the answer is nothing, it was a bad interview.

## Interviewing Ilya Sutskever for OpenAI

- Paul says OpenAI was created at Y Combinator in 2015 and was originally called YC Research. The reasoning was that AI was happening but was locked up inside Google, and YC wanted it open to the world and to YC startups, who would otherwise be in trouble.
- Sam emailed Paul and the other speaker to say Ilya was a great candidate for OpenAI co-founder. He asked them to interview Ilya and give a thumbs up before moving forward.
- Paul had been into neural nets in the 90s and says they always seemed like obviously the right way to do AI, though he wasn't smart enough to make them work.
- He asked Ilya why neural nets started working, given that in the 90s they were a dead end. Things changed in 2012 with AlexNet.
- Ilya's explanation included something basic that Paul found surprising. As Paul recalls it (and says he doesn't know if it's true), the field had been initializing weights with random numbers between roughly 0 and 0.2, and the right choice was a random number between -1 and 1.
- Paul's takeaway about research: often someone does something arbitrary and everyone else copies it without questioning it.
- In 2015 Ilya said that AI was the most important problem in the world, that we would have superhuman-level intelligence to solve all sorts of problems, and that it would change how we live. This was before AI belief was common.
- Paul says most founders say bold things in YC interviews and none of it happens (he mentions Web3), but every so often a founder describes the reality we now live in. Ilya's claims were "dead on" with hindsight.
