## Angel investing results: 32 companies, ~10% gain in 3 years

Paul started angel investing in 2006 assuming he didn't know what he was doing. His stated goals were to learn through experience, help startups, and not lose too much money. He has no single investing criterion: sometimes the idea looks good, sometimes the people, sometimes he's just curious what will happen. In every case he wants to be helpful and learn something.

## The numbers from 2006-2008 investments

- Invested about $1.21 million across 32 companies (2006 to 2008).
- About half were acquired or are dead/mostly dead. The other half are alive and seem to be doing well.
- The acquisitions to date total about $1.34 million, roughly a 10% gain. He calls it "not great, but at least I'm not losing too much."
- Only two companies are officially dead, with at least four "zombies" that exist in minimal form and are almost certainly worthless.
- Acquisitions by other private companies (for example ScanScout, bought by Tremor Media) count as "still alive and doing well", since they gave investors no liquidity.
- He expects the final return to be much better than 10% because the surviving group includes promising companies such as Meraki, Weebly and Wufoo. He treats any return as "gravy" since his primary goal was learning and being helpful.

## Exits: where the returns came from

- Only two exits returned more than 10x: Heroku (reported $212M sale to Salesforce) and Mint (reported $170M sale to Intuit).
- Both were among his smaller checks. He says this shows he doesn't know what he's doing, or at least that he needs to put more money into the best companies.
- Mint was oversubscribed. He didn't remember why he put less into Heroku, then edited the post to note it was also oversubscribed.
- Medium exits: AppJet/Etherpad (Google) and 280North (Motorola, reported $20M).
- Smaller but still good 2x-3x exits: Auctomatic, Parakey (Facebook) and Zenter (Google).
- On criticism of these small deals: "as much as I'd like to get a nice 10,000x exit, I'm certainly not going to complain when someone doubles my money!"

## Why he keeps investing in Y Combinator companies

- His highest return came from a YC company (Heroku, winter 2008). He had invested in YC companies almost from the start (Wufoo, winter 2006), and says they keep getting better.
- He argues YC attracts the best founders, and Heroku is "just the tip of the iceberg."
- It compounds: the more great YC companies there are, the more reasons other smart founders have to join. He finds himself asking non-YC companies why they aren't in YC yet.
- This trend contributed to his decision to join YC as a partner (announced November 2010, alongside Harj Taggar).

## His advice for new angel investors

- **Assume you'll lose your money.**
- **Plan on investing in a large number of companies.**
- Attend YC demo day (his third piece of advice).
- Have the right motivations (helping startups and learning to be a better investor) and the right expectations. Anyone planning to find the next Google, invest a bunch, and get super-rich "is going to be very disappointed."
- He says his own results validate this advice. He jokes that his current billion-dollar plan is finding the next Google and buying a 1% stake.

## Lessons he draws about his own investing

- Learning by doing is the only way he sees to get better, so he invested broadly across a variety of startups.
- Returns are concentrated in a very few winners, so the biggest lesson is to size up the best companies instead of spreading checks evenly.
- Oversubscribed rounds can limit how much a good investor gets to put in, which is part of why the biggest winners were small checks.
