## Abundance as a 10x or 100x jump in total intelligence

Paul frames the near future as an "age of abundance". Very soon, whether in 5 years or 50, the total intelligence on Earth (all people's intelligence added together, minus the waste from people fighting each other) gets 10x or 100x larger through AI. He prefers this framing to a flat "what will 2035 look like" question, and asks founders what they want to see in 10 years as a time-travel exercise ("I love the movie Back to the Future").

He argues you need a positive vision of where you want to go: "if you don't ever have a positive vision for the future you're never going to get there." The steps in between are messy and hard to figure out, but without a vision of the goal you make the wrong decisions. Negative scenarios are fine to think about, but they are already pushed on everyone constantly.

## How AI turned out safer than YC feared

Paul recalls that YC spun out OpenAI about 10 years ago (it was originally called YC Research) because the team believed AI was imminent, given the success of deep learning. At the time AI was best known for playing video games by maximizing a score, so the fear was an objective function like a paperclip maximizer, probably an evolutionary survival drive that could mean wiping out humans.

Things turned out better than imagined. The real breakthrough, in his view, is that the objective is just predicting the next token: "intelligence is just predicting what comes next." That produces intelligence without a drive to survive. He notes almost no science fiction anticipated this, and that Robin Hanson's "Age of Em" book (written about 15 years ago) assumed it would take 400 years to build new AI and that humans would be scanned instead. That prediction is already out of date.

## Cheap, clean energy as the first enabler

Paul says one of the first things that unlocks other good things is abundant cheap, clean energy, which he thinks will inevitably be some form of nuclear (fission, fusion, or both). Electricity at about one cent per kilowatt hour "would just be good for everything."

- He says there is no physical reason it can't happen; the blockers are politics and lack of intelligence.
- US energy usage has been almost flat for 10-20 years; a real AI race puts pressure on the US to streamline and push energy frontiers.
- He points to the recent renewed interest in nuclear, including Microsoft reopening Three Mile Island.
- His 10-30 year hope: fossil fuels are phased out not by suffocating the economy or banning air travel, but because the alternatives are better and cheaper.

## Cleaner environment, better materials, and AI coaching

Paul lists what he'd like to see in an age of abundance:

- The natural environment restored and cleaner within about 40 years.
- Much better materials, and getting rid of plastics, since "anything that accumulates in the natural environment also accumulates in our internal environment" (microplastics).
- Waste handled by incinerating and reusing atoms rather than pretending plastic is recyclable.
- Better transportation, including rockets to Australia in an hour and vacations on the moon ("once there's like Four Seasons on the moon").

He thinks a missing ingredient for many people is inspiration and coaching. The difference between good and terrible parents is a simple example. A really smart AI coach could help people with decisions, purpose and meaning, so everyone can be a better version of themselves, like "the little angel on your shoulder."

## Jobs, purpose and the risk of the transition

Paul is "pretty concerned about" the near term, because AI is good at eliminating jobs and he is not sure new ones will appear as quickly.

- He thinks self-driving in cities is close to solved within about 5 years, now mostly a matter of capital and implementation.
- He calls driving "universal basic labor", since almost anyone can do it and schedules are flexible. Trucking is among the most common jobs in many states.
- Customer service and outsourcing are also at risk.
- Technology concentrates wealth, and AI more so, toward whoever can pay for GPUs.
- Most people on UBI-like welfare don't enjoy their lives, and he cites native tribes with UBI-like systems as not going well. Work gives meaning that people struggle to find outside it.

His view: the biggest challenge of this age is meaning without work, not material need ("I'd rather struggle for meaning than food"). He expects a big market in helping people find purpose without work and in real human connection, and says education should be rethought around being happy and building real connection with other humans, which he guesses will be the main skill.

He is skeptical that UBI is the right answer, though he admits few alternatives have been articulated. His idea is "machine money" versus "human money": machine money is what technology produces, and you want massive deflation toward zero prices. Human money is earned by pro-social acts such as coaching Little League and spent on human things like art. How to fund machine money, he jokes, is something 10x more intelligence might answer.

## What he'd tell startups: sell the service, hire the agile

On advice to founders, Paul says the future has never been harder to predict, and things are moving faster than most people predicted 5 years ago. YC's fundamental rule still applies: back people who are really smart and agile, who keep re-evaluating and don't lock into a 5-year plan. That agility is an advantage over incumbents, who can't pivot as fast.

He says he doesn't get into sector-level detail (that is the group partners' job), but what he sees working best is startups that deliver a service rather than sell software, so customers get immediate ROI. His example: "we're going to take over your customer service." With AI tools, some YC companies reach about $10M in revenue within a year with roughly a dozen employees, which he says would not have been seen 10 years ago.

He also expects intelligence to be embedded everywhere, comparing it to microprocessors now sitting inside USB cables and power cords. He mentions talking toys, such as robotic cat toys and teddy bears that talk, as one example.

## Health: a CGM, ChatGPT as a second opinion, and AI plus doctors

Paul is enthusiastic about AI in healthcare and wellness. The medical system mostly treats vague symptoms, and he expects deeper, more mechanistic, individualized care. He sees AI as a way to deal with doctor shortages, including in places like villages in India with no other option.

- **Personal example:** he had trouble sleeping and worked it out in a ChatGPT conversation. Too much hot yoga was overactivating his sympathetic nervous system, acting "almost like caffeine" and raising his resting heart rate. He cut back and sleeps again.
- **Hospitals are dangerous:** his first child was born about 100 days early and spent months in the NICU, with five surgeries. He learned as much as he could so he could question doctors. A resident was about to start a third course of a medication that can destroy a child's kidneys; he says medical errors are one of the leading causes of death. His father was nearly harmed by an unprescribed insulin shot at admission.
- **Doctor plus AI model:** one YC company has a nurse and AI walk a patient through symptoms. The AI makes a recommendation and the doctor reviews and approves it. The doctor sees about 4x the patients, and the patient gets a longer, more detailed appointment.
- **Cheap testing:** he likes Sciox (photonics-based blood testing). It started with a mail-in business (a few drops of blood on paper) to prove the business while validating hardware, and the technology could scale down to something like a continuous glucose monitor.
- **Continuous glucose monitors (CGMs):** he recommends everyone try one for a couple of months (Dexcom, or Freestyle Libre). It gives readings roughly every five minutes and changes behavior because the feedback is immediate. Findings: a Fuji apple spikes his blood sugar a lot, and eating a dessert after a big meal affects him less than eating it on an empty stomach.
- **Lifestyle over pills:** much of healthcare is lifestyle change, but the system is incentivized around treating illness ("sick care"), with "no billing code" for lifestyle coaching. He thinks an always-present AI coach could align better with just helping you.

## Regulation, Uber, and routing around slow rules

On whether regulated industries (energy, healthcare) will improve, Paul says he hopes so, but often people just circumvent regulation. His example: in 2006 he lived in the city, saw how bad cab service was, and effectively had the idea for Uber ("my phone has a GPS, I should be able to press a button and a car shows up"), but dismissed it because of taxi medallions and legality. Uber "just went and did it," and it became legal afterward. He thinks many things will work this way, like people already using ChatGPT as a doctor.

He also sees AI transforming education through fully individualized instruction ("meet every child where they are"), while raising the question of what kids are being educated for.

## Other ideas: construction, robots and better persuasion

- **Construction as prompting:** housing is a big limiter because construction has become incredibly expensive and almost impossible. He imagines building a house as easy as using an AI art tool, refining the design then pressing Go. He also wants robotic honeybee drone swarms that build fantastical structures impossible with conventional techniques.
- **Humanoid robots:** they look on the way from lab demos, though others are skeptical about training data. He doesn't see data as a fundamental limit; current AI is just inefficient compared to humans, who learn from small amounts of data.
- **Politics and the blind men and the elephant:** he wants AI to help people understand multiple perspectives, since politics makes people think the other side is "stupid and evil." In the parable, each blind man holds a different part of the elephant and either fights or talks and builds a shared picture. He applies this to relationships and marriages too, where failure to communicate is common.
- **Persuasion:** AI is very good at convincing people and will give individualized messaging. He believes in positive-sum outcomes: if you can identify the positive-sum outcome and sell it individually to each person, that's a good use (his example: selling nuclear energy to everyone). He says nuclear fear is not unreasonable (he grew up near a nuclear plant with test sirens), but Chernobyl came from reckless testing and bad design, which greater intelligence can fix.
- **Pessimistic prediction:** he expects things to get a little worse before they get better in political strife and job losses, but thinks a positive vision gets people through.
