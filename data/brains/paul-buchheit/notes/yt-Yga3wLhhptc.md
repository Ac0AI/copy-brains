## Why LLM progress should keep going and where the next gains come from

- Paul would be surprised if models aren't "a lot better" a year from now. Big tech is pouring in resources and the models are compute-hungry, which he thinks explains why benchmarks look flat between releases.
- He expects gains from more than text. Video, other modalities and real-world physics give models far more to learn from.
- He expects models to generate their own training data by exploring many possible futures and "thinking about something for a long time", as a human does.
- He sees next-token prediction as "the Turing machine of intelligence". Predicting what comes next, such as what a human does in a video, forces a model to build a good model of the world and a theory of mind.
- Current models are "relatively shallow". Better algorithms and more compute should deepen them, and he expects the next step to be better reasoning.
- Companies will keep competing. Facebook and Microsoft have the cash and aren't "dumb", and Google has rediscovered that it is an AI company.

## Google's AI history and why the AI race stays open

- Google's "did you mean" spell corrector was an early form of AI. Noam Shazeer built it more than 20 years ago, and he later co-invented the Transformer.
- Google paid close to $3 billion to bring Noam back via the Character.ai deal. Paul says he left because OpenAI got to launch what Google had invented.
- Paul expects much more execution from Google now that Sergey, Noam and Jeff Dean are working on it.
- OpenAI began as a YC spin-out around 2015 to bring AI out of Google, where deep learning was mostly locked up.
- At the time success looked improbable. He cites an Elon email saying OpenAI had "a 0% chance". Paul counts that as normal for any startup taking on a giant.
- He hopes there will be many players, since each breakthrough gets matched by competitors.

## How AI search and defaults could disrupt Google

- Paul still habitually uses Google, but expects its business to change within about 10 years.
- AI answers beat ten blue links plus ads, though AI search still has gaps:
  - It often summarizes search results and doesn't do deeper research.
  - It launders sources. On Google you see the domain and judge reliability, while an AI answer strips that signal.
- He thinks integration is where it gets interesting, such as Apple Intelligence tied into the device so you don't think in terms of "search".
- Defaults are the real power. Chrome and Android exist mainly to defend search, which is why Google pays Apple and funds Mozilla. If paid defaults become illegal, Firefox's money is at risk.
- What flips people is a better result, not a feature list. He moved from AltaVista to Google after it failed him about three times, then never went back to AltaVista.
- A platform shift like AI makes things "kind of all up for grabs" and is the startup opportunity.

## The AI product Paul wants to see: universal access

- He works backwards from the question "what kind of world do we want to create?" The goal he describes is to help everyone live their best possible life.
- Doctors, lawyers, therapists and coaches are available mostly to the fortunate. Paul can text his doctor and get a fast answer, and most people sit on hold.
- Near term, he thinks AI can give everyone medical advice better than the median doctor, and legal help of high quality. He cites a Peter Thiel point that even single-digit millionaires can't really use the legal system.
- That raises privacy questions, such as whether your AI lawyer would report you to the police.
- His top political and technological worry is centralization of power. AI could make a perfect surveillance and censorship cage, especially as it gets better at "mind reading".
- He wants the opposite, a society that is as consensual as possible and expands freedoms.
- Open-source AI matters as a "canary in the coal mine". Regulation that outlaws it pushes models toward central control.
- He points to social media as a warning. After Elon bought Twitter, the Twitter Files showed how much was happening behind the scenes. He says advertisers acted as a cartel, and cable news gets a large share of its revenue from pharma.

## Social apps and AI

- Paul started FriendFeed in 2007 and sold it to Facebook in 2009, where the like button was created. Instagram started in 2010. Social felt locked up then, yet new winners kept appearing.
- He wonders whether AI could help people get along better online, with a social app that makes you feel good, "a happy place". He admits this may be an idealistic dream.
- He thinks approval from other humans matters at a deep evolutionary level. People want to believe a like comes from a human.
- AI friends such as Character.ai work partly because people can be honest without fear of judgment.
- He agrees that smaller, more private groups fit how people evolved better than broadcasting to the whole world.

## Narratives shape truth and how to pop them

- Paul argues that our whole perception of reality is filtered through narratives, including what counts as reliable information or science. People tend to find evidence for what they already believe, and "experts agree" is a filter too.
- A test he offers: if you can't tell a good story for why someone would take the other side, you don't understand the issue, and your own view lacks perspective.
- The same applies to alignment. "Whose human values?" has no single answer, and awareness of multiple narratives makes you less controlled by any one.
- His favorite author is Byron Katie, and he recommends the audio version of *Loving What Is*. Her method is to find the story you're stuck on and explore counter-narratives.
- Her line he uses most is "Who would you be without this story?" He found it useful on a forest trip when he was unhappy about something forgotten, because it let him notice the beauty around him.
- A relationship version he endorses is to preface a feeling with "the story I'm telling myself is...", for example "you don't love me because...", which is an honest statement of fact.

## Advice for founders: seek disconfirmation

- Paul says a big part of a YC partner's job is to pop the stories founders tell themselves. Founders usually arrive with a narrative about why people want their product, and often it is wrong.
- He used to get feedback like "I was angry at first, then realized you were right". He would tell founders he didn't believe anyone wanted what they were making and to go get LOIs.
- At the start of a batch he asks what the reason is this business will fail, and how to get the founder to failure as fast as possible so they can move past it.
- An LOI works as a truth test. People are encouraging in conversation, but signing a non-binding, free piece of paper often fails because the problem is "the 11th most important" one.
- Look for what makes the customer's life miserable and costs them sleep. Buying from a startup is risky, since the startup may fail and leave the customer stranded or looking foolish, so the customer needs a strong motivation.
- Schooling teaches people to get the A and avoid failure, which is the wrong lesson for startups.
- His reframe: "you can guarantee success by redefining success as learning a valuable lesson". A customer saying no then counts as success, because you learned what they don't want.
