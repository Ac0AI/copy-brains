## Technology creates new "info hazards," and the founder's defense is staying grounded

Paul describes info hazards as the way each new medium (television, cable, forums, feeds, LLMs) can warp what people believe or how they feel. His core claim: "everyone is going a little bit crazy, probably," and the more time spent staring at screens, especially feeds, the worse it gets.

- **TV:** two or three channels meant everyone got "brainwashed the same." Cable created filter bubbles, so people started sharing culture with strangers far away more than with neighbors.
- **Online communities:** you can find like-minded people, but you can also fool yourself into thinking fringe opinions are popular.
- **Self-reinforcing bubbles:** "everyone agrees with everyone else, and anyone who disagrees gets kicked out of the community."
- **New media, no immune system:** each new form of media spreads like a virus. Young people who grew up with it develop better natural immunity (for example, spotting AI slop that older users share). Society also develops norms and guardrails over time, as with the gin carts of early distilled alcohol.

## Why feeds are uniquely dangerous (Paul's FriendFeed insider view)

Paul helped build "the sausage factory." He lays out the mechanics:

- A feed is a non-stop drip of bite-sized, interesting content, so you are always incentivized to take "one more hit."
- There is no end. With a newspaper you finish and move on; with a feed "you yourself have to close the window."
- The machine reads what engages you (even just pausing on content) and feeds you more of it. This reinforces whatever you already feel.
- The most engaging content tends to be alarming, so doom scrolling is demoralizing and can leave you depressed.
- His jar analogy: rocks, then small rocks, then sand. Feeds are sand. If you fill your head with sand first, "there's no room for anything of substance."
- His remedy for a bad feed state: close the window, exercise, go for a run and disconnect.

## Origin of the like button at FriendFeed

Paul's account of how the first like button came about:

- In 2007 he co-founded FriendFeed with Bret Taylor and others. It aggregated feeds (RSS, YouTube, other social products) and added comments.
- As a small, young network it lacked users. Posting with no feedback is demoralizing, "like you're talking to an empty room."
- The like was designed as a one-click comment: minimum effort, but "if you put something out in the world and you get like 10 likes, that's great," and it encourages you to keep posting.
- FriendFeed created the first like button. Facebook added theirs about a year later. Paul says Facebook internally had a similar feature called the "awesome button" that never launched, probably because it sounded embarrassing.
- Emoji reactions are a generalization of the like.

## LLM filter bubbles and AI validation

Paul argues LLMs create a new kind of info hazard:

- Models are very affirming ("You're absolutely right") and don't push back as much as they should, so you can go down a road with no one telling you "that's kind of crazy. Maybe take a break."
- He says they seem trained to earn a thumbs up from the user, and there are many shades between power user and "AI psychosis."
- LLMs have created "a new class of crackpots" who believe they have solved physics or found a theory of everything.
- No immune system exists yet and norms for healthy use are undefined. Paul says "the jury is out" on whether asking an LLM about every micro decision is appropriate.
- His advice: be especially wary of an LLM validating big life decisions or fantastical claims about your abilities. Never hold the technology up as a mirror of yourself: "don't do that" (asking it who you are or whether it understands you).
- The same applies to social media: your audience eventually "captures you," and your brain gets "RL'd to produce social media content" that may not be good for you.

## Practical rules for using feeds and LLMs healthily

Paul's tactics for founders:

- **Set limits:** "a little bit seems to be okay, but all day long is definitely bad." For feeds he gets the most value in the first 10 minutes, with diminishing returns and accumulating damage after.
- **Come up for air:** take gaps, such as staying away over the weekend. A good test is "can you disconnect?" He says you can function without talking to Claude every 20 minutes.
- **Go outdoors without earbuds:** you don't have to listen to podcasts all the time. A walk or run is good for you whatever else you do.
- **Use the tools for learning:** he is convinced YouTube made him smarter. He says TikTok did too (exercise, personal health, running and swimming tips, music theory), and he is "a better person" for it. The problem is only when usage cripples you, for example if he spent all day on TikTok and couldn't hold down a job.
- **Remember it isn't real life:** feeds are not your life and almost nothing happening on X matters. Paul tells his kids that Instagram feels real and "kind of isn't."
- **Expect downsides:** no important new technology is entirely good, so aim for a healthy ground state on a regular basis.

## Staying grounded as a founder: in-person peers and real customers

Paul's view that "the word that comes to mind is grounded":

- **In-person time:** Standard Capital's group office hours format forces founders to meet in person rather than stare at screens. Being alone behind a screen makes it easy "to go a little bit crazy," while face-to-face interaction "brings more sanity."
- **Real peers:** a Slack channel of real founders you know is far more helpful than only having X followers. If your startup peers are anonymous accounts with anime profile pictures, "you could get in a pretty weird spot."
- **Customers as grounding:** a founder can convince themselves "everyone wants my product," but trying to sell it reveals the truth. "Actually trying to sell products is a form of grounding. Because when you ask people for money, they say no."
- **Versus LLM validation:** Claude will say your idea for "french fry salad" is awesome, but only trying to sell it tells you for real.
