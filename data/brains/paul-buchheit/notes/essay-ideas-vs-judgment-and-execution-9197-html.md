## Ideas are neither worthless nor valuable on their own

- Paul notes two camps. Normal people assume ideas are worth millions (the Pet Rock). Engineers, VCs and successful entrepreneurs say ideas are worthless, and Paul Graham offers a "proof": there is no market for startup ideas, so there is no demand.
- Paul has "ideas all of the time, many more than I have time for", so he leans toward "worthless". He still sees a "nagging inconsistency" in that view.
- The inconsistency: Marc Andreessen argues market is the most important factor, so a mediocre team building the right product succeeds and a brilliant team building the wrong product fails. Paul says that is a lot like saying the right idea does matter, and asks why great teams execute perfectly on bad ideas.
- His conclusion: both camps are wrong "when stated so simply".

## The mountain metaphor: products are mountains with gold at some summits

- Paul says to imagine products as mountains. To build a product you climb the mountain.
- Some mountains have a big pot of gold at the top and some do not. To make money you must pick the right mountain and also climb to the top and gather the gold.
- There are two ways to fail: choosing a mountain with little or no gold, or dying on the way up.
- Many mountains have never been climbed. Others were attempted and abandoned: the team froze, or there was no gold when they arrived.
- Mount Everest has fifteen recognized routes (per Wikipedia), some easier than others, so the route matters as well as the mountain.

## Judgment is the underrated half of execution

- Paul says judgment "isn't talked about as much as execution, but it's obviously very important".
- Climbing needs a level of technical ability that depends on the mountain and route. It also needs judgment to choose the right route, and to change course when the current path isn't working.
- His example: a technically brilliant team hits a sheer cliff and thinks, "this is the perfect opportunity to use Erlang!" (he calls Erlang just a funny example of fancy tech). A team with better judgment sees an easier route around the other side.
- Judgment also drives which mountain to climb, since the landscape holds millions of them.

## Why the people selling ideas get brushed off

- Paul pictures people wandering the flatlands near the mountains. They have ideas about which mountains hold gold, and some have drawn crude maps of an "easy" route.
- They try to sell the ideas and maps to climbers, and the climbers brush them off as worthless.
- When a team finds a huge cache of gold, the idea-sellers say, "I had that idea first! They stole my idea!"
- Paul grants they did have the idea, as did many other people. "Ideas are plentiful. The problem is that most ideas are bad": either there's no gold at the top, or the ascent is too hard with today's technology.
- What's valuable is "the judgment to know which mountains have the gold, and the team that can get to it".

## When an idea does have value: the track record of whoever holds it

- If a skilled climber who has chosen the right mountains before thinks he knows where another gold-rich mountain is, people will listen. The idea has value because it comes from someone "who has a history of being right".
- The identical idea from a random person with no experience and no ability to execute will probably be ignored, because there's not enough evidence it's good.
- That person, if they truly believe in it, has to prove it on their own. Paul calls this "the beauty of our system": they often can, even if everyone else thinks it's a bad idea.
- A person with a history of being right plus a capable team that has shown technical skill and judgment on other mountains is "very valuable" and will have no trouble getting an idea funded.

## Paul's formula

- Summary: Idea * Judgment * Ability * Determination * Luck = $$$
- It is a product of terms, so a weak factor drags the whole result down. Ideas are one input, not the whole thing.
