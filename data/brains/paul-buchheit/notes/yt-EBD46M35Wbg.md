## How Gmail started and what Paul built first

- Google gave Paul a wide-open mandate to work on "some kind of email or personal information system". There was no clear spec; the belief was that a lot of useful information was locked inside email.
- He started alone in late 2001 by building a tool to index and search his own email, then added features iteratively.
- He used and iterated on it for about two years before launch. Gmail launched April 1, 2004, invite-only.
- Early invites were so scarce that people sold them on eBay, in some cases for hundreds of dollars.
- Some ideas were prototyped but not shipped at launch (for example Vim integration, which was added about a year later).
- The core team at launch was about a dozen people.

## Designing from watching real users

- Paul's team watched colleagues use their email (with consent) and listened to the problems they hit.
- Key discovery: many people had two systems, the one they wanted to use (a big, carefully named folder hierarchy) and the one they actually used. Example: an "inbox 2" folder created because the inbox "got too big".
- In practice people left everything in the inbox because they fell behind on sorting.
- Paul's own habit became the design paradigm: respond or defer, then archive, and find things later by searching. Hence the "archive" button and search-first design.
- Automatic grouping into conversations came from seeing people waste time reassembling threads by subject.
- Principle: reduce manual labor and "accommodate" people who don't want to organize, since they had been neglected by existing mail clients.
- On guilt about messy inboxes: "get over the guilt". There is no single right way to do email; it comes down to personality.

## Why the JavaScript front end mattered

- Paul says the most influential aspect was building the whole interface in the browser with JavaScript.
- At the time webmail was seen as a toy, and serious email meant a desktop client like Outlook.
- Delivering a great email experience in the browser showed people "the web is real" and inspired others to build serious web apps, which he sees continuing in web office suites.

## Running an internal startup inside Google

- He was Google employee number 23, joining in June 1999 and leaving around 2006.
- Many inside Google questioned Gmail as an unnecessary distraction, since the company was focused on web search and ads and saw itself as search technology powering partners like Yahoo. Gmail also threatened those partnerships.
- Infrastructure assumptions from search did not carry over. When crawling the web, losing a few pages is fine because you can refetch them; email can never lose data, so reliability and instant updates affect the whole stack down to low-level decisions.
- Ads: Paul says ads targeted by email content were surprisingly relevant, and he even bought things from them. The product had to be optimized so operating cost was low enough that modest ad revenue covered it.
- On outages: they are annoying but rare, and he compares them to his home power going out more often than Gmail does.

## "Don't be evil" and company values

- Paul came up with "Don't be evil" at a company meeting around early 2001 about company values. He wanted something memorable that might make people slightly uncomfortable and make them think, rather than forgettable corporate values.
- He and another engineer kept it alive until it took on a life of its own.
- Its real value: it gives everyone in the company license to question decisions. He recalls a meeting where someone seriously asked "but wait, is this evil?" and the group debated it.
- He contrasts this with large organizations where the attitude is "it's not your job to worry about that, just do what you're told".
- Lesson on values: "it's important for things to be memorable". A perfectly crafted but long statement is forgettable and so doesn't matter.

## Why big companies lose their edge and how to manage creative teams

- When he joined Google, Paul had low expectations. AltaVista had hundreds of engineers and had just spent about $100 million on marketing, and the search market was crowded. He joined to learn from smart people, and expected to watch the startup get crushed.
- He had come from Intel, a huge company with a sea of cubicles, and found the energy at a small startup far better.
- How incentives shift with size: in a startup everyone succeeds or fails together. In a big company, stock price is driven by something like the ad market, so someone working on a new product has little direct impact, and incentives become mostly negative (don't screw up) with the positive ones disappearing.
- His example: if you could do something with a 10% chance of a billion-dollar business and a 90% chance of looking dumb, even willing people face a boss who won't go along.
- He thinks big companies apply industrial-era methods to creative work. In a factory you can measure output; with new product creation you can't measure people well.
- His alternative: push evaluation down to the team level and let people choose who they want to work with. People on a team know who the really good contributors are, and picking people to work with is a strong indicator.
- Teams also need complementary skills and personalities, so building effective teams is tricky; level labels hide big variation between people. Someone very productive who causes others to quit is a net negative.

## Competition: Bing, Yahoo, and underestimating leaders

- Paul found Bing the first Microsoft search product that seemed credible, but says there's a lot of Google technology outsiders don't know about, so it's hard to judge how far behind a competitor is.
- Example: Yahoo's Panama ad system was hyped as matching Google, but he says it was equivalent to what Google had about three years earlier.
- On the iPod and Apple: people assume a competitor "matched" a leader as if the leader is sitting counting money. A company focused on its franchise and continually innovating is very hard to overcome.
- His view: don't attack a strong leader head-on; there is easier, lower-hanging fruit elsewhere.

## Starting FriendFeed after Google

- After leaving Google he took roughly a year off, which he says was partly for family reasons (a newborn with medical issues), then got bored and wanted to work on something he cared about.
- A former Google colleague also left. They started talking about ideas and kept an eye on others exiting Google. They had dinners a couple of times a week with Bret Taylor and Jim Norris, who were behind Google Maps, to discuss ideas.
- They found they shared goals, the kind of company and products, and the underlying philosophy, and decided to merge into a single company, FriendFeed.
- Product: a tool for sharing things (blog posts, links, photos) with friends and having discussions around them, with comments and likes. It began as a way to keep up with friends.
- Positioning problem: users and the press saw it as an aggregator of Facebook and Twitter. Paul says the core concept was sharing, and aggregation was only a convenience feature (if you already share elsewhere, it should show up automatically). The feature created a perception that hurt communication of what it was: "sometimes features maybe even hurt you because it changes how people perceive what it is".
- Competitive problem: Twitter was bigger and its users evolved it toward link sharing, and Facebook added comments and likes. FriendFeed ended up in the same mental "bucket" as a much larger product. Paul says people have "different pockets for different products".
- Differentiation: threaded conversations in which commenters see each other (like a room of people talking) versus independent @replies.
- Facebook acquired FriendFeed in August 2009. Paul says the two were on convergent paths with the same ideas about the product they wanted to build, so joining made sense, and he sees Facebook as having the potential to be as influential as Google in setting the standard for the Valley.

## Lower friction, more sharing, and why Silicon Valley works

- Every time you lower the friction of sharing, people share more and the kinds of things shared change. Example: posting a photo of his new minimalist running shoes took seconds, so it happened; it would never have been worth the effort before.
- He started blogging when his daughter was in the hospital, posting a daily picture and update so he didn't have to repeat himself to each relative. His mother bought a computer for it. He cites it as a positive example of technology.
- Easy feedback channels change who gives feedback: instead of only extreme personalities writing letters, regular people post quick reviews. Brands now have to make customers happy rather than rely on throwing money at messaging. Example: his wife's Yelp review about missing bananas led the restaurant owner to contact her directly.
- Authenticity: real identities and real relationships on Facebook make reviews more meaningful and fakes harder, since you see that a review comes from a friend.
- On Silicon Valley: the heart of it is comfort with risk and with things that may not work. Quitting Intel to join Google was seen as reasonable there, whereas elsewhere people asked "did you get fired?". The perception of the people around you is what tips decisions about leaving for a startup.
- Privacy view: he trusts Google because it has strong incentives to protect data, and his bigger concern is that legal subpoena applies to any electronic communication. He worries this pushes companies to auto-delete email, destroying historical records.
