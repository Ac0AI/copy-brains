## Gmail shipped in a day, then improved by listening to users

- Paul's first Gmail prototype shipped within a day. He reused the Google Groups (Usenet) search indexer and mangled his own email to fit it.
- The first feedback was "it would be better if it had my email instead of yours." So version two scraped everyone's email and partitioned it by user.
- Next came requests to reply, then to send new messages. Paul built each feature in order of importance rather than building speculatively.
- He explains why he ships early: "if I work on something for a long time without shipping I get bored." His 1996 college attempt at web email failed for exactly that reason, after a few weeks.
- The launch metric was "100 happy users." Gmail went to the world only once 100 people inside Google said they were happy users, which "took quite a while."
- Paul went "door to door" at Google asking unhappy users what it would take. Some wanted an Outlook clone, so he crossed them off. Others needed something simple.
- He also sat with colleagues to watch their email process. Many had complex folder hierarchies but also an "inbox two" pile where everything actually lived. That made search the core organizing idea: read it, archive it, search when needed.

## Make something a small group loves, then grow the circle

- Paul's rule for startups: "better to make something that a small number of people really passionately care about than something that's just tolerable to a large number of people."
- Gmail was purposely a bit polarizing. Conversation view was new and weird, and one VP told him to clone AOL Mail.
- If a small circle says "this is the greatest thing ever," you have hit something real. Then you grow that circle, which is easier than turning a large indifferent population slightly less indifferent.
- He credits the freedom of being under-resourced. Gmail was one engineer, then two (Sanjeev Singh), then three. Few resources meant no turf fights. About 15 people were on it by launch.
- The ambition at Google was not to ship a knockoff of Yahoo Mail. The product had to be really good, with search central, and something he himself wanted to use for serious email.

## Speed as a product value

- Google's culture obsessed over speed. Paul recalls Larry Page calling Gmail "unbearably slow" at what Larry guessed was "at least 600 milliseconds." The server logs said 623 ms.
- Paul then wrote a program to train himself to judge time to roughly 100 ms resolution.
- Gmail was written in JavaScript and ran in the browser, avoiding a server round trip on every click. It used many small optimizations and could render the inbox in about 97 ms.
- It also pushed new replies into an open conversation without a page reload, which reduced people replying over each other.

## Gmail's chaotic launch and invite system

- The April 1 date was picked in advance and was somewhat aspirational, since the code was not all written.
- Someone leaked the launch to the New York Times, so the press release moved earlier to midnight UTC.
- At launch there was no landing page, and gmail.com did not resolve. No one had a Gmail account, including Paul, until he finished a feature.
- Google's infrastructure was built for web search. GFS kept three copies of data that could be inconsistent.
- Hardware covered only about 10,000 users at launch. That was a big reason for the invite system, because the system would otherwise have collapsed.
- The first days were mostly spent keeping it from falling over.

## FriendFeed and the open-API era

- Paul left Google in 2006. He and Sanjeev Singh wanted a fun place for engineers who like shipping fast.
- They teamed up with Bret Taylor and Jim Norris, key people behind Google Maps.
- FriendFeed aggregated friends' social feeds across the web (Flickr, YouTube, Delicious, blogs, RSS) and added comments around them.
- The like button debuted on FriendFeed in October 2007. Paul says it was, to his knowledge, the first like button anywhere.
- It depended on an era of open APIs and RSS. He says that product would be impossible today on Instagram or TikTok.

## Social networks: optimize for the network, not the single user

- Paul contrasts Google and Facebook. Early Google made the best product for the individual user, building things the team wanted. Facebook was more socially engineered and thought about the network.
- His main takeaway: "the number one feature of a social network is that your friends are there." No amount of clever features overcomes that.
- Facebook's internal "network ego" idea meant new users were boosted in places like People You May Know. That can make an existing user's experience slightly worse but helps the network grow.
- He says people from Apple or Google, focused on user-first design, often struggle with this trade-off.

## What he tells YC startups

- The first advice is the YC slogan: make something people want. About 90% of failures come from not doing so.
- He tries to force contact with reality fast. Before building, get non-binding letters of intent: "if your customer isn't willing to sign a non-binding, meaningless piece of paper... what are the odds they'll write a $100K check."
- Founders should talk to customers and sell early. Show you can acquire, convert and retain customers.
- He judges founders on slope: "a little bit of slope makes up for a lot of y-intercept." He checks whether they tried his suggestion plus five other things, or nothing happened.
- Iteration speed matters because almost everyone starts in the wrong place. He cites SpaceX blowing up many rockets while iterating, versus Boeing spending decades before a first launch.
- He admires Elon's simplification: "the best part is no part, the best system is no system." In his view, "any midwit can make things complicated." Pushing teams on basic, simple questions is a superpower.

## How to build a $100B company: timing and network effects

- Every $100B company sits on an underlying technological shift. Google rode internet growth, Microsoft rode microcomputers, and DoorDash and Uber exist because of smartphones. A slightly better mousetrap with no fundamental change will not get there.
- His exercise is the DeLorean: hop to 10 years in the future and ask what is new. Then ask what changed, and whether the company owns a chunk of that shift.
- You also need network effects to own the shift, such as DoorDash having the most restaurants, drivers and customers.
- Network effects are often not obvious early. Skeptics in 1998-99 called search a commodity, and Google's founders tried to sell it for $1 million with no takers.
- Google's edge turned out to be a technical lead that kept compounding. Paul thinks AI is likely not a commodity because an intelligence lead can be used to extend itself.

## AI, Google search and optimism

- He made his first neural net around 1995. Seeing GPT-3 a couple of years before this conversation convinced him "we basically have it figured out."
- He predicts AI disrupts search. As you type, the AI can complete the query and deliver the result in the format you want. A pre-AI product cannot keep up with that.
- On alignment, he says humans are not aligned with each other either. Current AI is a tool that extends our power, and how we use it is up to us.
- His positive vision: AI enables abundance, perhaps a world a thousand times wealthier. He says we need positive stories about the future, not only scary ones.

## Identity as a cage

- His blog post "I am nothing" argues that attaching to an identity ("I'm smart," "I'm a Java programmer") makes you defensive and vulnerable.
- He found that criticizing anyone's identity makes them hate you. His example is a negative remark about Java.
- Attaching to the idea that you're smart "really will make me stupid," because you spend effort defending it instead of learning.
- Letting go of identity makes you freer to take risks. He frames identity as a cage that keeps you safe but limits you.
