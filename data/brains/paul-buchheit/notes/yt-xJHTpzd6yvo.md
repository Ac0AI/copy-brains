## How Paul got into early Y Combinator

- Paul read Slashdot, which regularly posted Paul Graham essays. In 2005 PG posted one on the Summer Founders Program, and Paul loved that anyone could apply, with no need to know a founder or have a famous roommate.
- He followed YC from afar at first. His child was in the hospital at the time, so he couldn't take part.
- In fall 2005 PG announced the second batch would run in Mountain View (the first was in Cambridge). Paul cold emailed PG, introduced himself and offered to help.
- PG replied and introduced him to Jessica, who was writing Founders at Work. Paul then started showing up at the batch dinners and meeting the founders.
- The second batch was winter '06, with about 10 companies. In Paul's words, "almost all of them you've never heard of."

## What the early demo days were like

- Most of venture thought YC was "some kind of a joke with like horrible adverse selection," where no good company would ever apply.
- Demo day was first called Angel Day. The audience was about 20 people, whoever PG and Jessica could convince to show up.
- Presentations were rough. One founder building a database opened a terminal and typed SQL queries.
- One summer '06 team pitched two different ideas because they weren't confident in either.
- Almost no one was writing checks. Paul says the only investors at demo day were himself, PG and Trevor.
- Summer batches were still in Cambridge, so Paul flew to Boston for the summer '06 and summer '07 demo days.
- There was real raw talent (he names Jared's batch), but the ideas were basic. Mostly it was "a website where you can do X," such as rating your professor.

## The Wufoo angel investment and returns

- One of his first angel investments was Wufoo, an online form builder.
- Paul put in $50K and PG put in $50K, and that was the whole round.
- This was before the SAFE or the capped convertible note existed. It was a literal series seed for a $100K investment.
- They invested at about a $1 million valuation.
- Wufoo was later acquired by SurveyMonkey for "I think about $50 million," which Paul puts at roughly a 50x return.
- He also invested in Anywhere FM and says he was one of the only people on its cap table. He thinks it came from the summer '06 batch.

## Why he invested when almost no one else did

- Asked why he was smart enough to do it, Paul says he doesn't think he was. His approach: "optimize for learning."
- His logic was that investing some money would teach him something, and it did.
- Playing it overly safe means you never get the experience.
- "Startups are a power law business." It's fine if most investments are bad as long as a couple are very good.

## Why today's YC startups are more ambitious than 2005's

- Paul's example from a recent demo day: founders planning to manufacture solar cells on the moon. In the early days it was a site for rating professors.
- YC always tried to pump up founders' ambitions. This compounds across batches, because each batch sees what the last one did and raises its own bar.
- YC also attracts more ambitious people, and then pushes them to beat the founders who came before.
- Success brought capital. The original idea was that $20K let people live the grad-student lifestyle for a summer while building a website.
- Now demo day is "a huge feeding frenzy" with rounds of $10-20 million. That funds far bigger ideas, and a rocket company would have been unfundable back then.
- Investors now believe they could be funding the next DoorDash, Stripe or OpenAI, where 25 years ago they were looking at a form-filling tool.
- Paul still sees the same vibe of smart young hackers.
- The one thing he misses is the small size. He could know every company in the batch because they all sat at one dinner table. He can't know a thousand companies.

## Hard tech at YC

- Paul says he always loved deeper technology.
- His group of hard tech companies included Oklo (small fission reactors, now public), Helion (fusion, which he says is a couple of years from feeding energy onto the grid, and which recently raised another round) and Rigetti (quantum computers, also public now).
- The early YC space also had Trevor's robotics company Anybots, so the dinners happened next to walking robots and a homemade Segway. Paul says this is part of what made early YC cool.
- The first YC batch included Sam (OpenAI), Justin and Emmett (Twitch) and the Reddit founders. Paul's point: "a lot can come out of a small group of people that no one's ever heard of."

## What Paul brings to Standard Capital from early YC

- Build a community of really ambitious founders and bring them together.
- Push them to be more ambitious: "Why are your goals so sad?" Ask how to turn the company into a $100 billion or trillion dollar company.
- He says this is a lot of the value an investor can add, and it's what founders come for.
- The place is not for people who don't want to build something world changing.
- The goal is to curate the right people so that amazing things can happen, as they did when YC was new and small.
