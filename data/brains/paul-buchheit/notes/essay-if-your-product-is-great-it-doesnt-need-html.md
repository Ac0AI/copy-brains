## Pick three features and get them very right; everything else is noise

- Paul argues the common product-design mistake is the "more features = better" mindset. It explains why "so many otherwise smart people are bad at product design (e.g. most open source projects)."
- Reviews of the 2001 iPod and the iPad focused on "missing" features. Those features usually exist in unsuccessful competitors, which leads people to wrongly conclude that a successful product needs even more features.
- His example: if a MacBook with OSX and no keyboard were the right product, Microsoft's tablet would have succeeded years earlier. "Copying the mistakes of a failed product isn't a great formula for success."
- His rule for new products: "Pick three key attributes or features, get those things very, very right, and then forget about everything else." Those three define the product's essence and value, and "the rest is noise."
- He asks founders: what are the three (or fewer) key features that make the product so great you can cut or half-ass everything else? Is at least 80% of your effort going into getting those right?

## The original iPod as a three-attribute product

Paul's reading of the 2001 iPod:

1. Small enough to fit in your pocket.
2. Enough storage to hold many hours of music.
3. Easy to sync with your Mac. He guesses most hardware companies got this wrong because they can't make software.

- Everything else was absent: no wireless, no on-device playlist editing, no Ogg support.
- Slashdot's review dismissed it with "No wireless. Less space than a nomad. Lame."
- His point: the product was nothing but the essentials, well executed.

## How Gmail launched with a minimal feature set

- Gmail's three core attributes at launch: it was fast, it stored all your email (when 4MB quotas were the norm), and it had an innovative interface built around conversations and search.
- Secondary and tertiary features were minimal or missing. There was no rich text composer.
- The original address book was built in two days and did almost nothing. The engineer wanted five days; Paul talked him down to two "since I never use that feature anyway."
- Those features can be added or improved later, and Gmail improved a lot after launch. But "if the basic product isn't compelling, adding more features won't save it."

## Why constraint reveals the product's true value

- Focusing on a few core features in version one "forces you to find the true essence and value of the product."
- Test for innovation: if the product needs "everything" in order to be good, it's probably not very innovative. It might be a nice upgrade to an existing product, but not a new thing.
- His summary line: "if your product is great, it doesn't need to be good."

## Simplicity as a feature: the iPhone and iPad

- Paul says he spends more time browsing the web on his iPhone than his laptop.
- Part of the reason is simplicity. The iPhone is ready in under half a second; the laptop takes at least a few seconds to wake and has distracting activity running.
- The iPhone feels like "a simple appliance that I use without a second thought." The laptop feels like a complex machine that makes him "pause and consider if it's worth the effort right now."
- The iPhone's downsides are small size and slowness, though he counts smallness as a feature too.
- He is unsure where the iPad ends up, and says making it succeed is Apple's problem. He expects its value to come from new behaviors enabled by "a fast, simple, and sharable internet window" sitting around the house.
- Examples he gives: casually browsing the web at home, sharing photos in person, board games (an idea from Bret), and at the office an easy way to chat with remote people while discussing a presentation or document, such as audio iChat with a shared display.
- These are theoretically possible on laptops, but they end up "so clumsy and complicated that we don't bother."

## When this advice does and doesn't apply

- Paul says the advice probably applies only to consumer products, meaning ones where the purchaser is also the user. He includes some business products in that group.
- For markets with purchasing processes that have long feature-requirement lists, his advice is to "crank out as many features as possible and not waste time on simplicity or usability."
