## Wealth creation as the first test for a startup idea

- Paul says a key question when he evaluates a startup is "where is the wealth created?"
- He argues most people, and most politicians, have no real concept of how wealth is created.
- His example of a category with no visible wealth creation is NFTs, and some crypto token businesses. He describes the model as "we create this token and then we sell it to someone else" who sells it to someone else, with no point where value is created.
- He advises founders to understand how their startup creates wealth. The more wealth created for users and customers, the better. Not having a good answer to this is "a problem."

## Marketplaces create wealth by connecting supply and demand

- Paul says marketplaces are underappreciated. Many of the most valuable companies are marketplaces under the hood. Google and Facebook are both advertising marketplaces that bring buyers and sellers together on a platform.
- His mechanism: a producer makes a widget for $10, and a consumer would happily pay $100. Even with a million consumers, if buyer and seller can't find each other, the transaction never happens.
- A marketplace creates wealth by connecting producer and consumer. It is a positive-sum exchange where both parties end up better off. The marketplace typically collects a percentage of the revenue.
- Examples he endorses:
  - eBay: people have junk packed away in garages and storage, and someone else wants it.
  - Airbnb: Joe and Brian had a spare bedroom and rented it out during a design conference to make rent. It turned dead space into cash for the host and cheap lodging for the traveler.
  - Whatnot: a "modern eBay competitor" and one of his best investments at YC. He says many people make a living selling on its live streams.

## Positive-sum vs zero-sum thinking

- Paul says many people hold a zero-sum view of wealth: a fixed amount exists, so someone with a billion dollars must have stolen it. He says this is especially common at the socialist end of the spectrum.
- The positive-sum view sees a founder who created billions in wealth and kept a slice. Typically a founder who reaches IPO owns about 10% of the company they created.
- He compares the zero-sum view to a Scrooge McDuck money bin: "if only we could bust open Scrooge McDuck's money bin, then everyone could be wealthy." He says that approach has been tried and "generally ends in mass starvation."
- He cites Paul Graham's example of buying an old car, fixing it up and selling it for more. Labor produces more value than it cost, and no one was cheated.
- Wealth comes from physical labor and also from good decisions. His own business is the example: good investments are "fabulously profitable," and only wrong ones go to zero. The decision is where the wealth creation happens.

## Pick markets with no ceiling

- After "where's the wealth creation?", his next question is whether it has a limit. This ties to finite versus infinite markets.
- A weak idea takes all the revenue from an old business worth about $3 billion. He says you're "probably not doing a whole lot of wealth creation there."
- A strong idea sits in a massive, growing opportunity. When Google started in the '90s the internet was still small, so the value of organizing it was limited by its size. As the internet grew 10x, 100x and 1,000x, Google's opportunity to connect people to information and services grew with it. He says Google has "infinite potential for wealth creation."
- He says AI companies like OpenAI and Anthropic have no real limit on wealth creation because they create intelligence. He calls intelligence a fundamental ingredient of wealth: it leads to new products, curing diseases, fixing environmental issues and going into space.

## Value creation vs value capture

- Paul frames success as doing both value creation and value capture.
- He says founders often focus only on how to capture value. If you aren't creating value, capturing it is hard, and if you manage it you're "a parasite." He says "we don't want to fund parasites" and wants companies creating massive value.
- The more value you create, the easier it is to capture some fraction and grow a huge business.
- Value creation without capture is also possible. His example is Linux: Linus created enormous value but gave it away, so he captured only a tiny fraction, mostly through fame.
- His ideal: "the optimal startup is one that is creating infinite value and then capturing, you know, 10% of it."
- A way to check: compare the value customers get with the value the startup keeps. Google drives customers to sites and helps businesses get found, and the value captured by the company is a tiny fraction of the benefit to users.

## Be 10x better, and ask whether cheaper grows demand

- Paul says creating value doesn't mean being the lowest-cost option. Being slightly cheaper than a competitor "is not creating value."
- He wants a product that is "not just like 10% better, but like 10x better." The best combination is a product that is both better and cheaper.
- A follow-up test: when it gets cheaper, does that grow demand?
  - SpaceX: as launch cost falls, new possibilities open, such as asteroid mining companies and hotels on the moon, which only work if launch costs drop.
  - AI: GPUs, built for video games, made matrix multiplication cheap. He says the video game industry accidentally set the foundation for modern AI by driving that cost down.
- His closing advice: create more value than you capture, and have a clear idea of where your startup creates wealth.
