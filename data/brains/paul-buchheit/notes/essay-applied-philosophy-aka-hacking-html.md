## Hacking as applied philosophy: finding the actual rules

Paul defines hacking as applied philosophy. Every system has two sets of rules: the rules as intended or commonly perceived, and the actual rules ("reality"). In most complex systems the gap between them is huge. Once someone discovers the actual rules, it becomes possible to perform "miracles", meaning things that violate the perceived rules.

- Paul calls the discovery of actual rules the core of hacking.
- Computer hackers do this literally: they find the actual rules of a system (his example is buffer overflows) and use them to circumvent the intended rules (typically access controls). The same applies to people who break DRM.
- He thinks the common use of "hacker" for people who subvert computer systems is "essentially correct", even though the term is occasionally disputed.

## Hacking in code: elegant hacks and ugly hacks

Paul also uses "hacking" for writing clever or ugly code, where the hacker violates expectations of how software gets written.

- A project that should take months but gets hacked out in a single evening is "a small miracle, and a major hack".
- If the result is simple and beautiful because the hacker found a better solution, it is an "elegant" or "brilliant" hack.
- If the result is complex and hard to understand (for example, it violates many layers of abstraction), it is an "ugly hack".
- Ugly hacks are not all bad. One of his favorite personal hacks was messy code that demonstrated what would become AdSense. The code was quickly discarded, but "it did its job". He tells that story in his post "Communicating with code".

## Hacking applies to every system, not just computers

Paul argues that our entire reality is "systems of systems, all the way down", so wherever there are systems there is potential for hacking. His examples:

- Human relations: *The Game*, an account of people hacking human attraction.
- Health: Seth Roberts's ideas.
- Sports: Tim Ferriss's claim to have hacked the National Chinese Kickboxing championship.
- Finance: "too big to fail".

## "There are always shortcuts and loopholes"

Against the common advice that there are no shortcuts to success and that it is all hard work and doing what you're told, Paul says the hacking mindset takes the opposite approach: "**There are always shortcuts and loopholes**."

- Hacking is sometimes perceived as cheating or unfair, and it can be. Using social hacks to steal billions is wrong (he cites Madoff).
- Automation is his example of a good hack. Getting machines to do our work enabled a much higher standard of living, even though not everyone saw it that way (the Luddites were not fans).

## Important new businesses are usually hacks

Paul says important new businesses are usually some kind of hack. Established businesses think they understand the system and set up rules to guard their profits and prevent real competition. A new business must find a gap in those rules: something the incumbents either don't see or don't consider important.

Google is his example. The existing search engines thought of themselves as portals and believed two things:
- Search quality wasn't very important, because regular people can't tell the difference.
- Search wasn't very valuable anyway, because it sends people away from your site.

Google's success came in large part from recognizing that others were wrong on both points.

## Building wealth is itself a hack

Paul argues that the entire process of building a business and having other people and computers do the work for you is a big hack.

- "Nobody ever created a billion dollars through direct physical labor." Creating that much wealth requires major shortcuts, and by definition those shortcuts were mostly invisible to others (though many will dispute that after the fact).
- Startup investing takes the hack to the next level by having other people do the work of building the business. He notes that finding the right people and businesses is not easy.

## The hacker mindset creates the future

Paul says not everyone has the hacker mindset and that society needs a variety of personalities. But wherever there were people, there was someone "staring into the system, searching for the truth".

- Some people are content to find a truth. Others use their discoveries to hack the system and transform the world.
- Those people created the governments, businesses, religions and other machines that operate society, and they necessarily did it by hacking the prior systems. Incumbents don't give up easily, as anyone trying to establish a new government or religion would find.
- To discover great hacks, we must keep searching for the true nature of reality while acknowledging that we do not currently possess the truth and never will.
- His conclusion: hacking is much bigger than clever bits of code. "It's how we create the future."
- He closes with humility: "Or at least that's how I see it. Maybe I'll change my mind later."
- He points readers to "The Knack" and the need to disassemble things.
