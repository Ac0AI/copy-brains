## Listening means decoding the problem, not obeying the request

- Paul says listening to users is a process: let the words come in, decode them, and work out why people are saying what they say. He does not mean doing exactly what they ask.
- He uses the Henry Ford line ("a faster horse") as the model. The problem part of a request is usually right ("the horse is too slow"). The proposed solution is often a few seconds of thought and not the best answer.
- Early Gmail example: users suddenly asked for a way to "respond to messages directly from in the Inbox." He asked why. The servers had gotten slow, and the real message was "Gmail is too slow." They fixed performance and the requests stopped.
- He says the true message is sometimes "buried a little bit" beneath the surface request.

## Understand advice, don't follow it blindly

- He worries about people taking advice from speakers literally, because "I don't really know what the right way to do things is." He clarifies that this does not mean ignoring advice. It means understanding the context behind it.
- Everyone really only knows their own life, and only barely. Your situation differs in place, time and age.
- Cautionary story: an adviser told the Viaweb founders (per a Paul Graham essay) that success in software meant putting it in cardboard boxes and selling it at the computer store. They boxed a website and sold about two copies. The adviser overgeneralized a lesson that worked for his own product in his own era.
- Ask why the adviser thinks something is a good idea, and how their situation differs from yours.
- His disclaimer: his experience is consumer web software (Gmail, FriendFeed). For enterprise software competing with Oracle, his advice could be terrible, and users who are powerless might be worth ignoring.
- "Everyone has a unique set of resources and talents and abilities and insight, and if you try to play someone else's hand you're probably going to lose."

## Watch out for limiting beliefs

- When someone says "that's impossible," translate it as: "according to my limited experience and narrow understanding of reality, it's very unlikely." It is probably a hard-learned lesson for them, but things have changed, or they may have just done it wrong.
- When people invoke "reality," they are usually trying to crush your dreams.
- He prefers a lazy approach to invention: pay close attention to the world and "rip off little things all over the place." Notice how things work in nature, and notice a bad product with one cool feature. Pull out that one feature and emphasize it.
- He compares this to tuning a radio dial until a weak, distant signal comes in.
- Start from problems, not solutions. "There's plenty of problems in the world."

## Listening for early success: find one enthusiastic person

- Success starts small and subtle. Most launches get silence: "no one uses it, no one cares."
- You don't need a million happy users. You need at least one person who is genuinely enthusiastic and keeps emailing you. Follow that person, then work out whether there are more people like them.
- This is a reason to launch quickly rather than try to make the product perfect, which he calls "really sort of arrogant."
- If you can't find even one person who likes what you're doing, "maybe you're doing the wrong thing" and should find a new product.

## The Gmail "100 happy users" challenge

- Before launching, Gmail was challenged to get 100 happy users inside Google. Paul thinks the idea may have come from Eric Schmidt, but he can't remember for sure.
- He thought it would be easy with thousands of employees, but "happiness is a really high bar."
- They did it one user at a time, asking each person what it would take to make them happy.
  - Some asked for something very hard, so they accepted those people would not be happy with Gmail, possibly ever.
  - Others needed something small, so the team did the easy things first until they reached 100 happy people.
- "People are pretty similar to each other," so if you can make 100 people happy, you can usually make more.

## Listen to yourself: annoyance, energy and ideas

- Notice what is happening in your own body and mind. For usability, notice every time you wait, get slightly confused or feel irritated. You have to make yourself a little less happy, because you are irritated by everything wrong with everything. You only need to notice problems, not have solutions.
- This also drives product ideas. Most Gmail features came from Paul being annoyed that he couldn't find his email, then finding a fix.
- It applies to jobs, teams, projects and source code as well:
  - At Intel ("seas of gray cubicles") he felt drained every day and wondered if engineering was wrong for him. He quit because startups seemed more exciting.
  - At Google, when it was about 20 people in Palo Alto, he felt a "buzz of energy" and couldn't wait for Monday.
  - Years later he noticed he felt drained at Google again, did almost nothing for a few months, and decided to quit. His line: "now I go to work and I have fun."
- Excitement and energy are, in his view, a really good signal.
- He is not saying everyone should quit a job. The point is that a drained feeling means you should ask whether the system is wrong for you.

## Listening to opportunity: the Wozniak and Apple story

- Paul tells the story (from Jessica Livingston's book, Wozniak's interview) of Wozniak resisting quitting HP to found Apple. Steve Jobs lined up Wozniak's friends and family to tell him he had to quit, and was "relentless."
- If Wozniak had stayed, Apple wouldn't exist and he might have been just another obscure HP engineer, possibly laid off.
- His takeaway: there are probably thousands of Wozniaks at HP, Intel and similar companies who are nowhere near their potential. They spend days drained and bored, go home and watch TV, and never do anything significant because the system is wrong for them.
- He poses it as a question: are you one of those people?
- He adds a recruiting pitch for FriendFeed: for a brilliant engineer who gets things done, it is "basically the best opportunity you have right now."

## Q&A: prioritizing features, launching, hiring

- **Prioritizing features with limited resources.** "Some combination of laziness and selfishness": do what is easiest, or what he wants himself. He also checks whether a request is a special case or applies to many people.
  - Risk: a feature that matters to 1% of users can hurt the other 99% by complicating the interface and the code. He thinks that is usually a mistake "unless that 1% of people is really important."
- **Invites.**
  - Gmail and FriendFeed used invites to keep users away. They weren't sure the product was ready, and there is "some sustainable rate at which you can grow without bad things happening."
  - He doubts invites were a marketing masterstroke and can't tell whether they helped. He warns about "cargo cult" imitation of superficial traits (rounded logos, Delicious's dotted URL, invites).
- **Features he removed.** Most of his own Gmail ideas never shipped, because "I have lots of ideas and they aren't all good." He believes in trying things, since some of his worst-seeming ideas turned out good. One FriendFeed feature lived about an hour before he deleted it because everyone hated it.
- **When to hire.**
  - It depends on whether the business can support it. FriendFeed won't grow big without money coming in, because he doesn't want a business that "falls apart."
  - He has never had trouble finding work for really great people. Great people mostly take care of themselves.
  - He looks for people with technical skill and reasonable judgment, so they can act independently and the result is usually right.
