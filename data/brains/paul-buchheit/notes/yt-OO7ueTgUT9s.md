## AI as a replacement for middle management

Paul argues that the core of middle management, "gathering information from people and then combining it and then passing along to the person above," is work AI can do. He frames the trend as "AI eats management."
- People doing the actual work, especially physical work, are still required. The people coordinating that work are the ones who can be replaced.
- He calls this counterintuitive. Conventional wisdom says the least experienced people get replaced first.
- Gathering and organizing information is "exactly the kind of things that LLMs are great at."
- Monitoring and measuring is a big part of management, and AI is very good at it.
- Some founders in Paul's portfolio (funded but not yet announced) are already saying they don't need managers.
- He cites Coinbase as a public company that has officially said it has no more pure managers.

## What a manager actually does, from first principles

Paul describes management as a coordination role: "When you have a bunch of people working together, you need to make sure they're kind of pointed in the same direction." It also includes making sure everyone is showing up and doing their job.
- An early Google belief was that with really great engineers you need less management. Paul says he thinks that is still true.
- He points to Nvidia, where Jensen Huang has something like 100 direct reports. Paul doesn't remember the exact number, but says it shows a wide span of control is possible.
- Paul agrees the pieces of the job can be unbundled: checking what everyone does, and the emotional-support side. AI can plausibly cover both.
- On emotional support, he notes friends who switched from human therapists to AI. A human therapist "will inevitably insert their own issues," while AI is "infinitely good at listening" and supportive. He sees no reason the same dynamic can't play out in the workplace.
- What AI doesn't replace: Paul concedes you still need someone monitoring performance and handling the inevitable HR issues that come with humans. That was the lesson from Google's first no-managers experiment, though he adds that the setup "worked fine for me."

## Google's no-managers experiment

Paul was at Google in the early days (around 2001) when Larry Page tried removing engineering managers.
- Larry was frustrated that Google was moving too slowly.
- His solution was to get rid of all engineering managers. Engineers became tech leads on projects, and everyone reported up to the VP of engineering.
- It lasted until about 200 engineers, with roughly 200 people reporting to one VP of engineering.
- That was the point where it broke, and Google brought engineering directors back.
- Paul says Google went through "a few different cycles" of this, so the pattern repeated.
- His read on why it broke: some people needed more hands-on management, and someone has to monitor performance and deal with HR issues.

## How to replace managers with AI in practice

Paul's approach is the "AI native" one: as a startup, "you just keep scaling and fixing the problems as they occur with AI," solving each problem with AI.
- His mental model is to treat AI as a cognitive multiplier. Ask how you would do the job if you had unlimited time, then give the AI those same instructions, because it effectively has unlimited time.
- Concrete tactic he agrees with: use Claude to run a loop that reads literally everything each person in the organization is doing (check-ins, code) and summarizes it into a chunk you can make sense of, in real time, every day.
- One example he relays: an executive at a large tech company vibe-coded agents to do the work of middle managers. That gave a real-time window into what every individual contributor was doing, with immediate feedback, and the team moved much faster.
- Paul found it notable that this came from someone building it on the ground.

## Implications for how large companies can grow

Paul speculates that removing management layers changes how big a company can get while staying fast.
- Historically, big companies get "complex and inefficient and political" because of layers of middle management that add "confusion and slowness."
- If AI replaces those layers, in theory organizations could grow much larger without the usual decay.
- Anthropic is his possible example, flagged as speculation: it keeps launching major products "that seem to murder a batch of startups every week or so."
- His theory is that Anthropic uses its AI tools internally so that relatively small teams can ship high-impact products.
- His overall prediction: focus attention and resources on whoever creates the value. Everything will move faster "because we won't have all the layers of confusion that are introduced by middle management."

## Would AI managers make people happier?

Paul is optimistic that AI managers would make work better for the people being managed.
- Often "you hear stories of people who hate their manager." Humans bring their own issues to work.
- An AI manager could be "always available, who always listens, who's always fair, who isn't injecting their own politics or anything into the decision."
- He argues this could make people happier in their future jobs, and says it is "guaranteed."
- A shared observation he agrees with: no one says they love being 10 layers away from the CEO.
