## Assume the startup fails: judge decisions by the worst case and the best case, not the likely case

Paul argues most people ask "What is the most likely outcome?" and proceed if it looks good. That works for "normal" decisions like school, a job or a house. It also makes people ignore bad outcomes, so they feel bitter or cheated when one hits. His examples: "I have a degree, now where's my job!?", and house buyers who never considered that a new house could lose 20% of its value and leave them owing more than it was worth.

When advising on startups, he tells people to start from the assumption that the startup will fail and all their equity will become worthless. Many people say they couldn't stay motivated believing that. Paul finds it unfortunate that they feel they must lie to themselves to stay motivated, and he realized he simply uses a different method of evaluating risk.

## The two questions Paul asks: "What's the worst that could happen?" and "Could it be awesome?"

- Instead of the median outcome, he looks at roughly the 0.01 percentile and the 95th percentile outcomes.
- For a startup, the worst case is usually losing your entire investment (but learning a lot).
- The best case is making a large pile of money, creating something cool, and learning a lot.
- Thinking about the best case is easy and common, which is partly why it gets disrespected ("dreamer" isn't usually a compliment).
- The mistake is ignoring the worst case because thinking about bad things is uncomfortable. He links this to people killing themselves over investment losses, partly because they were not planning for the worst case.

## Why planning for the worst case is also an emotional buffer

- Thinking about the worst case protects you from dumb mistakes.
- It also provides an emotional buffer: "If I'm comfortable with the worst-case outcome, then I can move without fear and focus my attention on the opportunity."
- Caveat: when considering the worst case, count the impact on others. Even if you're fine with dying, that outcome may cause unacceptable harm to people in your life.

## Heuristics that raise a bet's expected value

Best and worst case alone is imperfect. Lottery tickets have an acceptable worst case (you lose $1) and a great best case (millions), yet are generally a bad deal. Paul says to use expected value when it's available (as with lottery tickets, so don't buy them). For most real decisions it isn't, because "the world is too complicated and math is hard", so he uses heuristics:

- Will I learn a lot from the experience? (failure can be very educational)
- Will it make my life more interesting? ("a predictable life is a boring life")
- Is it good for the world? (even if he doesn't benefit, maybe someone else will)

If these are mostly true, he is excited about the best case and comfortable with the worst case, it's "probably a good gamble."

## Which risks to ignore: why he picks the 0.01 percentile

- In the year he wrote this, the US had 37,261 motor vehicle fatalities in a population of 304,059,724, so his odds of dying in a car accident are very roughly 1/10,000 per year.
- He notes many of those deaths involved teenagers and alcoholics, so his own odds are probably a little better, but it's good as a rough estimate.
- Using this logic, he treats 1/10,000 as the cutoff for the worst case worth planning around, and largely ignores obscure 1/1,000,000 risks, which are too numerous and difficult to protect against anyway.

## Cynical optimist

Paul has been called both extremely cynical and unreasonably optimistic. He is fine being a "cynical optimist": cynical about the downside, optimistic about the upside. He points to related essays: "Why I'd rather be wrong" and "The other half of the story".
