## Startups most at risk are those building on a lab's roadmap

- Paul says startups building directly on someone else's roadmap, such as Anthropic's, "are going to get run over."
- He cites Google as precedent: it "did kill a lot of startups," especially those adjacent to something it already did. The Gmail example: "it's like Gmail, but it has one extra feature. That's out of business."
- He wants a product that isn't immediately adjacent to a lab's or incumbent's business, with some kind of wall that would be very difficult for them to overcome.
- Labs are heading toward public markets, possibly with trillion-dollar market caps, and will be "super hungry for revenue." A business making $100 million that is easy for someone else to take is exposed.

## The "turkey graph" startup

- Paul uses the "graph of the turkey's well-being": a thousand and one days of a Thanksgiving turkey's life that look wonderful until the day its head gets chopped off.
- A turkey graph startup has customers and revenue today but isn't defensible, and a lab or competitor can end it suddenly.
- He says building something people want is not enough if the business can be taken from you.
- He says it is annoying when investors ask "what if Google does this?", but argues the question now has teeth because the models keep improving.

## Betting against model progress

- Paul says some startups are implicitly betting that the models won't get smarter.
- Building workflows, adapters, guardrails and middleware around today's model limitations is a short bet on the labs. Next year's model may not need any of it, or may produce it on its own.
- The analogy is "googledogs.com powered by Google," a startup that only works if Google stays bad at searching for dogs.
- The question to ask: is next year's model going to make you more successful, or eliminate the need for your business?

## Software is about to become clonable

- Historically software was expensive, slow and painful to build. A complex product was hard to catch up with because the incumbent had the customers, engineers and user base.
- Paul predicts that with the new coding models, "probably next year just about any piece of software will be clonable." If your whole product is software, you're in trouble even if the labs never clone you.
- Cloning could come from an agent: someone's open-claw agent doesn't want to pay for your software, so it clones it, makes an open-source version and shares it with other people's agents. He expects this next year.
- A pure software business whose moat is that it's hard to build or clone "is going to have a bad time."

## Diagnostic: compete with yourself using next year's model

- Imagine you can press a button and say "clone Microsoft Office," or clone whatever your product is.
- Picture yourself as an ambitious, smart 19-year-old joining Y Combinator next year who wants to take down someone else's business with that future AI. What would you do?
- Assume you already have the future AI, then compete against yourself.

## What is defensible: network effects and existing usage

- Paul says the best businesses have always had network effects. The test: if you could open-source your product and give it away without much difference, it's defensible.
- DoorDash example: even a perfect clone of the app is worthless without the restaurants, drivers and customers.
- He says you want "a product where the strength comes from the fact that everyone is already using it." Those are incredibly hard to displace.
- Ticketmaster example: people have long wanted to replace it, but distribution agreements and similar ties make it very difficult to displace.
- Regulation can also protect a business, and startups can get a head start by owning data, users and customers where it's harder for labs to go (regulatory, technical or other barriers).
- Regulatory protection may erode too. Uber was technically illegal when it started, did it anyway and got the law changed afterward, and Paul thinks AGI presents opportunities to circumvent some regulations.

## What an established company should do

- Paul's first answer for a Series B SaaS company: "I'd panic," then it depends on the business.
- He frames AI as a wave: you are either pushed forward by it or smashed by it.
- The task is to work out how the models help improve and grow your business faster, using the tools aggressively and staying ahead of any competitor.
- Internet-era lesson: media distribution businesses (CDs, Blockbuster, newspapers, bookstores) were crushed and had little move other than pivoting. Businesses whose distribution the internet didn't touch, like car companies, were largely unaffected. Banks survived partly because of regulation.
- Incumbents often haven't needed to innovate in so long that they don't know how. He says Sears had the pieces to be Amazon (cities, delivery infrastructure, a catalog business) but lacked the capacity to evolve.

## Why AI grows the pie

- Paul says wealth comes down to energy and intelligence, and AI is on the cusp of "10xing the amount of intelligence."
- He expects more medical progress in the next 10 to 20 years than at any other time in our lifetimes, along with new materials and a changed understanding of science and reality.
- He thinks energy will also open up, driven by the need to scale it and by new technologies. He says we're going to 10x total wealth, creating more opportunity than has ever existed.
- He says "every problem is a physics problem if you boil it down enough," a mix of physics and engineering. With unlimited engineers and unlimited energy, nearly every problem is solvable.
- Recycling example: it's a scam today because turning plastic into something new is hard, but with abundant energy you could incinerate trash and reuse the atoms, so out comes new raw material.
- He admits he doesn't know for certain that startups will survive and allows that AGI could "turn us all into paperclips," but says Standard Capital is betting that it won't.
