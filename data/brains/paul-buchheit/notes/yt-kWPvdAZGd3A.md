## Fundraising: what makes an investor "bad"

Paul says bad investor behavior mostly comes from aversion to loss. In startups, especially at the early stage, an investor has to accept that most deals will lose money.

- The expected case at seed is that most deals go to zero.
- What Paul looks for is the one deal that returns "a thousand extra turn or hopefully better," which pays for all the zeros.
- That logic is the opposite of real estate or stock investing, where losing everything means something went badly wrong.
- Investors who can't accept this keep trying to defend against loss, and that shows up as bad behavior.

## Skittish diligence and obscure risks

Paul argues endless diligence is a sign of an unsophisticated investor.

- At an early stage there is always something wrong with the company. If he can't think of five reasons it might fail, he shouldn't invest: "it means I don't have insight," or it's a scam.
- Asking for three meetings, four people on the call, and three-year financial projections from a two-person startup is "silly crap."
- Paul says he has been scammed a couple of times, but it was less than 1% of his money. Losing half a percent to fraud isn't worth ruining the good deals by being paranoid.
- He remembers the era of handing a founder a check after a few minutes' conversation, and says that experience is why founders rated him highly.
- Over-process usually comes from needing to justify a loss to whoever's money you manage ("we did all this diligence, we met them 15 times").
- His "higher order bit" idea: in a number, the leftmost digit matters more than all the others combined. In startups, the one that matters is whether they're building something people want and can grow it into a huge company. Everything else is noise.

## Control, panic and activist investors

- Trying to control the company also comes from fear of loss. It's worst at the venture stage, when a junior partner's career is tied to one deal and they start "managing the founder." Paul calls that a nightmare for the founder and bad for the company.
- A good investor bets on the team and supports them, and accepts that if the team is no good the money is gone. Being overbearing won't get it back.
- Paul's rule for angels: step one is write the check, step two is "kiss the money goodbye." Mark it at zero. If it comes back, that's great news.
- Never try to recover an investment. A common version is an investor demanding their money back when things go badly, which is "not how it works."
- Founders are already under pressure and don't need an investor emailing weekly about an article they read, or sending random intros ("my neighbor knows about computers").
- He prefers a clean shutdown to a fake positive exit. If a failing startup sells to another failing startup, investors get a tiny slice of equity in an unknown company, more paperwork, and no tax write-off yet because the loss hasn't happened. His advice: pay everyone, shut down, "give me a clean zero."

## Investors with no money, tiny budgets or big egos

Paul describes several types of bad investor to screen out.

- People with no money who try to be a middleman between a startup and someone who has money.
- People whose budget is tiny, so they meet hundreds of founders to make one investment.
- People who enjoy the power differential of founders coming to pitch them.
- Successful people from another industry who overestimate themselves and think they should tell the "kid asking for money" how things are done.
- Paul's own stance: "What I'm looking for is someone who's going to start a trillion-dollar company, and I've never started a trillion-dollar company. So, I'm looking for someone better than myself." He wants to be convinced the founder knows better than he does, and he's funding them because they're the expert.
- Paul notes YC's internal investor database lets you see how many deals an investor has actually done.

## Why to write many small checks

Paul explains the diversification logic he gives to people new to angel investing.

- It's very hard to pick the winners early because reality is unpredictable. DoorDash is one of YC's greatest investments, roughly a hundred-billion-dollar company, but at one point it was two weeks from bankruptcy. A slightly different history would have made it a zero.
- Given that uncertainty, the sensible approach is a diversified portfolio rather than one large check.
- Paul points to the Kelly criterion, which as he remembers it suggests about 20 investments.

## Speed, slow processes and adverse selection

Paul's rule is that speed is always of the utmost importance.

- Slow investors slow you down. A six-month or three-month fundraise is a bad sign, and he doesn't know anyone good who runs that way.
- He says Facebook bought his company in a weekend, and deals don't take months "if you're working with people who are competent and willing to move fast."
- Slow investors suffer adverse selection: no good founder tolerates a slow process, so they only attract the struggling startups with no other choice. That is why their returns are worst.
- Investors who think being slow and careful screens for quality are "shooting themselves in the foot."
- Founders who tolerate a prolonged process are selecting themselves into the bottom of the market. Paul says founders are "actually better off not raising."
