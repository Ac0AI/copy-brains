## Gmail was built on a stagnant market, and the lesson was iteration speed

- Paul says Gmail could innovate so much because web mail had stagnated. Competitors Hotmail and Yahoo Mail were "incredibly clunky": the whole page reloaded on every action, they were covered in ads, and Hotmail's default quota was 2 megabytes.
- He notes an iPhone photo is roughly 5 MB, so those products were far behind. Nobody had improved them in a long time, and if anything they were getting worse.
- His read: big room to innovate exists when incumbents have stopped shipping. He sees no such situation in AI chat interfaces, where Google, OpenAI and Anthropic are all competing hard, so he would not try to compete on interface there.
- Gmail launched April 1, 2004. At the time Google staff were terrified of Microsoft. Twenty years later both are multi-trillion-dollar companies that coexist, and Microsoft's business shifted from the Windows and Office monopoly to enterprise software. His point: big competitive threats rarely play out as expected.

## Google Video lost to YouTube because it was overly cautious

- Google Video launched before YouTube. Internally the team doubted anyone would upload anything good: "the only thing we could think of is like well probably they're just going to upload like copyrighted content and porn."
- Google Video made uploaders fill out a big form listing actors and directors, as if it were a Hollywood production, then put the video through a review process.
- YouTube simply let people upload and go live, with no gates. It went viral early with copyrighted clips such as the SNL "Lazy Sunday" sketch.
- YouTube later became what Paul calls possibly "the greatest like educational resource that has ever existed". His son uses it for 3D printing and material science.
- Takeaways he draws: unexpected uses are hard to foresee ("in hindsight it's like super easy but ... at the time it's never as easy as it looks"), and process and caution can kill a product against a startup that just ships.

## How Paul stress-tests a startup: the 10-year time machine

- Startups operate on roughly a 10-year horizon from seed to IPO. DoorDash was funded in 2013 and IPO'd in 2021. Reddit was funded in 2005 and IPOs about 20 years later.
- His exercise: imagine stepping out of a DeLorean in March 2034, clearing the time fog, and asking what changed.
- His core theory: a massive success never happens on its own. It rides an underlying technological shift. Google rode the explosion of the internet. DoorDash and Uber exist because of the smartphone.
- He asks founders which underlying shift lets them become a $100 billion or trillion-dollar company in 10 years.
- Since about 2017 he has found it harder to look 10 years out, and he says he no longer knows what happens in 10 years because AI is approaching an event horizon. In his view 2024 is a pivotal year while things get sorted out.

## What Y Combinator selects for: founders and iteration speed, not ideas

- Paul says YC does not pick ideas: "we pick the founders and the founders bring the ideas." People wrongly assume YC decides what is hot that year, which he says is never the case.
- Founders often pivot, so the starting idea is frequently not the good one. The aim is people who "are able to move fast and iterate and make intelligent decisions."
- One of the biggest predictors of startup success, in his view, is how quickly a person iterates. A dumb starting idea is fine. Getting stuck on it is not.
- A lot of YC's work is pushing founders to go talk to customers.
- On competition, he says concerns tend to be overblown. His example of a weak pitch: "it's like Gmail but with one extra feature" is not a viable business. A business should not be something a larger competitor can trivially do.
- YC takes a "law of large numbers" approach: a couple hundred startups per batch, so it can make many bets.
- He declines to say what he would build starting out in 2024. The idea has to come from the founder's own experience and insights.

## Why a startup can beat an incumbent: the innovator's dilemma (Perplexity example)

- Paul likes Perplexity because it iterates quickly, keeps improving the product and keeps engaging users.
- Google's problem is that its search business is hugely profitable, and years of near-monopoly have filled results pages with ads, sometimes a whole first screen of them.
- Perplexity has no legacy business to protect. His rule: if the new business is only 10% as good as the old one, that is terrible for the incumbent but still great for a startup.
- He still concedes that competing directly with Google is hard, and that Perplexity and Google can coexist.
- He is also interested in AI storytelling and media. Within about a year he expects an amateur to produce animation as good as work that currently needs a large budget, such as turning his daughter's fan fiction into animated shows.

## AI changes what being a developer means, and makes prediction harder

- On whether AI replaces or enhances developers, Paul says "both." The tools make building more accessible, and being a developer will increasingly mean describing what you want to an AI and iterating through dialogue.
- He declines to forecast five years out because "things are moving so quickly."
- His own past forecasting record included mobile, cloud and Facebook. He jokes he picked the wrong TV personality to win the 2016 election, thinking Stephen Colbert rather than Donald Trump.
- On interfaces, he expects the next step beyond chat boxes is an always-available agent: open the camera and microphone and have a real-time conversation about what the camera sees. He thinks Apple is in a strong position because it owns the device in your pocket, though it looked late on models.
- He cautions that a research prototype is very different from a shipped product facing millions of adversarial users, and that deployed products learn from interaction. That is why Google could not just launch something better the day after ChatGPT.

## Email's lasting role and when not to use it

- Email's strength is that it is universal and owned by no platform: "it's the base layer." When you sign up for Amazon it emails you, it does not DM you on Instagram.
- He is glad other channels (Slack, DMs) take over some communication. His guidance on use:
  - Good for factual things: a document, a receipt.
  - Avoid it for anything complicated.
  - Never use it for a stressful issue.

## AI risk is centralization of power, and open source plus competition is the hedge

- Paul's main fear is humans using AI for centralized control, not a rogue AI. He gives examples: monitoring everything anyone says, and altering reality in real time, such as faking a video call with a relative.
- He says AI can be either "the most horribly oppressive and dystopian thing we've ever done or ... the best thing we've ever done," and that the outcome is undecided and up to us.
- Because training models needs billions of dollars of compute, AI tends toward centralization. His counter: support many competing AI developers (Google, OpenAI, Facebook, Anthropic) and open-source AI.
- He calls locking AI down in one centralized effort "probably the most dangerous thing we can do." He argues that handing power to experts "acting for the common good" tends to end up like the Soviet Union, because centralized systems reward political skill and the winners are "the most effective Psychopaths." Markets force you to deliver value, which he contrasts with political systems where power can simply be captured.
- He is glad Facebook became "the unexpected hero" for open-source AI and says the thing to watch for is legislation that would prohibit open models. He allows that open-sourcing has legitimate risks and that the debate should stay open.
- He argues an AI's character derives from the society that builds it, so a free society should embed freedom and individual dignity in it.

## Narrative control, free speech, and a "meta narrative"

- Paul calls freedom of speech the most fundamental freedom, because without it everything else can be lied about. His example is the COVID lab-leak question, which he says he puts at about 95% likely and which people were censored and slandered for raising four years earlier.
- He credits Elon Musk buying Twitter as a major factor in the shift. In his telling, the old Twitter enforced the "institutional narrative" through bans and through the loudest voices being original blue checks aligned with it. Removing that class mattered more than is widely realized.
- He describes how language is used to shut down debate, by labeling a question a racist conspiracy theory so advertisers and platforms justify censorship. He recommends Orwell's *Homage to Catalonia*, in which Orwell's own faction was reclassified as fascist.
- He says most people drawn to these movements have good intentions and respond to incentives, so "it's bad ideas it's bad narratives" rather than bad people. He cites Malthusian zero-sum thinking and the one-child policy as examples.
- His proposed remedy is awareness: a news product that reports from a "meta narrative" view, laying both (or three) narratives side by side on roughly equal footing so people see them as stories. He says he has not pursued this enough.
- He points to Byron Katie's *Loving What Is*, where you identify a painful thought and write turnarounds, and says "you don't let go of the story, the story lets go of you." He calls this awareness "the beginning of alignment," meaning both human and AI alignment.
- On Elon Musk's effectiveness, he points to the Walter Isaacson book: Musk shows up at production bottlenecks and questions assumptions, such as a plastic battery part that turned out not to be needed ("the best part is no part"). Paul thinks that micromanagement gets into the culture.
