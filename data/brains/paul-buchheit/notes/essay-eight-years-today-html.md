## Accepting a hard present by accepting the past

Paul describes a practice he began while his daughter was in the hospital: "Accept. Accept. Accept." He applies it to everything, including a slow driver in traffic. His reasoning is "I can't even see the other timelines, maybe this one is somehow better."

- Repetition is the key to the practice: every day, every moment.
- Small annoyances (traffic) give him daily reps for the larger things (a baby who may not survive).
- He concludes that to be at peace with the present, we must be at peace with the past, because "the present is a product of the past."
- "What happened, happened." Learn to love the present moment.

## Trusting that the pieces fit without bargaining for meaning

Paul says he keeps looking for meaning in loss and has found little beyond acceptance. He notes that our lives are "mere brush strokes on the canvas of reality," so the big picture is hard to see.

- Trusting that it all fits together into something beautiful is, in his words, "the purest form of faith."
- Anything else is "a dangerous distraction."
- No contracts with God, no expectations of reward, "just trust."
- He admits that life is unfair: his brother Steve died of pancreatic cancer at 33, three months after diagnosis, and weeks later Gmail launched and that summer Google went public.

## Work as a place to focus the mind during grief

After his brother's funeral in March 2004, Paul went back to work. He says it can be wonderful to focus on something simple that he can control, like computers.

- Gmail launched a few weeks after Steve died (2004).
- Google's IPO followed that summer.
- Paul's point is that building can be a refuge, while also being a reminder that success and fairness are unrelated ("Life is unfair. Very unfair.").

## Startups and money are games; being good to people is the point

Paul worries that when he writes about startups and other interests, he may be communicating the wrong priorities.

- Investing money and creating new products "are wonderful games and can be a lot of fun, but it's important to remember that it's all just a game."
- What matters most is being good to each other and to ourselves.
- He argues you can't do only one: "we must do both or neither."
- "If we 'win', but have failed to do that, then we have lost. Winning is nothing."

## Push hard for its own sake, not for a future reward

Paul says pushing yourself and stretching your limits is healthy, but only when done for its own sake.

- The people who learn to love what they do are the ones who accomplish the most anyway.
- Those who push only for a future reward, or to avoid failure, "very often burn out, sometimes tragically."
- His direct plea: "Please don't do that."
- He closes with: "Please be good to each other, and your self."
