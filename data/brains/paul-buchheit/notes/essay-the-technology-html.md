## Seeking deep appeal and the danger of experience

- Paul advises startups to seek deep appeal: "create something that a few people love, even if most people don't get it right away." He notes the business is "defined by outliers."
- His first pattern is "I don't know anything." If he believes he already has the answer, he isn't open to learning larger truths. Founders should own their own programming rather than take his word as authority.
- Experience is a danger because it tells you an idea won't work. Naive, young founders often start the most successful companies because "they just don't know any better," and they're often too arrogant to listen to those who do. What didn't work before may work now, and what worked before may not work again.
- The best opportunities "live in our collective blind spots." They look like bad ideas or unimportant ones. If everyone could see the opportunity, someone else would already have taken it.
- Example: in 1997 Larry and Sergey tried to sell Google for a million dollars and found no buyer. The conventional wisdom was that search was neither important nor valuable.
- Dogma and ideology are worse than experience. They give you answers and put boundaries around your thinking. He suspects this is why more ideological societies are less innovative: you can't find missed opportunities if you can't wander outside conventional thinking.
- From the inside, dogma "looks like truth and reality." That includes logic and reason: "If you believe yourself to be a rational person, then you're in the trap."

## Kill the daemon processes: clearing the voices of doubt

- Paul's second pattern is "Kill all daemon processes." He uses the operating-system analogy: background programs that run out of control and eat memory and CPU, which is why a reboot often makes a machine work better.
- The brain equivalent is looping voices of doubt, anger and self-loathing. They may be internalized voices of parents, peers, the media or random people online, or they may pose as your own voice. They show up whenever you take a risk or move in a new direction.
- Building a new product often means years on something most people doubt. That is hard "with a head full of noise."
- Example: before Gmail launched, many people inside Google thought the project should be scrapped. One notable executive predicted it would never reach a million users.
- The practice:
  - Stop, observe your breath, and enjoy a moment of stillness.
  - The voices that keep interrupting the silence are the runaway processes. Keep dismissing them until none are left.
  - Use spare moments for this instead of "Flappy Bird or Facebook." It's the practice that matters, even when it doesn't always succeed. Walking in nature also helps.
- The voices resist because asserting their own importance is how they survive, and they keep coming back.

## "Yes, and thank you": turning setbacks into advantage

- The third pattern is a default response to events: "Yes, and thank you."
- Life rarely goes the way you want. Taking risks means plans often won't work out, and keeping life narrow and risk-free doesn't prevent that either. You can get angry and stuck, or accept and move forward, "assuming that whatever happened is somehow for the best."
- He calls this a great predictor of startup success. All startups face setbacks; some use them to their advantage, and others "keep slamming their head against the same wall."
- Example: Uber turned the London taxi strike into a huge growth opportunity.
- In his own life, many of the best things are rooted in some of the worst events, and he wouldn't have one without the other.
- It's about small decisions more than big ones. He treats every daily setback or disappointment as a gift, however improbable that seems at the time. He says the pattern has an "almost magical way of transforming reality and maintaining the forward flow of life."

## Choosing the more interesting path: how he picks startups

- The fourth pattern: choose the more interesting path. He says it is a big part of how he decides which startups to invest in.
- Justin.tv example (early 2007):
  - His first response was to laugh and ask if they were serious. The plan was for Justin Kan to attach a camera to his head and stream it live, 24/7.
  - He invested anyway because he was curious what would happen, and the founders seemed to genuinely believe they were on to something.
  - First came trolling. Then anyone could stream their life. Most of it was boring or possibly illegal, but video game streaming caught on, and the company became Twitch.tv to focus on competitive gaming.
  - Twitch's average daily viewer watches over 100 minutes per day, and it is the 4th largest source of US Internet traffic after Netflix, Google and Apple. He says he had no idea that would happen.
- Interestingness signals unexplored or under-explored territory. A known outcome isn't interesting, and pure randomness like gambling isn't either. Great startups sit in "a space of productive uncertainty."
- Same logic when he joined Google in 1999: he expected the much larger Alta Vista to squash it, but the people were very smart and he expected to learn a lot.
- Redefine success to include learning something interesting and "I can guarantee success." A startup with only one definition of success is "setting yourself up for failure."
- He calls it tragic how many people sacrifice their lives to a startup they don't care about, chasing external success they'll likely never reach.

## Love what you do, not "do what you love"

- The fifth pattern: "Love what you do." He calls "do what you love" mostly bad advice because it pushes people to grind through life chasing unattainable goals like being a movie star or a billionaire founder. Even those who make it often find the reality nothing like they imagined.
- "Do what you love is in the future. Love what you do is right now." It guides small moment-to-moment decisions and is less about changing what you do than how you do it.
- A goal-oriented, extrinsic mindset treats the time before completion as an annoying obstacle. On something difficult, uncertain and long, like building a company, that gambles away a big chunk of your life. It can also push you to pick smaller, more realistic goals, and it's unpleasant.
- His Google hours came from genuinely loving building things, not from pressure: "They weren't whipping us." He typically enjoyed at least 80% of his day.
- What you love isn't fixed. Example: he used to hate running. After reading a book arguing we are born to run, he tried running just for fun, focused on the quality of each step and dropped the goal-completion aspect. He learned to enjoy it quickly and now orients his whole relationship with fitness toward enjoyment.
- An intrinsic approach improves the quality of effort, which tends to bring greater extrinsic rewards too. Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation are best when pointed the same direction.
- Startups involve grinding work: "It's like having a baby. It's 5% cute, adorable moments, and 95% dirty diapers and vomit." The key to loving the unpleasant moments is meaning. If you believe in the mission, hard times take on a more heroic quality.
- Daily plans must be immediate and actionable (write code, talk to users), but he thinks a meaningful, inspiring vision matters too.

## A healthy disregard for the impossible

- The sixth pattern, borrowed from Google: "Maintain a healthy disregard for the impossible." He thinks Larry Page learned it as a kid at summer camp, and he sees it as the company's innovative spirit.
- Early Google was a tiny, unknown startup. He had to explain it as "Yahoo minus all of the features other than search," and people gave him a look that said "I'm sorry you can't get a real job." Inside, absurdly ambitious ideas made it feel like they'd take over the world.
- Examples from Larry:
  - Store and search the whole web in memory when the machines had only 1/4 GB of RAM. It was unrealistic then, but Moore's law moved fast and they did it, "but only because everyone's thinking was already oriented in that direction."
  - Self-driving cars that deliver hamburgers. That hasn't happened yet, but he bets it will.
- He finds potentially impossible goals more inspiring than realistic ones: "I'd rather fail at something awesome, than succeed at something inconsequential."
- Gmail example: when he decided to write the Gmail interface in Javascript, nearly everyone who knew Javascript or browsers said it was a bad idea, since it had been tried and ended in disaster. He was in an environment where doing impossible things was encouraged. After launch, "the impossible quickly became the new normal," changing how people think about web apps.

## Startups as machines for self-interest, and the "full-stack hacker" mission

- He sees startups as more than a clever way to make money: "machines for harnessing the fire of human self-interest, creating a self-sustaining reaction capable of rapidly transforming the world." Self-interest is often treated as dirty, but "NASA didn't get to the moon by vilifying gravity."
- Saying business is all about money is "like saying that rockets are all about rocket fuel." You need fuel to leave the launch pad, but that misses the machine's purpose. Among the truly significant founders he has known, "there's always a larger purpose."
- His impossible goal: for the first time we have the technology and resources to provide adequate food, housing, education and healthcare for everyone using only a fraction of labor and resources, which would end "wage-slavery." He says he chooses to work rather than has to, and believes everyone deserves that freedom. He argues it is also economically superior, so everyone would have more wealth.
- Many people are as brilliant as Steve Jobs, but likely lacked great parents, amazing teachers, an environment where innovation was the norm, or a Steve Wozniak down the street. "Economically, we don't need more jobs. We need more Steve Jobs." Setting everyone free enables outliers everywhere. The impossible part: "First we have to learn how to get along with each other, and with ourselves."
- He is looking for "full-stack hackers" who understand that the same patterns exist everywhere. "Capitalism is a technology," like the internal combustion engine, valuable but improvable, and so are government and religion.
- Founders must start small and work with the grain of what is. The path is never obvious. Examples:
  - The personal computer was first dismissed as a toy.
  - Instagram looks like photo filters, but helping people see the world through others' eyes is a step toward world peace, and it grew to over 200 million users in under four years.
- Don't be discouraged by people who dismiss your work as trivial because it isn't curing cancer or going to Mars. He quotes Feynman: "The worthwhile problems are the ones you can really solve or help solve, the ones you can really contribute something to." The patterns aim at an independent mind free of other people's limits, so you can judge for yourself what is worthwhile and act with conviction.
