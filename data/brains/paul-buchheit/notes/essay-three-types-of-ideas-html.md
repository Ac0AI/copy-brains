## Obvious ideas split into two groups that are hard or overhyped

Paul divides product ideas into three categories. The first two are the "obviously good" ones.

- **Category 1:** obviously good ideas that are very difficult to implement. Examples: efficient cold fusion, flying cars, other sci-fi ideas.
- **Category 2:** obviously "good" ideas that seem possible but haven't happened yet. Examples: video phones and HDTV for a long time.
- He thinks category 2 happens when people get excited about technology and overestimate the benefits (and possibly underestimate the cost). On video phones: "I just don't care that much about having a video phone."
- Category 1 only makes sense for a "super-genius researcher" dedicating a life to a really important problem they may never solve.
- Category 2 only makes sense if you find a clever way to dramatically cut implementation cost, as Skype did for the video phone.

## Category 3: "bad" ideas are indistinguishable from great ones in advance

- Category 3 is "bad" ideas. Many are truly bad, but some turn out in hindsight to have been very good, and the two are hard to tell apart without hindsight.
- Examples he gives of ideas that looked bad:
  - The personal computer: "why would anyone need a computer?"
  - Google: "there are already too many search engines, and besides, search engines don't make money."
  - Blogger: "can't you just use Geocities, and besides, are there really that many people with something worth saying?"
  - More recent and still controversial: Facebook and Twitter.
- He knowingly groups "truly bad" and "seemed bad but turned out very good" together because they can't be told apart beforehand.
- His core claim: "The best product ideas are often found in the 'bad ideas' category!"

## Why category 3 is the "low hanging fruit"

- Outside the super-genius research case and the cost-collapse case, he says the real low-hanging fruit is likely in the "bad ideas" category.
- The price is dealing with "annoying skeptics and haters" who say you're wasting your time.
- The payoff is that sometimes you create something hugely important, or at least moderately successful, and the skeptics don't.

## Experts are bad at picking winners (the Google Video lesson)

- He says everyone thinks they can tell the good ideas, but observation suggests they can't.
- Some people are better at picking winners than others, but "at best they have maybe 50% accuracy". He has seen very smart people, including at Google, be very wrong.
- Example: when "upload videos and put them on the web" Google Video was first developed, nearly everyone at Google, including Paul, was skeptical that anything worthwhile would be uploaded. The common prediction was "movies and porn."
- The skeptics were completely wrong about the lack of worthwhile content. He calls uploaded video "one of the most important developments of the past several years."
- Google Video had an "incredibly bad upload process" (it required installing a Windows client). YouTube, started after Google Video launched, took over.
- He believes that mistake was partly caused by the negative expectations around the idea.

## Test ideas instead of debating them

- Lesson: "Instead of endlessly debating whether an idea is good or not, we should find faster and cheaper ways of testing them."
- This is why open systems like the Internet or market economies develop faster than closed systems like communism or big companies: individuals and small groups can build new things without anyone's approval.

## Your idea is worth little until you build it

- Lesson: "Your product idea is generally worthless, because to most people it's indistinguishable [from] other bad ideas."
- Very few people will buy, steal or take your idea for free, because they already have their own bad ideas they like better.
- Paul says the way to make an idea valuable is to demonstrate it's not bad by building a real product. This also demonstrates your ability to execute.
