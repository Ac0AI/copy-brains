## How Paul got into building and why he avoided big companies

- Paul's father was an engineer at Xerox who loved building things. Christmas presents were tools, and Paul has a photo of himself at 4 or 5 in footie pajamas with a bench vise.
- His father disliked working at Xerox, so Paul grew up wanting to avoid big companies and to create something new through startups.
- At 15 he bought a computer and taught himself C from a book, with no internet and no one to ask.
- What hooked him about software: "you really can create your own world." Compile, run, crash, reboot and start over is far faster than waiting for something biological to grow.
- After graduating from Case Western Reserve in 1998, he took a job at Intel in Santa Clara because startups seemed to be in California. After about a year it felt "drained and lifeless" because it was just a big company.

## Finding Google and how to evaluate a startup job

- Paul had used Linux since 1993 and wanted a startup built on it, when serious businesses ran on Windows NT. He found Google through Slashdot's coverage of its Linux clusters.
- He applied to about six startups and got one offer, from Google. He became roughly the 23rd employee.
- Interviewing is two-way. Google's interviewers asked smart questions, which showed they were smart people.
- His advice: "if they ask you dumb questions, like don't take the job because it means they're hiring dumb people."

## Starting Gmail and the iterative build method

- Paul first tried a web-based email in 1996, driven by the belief that information would live on the internet and the desktop would be a "stateless cache."
- The official project began in summer 2001. Larry and VP of engineering Wayne Rosing (named "Wayne Rosen" in the transcript) had removed the managers because Google felt too slow. Paul was told to build "some kind of email thing," and that was the whole spec.
- He believes in iterative product design, partly because he gets bored if he goes too long without shipping.
- Version zero took one day. He reused his Google Groups (Usenet search) code and pointed it at his own email, then emailed it to Google's engineers.
- Feedback was that it was useful but would be better with their own email. Version two indexed everyone's email, segmented by user.
- From there he shipped something new almost daily, driven by actual user requests: reply, then compose, and so on.
- He does not build exactly what users ask for. Users often name the wrong solution. One user wanted to reply from inside the inbox, but the real problem was that the product was too slow. After he made it fast, people stopped asking.
- His rule: "it's important to listen to users, but not to believe everything that they say."

## What Gmail changed: speed, storage and search

- Pre-Gmail email was split between desktop clients like Outlook, which downloaded mail to the laptop, and webmail like Hotmail and Yahoo, which was slow and clunky. Paul says people used Yahoo for spam sign-ups.
- Storage was tiny: Yahoo gave 4 MB and Hotmail 2 MB. Some corporate quotas were around 30 MB.
- His idea was that you should be able to keep all your email forever. Good search replaces manual folder hierarchies, which people never maintained anyway.
- Gmail gave 1,000 MB for free. Early adopters included reporters, who want to keep everything for research, while their employers' email admins at places like the New York Times said storage was hard. Gmail "completely wrecked that narrative."
- The whole app was written in JavaScript, which then had a reputation for being slow. The team was obsessive about measuring performance.
- Paul made sure the inbox rendered in under 100 milliseconds, the rough threshold of human perception, so everything feels instantaneous.
- This paradigm was later named Ajax. When Gmail launched, people disassembled its JavaScript to see how it worked. It raised expectations for web apps and for email.
- Google used Gmail internally from day one, so it had to handle many gigabytes of mail before launch.

## Launch: April 1st, the invite system and going free

- Gmail launched on April 1, 2004. It was good enough that many people thought it was a prank. AP reporter Mike Liedtke (spelled "Lindkey" in the transcript) got emails telling him he'd been tricked by a Google April Fools' joke.
- Paul says it needed no marketing: it was "two orders of magnitude better" than competitors (1,000 MB versus 2 MB).
- The product was not quite ready. Code was still being written on launch day, and there was only hardware for about 10,000 users.
- He created the invite system to control growth, because an open launch would have crashed in the first hour. Internally it was spun as a growth tactic, since limiting growth was unpopular.
- It worked as one. Invites created exclusivity and status. People traded them, and they sold on eBay for around $100. A Gmail address became "something professional" compared with a Hotmail or Yahoo address.
- Why free: "People love free." Charging loses a huge fraction of users.
- Strategically, Google's search business needed to stay close to the user. Some people inside Google opposed Gmail as a distraction. Paul compares it to Chrome and Android: owning the user relationship means people don't switch the moment a better product appears.

## Leaving Google and FriendFeed

- After his first child was born 100 days premature in 2005, Paul spent about 3 months in the hospital and took roughly 7 months of leave. Returning, he found Google felt like a big company again, with meetings full of people he didn't know. He left in 2007.
- He had followed Y Combinator since Paul Graham announced the Summer Founders Program in 2005. In fall 2005 he emailed Graham to offer help, started attending YC dinners, and made his first angel investment in a winter 2006 YC company. He has invested in roughly 400 YC startups.
- In 2007 he founded FriendFeed with friends from Google, while still angel investing. He calls doing both a "red flag," though it worked.
- FriendFeed was an early social network competing with Twitter and Facebook. Paul admits it never found enough differentiation from them.
- The like button launched on FriendFeed in October 2007. Facebook added it about a year later.
- Facebook acquired FriendFeed in 2009. Paul says it was a team deal: the team was highly regarded, mostly smart Google hires, and shipped fast. FriendFeed was good at generating buzz and attention but not at growing users, with a small base of passionate users.
- Co-founder Bret Taylor became Facebook's CTO and redesigned its API, later became CEO of Salesforce for a time, and now runs an AI startup. Paul stayed at Facebook about a year and again concluded that big companies weren't for him.

## The like button and cold-start social networks

- A new social network feels like "talking into an empty room," so FriendFeed wanted to encourage interaction.
- The like was conceived as a "one-click comment." Often you appreciate a share but have nothing to say, and a like is easier than a comment.
- Voting already existed on sites like Reddit. What distinguishes a like is that it carries a name and is a specific social gesture: "Paul liked your post" tells the poster who responded.
- Three friends liking your post has more social meaning than "three upvotes."

## Paul's view of AI

- Paul says the most urgent question is a vision for the future of humanity, not "a nihilistic pursuit of graphs that go up and to the right."
- He ranks the major technological advances as fire, language (coordination), agriculture (scaling to cities of millions), and AI as the fourth, which he says changes who we are as a species.
- With AI, "we're literally inventing kind of our successors."
- It could be very good or very bad. His aspiration is to nudge it toward expanding freedom, potential and individual liberties, and away from "the ultimate dystopian totalitarian technology."
