## Product-market fit matters more than team, tech, or design

Paul Buchheit agrees with Marc Andreessen that market is the most important factor in a startup's success. Andreessen's argument: in a great market, "the market pulls product out of the startup." The first viable product wins, and the product only has to "basically work." Paul's version of the point is that a product nobody wants is not a great product, and a team that keeps building something nobody wants is not a great team.

He draws two lessons from it:
- **Even the best people fail if people don't want the product.** The smartest, most experienced, best-connected team can build the most stunningly designed and technically advanced product and still fail. His example is the Segway.
- **Ordinary people can win by building the right product.** A team that isn't the smartest, with a product that is "kind of ugly and broken," can still be very successful. His examples are YouTube and MySpace.

## Why strong teams build the wrong product: overrating their own skills

Paul says good teams fail because of attitude. They overestimate how much their own skills matter:
- Engineers think success is about fancy technology and complex engineering (he adds "hello Google").
- Designers think it is about beautiful design.
- MBAs think it is about knowing the right people, or spreadsheets.

He says especially smart or successful teams can be worse. They tend to be arrogant and overconfident, so they question their assumptions less.

His examples of the wrong attitude:
- When Google acquired YouTube, many people inside Google were flabbergasted: "But they have no technology!?" Paul's reply: you only need enough technology to make the product work, and any more means you probably have the wrong priorities.
- Similar complaints get aimed at Facebook, MySpace and other popular sites. People say MySpace or even Google has "no design" or "bad design". Paul says they have enough design, or the right design, for their users.

## The right attitude is humility: pay attention and iterate

Paul's answer to the wrong attitude is humility. However smart, successful and qualified you are, "you simply don't know what you're doing." Nobody else does either, though some are foolish enough to think they do, and that is why you can beat them.

His humble approach to product design:
- Pay attention. Notice which things are working and which aren't.
- Experiment and iterate.
- Question your assumptions, and remember you are wrong about a lot of things.
- Watch for the signals.
- Lose your technical and design snobbery. "Whatever works, works."

## MySpace custom profiles: noticing an accident and keeping it

Paul uses MySpace's custom profile layouts as his example of paying attention. He is "pretty sure" they were an accident:
- MySpace didn't properly escape the text users put on their profiles.
- That let users include arbitrary HTML and CSS in their pages.
- This is a common bug. Most teams would have fixed it and moved on, and really great engineers wouldn't have had the bug at all.
- MySpace instead noticed the feature was popular and found a way to preserve it.
- The result is mostly ugly but extremely popular.

He says "accidental" isn't the same as "random". There are clues all around, and you need to watch more closely to catch them.

## Release early and iterate: how Gmail started

For web products, Paul calls release early and iterate a "very powerful technique." The sooner you test ideas, the sooner you can fix them.

His account of Gmail:
- He wrote the first version in one day, and it was not very impressive. He stuffed his own email into the Google Groups (Usenet) indexing engine.
- He sent it to a few people. They said it was somewhat useful but would be better searching their email instead of his. That was version two.
- After that release, people wanted to respond to email too. That was version three.
- That loop ran for a couple of years inside Google before the public release.

## Startups must launch much sooner than Gmail did, as with FriendFeed

Gmail could iterate privately because Google had hundreds of internal users. Startups don't, so Paul says they need to release to the world much sooner.

His FriendFeed example:
- The product was semi-released (private beta) in October 2007, when it was only about two months old.
- It was 99.9% written by two people, Bret and Jim.
- Paul says the product by February 2008 was much better than what they would have built without launching, because "we have users, and we listen to them, and we see which things work and which don't."

His summary line: "Find the gradient, then follow it."
