## Picking people: the "10% of a classmate" test

Warren asks listeners to imagine they could own 10% of one classmate's lifetime earnings, and must also short 10% of another's. He argues the winner is not the one with the highest grades.

- What separates big winners are self-made qualities: "Integrity, honesty, generosity, being willing to do more than your share."
- The short side fails on character, not grades: taking credit for work they didn't do, cutting corners, being unreliable.
- When hiring he looks for three things: intelligence, initiative or energy, and integrity. Without integrity, "the first two will kill you", because you want a dishonest person "lazy and dumb," not smart and energetic.
- Character is a habit pattern you choose. Warren quotes "the chains of habit are too light to be felt until they're too heavy to be broken," and notes people at 50 or 60 are often imprisoned by self-destructive habits.
- Ben Franklin and Ben Graham decided in their young teens whom they admired and why, then behaved that way.

## Work for someone you admire

Warren's career advice is to go work for the person or institution you admire most, not for a resume line or slightly higher starting pay.

- He told a Harvard student planning a consulting stint to "round out" his resume: "Your plan sounds to me a lot like saving up sex for your old age."
- Following this advice, he says, means you "jump out of bed in the morning" and have fun.
- He jokes that Harvard's dean called later to say the students he spoke to were all becoming self-employed.

## Circle of competence

Warren says he only expects to make money in businesses whose long-term economics he understands. That is different from knowing what a product does.

- Wrigley's gum is within his circle: he knows the Internet won't change which gum people chew, so he can roughly picture the business 10 years out.
- A big industry does not mean profitable investors. There were 2,000 auto companies at one point, and only three basically survived. Studebaker, Nash, Kelvinator and Hudson were once in the Dow and "got creamed."
- Easier than picking winners is identifying losers: in 1905 the smart move was to short horses (about 20 million then, about 4 million now).
- Airlines: over 4,100 airplane companies in the 1920s and 30s. "If there had been a capitalist at Kitty Hawk he should have shot Orville down." At the end of 1991 the industry's aggregate earnings since Kitty Hawk were less than zero, even as passengers grew every year.
- TV sets and radios: huge growth, almost no US manufacturers left or profitable.
- Coca-Cola, by contrast, now sells roughly 1.1 billion 8-ounce servings a day, a durable economic franchise.
- On the Internet and tech: he enjoys the technology and orders books from Amazon, but "I don't know who's going to win, and unless I know who's going to win I'm not interested in investing."
- The size of the circle doesn't matter; knowing its perimeter and staying inside it does. He quotes Tom Watson Sr.: "I'm no genius, but I'm smart in spots, and I stay around those spots."

## Intrinsic value

Warren defines intrinsic value as all the cash a business will give you between now and Judgment Day, discounted at the proper rate.

- A bond has its cash flows printed on it; a stock certificate doesn't, so the analyst's job is to turn the stock into a bond by estimating future payouts.
- It applies equally to a farm, an apartment house, oil in the ground, a new carpet machine at Shaw, or the whole of Coca-Cola.
- It does not depend on analyst recommendations, trading volume or chart patterns.
- Esop's "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" is incomplete: you need to know how many birds are in the bush, when you will get them, and how sure you are. At 15% rates you need two birds in 5 years to beat the one in hand; at 3% you can wait 20 years.
- Test for any stock: if you were buying the whole company at its market price, could it distribute enough cash soon enough to make sense at current interest rates? If you can't answer, you're gambling. He can't answer it for Internet companies, so he stays away.
- A hypothetical Internet company with 100 million shares at $100 is a $10 billion business, and would need to produce roughly $700-800 million next year and grow after that.
- Berkshire has never distributed anything to shareholders, but its ability to distribute rises as the value of its businesses rises.

## Mistakes: cigar butts, Berkshire and omission

Warren is open about his errors and says the biggest are mistakes of omission, which never show up in conventional accounting.

- Cigar butts: following Ben Graham, he bought statistically cheap stocks with "one puff left." Berkshire Hathaway traded below working capital, so he bought it, and "20 years later I was still running a lousy business." Berkshire was a mistake as a platform; he'd have done better starting a fresh entity and buying better businesses.
- Quality over price: "I'd rather buy a wonderful business at a fair price than a fair business at a wonderful price." Time is the friend of a wonderful business and the enemy of a lousy one.
- US Air: he bought preferred in 1989 and the company went into the red as soon as his check cleared. He jokes about an 800 number to call at 3 a.m. to talk him out of airline stocks.
- A Sinclair filling station at about age 20: he put in $2,000 of roughly $10,000 and lost it all. At Berkshire's compounding, the opportunity cost is about $6 billion.
- Biggest mistakes: things within his circle of competence that he passed on while "sucking my thumb." Example: Fannie Mae, when it had troubles roughly 20 years earlier and could have been bought for practically nothing, cost Berkshire at least $5 billion. Microsoft isn't a similar regret because it was outside his circle.
- The "punch card" idea: imagine you got only 20 punches for the rest of your life, one per financial decision. You'd think hard, make fewer and bigger decisions, and probably not use all 20. He warns against dabbling, especially in bull markets and with online trading.

## Selling, holding and Berkshire's promises to partners

Warren explains his approach to selling and says he treats shareholders as partners for life.

- Early on he had more ideas than money (he combed Moody's manual page by page, found stocks at one or two times earnings) and sold his least favorite holding to fund a new one. Now Berkshire has "more money than ideas," generating at least $5 billion of cash a year, about $100 million a week.
- Wholly owned businesses are not for sale at any price: Seas Candy, the Buffalo News and Borsheims won't be sold even at three times value. Selling would be "like selling one of my children."
- He puts this in the "economic principles of Berkshire" so partners know his thinking in advance and aren't disappointed.
- Stocks are sold mainly if management disappoints him badly or the business's economics change significantly, not simply because the price looks high.
- Marriage analogy: a lasting marriage depends on low expectations, so he wants partners to start with low expectations.
- Berkshire has 112,000 employees and, he says, only 13.8 people at headquarters; units are kept small for nimbleness and customer responsiveness.

## Market cycles, temperament and the Fed

Warren says markets swing on human emotion while the economy keeps growing, and that temperament, not brains, produces investing results.

- US GDP per capita rose about 610% in the 20th century, up every decade including the 1930s. Yet the Dow went from 66 to 71 between 1900 and 1921, then to 381 by September 1929, back to about 180 by 1948, to about 1,000 by 1965, flat at 865 in 1981 versus 874 in 1964, then up enormously. By his count, about 43 and 3/4 years of big bull markets and 56 and 1/4 years of stagnation.
- Investors look in the rearview mirror: "I made money last year, so I'll borrow and make more." They get greedy when others are greedy and fearful when others are fearful.
- Pension funds put 10-plus percent of new money in stocks in 1970 but a record-low 9% in 1978 when stocks were far cheaper.
- At 21 he couldn't sell Kansas City Life stock to the company's own agent, though it traded under three times earnings (a roughly 35% yield) and its policies assumed 2% interest.
- "If you can detach yourself temperamentally from the crowd you get very rich and you won't have to be very bright." Bubbles end: quoting Herb Stein, "anything that can't go on forever will end."
- Fed: he likes Greenspan, but says the Fed's brakes are stronger than its gas pedal (Volcker broke inflation by braking). Japan shows zero rates don't guarantee stimulus. Economics has hundreds of changing variables, and "history doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes."

## Small business, philanthropy and taxes

Warren says small companies can beat large ones in many fields, and describes his giving philosophy.

- Scale matters in some businesses, but Walmart beat Sears despite worse credit, supplier access and real estate, and Southwest Airlines didn't need to be biggest.
- Nebraska Furniture Mart: Rose Blumkin arrived from Russia in 1921 speaking no English, saved $500 by 1937, and built the largest home-furnishing store in the world on 72 acres doing about $325 million in one location. She relied on customer focus and low prices, never read a balance sheet, and worked until she was 103. Her word, he says, was worth more than an audit. Bill Child at Utah's furniture business and Shaw in carpet are other examples.
- Philanthropy: nearly all his net worth goes to a foundation after he and his wife die. He picked few, high-grade trustees (not 30), gave no specific instructions because their judgment above ground beats his from "6 feet underground," and wants them to take on hard problems that lack a natural funding constituency. He praises Bill Gates for aiming to save as many lives per dollar as possible.
- He credits luck: born in 1930 in the US, where odds were "50 to 1 against" it, wired for asset allocation in a society that pays for it, and says the money should go back to society.
- Taxes: legal benefits such as low-income housing credits are fine, aggressive shelters bought with shopped legal opinions are close to fraud, and Berkshire won't move to Bermuda to avoid taxes.
