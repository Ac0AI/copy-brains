## Why 1999 was Warren's worst year, and what he blames

- Berkshire's 1999 net worth gain was $358 million, or 0.5% per share, the worst absolute performance of his tenure and the worst relative to the S&P. Over 35 years per-share book value grew from $19 to $37,987, 24.0% compounded.
- He puts the blame on himself: "My 'one subject' is capital allocation, and my grade for 1999 most assuredly is a D."
- The cause was the equity portfolio. Several large investees lagged the market on disappointing operating results. He still likes the businesses but says a quick recovery is "no sure thing."
- Relative results are what concern him: "Over time, bad relative numbers will produce unsatisfactory absolute results."
- Stock and business can diverge. In 1998 the stock outperformed the business. In 1999 the business did much better than the stock. Over time the stock must roughly match the business.
- He and Charlie expect intrinsic value gains over the next decade to "modestly exceed" the S&P's. He backs this with over 99% of his net worth in Berkshire. He and his wife have never sold a share.
- Large superiority over the S&P is a thing of the past. Businesses and stocks were cheaper then, and the capital base was much smaller, allowing a wider range of opportunities.

## Intrinsic value versus book value

- Intrinsic value is "a key, though far from precise" measure used in buying businesses and stocks.
- He splits Berkshire into two parts: per-share investments (including cash) and pre-tax operating earnings per share with all investment income excluded.
  - Investments per share: $45 (1969), $577 (1979), $7,200 (1989), $47,339 (1999). Growth was 25.4% annually over 1969-1999.
  - Operating earnings per share excluding investment income: $4.39, $13.07, $108.86, then a 1999 loss of $(458.55), caused mainly by a huge General Re underwriting loss.
- Book value was $57.8 billion, which he says intrinsic value "far exceeds."
- The goodwill amortization charge (about $500 million a year) widens the gap between book and intrinsic value. This is accounting, unrelated to true economic goodwill, which "increases in most years."
- See's and Buffalo News are worth fifteen to twenty times their carrying values. The goal is to keep widening that spread at every subsidiary.
- Look-through earnings give a better picture than reported earnings. They are operating earnings, plus Berkshire's share of investees' retained operating earnings, less an allowance for tax (14% here).
  - 1999 figures: $707 million of undistributed investee earnings, minus $99 million hypothetical tax, plus $1,318 million reported operating earnings, for $1,926 million.
- He prefers investees retaining earnings when they can reinvest at high rates of return: "why should we want them paid out?"

## Managers: autonomy, no contracts, owner mentality

- Many Berkshire managers are independently wealthy and work because they love their businesses. There are no contracts.
- There are no budgets to approve, no show-and-tell presentations and no capital expenditure dictums from Omaha. Managers are asked to run the company "as if these are the sole asset of their families and will remain so for the next century."
- Warren and Charlie treat managers the way they would want to be treated in their position.
- R. C. Willey example: Bill Child built the business from $250,000 of sales in 1954 to $342 million in 1999 while keeping stores closed on Sundays for religious reasons.
  - Warren was skeptical of taking a no-Sunday policy to Boise, but told Bill to follow his business judgment and convictions.
  - Bill insisted on buying the land and building the store himself (about $9 million). Berkshire would buy it at his cost if it succeeded, and owe him nothing if it failed.
  - The store succeeded. Berkshire paid Bill his cost, and he refused interest on the capital tied up for two years.
  - Warren says he has never heard of a manager at another public company behaving similarly.
- Chuck Huggins at See's: Berkshire paid $25 million in 1972 and the business has earned $857 million pre-tax since, with little additional capital. 1999 operating margin was a record 24%.

## How to evaluate an insurance company: float and its cost

- Three determinants: (1) the amount of float, (2) its cost, and (3) "most critical of all," the long-term outlook for both.
- Float is "money we hold but don't own." Premiums arrive before losses are paid, sometimes years later, and the insurer invests the money.
- An underwriting loss is the cost of float. The business has value if cost of float is below what the company would otherwise pay for funds. It is "a lemon" if cost exceeds market rates.
- Caution: loss costs must be estimated, so insurers have "enormous latitude" in reporting results. Reserving errors, usually innocent but sometimes not, can be huge and flow straight into earnings. In 1999 several insurers made reserve adjustments that "made a mockery" of previously reported earnings.
- Berkshire float: $20 million (1967), $1,508 million (1987), $22,754 million (1998), $25,298 million (1999), with GEICO at $3,444 million, General Re $15,166 million, other reinsurance $6,285 million and other primary $403 million.
- In 1999 Berkshire had a $1.4 billion underwriting loss, a float cost of 5.8%. About $400 million of that loss came from business expected to deliver exceptional float for the next decade. Cumulative results through 1998 were an underwriting profit.
- Ajit Jain, super-cat reinsurance: he has the intelligence to rate risks, the realism to skip those he can't evaluate, the courage to write huge policies at an appropriate premium, and the discipline to reject even small risks when the premium is inadequate. His business holds $6.3 billion of float with an underwriting profit over his tenure. Warren notes it is volatile and Berkshire has benefited from good luck.
- General Re: 1999 business was badly underpriced. Incentive compensation at General Re and Cologne is now tied to float growth and cost of float, "the same variables that determine value for owners."
- The "other primary" insurers delivered a $192 million underwriting profit over five years.

## GEICO: spending on growth, compensation design, economics

- GEICO policies in force rose from 2,011,055 (1993) to 4,328,900 (1999). New auto policies rose from 346,882 to 1,648,095. Tony Nicely was handed the keys in 1996 and asked to run it as if he owned 100%.
- Marketing spend: $33 million in 1995, $242 million in 1999, with $300-$350 million planned for 2000. Counselors grew from 652 to 2,631. Warren would "happily commit $1 billion annually" if the business could be handled smoothly and the last dollar produced new business at an attractive cost.
- Acquisition costs are rising: media rates up and diminishing returns on ads, partly offset by a better closure ratio. GEICO's renewal operating costs are the lowest among broad-based national auto insurers. Competitors "may copy our model, but they will be unable to replicate our economics."
- Customer value: each household sends about $1,100 a year. In 1999 GEICO added 766,256 customers and gained $590 million of cash from operating earnings and float growth. He notes "no Internet economics here." Market share rose from 2.7% to 4.1% in three years.
- Referrals are the best source of new business. He guesses about $50 million a year of advertising could hold the policy count, but advertising beyond that is an optional investment he considers "the best investment Berkshire can make."
- Compensation uses only two variables: percentage growth in policyholders and earnings on "seasoned" policies (more than one year old). New-business costs are excluded so associates aren't penalized for growth. 1999 profit sharing was 28.4% of salary ($113.3 million).
- Warren predicted auto insurer margins would fall about three percentage points in 2000. Rate increases are slow because of regulation and contract terms, and reserve releases from earlier overestimates have largely dried up.

## Aviation, furniture and how Berkshire finds acquisitions

- FlightSafety: capital-intensive (a simulator can cost up to $15 million, with 222 owned). One trainee at a time per simulator, so operating margins must be high to earn a reasonable return. 1999 capex was $215 million.
- EJA (NetJets): customers own the aircraft, so modest pre-tax margins can still produce good returns on equity. Customers own over $2 billion of planes, with $4.2 billion on order. Recurring revenue rose 46%. Scale matters: "Buy a fraction, get a fleet." EJA sells planes from five suppliers, while its largest rivals are tied to manufacturers' own aircraft. About 65% of new owners come from referrals. Everyone, including Warren, pays the same price ("most favored nations").
- Warren's acquisition strategy is "simply to wait for the phone to ring," typically because a prior seller recommended Berkshire to a friend.
- He mocks auction "books" projecting earnings precisely for years while the authors won't forecast their own firm's next month. On Scott Fetzer, which a bank failed to sell in 1985, he wrote to CEO Ralph Schey and had a deal within a week. Charlie told the banker: "I'll pay $2.5 million not to read it."
- Furniture chain: Nebraska Furniture Mart (1983), R. C. Willey (1995), Star Furniture (1997), Jordan's (1999). Jordan's has the highest sales per square foot of any major US furniture operation. The Tatelmans paid employees 50¢ per hour worked, $9 million from their own pockets.
- MidAmerican Energy: about $2 billion for an 11% fixed-income security plus common and exchangeable preferred. This gives Berkshire just under 10% of the votes but about 76% of the equity, structured around the Public Utility Holding Company Act. Walter Scott and family invest about $280 million in cash alongside. Warren says more utility commitments, possibly large, may follow.
- Jordan's and MidAmerican were both bought for cash, issuing no Berkshire shares, which Warren and Charlie "vastly prefer."

## Acquisition accounting, goodwill and deal structure

- Pooling versus purchase: managements like pooling because it avoids a goodwill account and its annual charge. Warren agrees with the FASB that most mergers are purchases ("ask employees severed from their jobs which company was the conqueror").
- He argues goodwill amortization is usually spurious because economic goodwill often grows. Depreciation and similar charges describe something that truly occurs. See's economic goodwill has grown for 78 years.
- His proposed rule: record the purchase at fair value, create the goodwill asset, don't amortize it, and write it down only if it becomes impaired. Apply it retroactively. He predicts managers would then choose cash or stock on real shareholder consequences, not reported earnings.
- Tax structure: Berkshire bought Jordan's assets for cash, so goodwill is tax-deductible over 15 years. This raises cash but lowers reported earnings. A stock purchase for cash gives no deduction.
- A stock-for-stock deal is the worst for the acquirer, often with no tax basis step-up. Berkshire has done some inefficient structures at sellers' insistence, but never to make its figures look better.

## Circle of competence, market valuation and buybacks

- Berkshire owns no tech stocks. Warren and Charlie can't tell which firms have durable competitive advantage. Predicting long-term economics in fast-changing industries is beyond their perimeter. "If we have a strength, it is in recognizing when we are operating well within our circle of competence and when we are approaching the perimeter."
- If others claim predictive skill and the market validates it, "we neither envy nor emulate them."
- Price versus business: "If the choice is between a questionable business at a comfortable price or a comfortable business at a questionable price, we much prefer the latter. What really gets our attention, however, is a comfortable business at a comfortable price." In 1999 he felt better about the businesses than their stocks, so he didn't add.
- Market expectations: he never forecasts the market, but sees profits tracking GDP at about 3% real growth plus 2% inflation (the TIPS spread), so roughly 5%. Valuations are unlikely to climb much beyond that, so equity returns will be far below what investors expect. He expects a severe adjustment, especially in speculative sectors. "Fools give you reasons, wise men never try."
- Buyback conditions: repurchase only when (1) funds exceed the business's needs (maintenance and value-creating growth spending) and (2) the stock trades below conservatively calculated intrinsic value. Shareholders must also have the information to estimate that value.
- In the 1970s Warren looked for heavy repurchasers as a sign of undervaluation and shareholder-oriented management.
- Now buybacks are often to prop up the price. "Buying dollar bills for $1.10 is not good business for those who stick around."
- Repurchasing to offset option exercises is "buy high, sell low." Issuing and repurchasing decisions should each stand on their own.
- Math: repurchasing 2% of shares at a 25% discount to intrinsic value adds at most about ½% to per-share value.
- Berkshire will buy only below intrinsic value, never to stem a decline, and won't talk the stock up or down. A continuing holder should want the stock to fall and trade enough volume for Berkshire to buy a lot.
