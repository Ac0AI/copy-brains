## Book value record and what Warren treats as the right performance yardstick

- Per-share book value rose 13% in 2010. Over 46 years it grew from $19 to $95,453, a 20.2% compounded annual rate.
- Warren says managers of other people's money should set performance goals at the start. Otherwise they "shoot the arrow of performance and then paint the bull's-eye around wherever it lands."
- Berkshire's stated goal: grow per-share intrinsic value faster than the S&P 500 including dividends. If it can't over time, "we have done nothing for our investors," who could have owned an index fund.
- Intrinsic value can't be calculated precisely. He and Charlie, asked separately, would give two different answers. So Berkshire tracks book value, an understated proxy whose premium to true value seldom swings wildly year to year.
- Size caps returns. "The bountiful years... will never return." Going forward he hopes to beat the S&P by "several points," which would mean better relative results in bad markets and poorer ones in strong markets.
- Yearly figures are "neither to be ignored nor viewed as all-important." Investment and operating decisions don't run on the earth's orbit. GEICO spent $900 million on advertising in 2010 to win policyholders who bring no immediate profit, and he would spend twice that if it could be done productively, even at the cost of short-term results.

## The three pillars of intrinsic value, and the "what will they do with the money" test

- **Investments:** stocks, bonds and cash equivalents totaled $158 billion at market value at yearend 2010. Insurance float funds $66 billion of that. Float is "free" if underwriting breaks even. Per-share investments grew 19.9% a year over 40 years, but only 6.6% a year in 2000-2010 as capital shifted to buying operating businesses.
- **Operating earnings from non-insurance, non-investment businesses (68 companies):** per-share pre-tax earnings went from $2.87 in 1970 to $5,926.04 in 2010, 21.0% compounded. Berkshire's stock price rose 22.1% a year over the same span.
- **Normal earning power** (no mega-catastrophe, a business climate better than 2010 but weaker than 2005-06): about $17 billion pre-tax and $12 billion after-tax, excluding capital gains.
- **Third, subjective element:** how well retained earnings will be redeployed. "Some companies will turn these retained dollars into fifty-cent pieces, others into two-dollar bills."
- An outside investor "stands by helplessly" while management reinvests his share of earnings. If the CEO is good, reinvestment prospects add to value. If the CEO's talents or motives are suspect, today's value must be discounted.
- His example: a dollar in the hands of the CEOs of Sears Roebuck or Montgomery Ward in the late 1960s had a very different destiny from a dollar entrusted to Sam Walton.
- Market price and intrinsic value "often follow very different paths – sometimes for extended periods – but eventually they meet."

## Why Berkshire's capital allocation is an advantage, and the textile mistake

- Most companies reinvest only within their own industry, a universe that is "tiny and quite inferior" to what the wider world offers. Competition for the few opportunities makes the seller king.
- Berkshire faces no institutional restraints. Warren and Charlie are "limited only by our ability to understand the likely future of a possible acquisition." Once that hurdle is cleared, they can compare any opportunity against many others.
- They also compare businesses against marketable securities, which most managements don't. "Often, businesses are priced ridiculously high against what can likely be earned from investments in stocks or bonds. At such moments, we buy securities and bide our time."
- Cash from low-reinvestment businesses such as See's Candies and Business Wire was used as part of the stake to buy BNSF.
- His own error in 1965: Berkshire was a money-losing textile business. "The dumbest thing I could have done was to pursue 'opportunities' to improve and expand the existing textile operation – so for years that's exactly what I did," culminating in buying another textile company. He eventually moved into insurance and then other industries.
- Some businesses earn 25% after tax to over 100% on unleveraged net tangible assets, others 12-20%, and a few very poor returns because he "misjudged either the competitive strength of the business I was purchasing or the future economics of the industry." He tries to look out ten or twenty years, "but sometimes my eyesight has been poor."

## Management model and culture: volunteers, owners, and a tiny headquarters

- "Hire well, manage little." Managers get a letter from Warren every two years and call when they wish. They have no headquarters meetings, financing worries or Wall Street harassment. "Our trust is in people rather than process."
- Many CEOs are independently wealthy and work because they love it. "They are volunteers, not mercenaries," and no one can offer them a more enjoyable job. Styles vary widely (MBAs and non-graduates, budget-driven and seat-of-the-pants), like an all-star team with different batting styles.
- Directors act like owners. They get token pay, no options or restricted stock, and no D&O insurance. They and their families own over $3 billion of stock. "If they mess up with your money, they will lose their money as well."
- Many managers sought out Berkshire as the buyer of a family-owned business and arrive with an owner's mindset.
- Cultures self-propagate. He quotes Churchill: "You shape your houses and then they shape you." Bureaucratic procedures beget bureaucracy and "imperial corporate palaces induce imperious behavior."
- Headquarters rent is $270,212 a year, and the home-office investment in furniture, art, equipment and a Coke dispenser totals $301,363. Twenty people work on one floor.
- Compensation, the annual meeting and the annual report are designed to reinforce a culture that will "repel and expel managers of a different bent."
- Reputation memo to managers: "We can afford to lose money – even a lot of money. But we can't afford to lose reputation – even a shred of reputation." Test every act against what an unfriendly but intelligent reporter could write on a newspaper front page. "Everybody else is doing it" signals the lack of a good reason. If an action evokes hesitation, "it's too close to the line and should be abandoned. There's plenty of money to be made in the center of the court."
- He wants bad news promptly. Delay turned a Salomon problem "that could have easily been disposed of into one that almost caused the demise of a firm with 8,000 employees." Culture, "more than rule books, determines how an organization behaves."
- He asks managers for a written succession recommendation (who takes over if they are incapacitated overnight), marked "Personal for Warren."

## GEICO: how goodwill can far exceed book value

- Warren, then a 20-year-old Columbia student, found that his teacher Ben Graham chaired GEICO. He took a train to Washington on a Saturday, found the building closed, banged on a door until a janitor let him in, and spent four hours learning insurance from Lorimer "Davy" Davidson.
- He put 75% of his $9,800 portfolio into GEICO soon after, though he "felt over-diversified."
- In the mid-1970s the stock fell over 95%. Berkshire bought about one-third in the market, rising to 50% through GEICO's buybacks, at a cost of $46 million, then bought the other 50% in 1996 for about $2.3 billion.
- The 1996 price implied $4.6 billion for 100% against tangible net worth of $1.9 billion. The $2.7 billion excess was goodwill, about 97% of the $2.8 billion in premiums paid in 1995. That is a very high price by industry standards, but justified by low costs and consistently profitable, unusually loyal policyholders.
- Today premium volume is $14.3 billion, while goodwill is carried at only $1.4 billion because accounting rules write goodwill down but never up. Using the same 97% yardstick, economic goodwill is about $14 billion.
- Under Tony Nicely (CEO from 1993), market share rose from 2.0% (stuck for over a decade) to 8.8%, with underwriting discipline and low costs. The guiding goal: "get the policyholder's business by deserving his business."

## Insurance float and the four underwriting disciplines

- P/C insurers collect premiums upfront and pay claims later, holding float that stays stable relative to premium volume. Berkshire's float: $39 million (1970), $237 million (1980), $1.6 billion (1990), $27.9 billion (2000), $65.8 billion (2010).
- The industry generally operates at an underwriting loss, which is what it pays to hold float. State Farm had an underwriting loss in seven of ten years, totaling over $20 billion. Berkshire has had eight straight years of underwriting profit, totaling $17 billion, so its float is better than cost-free, "as if some party deposited $66 billion with us, paid us a fee for holding its money."
- The industry's return on tangible equity has fallen short of average American industry for decades.
- Four disciplines of sound insurance:
  1. Understand all exposures that might cause losses.
  2. Conservatively evaluate the likelihood of a loss and its probable cost.
  3. Set a premium that earns a profit after loss costs and expenses.
  4. Be willing to walk away if the right premium can't be obtained.
- "Many insurers pass the first three tests and flunk the fourth," driven by Wall Street, agents and brokers, or a "testosterone-driven CEO." "The other guy is doing it so we must as well" spells trouble in any business, most of all insurance.
- Ajit Jain's reinsurance unit insures risks no one else wants or has the capital for, without exposing Berkshire inappropriately. From 1985 it grew to $30 billion of float, and Warren says Berkshire is far more conservative than most large insurers.
- 2010 underwriting profits: GEICO $1,117 million, General Re $452 million, BH Reinsurance $176 million, other primary $268 million, for $2,013 million total.

## Derivatives as insurance, Black-Scholes, and ignoring net income

- Warren treats Berkshire's derivatives (203 contracts) as insurance-like activity, taking premiums to assume risks others want to shed. Premiums are received upfront, so there is no counterparty risk.
- High-yield credit contracts (2004-2008): $3.4 billion of premiums, $2.5 billion of losses through the crisis, now almost certain to be an underwriting profit, plus about $2 billion of average interest-free float. "We charged the right premium."
- Equity puts: $4.8 billion of premiums on 47 contracts, most 15-year, payable only on the termination date. 39 remain with $4.2 billion of original premiums. Carried at $6.7 billion on the balance sheet versus a $3.8 billion settlement value if index levels are unchanged.
- Almost all contracts require no collateral posting. That cut the premiums Berkshire could charge but gave comfort in the crisis and let it commit to advantageous purchases.
- He and Charlie believe Black-Scholes gives "wildly inappropriate values" for long-dated options, but Berkshire uses it because auditors and counterparties do. "We would rather be approximately right than precisely wrong." A Black-Scholes liability change cut one quarter's net income by $455 million even as index prices rose.
- He urges schools to stop teaching Black-Scholes as revealed truth: "You can be highly successful as an investor without having the slightest ability to value an option. What students should be learning is how to value a business."
- Net income is "almost always meaningless at Berkshire" because realized gains count and unrealized gains don't, so it can be steered at will. He says they never sell a security to affect reported net income. Watch book value and operating earnings instead.

## Leverage, liquidity, temperament and the American outlook

- "To finish first, you must first finish." Leverage is addictive, and "any series of positive numbers... evaporates when multiplied by a single zero." Credit is like oxygen: unnoticed when abundant, all that matters when absent.
- Berkshire pledges at least $10 billion of cash (excluding utility and railroad cash) and customarily keeps $20 billion or more to survive record insurance losses (about $3 billion from Katrina) and seize opportunities. The idea comes from his grandfather Ernest's $1,000 letters to his children.
- Cash is held largely in Treasury bills. He quotes Ray DeVoe: "More money has been lost reaching for yield than at the point of a gun." Berkshire relies on no bank lines and avoids contracts with sizeable collateral calls.
- No cash has gone to dividends or buybacks in 40 years. Retained earnings run about $1 billion a month, and net worth grew from $48 million to $157 billion. The cost of caution is "a minor amount" of return, and it lets Berkshire "play offense while others scramble for survival," including investing $15.6 billion in 25 days after Lehman's bankruptcy.
- On uncertainty: tomorrow is always uncertain (December 6, 1941, October 18, 1987, September 10, 2001). Americans live "six times better" than when he was born, and "America's best days lie ahead."
- On housing: Clayton keeps its own mortgages, requires meaningful down payments and sets fixed payments at a sensible share of income. Borrowers with an average 648 FICO score had annual losses of 1.17%-1.86% from 2006 to 2010. A home can be "a nightmare if the buyer's eyes are bigger than his wallet." The goal should be a house people can afford, not "the house of their dreams."
- Owning a quality business: Coke's dividend to Berkshire was $88 million in 1995 and will be about $376 million in 2011, likely doubling within ten years. "Time is the friend of the wonderful business."

## Regulated capital-intensive businesses (BNSF, MidAmerican) and investment manager selection

- BNSF cost $22 billion of cash and 6% more shares, and should lift normal earning power by nearly 40% pre-tax. Rail moves each ton 500 miles on a gallon of diesel, three times as efficiently as trucking. BNSF carries about 11% of all U.S. inter-city ton-miles and will spend about $2 billion more than depreciation in 2011.
- Both BNSF and MidAmerican rely on a "social compact": they invest continually and, if they serve well, regulators allow reasonable returns. Their long-term debt isn't guaranteed by Berkshire, and BNSF's 2010 interest coverage was 6:1 even in a recession.
- MidAmerican retains all earnings, unlike most utilities, and has invested or committed $5.4 billion to wind. Iowa rates are unchanged since 1999, while the other major Iowa utility raised prices over 70%.
- Choosing investment managers: past results matter but don't suffice. What counts is how the record was achieved and understanding of risk (not beta), including the ability to anticipate "economic scenarios not previously observed," plus someone for whom Berkshire is "far more than a job."
- Pay for Todd Combs: salary plus a payout based on performance versus the S&P, with deferrals and carryforwards to prevent undeserved payments after see-saw results. He criticizes hedge-fund general partners who take upside and walk away after losses. "Our only style box is 'smart.'" The aim was "a 2-year-old Secretariat, not a 10-year-old Seabiscuit."
