## Surviving the 2008 crisis: Berkshire's four goals and what stays constant

- **Performance in 2008:** Book value fell 9.6% (a $11.5 billion drop), the worst year for Berkshire and the S&P 500 in the 44-year record. Warren notes book value has compounded at 20.3% annually since 1965, from $19 to $70,530 per share.
- **Four goals in good years and bad:**
  1. Maintain a "Gibraltar-like" financial position with huge excess liquidity, modest near-term obligations and dozens of sources of earnings and cash.
  2. Widen the "moats" that give operating businesses durable competitive advantages.
  3. Acquire and develop new and varied streams of earnings.
  4. Expand and nurture the cadre of outstanding operating managers.
- **No market forecasting:** The S&P gained in 75% of the 44 years, and Warren guesses a similar share of the next 44 will be positive. He says neither he nor Charlie can predict winning and losing years, and "we don't think anyone else can either." He was certain the economy would be in shambles in 2009, but says that tells nothing about whether stocks rise or fall.
- **Long-run optimism:** The real U.S. standard of living rose nearly seven-fold in the 1900s while the Dow went from 66 to 11,497, despite two world wars, a dozen panics and recessions, a 21.5% prime rate in 1980 and the Depression. "America's best days lie ahead."
- **Liquidity over profit:** He sold part of Johnson & Johnson, Procter & Gamble and ConocoPhillips to fund purchases rather than lean on credit. "We never want to count on the kindness of strangers in order to meet tomorrow's obligations. When forced to choose, I will not trade even a night's sleep for the chance of extra profits."

## Buying when others are fearful: 2008 capital allocation

- **Principle:** "When investing, pessimism is your friend, euphoria the enemy." He recalls Ben Graham's line "Price is what you pay; value is what you get," and says he likes buying quality merchandise marked down, "whether we're talking about socks or stocks."
- **Falling prices are welcome** if funds are available to increase positions.
- **Fixed-income deals:** $14.5 billion of securities issued by Wrigley, Goldman Sachs and General Electric. High current yields made them satisfactory on their own, and each carried a substantial equity participation as a bonus.
- **Three large insurance-portfolio investments** on terms unavailable in normal markets, expected to add about $1.5 billion pre-tax to annual earnings, plus the Marmon acquisition (64% owned, remainder to be bought over six years) and several tuck-in acquisitions.
- **Risk repricing:** The investment world went "from underpricing risk to overpricing it." Good-grade municipal and corporate bonds offered yields that would have seemed unthinkable earlier, while Treasuries paid a pittance. He calls the late-2008 Treasury bond "bubble" nearly as extraordinary as the Internet and housing bubbles.
- **Cash is not safe:** Holding cash equivalents or long-term governments at present yields "is almost certainly a terrible policy if continued for long," because purchasing power erodes.
- **Against applause:** "Approval... is often counter-productive because it sedates the brain." He warns: "Beware the investment activity that produces applause; the great moves are usually greeted by yawns."
- **Operating businesses over securities:** Berkshire's focus remains the earnings segment. "We like buying underpriced securities, but we like buying fairly-priced operating businesses even more."

## Admitting mistakes: ConocoPhillips and the Irish banks

- **ConocoPhillips:** He bought a large stake when oil and gas prices were near their peak, did not anticipate the second-half collapse, and says the terrible timing "has cost Berkshire several billion dollars." He still thinks the odds are good that oil sells far higher than the then-current $40-$50, but admits "so far I have been dead wrong."
- **Irish banks:** He spent $244 million on two Irish bank stocks that "appeared cheap to me." They were written down to $27 million, an 89% loss, and fell further afterward. He calls them "unforced errors."
- **Errors of omission:** He admits "sucking my thumb when new facts came in that should have caused me to re-examine my thinking and promptly take action."
- **Unrealized declines:** Holdings that fell with the market did not bother him. Investments per share fell from $90,343 to $77,793, caused by market prices rather than sales.

## Insurance float and underwriting discipline

- **Float:** $58.5 billion of money held and invested for Berkshire's benefit. With a sixth straight underwriting gain, the float cost less than zero; Berkshire was paid $2.8 billion to hold it in 2008.
- **Industry contrast:** Over 25 years to 2007, insurers' return on net worth averaged 8.5% versus 14.0% for the Fortune 500. Most insurers eventually book substantial underwriting losses. Warren expects Berkshire to average an underwriting profit, because its managers run entrenched, valuable franchises, while acknowledging it will have terrible years too.
- **GEICO (Tony Nicely):** Market share grew from 2.0% in 1993 to 7.7%, making it the number three auto insurer. Policies per employee rose from 299 to 439 in five years. It grows because it saves drivers money, and efficiency is the key to low cost. It also expanded in motorcycles (+23.4%), RVs, ATVs and commercial autos.
- **General Re:** Culture had deteriorated (loose underwriting, reserving and expenses) in a problem Warren says he failed to detect at the 1998 purchase. Joe Brandon and Tad Montross restored discipline. In reinsurance, "a promise is no better than the person or institution making it," and General Re is the only reinsurer backed by an AAA corporation.
- **Ajit Jain's reinsurance division:** 31 employees writing very large, unusual risks with speed. Year-end float by unit: General Re $21.1B, BH Reinsurance $24.2B, GEICO $8.5B, Other Primary $4.7B.
- **Manager quality:** He credits managers who excel "to a degree Charlie and I never dreamed possible," even without a favorable industry tailwind.

## Tax-exempt bond insurance and the flaw in back-tested models

- **BHAC:** Berkshire Hathaway Assurance Company was activated in 2008 to insure municipal bonds. The monolines had drifted into ever-riskier mortgage-related business under competitive pressure ("I was Snow White, but I drifted").
- **A mistake that worked out:** Berkshire offered to take over about $822 billion of insured bonds from the three largest monolines at a 1.5% rate. They refused, and Warren later saw the offer was severely underpriced. Berkshire instead wrote about $15.6 billion of secondary-market "second-to-pay" insurance, averaging 3.3% rates, plus $3.7 billion of primary business for a $96 million premium.
- **Why the business is dangerous:** Historic low default rates reflect uninsured bonds. Local holders of uninsured bonds (as in 1975 New York City) feel pressure to make sacrifices. If everything is insured, cities will prefer pain for a "far-away bond insurer" over tax increases. Losses would also be highly correlated, much like natural-catastrophe insurance, where a string of loss-free years can precede a devastating one. Pension underfunding adds to the fiscal stress.
- **Lesson on models:** History-based models failed on mortgages because they ignored that house prices had skyrocketed and lending standards had deteriorated, so the "past" universe differed from the "current" one. "If merely looking up past financial data would tell you what the future holds, the Forbes 400 would consist of librarians." His advice: "Beware of geeks bearing formulas."

## Lending discipline: Clayton Homes and the housing crisis

- **Clayton:** The largest manufactured-home company (27,499 units, about 34% of the industry) with 198,888 borrowers who kept paying through the housing crash. Median FICO was 644 versus a national 723, and about 35% were below 620. Delinquency was 3.6% and foreclosures 3.0% of originated loans.
- **Why it worked:** Borrowers compared full-bore payments to actual, not hoped-for, income. They did not rely on refinancing or teaser rates, and did not assume they could always sell at a profit. "Lending 101."
- **Industry folly:** Both the 1997-2000 manufactured-housing bust and the 2004-07 conventional boom involved "borrowers who shouldn't have borrowed being financed by lenders who shouldn't have lent," relying on house-price appreciation.
- **Foreclosure driver:** Most foreclosures happen because borrowers can't make the monthly payment, not merely because homes are worth less than the mortgage.
- **Policy lessons:** Require a real down payment of at least 10%, payments the borrower's verified income can handle, and purchases motivated by enjoyment and utility rather than profit or refi possibilities. "Putting people into homes... shouldn't be our country's primary objective. Keeping them in their homes should be the ambition."
- **Funding-cost distortion:** Government-guaranteed lenders had minimal funding costs while AAA Berkshire faced record spreads. "At the moment, it is much better to be a financial cripple with a government guarantee than a Gibraltar without one."

## Derivatives: danger, and why Berkshire still writes some

- **Core warning:** Derivatives raise leverage and risk and make big banks nearly impossible to analyze. Warren points to Freddie and Fannie's years of misstatement despite 100+ regulatory staff, and to Gen Re's 23,218 contracts with 884 counterparties, which cost over $400 million and five years to unwind. His takeaway: "I liked you better before I got to know you so well."
- **Transparency won't fix it:** Auditors can't audit and regulators can't regulate such books. Counterparty claims built over years create "a frightening web of mutual dependence."
- **Why Berkshire writes them:** He believes each of its 251 contracts was mispriced at inception. Counterparties pay premiums up front, so Berkshire holds the money and carries no meaningful counterparty risk. The derivatives "float" was $8.1 billion. He initiates and monitors every position, since "the CEO of any large financial organization must be the Chief Risk Officer as well."
- **Equity puts:** $37.1 billion across the S&P 500, FTSE 100, Euro Stoxx 50 and Nikkei 225, maturing 2019-2028, with $4.9 billion of premiums received. Payment is due only if the index is below its starting level at maturity. The recorded liability was $10 billion (a $5.1 billion mark-to-market loss). Collateral posting was under 1% of the securities portfolio, a lethal risk at other institutions.
- **Credit default contracts:** High-yield index contracts brought $3.4 billion of premiums versus $542 million of losses paid. Single-name CDS were $4 billion across 42 companies ($93 million annual premium). He avoids contracts requiring collateral posting.
- **Mark-to-market swings:** He endorses the accounting but says the resulting earnings swings "neither cheer nor bother" him, and that the "downs" can offer chances to expand positions on good terms.
- **Black-Scholes critique:** For a hypothetical 100-year $1 billion S&P put at 903, the formula gives a $2.5 million premium. Warren sees a under-1% chance of decline and a likely 50% maximum loss, so expected loss is $5 million; the premium would need to earn only 0.7% a year to cover it. Even in the worst case the implied borrowing cost is 6.2%. He argues volatility from past price moves is irrelevant over long periods (like valuing a farm from a manic-depressive neighbor's daily quotes), and that the formula overstates the liability on long-term puts. He still uses it for reporting to avoid appearing to be among CEOs who invent flattering valuations.

## Utilities, acquisitions and being the "buyer of choice"

- **Goal:** Be the "buyer of choice," especially for family-built businesses, by deserving it: keep promises, avoid leveraging acquired businesses, grant unusual autonomy, and hold through thick and thin.
- **Contrast with private equity:** Warren says such buyers treat acquisitions as "merchandise" with exit strategies, and that "private equity" is misleading because buyouts usually cut the acquiree's equity share. Acquirees bought two to three years earlier were in danger from piled-on debt, with bank debt below 70 cents on the dollar.
- **Regulated utilities:** MidAmerican (87.4% diluted ownership) reinvests all earnings. In 2008 it spent $1.8 billion on wind versus $1.1 billion pre-tax earnings at the Western and Iowa utilities, and it has paid no dividend since 2000. Iowa electric prices have been unchanged since purchase and are promised steady through 2013. Wind in Iowa went from zero to nearly 20% of capacity, and PacifiCorp wind went from 33 to 794 megawatts. In return, regulators allow a fair return on the sums invested.
- **Regulators judge history:** They call counterparts in other states, so behavior in current jurisdictions determines future welcome. Kern River moved from 9th to 1st in customer satisfaction, Northern Natural from 39th to 3rd.
- **Operating group:** The non-insurance businesses earned 17.9% on average tangible net worth with little leverage, but only 8.1% on carrying value because of premiums paid. Warren credits tuck-in acquisitions (Iscar's Tungaloy purchase, etc.) enabled by financial strength, and notes CTB earned $89 million pre-tax in 2008 versus the $140 million paid in 2002.
