## Scale, float and the "free money" engine

- Warren reports a 1996 net worth gain of $6.2 billion (36.1%). Per-share book value grew 31.8%, and has compounded from $19 to $19,011 over 32 years, or 23.8% a year.
- He says book value only roughly tracks progress: "what counts at Berkshire is intrinsic value, not book value."
- He tracks two value indices per share. Investments (including cash) went from $4 in 1965 to $22,088 in 1995 and $28,500 in 1996. Pre-tax operating earnings excluding all investment income went from $4.08 to $258.20 in 1995 and $421.39 in 1996.
- Growth in those indices was 29.0% and 63.2% in 1996.
- He warns the past growth rates "cannot be matched nor even approached." Fewer than ten American businesses have more equity capital, and "an abundance of funds tends to dampen returns."

## Float, super-cat insurance and underwriting discipline

**Float**
- Float is "money we hold but don't own." Premiums arrive before losses are paid.
- An underwriting loss is the cost of float. The insurance business has value only if its cost of float is below what the company would otherwise pay for funds. Otherwise it is "an albatross."
- Berkshire's float grew from $17.3 million in 1967 to $6,702 million in 1996, a 22.3% compound rate. Its cost of float was below zero in more years than not, so Berkshire was in effect "paid for holding money."
- Float is a major part of intrinsic value that book value does not show.

**Super-cat**
- Berkshire sells mega-catastrophe cover to insurers and reinsurers. In 1996 it wrote an Allstate Florida hurricane contract. It also wrote a California Earthquake Authority layer of about $1 billion, effective April 1, 1997 and triggered if the Authority's aggregate losses exceed about $5 billion by March 31, 2001.
- Warren estimates the after-tax worst-case loss at probably no more than $600 million, under 3% of book value and 1.5% of market value.
- The business will have a "truly terrible year": "not a possibility - it's a certainty. The only question is when it will come."
- He prices so that about 90% of premiums are eventually paid out in losses and expenses.
- He distrusts computer models: "the precision they project is a chimera." Models can lull decision-makers into false security. He cites portfolio insurance in 1987.
- You do not need precision to underwrite sensibly: "you need not know a man's precise age to know that he is old enough to vote." He adds that in insurance "virtually all surprises are unpleasant."
- Berkshire's three advantages in super-cat:
  - Buyers know it will pay in the worst case. Berkshire never lays off risk, partly because it doubts it could collect from others.
  - After a mega-cat, long-standing clients get first call on its capacity. Reinsurers already pay stand-by fees to lock that in.
  - It offers dollar coverage sizes no one else matches, from a single phone call.
- Ajit Jain runs it. Warren says he has made "all too many" bad reinsurance deals himself, as in the 1970s.

**Volatility**
- "Charlie and I would much rather earn a lumpy 15% over time than a smooth 12%."
- He wants shareholders who can tolerate volatility. If a crash would make you panic-sell, you should not own stocks or Berkshire.
- He quotes Robert Woodruff of Coca-Cola on when to sell Coke: "I don't know. I've never sold any."
- He says "Selling fine businesses on 'scary' news is usually a bad decision."

**Reverse engineering**
- He quotes Charlie Munger: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die so I'll never go there."
- If a consequence cannot be tolerated, even a remote one, Berkshire avoids planting its seeds. That is why it does not borrow big amounts and caps super-cat losses at a level that will not dent intrinsic value.

## GEICO: low cost, a moat, and focused incentives

- Berkshire bought the 49% of GEICO it did not own for $2.3 billion in 1996. Warren first liked the company in 1951, when the whole business was valued at $7 million.
- The flywheel is low cost, then low prices, then good policyholders, then referrals. GEICO gets more than one million referrals a year, producing over half of new business, which cuts acquisition costs further.
- Voluntary auto policies grew 10% in 1996. The previous best year in 20 had been 8%, achieved once.
- The goal is to widen the price advantage over customers, not the profit margin. Scale and higher penetration should widen "the protective moat surrounding our economic castle."
- Bonuses for dozens of top executives, starting with Tony Nicely, rest on only two variables:
  - growth in voluntary auto policies;
  - underwriting profit on seasoned auto business (policies on the books more than a year).
- The profit-sharing plan uses the same two yardsticks. In 1996 it paid a record 16.9% ($40 million), against a prior five-year average under 10%. The plan went "off the chart" and Warren's fix was: "Enlarge the chart." He adds: "we never greet good work by raising the bar."
- Warren's incentive principles:
  - goals tailored to the specific business's economics;
  - simple, easily measured goals;
  - goals directly tied to participants' daily activities.
- He shuns "lottery ticket" pay such as Berkshire options, whose value is outside the manager's control.
- Lou Simpson's investment pay is tied to four-year investment results, not to underwriting or company-wide results. Warren calls it foolish to let great work on one side be cancelled by bad work on the other. In 1996 Lou's equities beat the S&P 500 by 6.2 points.
- Smaller primary units had combined ratios well under 100:
  - National Indemnity traditional business: 74.2 (3-year average 83.0).
  - Homestate: 87.1 (3-year 83.2).

## Buying businesses: the 1996 acquisitions and how deals arrive

- Warren seeks "excellent business economics and an outstanding manager."
- Kansas Bankers Surety cost $75 million, placed with 80%-owned Wesco. It came from a chance remark at a birthday party on January 26. Warren got the financials on February 12 and bid $75 million on February 13. He says he treasures managers like Don Towle who think of the company as "his."
- FlightSafety International cost about $1.5 billion. It trains pilots at 41 locations with 175 simulators, which can cost as much as $19 million each. About half of revenue is corporate pilot training. It was suggested by a Berkshire shareholder, and Warren says that in "about 60 seconds" he knew Al Ueltschi was his kind of manager.
- The consideration was about 51% cash, 41% Class A and 8% Class B. The structure encouraged tax-indifferent holders to take cash, to limit new Berkshire shares.
- Warren keeps managers past 70, saying "We never fool with success." He says "It's difficult to teach a new dog old tricks."
- He invites owners of large businesses with good economics to call. His requirements are on page 21 of the report.

## Investing philosophy: inactivity, Inevitables and the circle of competence

**Inactivity**
- "We continue to make more money when snoring than when active."
- Buying stocks is the same art as acquiring subsidiaries: buy at a sensible price a business with excellent economics and able, honest management, then monitor whether those qualities persist.
- Concentration is a natural result. He compares it to owning 20% of the future earnings of several college basketball stars. Selling winners because they dominate the portfolio would be like the Bulls trading Michael Jordan.

**Predictability**
- He favors businesses unlikely to change much, seeking operations "virtually certain to possess enormous competitive strength ten or twenty years from now." In fermenting industries, "we applaud the endeavor but prefer to skip the ride."
- See's candy: the reasons people buy boxed chocolates, and from See's, are unchanged since the 1920s.
- Coca-Cola: he read the 1896 annual report. Sales that year were $148,000. Syrup sales were 116,492 gallons, against about 3.2 billion gallons in 1996.
- He calls Coke and Gillette "The Inevitables." Dominance is near-certain for an investment lifetime, and he would rather "be certain of a good result than hopeful of a great one."
- He warns that for every Inevitable there are dozens of "Impostors." General Motors, IBM and Sears all once looked invincible.
- He adds "Highly Probables" because they can find so few Inevitables.

**Risks to a great holding**
- Overpayment: in 1996 he says the risk "may now be quite high" for nearly all stocks, Inevitables included. Even an outstanding company may take years to catch up with a price paid in an overheated market.
- Loss of focus: management buying so-so businesses (Coke once grew shrimp, Gillette once explored for oil) or boredom and hubris that lets attention wander.

**Advice for your own portfolio**
- Most investors should hold a low-fee index fund, which will beat most professionals after fees.
- Costs matter. Equity mutual fund expenses of about 100 basis points can cut investor returns by 10% or more over time.
- Intelligent investing "is not complex, though that is far from saying that it is easy." You need to evaluate selected businesses inside your circle of competence: "The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital."
- You do not need beta, efficient markets, modern portfolio theory, option pricing or emerging markets. Business schools need only two courses: How to Value a Business, and How to Think About Market Prices.
- The goal is to buy, at a rational price, part of an understandable business whose earnings are virtually certain to be materially higher in five, ten and twenty years. Buy a meaningful amount when you find one.
- "If you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes."
- Portfolio value follows aggregate earnings upward over time.

## Look-through earnings and retained earnings

- Reported earnings understate economic progress. Berkshire books only dividends from investees, but their undistributed earnings are worth more to Berkshire because investees can reinvest at high rates.
- Look-through earnings equal operating earnings, plus Berkshire's share of investees' retained operating earnings, less a hypothetical tax on those. The tax rate used is 14%, the rate Berkshire pays on dividends.
- 1996 look-through earnings were about $1,522 million: reported operating earnings of $954 million, plus $661 million of undistributed investee earnings, less $93 million of hypothetical tax. Warren says the figures are approximate, built on judgment calls.
- Largest undistributed earnings shares:
  - Coca-Cola: $180 million, 8.1% owned.
  - American Express: $132 million, 10.5% owned.
  - Wells Fargo: $84 million.
  - Freddie Mac: $77 million.
  - Gillette: $73 million.
- 12/31/96 market values:
  - Coca-Cola: 200 million shares, cost $1,298.9 million, market $10,525.0 million.
  - Gillette: 48 million shares, cost $600 million, market $3,732 million.
  - American Express: cost $1,392.7 million, market $2,794.3 million.
  - Total common stocks: cost $7,910.2 million, market $27,750.6 million.
- Berkshire's own reporting shows purchase-accounting adjustments separately, to show business earnings as if never purchased. He says this is more useful to investors and managers than GAAP. He wants to give shareholders the financial information "that we would wish you to give us if our positions were reversed."

## Mistakes, fairness and alignment with owners

**USAir**
- In 1989 he invested $358 million in a 9.25% USAir preferred, beguiled by its history of profits and by the apparent protection of a senior security.
- He admits his analysis was "superficial and wrong." The company's revenues faced deregulated, fierce competition while its costs were a holdover from regulated days. USAir lost $2.4 billion in 1990-1994.
- Berkshire wrote the position down 75% to $89.5 million. He tried to sell at half of face value, and again in 1996 for about $335 million, and failed both times. He calls that lucky.
- The saving feature was a penalty-dividend clause: unpaid dividends accrued at prime plus five points, compounding at 13.25% to 14%. USAir paid $47.9 million in the second half of 1996.
- The preferred was then probably worth par, and Berkshire had collected $240.5 million in dividends over the years.
- Quips: "If history supplied all of the answers, the Forbes 400 would consist of librarians." And on airlines: "Start as a billionaire and then buy an airline."

**Price versus value and fairness**
- When a stock over- or underperforms the business, buyers or sellers get "outsized benefits at the expense of those they trade with." He wants market price and intrinsic value kept roughly in sync, as fairness in a family partnership would require.
- Over time shareholders' gains must match the company's business gains.
- He wants long-term owners. Berkshire probably ranks first among large US companies in the share held by long-term owners.
- In 1996 intrinsic value rose sharply, largely from GEICO, while the share price was flat. He says price versus value was then "more appropriate."

**Class B shares**
- In May 1996 he sold 517,500 Class B shares for $565 million net. Each is 1/30 of a Class A.
- The goal was to head off unit trusts marketed as Berkshire look-alikes. They would have used a non-repeatable record to lure small investors, charged high fees and driven a speculative bubble.
- He built the offering to deter flippers: a 1.5% commission, the lowest he had seen, and an open-ended size. About 40,000 shareholders were added. Salomon earned perhaps a tenth of what a standard deal would have paid, yet he was glad to pay it.
- He says he is happy to pay fees "commensurate with performance."

**Costs and owner orientation**
- Headquarters expense is under two basis points of net worth.
- "We are here to make money with you, not off you."
- He keeps the shareholder-designated contributions program: 97.2% of eligible shares participated, and $13.3 million went to 3,910 charities. He counts it as a shareholder benefit, not an expense.
- Berkshire paid $860 million in income taxes in 1996. He calls large tax payments "entirely fitting" because "Berkshire prospers in America as it would nowhere else."
