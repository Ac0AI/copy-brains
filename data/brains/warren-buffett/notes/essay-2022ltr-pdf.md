## Who he and Charlie work for

- Warren says he and Charlie manage "the savings of a great number of individuals," often held for much of their adult lives. Those dedicated savers are foremost in his mind.
- He calls Berkshire holders "once-a-saver, always-a-saver." They live well but eventually give most of their money to philanthropy.
- He notes how rarely they choose "look-at-me assets and dynasty-building." His line: "The disposition of money unmasks humans."

## What Berkshire does: business-pickers, not stock-pickers

- Capital is allocated across two forms of ownership.
  - Controlled businesses, usually bought 100%. Berkshire directs capital allocation and picks the CEOs who run day-to-day operations.
  - Passive holdings of publicly-traded stocks, with no say in management.
- The goal in both is meaningful investments in businesses with long-lasting favorable economics and trustworthy managers.
- Public stocks are owned for expected long-term business performance, not as trading vehicles. "Charlie and I are not stock-pickers; we are business-pickers."
- On management, Berkshire emphasizes trust over rules "to an unusual – some would say extreme – degree." Warren says: "We are understanding about business mistakes; our tolerance for personal misconduct is zero."
- Warren admits his mistakes. The portfolio holds a few businesses with truly extraordinary economics, many with very good economics, and a large marginal group. Some businesses he invested in died.
- He cites Schumpeter's "creative destruction": capitalism builds a pile of losers while delivering a gusher of improved goods and services.

## Public stocks vs controlled businesses: pricing

- Stocks often trade at "truly foolish prices, both high and low." "'Efficient' markets exist only in textbooks."
- Marketable stocks and bonds are baffling, and their behavior is usually understandable only in retrospect.
- Episodically, it becomes easy to buy pieces of wonderful businesses at wonderful prices.
- Controlled businesses are different. They sometimes command ridiculously high prices but are almost never available at bargain valuations. Owners do not sell at a panic valuation unless under duress.

## Capital allocation report card and the "secret sauce"

- In 58 years, most of Warren's capital-allocation decisions were "no better than so-so." Some bad moves were rescued by luck (USAir and Salomon).
- Satisfactory results came from about a dozen truly good decisions, roughly one every five years, plus the advantage of long-term holding.
- Coca-Cola:
  - Berkshire completed the purchase of 400 million shares in August 1994 over seven years, for $1.3 billion.
  - The annual dividend rose from $75 million in 1994 to $704 million in 2022, growing every year.
  - The stake was worth $25 billion at yearend 2022.
- American Express:
  - Purchases were essentially complete in 1995, also costing $1.3 billion.
  - Annual dividends grew from $41 million to $302 million.
  - The stake was recorded at $22 billion.
- Each holding is roughly 5% of Berkshire's net worth, about its weighting long ago.
- Compare a $1.3 billion mistake that flat-lined, such as a high-grade 30-year bond. It would now be 0.3% of net worth and deliver about $80 million a year.
- Lesson: "The weeds wither away in significance as the flowers bloom." A few winners work wonders over time, and it helps to start early and live into your 90s.

## Operating earnings, GAAP noise and accounting manipulation

- Warren urges focusing on operating earnings (GAAP income excluding gains or losses on equity holdings). 2022 operating earnings were a record $30.8 billion.
- GAAP quarterly swings in 2022 (in $ billions):

| Quarter | Operating | GAAP |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 7.0 | 5.5 |
| Q2 | 9.3 | (43.8) |
| Q3 | 7.8 | (2.7) |
| Q4 | 6.7 | 18.2 |

- He calls quarterly or annual GAAP earnings "100% misleading" and says the media's headlines on them "totally misinform investors." Capital gains remain hugely important over decades.
- Even operating earnings can be manipulated. Warren calls "beating expectations" games "disgusting": "It requires no talent to manipulate numbers: Only a deep desire to deceive is required."
- He cites a CEO's phrase, "Bold imaginative accounting," as "one of the shames of capitalism."

## Insurance float, Alleghany and share repurchases

- Berkshire bought Alleghany, a property-casualty insurer run by Joe Brandon. Berkshire's financial strength lets its insurers follow investment strategies unavailable to virtually all competitors.
- Float grew from $147 billion to $164 billion in 2022. With disciplined underwriting, it has a decent chance of being cost-free over time.
- Float has grown 8,000-fold since the first property-casualty insurer was bought in 1967. It is an extraordinary asset not recognized in the financial statements.
- Berkshire bought back 1.2% of its shares in 2022. Apple and American Express repurchases raised Berkshire's ownership at no cost to it.
- The math: fewer shares means a larger interest in the businesses. This works only at value-accretive prices. Overpaying benefits only the selling shareholders "and the friendly, but expensive, investment banker."
- Warren's local auto dealership example: three informed owners, one managing, and one passive owner sells back at a price attractive to the other two. No one is harmed, the manager is not favored, and the public is not hurt.
- Anyone saying all repurchases are harmful is "either an economic illiterate or a silver-tongued demagogue."

## Berkshire's structure, staying power and the American Tailwind

- Berkshire was a "one-trick pony" in 1965, a doomed New England textile operation. National Indemnity became available in 1967, shifting resources to insurance.
- The drivers of results: retained earnings, compounding, avoiding major mistakes, and "most important of all" the American Tailwind. "America would have done fine without Berkshire. The reverse is not true."
- Scale facts, using 2021 earnings:
  - The S&P 500 earned $1.8 trillion.
  - Only 128 of the 500 (including Berkshire) earned $3 billion or more, and 23 lost money.
- At yearend 2022, Berkshire was the largest owner of eight S&P 500 companies:
  - American Express
  - Bank of America
  - Chevron
  - Coca-Cola
  - HP Inc.
  - Moody's
  - Occidental Petroleum
  - Paramount Global
- Berkshire also owns 100% of BNSF ($5.9 billion earnings) and 92% of BH Energy ($4.3 billion).
- Permanent policies:
  - Hold a "boatload of cash and U.S. Treasury bills."
  - Avoid any behavior that could cause uncomfortable cash needs in panics or unprecedented insurance losses.
  - The CEO is always the Chief Risk Officer, which is irresponsible to delegate.
  - Future CEOs must hold a significant part of their net worth in Berkshire shares, bought with their own money.
- Warren says near-term economic and market forecasts are "worse than useless." He plans for acceptable results over time and for staying power in panics and severe recessions.
- Berkshire offers modest but imperfect protection from runaway inflation. "Huge and entrenched fiscal deficits have consequences."
- On America: after 80 years of investing, "I have yet to see a time when it made sense to make a long-term bet against America."

## Taxes

- In the decade through 2021, the Treasury collected about $32.3 trillion and spent $43.9 trillion.
- Berkshire paid $32 billion of corporate income tax over 2012-21, almost exactly 0.1% of all collections.
- About 1,000 taxpayers matching that payment would have covered all federal taxes, with no other business or household paying anything.
- Warren expects and hopes Berkshire pays much more in the next decade.

## Charlie Munger's lessons and the value of a partner

Warren shares Charlie's thoughts from a recent podcast:

- "The world is full of foolish gamblers, and they will not do as well as the patient investor."
- "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there." Also: write your desired obituary early, then behave accordingly.
- Rationality must be worked on, or you stay irrational and get lousy results.
- Patience can be learned. A long attention span is a huge advantage.
- Learn from dead people, both the admired and the detested.
- "Don't bail away in a sinking boat if you can swim to one that is seaworthy."
- "A great company keeps working after you are not; a mediocre company won't do that."
- Seek good long-term investments and hold them stubbornly. Ignore market froth.
- Graham: the market is a voting machine day to day and a weighing machine long term.
- No investment is a 100% sure thing, so leverage is dangerous: "A string of wonderful numbers times zero will always equal zero. Don't count on getting rich twice."
- You don't need to own a lot of things to get rich.
- Keep learning as the world changes. Warren and Charlie hated railroad stocks for decades, then recognized that four huge railroads had become vital. "Better late than never."
- Charlie's decision-clincher: "Warren, think more about it. You're smart and I'm right."

Warren's own rule: find a very smart, high-grade partner, preferably slightly older, and listen very carefully.

## See's Candies and the 2022 annual meeting

- At the first in-person meeting in three years, See's sold 2,690 transactions on Friday (noon to 5 p.m.) and 3,931 on Saturday.
- Volume was $400,309 over the two days, about 10 sales per minute in prime time. The kiosk sold eleven tons of candy.
- The products "haven't been materially altered in 101 years." "What worked for See's in the days of Henry Ford's model T works now."
- Warren was 92 and Charlie 99. The next meeting is May 5-6, 2023 in Omaha.
