## Why GAAP earnings and book value mislead at Berkshire

- Warren's 2018 GAAP earnings were $4.0 billion: $24.8 billion operating earnings, a $3.0 billion intangible impairment (mostly Kraft Heinz), $2.8 billion realized gains, and a $20.6 billion unrealized loss.
- A new GAAP rule forces unrealized gains and losses on stocks into earnings. Warren and Charlie think it is not sensible and predicted "wild and capricious swings in our bottom line."
- Quarterly GAAP results swung from a $1.1 billion loss, to $12 billion and $18.5 billion profits, to a $25.4 billion loss, while operating businesses were steady. Operating earnings for the year beat the 2016 high of $17.6 billion by 41%.
- His advice: "Focus on operating earnings, paying little attention to gains or losses of any variety."
- He dropped per-share book value from the letter's opening because:
  - Berkshire's value has shifted from marketable stocks to operating businesses.
  - Accounting carries those businesses in book value far below their true value.
  - Buybacks below intrinsic value but above book raise per-share intrinsic value while lowering per-share book value.
- He will instead emphasize Berkshire's market price, which over time is the best measure of business performance.

## Valuing Berkshire by "groves" rather than trees

- Warren says investors need not analyze every business ("trees"). Berkshire's intrinsic value can be approximated by appraising five "groves": non-insurance subsidiaries, marketable equities, shared-control businesses, cash and fixed income, and insurance.
- Intrinsic value is roughly the sum of the four asset groves, minus taxes eventually payable on selling marketable securities.
- No deduction for taxes on selling wholly owned businesses: "It would be foolish for us to sell any of our wonderful companies even if no tax would be payable... Truly good businesses are exceptionally hard to find."
- Insurance float funds much of the other four groves, and interest on debt is already deducted from subsidiary earnings.
- Combining the groves in one entity lets Berkshire allocate capital objectively, eliminate enterprise risk, avoid insularity, fund assets cheaply, use tax efficiencies and minimize overhead. "The whole is greater – considerably greater – than the sum of the parts."
- Prime goal: buy ably managed businesses, in whole or part, with favorable and durable economics, at sensible prices. He buys control sometimes, but far more often finds the attributes in public companies, where Berkshire takes 5% to 10%.
- In 2018 stocks offered far more value than whole-business purchases, so Berkshire bought about $43 billion of equities and sold $19 billion.

## What counts as real earnings

- Non-insurance subsidiaries earned $16.8 billion after tax, interest, managerial compensation (cash or stock), restructuring, depreciation, amortization and home-office overhead.
- He criticizes "adjusted EBITDA." On stock-based compensation not counting as an expense: "What else could it be – a gift from shareholders?" Restructurings are common in business and shareholders always bear the cost.
- Quoting Lincoln: calling a dog's tail a leg doesn't make it one. "Abe would have felt lonely on Wall Street."
- He adds back $1.4 billion of acquisition-related amortization as not a true economic cost, both for private businesses and stocks.
- The $8.4 billion depreciation charge understates true cost: Berkshire must spend more than that just to stay competitive. It invested a record $14.5 billion in fixed assets in 2018, 89% in America.

## Owning stocks as owning businesses

- Warren and Charlie do not see the $172.8 billion portfolio as ticker symbols to sell on downgrades, Fed actions, politics or forecasts. They see companies earning about 20% on net tangible equity without excessive debt, versus 3% or less on 30-year Treasuries.
- Retained earnings matter more than dividends. For the five largest holdings (American Express, Apple, Bank of America, Coca-Cola, Wells Fargo), Berkshire's share of dividends was $2,966 million and of retained earnings $6,837 million. Historically, retained earnings of investees have delivered more than one dollar of capital gain for each dollar reinvested.
- He welcomes investee buybacks when the stock is underpriced. Berkshire's American Express holdings were unchanged for eight years while ownership rose from 12.6% to 17.9%. Its $1.2 billion share of 2018 earnings was about 96% of the $1.3 billion cost.
- Overpaying can turn a splendid business into a poor investment for a painfully long time, but "investment performance converges with business performance."
- Deployment: he expects more stock purchases in 2019 because business prices are "sky-high," while still hoping for an "elephant-sized acquisition." This is not a market call: "Charlie and I have no idea as to how stocks will behave next week or next year."
- The test is whether part of an attractive business is worth more than its market price.

## Buybacks, transparency and avoiding Wall Street games

- Buybacks below intrinsic value benefit continuing shareholders: if a departing partner is priced at 90 cents on the dollar, per-share value rises with each repurchase. "Blindly buying an overpriced stock is value-destructive."
- Shareholders must get the information to estimate value so no seller is misled; the letter exists to do that.
- Decisions are made for the shareholders who are staying, not those leaving, so he ignores current-quarter results.
- Berkshire prepares no monthly company-wide earnings reports or balance sheets, has no company-wide budget and no quarterly "number" to hit.
- He warns that meeting Wall Street expectations breeds bad behavior: quarter-end trade-loading, ignoring rising insurance losses, cookie-jar reserves. "Playing with the numbers 'just this once'" can become fraud, and subordinates copy the boss.

## Insurance float and funding

- Float is premiums collected upfront and claims paid later, invested meanwhile. Berkshire's grew from $39 million (1970) to $237 million (1980), $1,632 million (1990), $27,871 million (2000), $65,832 million (2010) and $122,732 million (2018).
- Any decline should be very gradual, at most 3% a year, and contracts can't create immediate demands on cash. If premiums exceed expenses and losses, Berkshire enjoys free money and gets paid to hold it.
- Competition often pushes the industry into underwriting losses and subnormal returns. Berkshire's financial strength gives it more investment flexibility. It had an underwriting profit in 15 of 16 years (the exception being a $3.2 billion pre-tax loss in 2017); the 16-year gain was $27 billion.
- Underwriting discipline: "All insurers give that message lip service. At Berkshire it is a religion, Old Testament style."
- A mega-catastrophe will come, possibly a cyber attack; Berkshire will take big losses but "will be looking to add business the next day."
- Debt: used sparingly. "Rational people don't risk what they have and need for what they don't have and don't need." Debt becomes fatal when credit vanishes. Most debt sits at railroad and energy subsidiaries, not guaranteed by Berkshire.
- Equity of $349 billion came from retaining all earnings; a 100% payout would have left the original $22 million.
- Other funding: deferred taxes ($50.5 billion), including $14.7 billion on unrealized stock gains and $28.3 billion from accelerated depreciation, both effectively interest-free loans.
- Financial fortress: $112 billion in T-bills and cash equivalents, plus $20 billion in other fixed income. Berkshire pledges to always hold at least $20 billion in cash equivalents. "I will never risk getting caught short of cash."

## GEICO, Tony Nicely and management

- In the mid-1970s GEICO nearly went bankrupt after underpricing its policies. Warren backed new CEO Jack Byrne and bought about a third of the company, later about half through GEICO's buybacks "without our spending a dime." The half cost $47 million.
- In 1995 Berkshire paid $2.3 billion for the other half, about 50 times the cost of the first half.
- Under Tony Nicely, GEICO became the No. 2 US auto insurer with sales up 1,200%, $15.5 billion in pre-tax underwriting profit, and float from $2.5 billion to $22.1 billion. Warren estimates Tony added more than $50 billion to intrinsic value.
- He praises Tony as a model manager who helps 40,000 associates find abilities they didn't know they had. He succeeded with Bill Roberts, whom Warren has watched for decades.
- Early 2018: Ajit Jain took over insurance and Greg Abel all other operations. Berkshire is "far better managed" than when Warren alone supervised. He says Berkshire blood flows through their veins.

## The American Tailwind and temperament

- Warren's first investment was in March 1942, age 11: three shares of Cities Service preferred for $114.75. Over 77 years, a no-fee S&P 500 fund with dividends reinvested would have turned it into $606,811 pre-tax (5,288 for 1). A tax-free $1 million would be about $5.3 billion.
- Paying 1% a year to helpers would cut that to $2.65 billion: the 11.8% annual return becomes 10.8%.
- The national debt rose roughly 400-fold in that period. Buying 3¼ ounces of gold with $114.75 would be worth about $4,200 now, under 1% of the stock outcome. "The magical metal was no match for the American mettle."
- Growth came through wars, a 21% prime rate, a presidential resignation, collapsing home values and financial panic, across seven Republican and seven Democratic presidents. All the scary headlines are now history.
- Saving and retained earnings drive prosperity for nations as for Berkshire. Berkshire's success is largely "The American Tailwind," and boasting of doing it alone is "beyond arrogance." He hopes to invest across borders too, but expects America to supply most gains over the next 77 years.
- He and Charlie love their jobs: they do what they find interesting with people they like and trust.
