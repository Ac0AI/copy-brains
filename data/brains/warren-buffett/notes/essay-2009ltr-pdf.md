## Berkshire measures itself against the S&P 500, using book value as a proxy

- Warren picked the S&P 500 as the bogey because shareholders "at virtually no cost" can match it with an index fund. His question: "Why should they pay us for merely duplicating that result?"
- A fixed, unbending yardstick stops managers from "seeing where the arrow of performance lands and then painting the bull's eye around it."
- The ideal measure is the change in per-share intrinsic value, but it can't be calculated with precision. Per-share book value is the crude proxy. Book value understates intrinsic value at most companies, and Berkshire's insurance business shows a huge gap.
- 2009: net worth rose $21.8 billion, and per-share book value rose 19.8%. Since 1965 book value went from $19 to $84,487, which is 20.3% compounded annually.
- Market price compounded at 22% a year over the same 45 years. Market gain was 801,516% against 434,057% for book value. Berkshire shares sold at a discount to book in 1965 (underearning textile assets) and now sell at a premium to its first-class businesses.
- Market prices alone can mislead over a decade. Starting prices can be "nosebleed" levels, as Microsoft and GE found.
- Track record: in all 41 five-year periods from 1965-69 to 2005-09, Berkshire's book value gain beat the S&P's.
- Berkshire beat the S&P in all eleven years the S&P was negative. Warren says "our defense has been better than our offense."
- The downside: the advantage shrinks as size grows. "Huge sums forge their own anchor."

## What Berkshire doesn't do: invert, avoid, and stay liquid

- Warren cites Charlie Munger: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there." This is Jacobi's "Invert, always invert."
- **Avoid businesses whose futures can't be evaluated, however exciting the product.**
  - Autos (1910), aircraft (1930) and TV sets (1950) had obvious growth. Competition then decimated almost all entrants, and survivors "tended to come away bleeding."
  - Seeing growth doesn't tell you the profit margins and returns on capital once competitors battle.
  - Berkshire sticks with businesses whose profit picture for decades is reasonably predictable. He still expects mistakes.
- **Never depend on "the kindness of strangers."**
  - Too-big-to-fail isn't a fallback. Cash needs should be "dwarfed by our own liquidity," refreshed by earnings from many businesses.
  - At the peak of the September 2008 crisis Berkshire put in $15.5 billion. $9 billion bolstered capital at three American businesses. $6.5 billion funded the Wrigley purchase.
  - The cost is steep: $20 billion-plus of cash equivalents earns "a pittance." But "we sleep well."
- **Don't woo Wall Street.** He wants partners who buy a business they understand and agree with its policies. Common goals make a good owner-manager "marriage."
- Berkshire posts financials early on weekends so owners can digest them in a non-trading period.
- Warren prefers direct, unabridged communication. A 2008 letter line ("the economy will be in shambles... but that conclusion does not tell us whether the market will rise or fall") was reported with only the first half. The Dow was 7,063 that day and 10,428 at year-end.

## Decentralized management: pay the visible cost of bad decisions, avoid bureaucracy

- Subsidiaries run on their own with little supervision or monitoring.
- The cost: Berkshire is sometimes late spotting management problems, and some decisions would have been made differently.
- The gain: an owner-oriented attitude "invaluable and too seldom found in huge organizations."
- "We would rather suffer the visible costs of a few bad decisions than incur the many invisible costs that come from decisions made too slowly – or not at all – because of a stifling bureaucracy."
- After BNSF, Berkshire has about 257,000 employees and hundreds of operating units. It will not become a monolith with committees, budget presentations and layers of management.
- Warren and Charlie limit themselves to four jobs: allocating capital, controlling enterprise risk, choosing managers and setting their compensation.
- Risk control is never delegated. Warren personally initiates and monitors every derivatives contract (with small exceptions at a few subsidiaries). "If Berkshire ever gets in trouble, it will be my fault. It will not be because of misjudgments made by a Risk Committee or Chief Risk Officer."

## Insurance float and the economics of property-casualty

- P/C insurance has been Berkshire's growth engine. The P/C companies are carried at $15.5 billion above net tangible assets, but are worth far more.
- Insurers collect premiums upfront and pay claims later (sometimes over decades). The held money is "float" that is invested for Berkshire. Float stays stable relative to premium volume, so it grows as business grows.
- With an underwriting profit, Berkshire gets "the use of free money" and is "paid for holding it."
- Competition usually pushes the industry to an underwriting loss, which is what it pays to hold float. Catastrophe-heavy years can wipe out float income.
- The industry's return on tangible equity has lagged the S&P 500 for decades. Berkshire's results come from outstanding managers running unusual businesses.
- Float grew from $16 million in 1967 to $62 billion at the end of 2009 ($61.9 billion). Berkshire has had seven straight years of underwriting profit. He expects profit in most, but not all, future years, which would make the float cost-free.
- Underwriting profit and year-end float in 2009 (2008 in brackets):
  - General Re: $477M profit (342), float $21.0B
  - BH Reinsurance: $349M (1,324), float $26.2B
  - GEICO: $649M (916), float $9.6B
  - Other primary: $84M (210), float $5.1B
  - Total: $1,559M (2,792)
- **GEICO:**
  - Run by Tony Nicely, who joined at 18.
  - Advertising budget is $800 million, close to twice the runner-up.
  - Market share rose from 2.5% to 8.1% since the 1996 control purchase, a net seven million policyholders.
  - Berkshire bought about one-third for $47 million in 1976-80, then the remaining 50% for $2.3 billion in January 1996, "about 50 times the cost of our original purchase."
  - GEICO went from sixth to third largest auto insurer, with float from $2.7 billion to $9.6 billion. It had an underwriting profit in 13 of 14 years.
  - Its edge is "low-cost producer" status. Customers stay for service and price.
- **Ajit Jain** (joined 1985) built the reinsurance operation with just 30 people.
  - He writes billion-dollar limits and keeps every dime of the risk.
  - He took over Lloyds liabilities with a single $7.1 billion premium.
  - In 2009 he negotiated a life reinsurance contract that could bring $50 billion of premium over about 50 years.
  - "If Charlie, I and Ajit are ever in a sinking boat... swim to Ajit."
- **General Re** (Tad Montross) had an outstanding underwriting year and large float per dollar of premium. It reached 100% ownership of Cologne Re.

## Mistakes and own-the-error examples

- **GEICO credit card.**
  - Warren thought GEICO policyholders would be good credit risks.
  - They got business "of the wrong type."
  - Pre-tax losses were about $6.3 million. The $98 million receivables portfolio sold for 55 cents on the dollar, an additional $44 million loss.
  - GEICO's managers had warned him. He wrote: "I was just older."
- **NetJets.**
  - In eleven years of ownership the company had an aggregate pre-tax loss of $157 million.
  - Debt rose from $102 million to $1.9 billion in April 2009. Without Berkshire's guarantee it "would have been out of business."
  - Warren: "I failed you in letting NetJets descend into this condition."
  - Dave Sokol became CEO in August. Debt fell to $1.4 billion, and the company turned solidly profitable after a $711 million loss in 2009.
  - Safety and service standards stay at the top. Warren and his family fly NetJets on the same terms as other owners: "we eat our own cooking."
- **Bond opportunity missed.** Corporate and municipal bonds were "ridiculously cheap" relative to Treasuries in the crisis. Warren bought some but "should have done far more." His lesson: "Big opportunities come infrequently. When it's raining gold, reach for a bucket, not a thimble."

## Capital-intensive businesses: utilities, railroads, and the shift in appetite

- Berkshire owns 89.5% of MidAmerican Energy Holdings (Dave Sokol, Greg Abel; Walter Scott family is a major owner). 2009 net earnings were $1,157 million ($1,071 million applicable to Berkshire).
- It owns UK utilities, Iowa and western US utilities, and pipelines carrying about 8% of US gas.
- MidAmerican has paid no dividend in ten years. In the last three years the Iowa and Western utilities earned $2.5 billion and spent $3 billion on wind.
- Regulated utilities and BNSF share traits:
  - Essential services.
  - Heavy investment far above depreciation for decades.
  - Long planning horizons.
  - Dependence on regulators who allow a fair return.
  - Warren calls the arrangement a "social compact." If either side shirks, both suffer.
- BNSF results will be reported in this regulated utility section. Both businesses use substantial debt not guaranteed by Berkshire and retain most earnings.
- Warren's view shifted: "In earlier days, Charlie and I shunned capital-intensive businesses." Still, "the best businesses by far for owners continue to be those that have high returns on capital and that require little incremental investment to grow."
- Because Berkshire will generate ever more cash, it is now willing to buy businesses that need large capital spending, provided they can reasonably earn decent returns on incremental capital. He expects "above-average, though certainly not spectacular, returns."

## Stock as currency: why undervalued shares make bad acquisition currency

- Issuing about 95,000 shares (6.1% of shares outstanding) for BNSF was distasteful. "If we wouldn't dream of selling Berkshire in its entirety at the current market price, why in the world should we 'sell' a significant part of the company at that same inadequate price?"
- Buying with undervalued stock hurts your shareholders. Example:
  - Companies A and B are both worth $100 per share but trade at $80.
  - A's CEO offers 1.25 A shares per B share.
  - B's holders end up with 55.6% of the combined company and A's holders with 44.4%.
  - The CEO still gains a bigger company, "where size tends to correlate with both prestige and compensation."
- If the acquirer's stock is overvalued, using it works to its advantage. Bubbles lead to serial stock issuance by promoters spending "counterfeit money" in "air-for-assets" deals, as in the late 1960s.
- BNSF: sellers valued the offer at $100 per share. 40% was paid in Berkshire stock, but only about 30% of the total cost counted Berkshire's earlier cash purchases of BNSF shares.
- It was a close decision, justified by deploying $22 billion of cash in a business he understood and liked for the long term, run by Matt Rose. More stock would have made it senseless.
- Board advice: bankers detail why the target is worth more than its market price, but never value what is given up when stock pays. Warren suggests directors hire a second advisor, paid only if the deal fails, to argue against it. Otherwise: "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut."
- Anecdote:
  - A well-run bank trading near book value at a very low P/E bought a much smaller bank for nearly three times book value in stock.
  - The excuse was "we need to show that we are in the hunt."
  - Charlie's reply: "Are we supposed to applaud because the dog that fouls our lawn is a Chihuahua rather than a Saint Bernard?"

## Investing in fear, derivatives, and CEO accountability

- Cash equivalents fell from $44.3 billion entering 2008 to $30.6 billion at year-end 2009 ($8 billion earmarked for BNSF), after $17 billion of retained operating earnings.
- Warren: "A climate of fear is their best friend. Those who invest only when commentators are upbeat end up paying a heavy price for meaningless reassurance."
- "What counts in investing is what you pay for a business – through the purchase of a small piece of it in the stock market – and what that business earns in the succeeding decade or two."
- Preferred-style positions bought in 18 months: Dow, GE, Goldman Sachs, Swiss Re and Wrigley, cost $21.1 billion, carried at $26.0 billion, paying about $2.1 billion a year in dividends and interest.
- Big 2009 stock sales: ConocoPhillips, Moody's, Procter & Gamble and Johnson & Johnson. He still expected those stocks to rise.
- Biggest holdings by market value: Coca-Cola $11.4B (cost $1,299M), Wells Fargo $9.0B (cost $7,394M), American Express $6.1B (cost $1,287M), Procter & Gamble $5.0B (cost $533M).
- Derivatives: Berkshire holds contracts it thinks are mispriced, as it does with stocks and bonds.
  - Derivatives float was about $6.3 billion.
  - Only a few contracts require collateral. At the low point the posting requirement was $1.7 billion.
  - Valuation swings are large (2009 quarterly: -1.517, +2.357, +1.732, +1.052 billion) but "neither cheer nor bother" him, since they don't affect cash or investment holdings.
  - Danger comes from extreme leverage or counterparty risk, which Berkshire avoids.
- CEO accountability:
  - Boards of huge financial institutions are "derelict" if CEOs don't bear full responsibility for risk control.
  - Shareholders lost 90% or more in failures, over $500 billion in the four biggest fiascos.
  - CEOs and directors "still live in grand style."
  - He argues they should pay "a heavy price" not reimbursable by the company or insurance. "Some meaningful sticks now need to be part of their employment picture."

## Operating businesses, housing, and Warren's view of his own luck

- **McLane:** pre-tax earnings of $344 million on $31.2 billion sales, slightly over one cent per dollar. Warren says its "prime asset... is Grady" Rosier.
- Several businesses grew profits as sales shrank, "always an exceptional managerial achievement." Among them were See's, Benjamin Moore, Borsheims and Nebraska Furniture Mart.
- **Marmon** had a record 13.5% pre-tax margin on sales down 27%. **Iscar** stayed profitable while competitors lost money.
- Construction-linked businesses (Shaw, Johns Manville, Acme Brick, MiTek) earned $227 million combined, down 82.5% from $1.295 billion in 2006.
- **Housing:**
  - US starts were 554,000 in 2009, the lowest in 50 years of data. Warren calls this "good news" for clearing the overhang.
  - Starts had run about 2 million a year against household formations of about 1.2 million.
  - Clayton's industry output fell from 382,000 units (1999) to 60,000 (2009). Fleetwood, Champion and Oakwood went bankrupt.
  - Conventional site-built buyers get about 5¼% mortgages through agency guarantees. Factory-built buyers pay about 9%. Warren flags that Berkshire has "a dog in this fight."
  - Clayton buyers performed well because they intended to live in the home, not resell or refinance. Loans were based on verified incomes ("we weren't making 'liar's loans'").
  - Berkshire uses its credit to support Clayton's mortgages and charges Clayton a fee ($116 million in 2009).
- Berkshire became a 50% owner of Berkadia Commercial Mortgage, a servicer of a $235 billion portfolio, with Leucadia's Joe Steinberg and Ian Cumming.
- Closing note: Warren and Charlie are "lucky beyond our dreams" and "would gladly pay substantial sums to have our jobs."
