## Performance, scale, and the "happy zone"

- 1994: net worth up $1.45 billion, or 13.9%. Per-share book value went from $19 to $10,083 over 30 years, 23% compounded.
- Warren and Charlie "make few predictions," but one is confident: future performance "won't come close to matching the performance of the past."
- The formula is unchanged: "the purchase at sensible prices of businesses that have good underlying economics and are run by honest and able people."
- "A fat wallet... is the enemy of superior investment results." Net worth was $11.9 billion versus about $22 million when he and Charlie took over.
- Berkshire now considers a security only if it can deploy at least $100 million in it. Charlie's rule: "If something is not worth doing at all, it's not worth doing well."
- Warren cites Ted Williams: to be a good hitter you need a good ball to hit. Swinging outside the "happy zone" turns a .344 hitter into a .250 hitter. Berkshire waits for opportunities well within its own happy zone.

## Ignoring forecasts, pricing instead of timing

- Berkshire ignores political and economic forecasts, "an expensive distraction."
- Over 30 years, nobody foresaw Vietnam's escalation, wage and price controls, two oil shocks, a presidential resignation, the Soviet collapse, a 508-point one-day Dow drop, or T-bill yields swinging between 2.8% and 17.4%.
- None of it dented Ben Graham's principles. Deferring capital deployment out of fear of unknowns would have been costly.
- The best purchases usually came when worries about some macro event peaked: "Fear is the foe of the faddist, but the friend of the fundamentalist."
- "We try to price, rather than time, purchases." It is folly to skip an outstanding business with a predictable long-term future over short-term worries about an unpredictable economy: "Why scrap an informed decision because of an uninformed guess?"
- The same logic applies to whole businesses and stock-market pieces. National Indemnity (1967), See's (1972), Buffalo News (1977), Nebraska Furniture Mart (1983) and Scott Fetzer (1986) were bought because they became available at acceptable prices, not because of the Dow or the Fed.

## Book value versus intrinsic value

- Book value is easy to calculate but of limited use. What counts is intrinsic value, "impossible to pinpoint but essential to estimate."
- Definition: "the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life." It is subjective and shifts with cash flow estimates and interest rates, but it is "the only logical way to evaluate the relative attractiveness of investments and businesses."
- In 1964 book value was $19.46, but the figure overstated intrinsic value because everything was tied up in sub-profitable textiles. Today many controlled businesses are worth far more than carrying value, so book value is a "significantly understated" tracking measure. In 1994 both rose about 13.9%.
- College analogy: cost (including foregone earnings) is "book value." Intrinsic value is the discounted excess lifetime earnings. Book value is meaningless as an indicator of intrinsic value.

## Scott Fetzer: a case study

- Bought at the start of 1986 for $315.2 million against $172.6 million of book value. The $142.6 million premium reflected a belief that intrinsic value was close to double book.
- 22 businesses then and now; main units World Book, Kirby and Campbell Hausfeld. No additions or disposals, no leverage, no sale-leasebacks, no receivable sales.
- Excess cash let it pay $125 million of dividends in 1986 on $40.3 million of earnings. Earnings rose to $79.3 million in 1994; book value ended 1994 at $94.0 million.
- Return on equity would have ranked 4th on the 1993 Fortune 500, and first excluding three companies whose earnings came from bankruptcy debt forgiveness. It was double the number 10 company's.
- Not from a cyclical peak, monopoly or leverage, but from CEO Ralph Schey's management.
- Accounting lesson: the purchase premium is written off through annual charges (to $54.2 million by 1994). Warren says these charges have no effect on cash or taxes and are not, in his view, an economic cost. Carrying value fell to $148.2 million, less than half the purchase level, while earnings roughly doubled.
- Ralph's formula: "in investing it is not necessary to do extraordinary things to get extraordinary results" (Ben Graham, 45 years earlier), and it holds in management too. Handle the basics, set the right goals, don't get diverted.
- Berkshire looks at performance, not the calendar. Many managers are over 65, and Warren (71) and Charlie (64) keep George Foreman's picture on their desks.

## Capital allocation and acquisitions

- Managers must act to increase per-share intrinsic value, including in share repurchases.
- Focusing on immediate EPS (or book value) dilution is dangerous. Analogy: an MBA student "merging" with a day laborer share-for-share would boost near-term earnings, which is silly. Differences in prospects, non-operating assets or capital structure matter more.
- Berkshire has rejected deals that would have raised near-term earnings but cut intrinsic value per share. Advice from Wayne Gretzky: "Go to where the puck is going to be, not to where it is."
- Most big acquisitions enrich acquiree shareholders, the acquirer's management and bankers, but usually reduce acquirer shareholders' wealth because it gives up more intrinsic value than it gets. Quote from John Medlin: "you are running a chain letter in reverse."
- A good business throws off more cash than it can use. Asking planners, consultants or bankers whether to acquire "is like asking your interior decorator whether you need a $50,000 rug."
- CEOs are selected partly for "animal spirits and ego." Advisors urging deals is not a push they need. A CEO friend's rationale: "Aw, fellas, all the other kids have one."
- Berkshire's system: managers first reinvest earnings in their businesses; surplus goes to Omaha for Warren and Charlie to deploy into whole or partial businesses they understand, with good sustainable economics, run by managers "we like, admire and trust."

## Compensation and alignment

- Pay managers on results of the unit they control, not Berkshire as a whole. Ralph is paid on Scott Fetzer. Tying him to Berkshire would be "capricious."
- Hold out large carrots tied directly to controllable results. Charge managers a high rate for incremental capital and credit them equally for capital released.
- The bonus is symmetrical: returns above the hurdle raise it, sub-standard returns cost the manager, so it pays well to send unused cash to Omaha.
- "Alignment means being a partner in both directions, not just on the upside." Many plans are "heads I win, tails you lose."
- Typical options flaw: no periodic increase in strike price for retained earnings, so a ten-year option, low payout and compounding can enrich a manager who merely "tread water." Low dividends even raise the option holder's profit.
- Ralph's arrangement was worked out in about five minutes, without lawyers or consultants, and has never changed. Other units' deals are similarly simple.
- Warren and Charlie, as the only people responsible for the whole business, are the only ones who logically should be paid on the whole. They don't want it, and would be happy in their jobs unpaid. Their pledge: no compensation giving more upside than downside, and personal fortunes stay overwhelmingly in Berkshire shares.

## Insurance float and super-cat

- Float is "money we hold but don't own," arising from prepaid premiums and the delay before claims are resolved. An underwriting loss is the cost of float. The business is valuable when float costs less than alternative funding.
- Average float rose from $17.3 million (1967) to $3,056.6 million (1994). Underwriting was profitable in 1993 and 1994, so float cost was negative. Warren cautions the insurance business "is not as good as it currently looks."
- Super-cat policies are few, large and non-standardized, requiring judgment (manager Ajit Jain). Berkshire's financial strength means buyers are certain it will pay after a mega-catastrophe, and it can write amounts others won't. Example: a $400 million short-term California earthquake policy written immediately; quotes up to $500 million the same day.
- Berkshire accepts lumpy results: insurers who "smooth" earnings follow sub-optimum strategies. They expect higher long-term returns from accepting volatility rather than pursuing predictability.
- Worst-case super-cat loss estimated at about $600 million after tax, slightly above annual earnings from other sources. Shareholders uncomfortable with that should sell now.
- Pricing on garden-variety policies fell as new capital chased premiums, so 1995 volume would probably be lower.

## Investing approach, look-through earnings and mistakes

- The big investment idea "can usually be explained in a short paragraph": a business with enduring competitive advantages, run by able, owner-oriented people, bought at a sensible price.
- "Degree-of-difficulty doesn't count." If one easy-to-understand, enduring factor drives value, the payoff equals that from a complex analysis.
- Consider adding to existing holdings first. In 1994 Berkshire added to Coca-Cola and American Express. Long familiarity with a company helps.
- Partial ownership analogies: Coke sold about 280 billion servings and earned under a penny on each, giving Berkshire's 7.8% stake about $200 million of "soft-drink earnings." Gillette's 7% market share was about $250 million of sales. Wells Fargo's 13% was a $7 billion "Berkshire Bank" earning about $100 million. "It's far better to own a significant portion of the Hope diamond than 100% of a rhinestone."
- Look-through earnings = operating earnings + retained investee earnings − hypothetical tax (14% rate). 1994: $1,030 million versus $841 million in 1993. Target intrinsic value growth is 15%, implying about $1.85 billion of look-through earnings by 2000.
- Amex: about 40% of Buffett Partnership capital in the mid-1960s after the salad-oil scandal; Berkshire owns just under 10% at a $1.36 billion cost.
- Mistakes: selling 10 million Cap Cities shares at $63 (price $85.25 at year-end, about $222.5 million forgone). Top "mistake du jour": the $358 million USAir preferred, an "unforced error" from sloppy analysis. Warren missed the rule that in an unregulated commodity business a company must lower costs to competitive levels or face extinction. Written down to $89.5 million (25 cents on the dollar), with the lesson: "You don't have to make it back the way that you lost it."
