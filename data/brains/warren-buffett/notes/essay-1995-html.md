## Two paths to ownership: negotiated whole-company purchases and stock-market part-interests

- Warren and Charlie Munger want a collection of companies, wholly or partly owned, with excellent economics and outstanding managers.
- His favorite deal is a negotiated purchase of 100% of such a business at a fair price. He is almost as happy when the market offers a modest percentage of an outstanding business at a pro-rata price well below what buying 100% would cost.
- This "double-barrelled approach" is an advantage over capital allocators who stick to a single course. His joke: being eclectic "doubles your chances for a date on Saturday night."
- Berkshire's marketable securities per share went from $4 (1965) to $159 (1975), $2,443 (1985) and $22,088 (1995), a 33.4% yearly growth rate. Per-share operating earnings excluding investment income went from $4.08 (1965) to $(6.48) (1975), $18.86 (1985) and $258.20 (1995), 14.7% yearly.
- None of this came from a master plan. He says they had no road map in 1965 and have none now.
- 1995 results: net worth rose $5.3 billion (45.0%), per-share book value 43.1%, and since 1964 book value per share grew from $19 to $14,426 (23.6% compounded). He gives the market's help most of the credit: "a rising tide lifts all yachts."
- Size is the main disadvantage: early on Berkshire needed good ideas, now it needs "good big ideas," and finding them gets harder in direct proportion to financial success.
- His best hope is to double per-share intrinsic value every five years on average, and he says they may fall far short.

## Why most acquisitions hurt the buyer, and how Berkshire tries to avoid that

- Warren believes most deals do damage to the acquirer's shareholders. Sellers' projections have "more entertainment value than educational value."
- He and Charlie never glance at seller-prepared projections. His story: a man tells a vet his horse sometimes walks fine and sometimes limps; the vet says "when he's walking fine, sell him." Sellers pick the time to sell, when the business is walking fine.
- The seller knows far more about the business than the buyer.
- Berkshire's advantages:
  - It has no strategic plan, so no pressure to proceed in an ordained direction, which "almost invariably" leads to silly prices.
  - It mentally compares every acquisition with dozens of other opportunities, including buying small pieces of the best businesses via the stock market. Managers focused on expansion seldom use this discipline.
- He cites Peter Drucker: "Dealmaking beats working... Dealmaking is romantic, sexy. That's why you have deals that make no sense."
- Diversification-by-consultant anecdote: a company with a glamorless but leading product bought many businesses after costly studies. Result: "When we started, we were getting 100% of our earnings from the original business. After ten years, we were getting 150%."
- Berkshire stock is useful currency for sellers who want to defer taxes indefinitely. Managers get extraordinary autonomy, and the "we are buying to keep" promise is credible given the ownership structure.
- Criteria offered in the letter: a business earning $25 million or more pre-tax, with good economics and excellent management. Discussions are confidential.
- He says most owners who care about their people are less likely to hold unpleasant surprises than a seller simply auctioning the business.

## Helzberg's and R.C. Willey: finding deals "by walking around," and retail's risks

- Helzberg's Diamond Shops: Barnett Helzberg, who owned four Berkshire shares, stopped Warren on a New York street in May 1994. The company had 134 stores in 23 states and sales of $10 million (1974), $53 million (1984) and $282 million (1994). Average store sales of about $2 million, far above competitors' similar-size stores, drive the profits.
- Warren says he would not have bought without CEO Jeff Comment: "Buying a retailer without good management is like buying the Eiffel Tower without an elevator." The deal was a tax-free stock exchange, the only kind that interested the seller. Barnett shared part of his proceeds with associates, which signaled to Warren that Berkshire would be treated right.
- R.C. Willey: Irv Blumkin of Nebraska Furniture Mart introduced CEO Bill Child. Warren wrote a letter stating his idea of value, they quickly agreed on a number, and the deal closed by mid-1995. Bill took over the business in 1954 when sales were about $250,000. 1995 sales were $257 million, with over 50% of Utah's furniture business and a sixth major store about to open.
- Retail is tough: he has watched many retailers enjoy terrific growth and high returns on equity and then "nosedive," often to bankruptcy. A retailer must stay smart every day because competitors copy and top whatever you do ("to coast is to fail").
- Contrast with "have-to-be-smart-once" businesses (e.g., an early network TV station), which could survive a shiftless nephew running them. For a retailer, that nephew is "an express ticket to bankruptcy."
- Management principle: managers operate autonomously with no second-guessing. Warren wants to behave toward managers as he likes owners to behave toward him, and he rejects the attitude of the alumnus telling the coach "I'm 100% with you - win or tie."
- How many reports can he handle? "If I have one person reporting to me and he is a lemon, that's one too many," while with managers like these the number can be almost unlimited.
- He says many managers don't have to work for a living but perform for the same reason wealthy golfers stay on the tour, and that no key manager has left Berkshire to join another employer between 1965 and 1995.

## GEICO: the history, the moat, and the lesson of selling a wonderful business

- Warren studied under Ben Graham at Columbia in 1950-51, then found Graham was chairman of GEICO. On a Saturday in January 1951 he took a train to Washington and pounded on the locked door. Lorimer "Davy" Davidson spent about four hours teaching him how insurance works and what lets one company excel.
- Davy's key point: GEICO's direct marketing gave it an enormous cost advantage over agent-based competitors, who couldn't give up their distribution.
- In 1951 he bought 350 GEICO shares in four purchases for $10,282, more than 50% of his net worth at the time of the last purchase. By year-end the stake was worth $13,125, over 65% of his net worth.
- He sold in 1952 for $15,259 to switch into Western Insurance Securities (about one times earnings). The GEICO shares he sold grew to about $1.3 million over the next 20 years. The lesson: "the inadvisability of selling a stake in an identifiably-wonderful company."
- In the early 1970s GEICO under-estimated claims costs, underpriced policies and nearly went bankrupt. Jack Byrne became CEO in 1976 and took drastic remedial steps. Berkshire bought heavily in the second half of 1976. By year-end 1980 it had invested $45.7 million for 33.3% of the company. The stake rose to about 50% by 1995 because GEICO repurchased shares.
- In 1995 Berkshire agreed to pay $2.3 billion for the half it didn't own: "a steep price," but it buys full ownership of a growing enterprise that is exceptional for the same reasons as in 1951.
- Key people: Tony Nicely (insurance operations, 34 years at GEICO, "brains, energy, integrity and focus") and Lou Simpson (investments: 22.8% annual return versus 15.7% for the S&P, 1980-95; concentrated, conservative approach like Berkshire's). Lou's presence also means a professional would be available to manage investments if something happened to Warren and Charlie.
- The moat: rock-bottom operating costs. Underwriting and loss adjustment expenses fell to 23.6% of premiums in 1995. Warren says "I look for economic castles protected by unbreachable 'moats,'" and GEICO's moat widened.
- GEICO insured about 3.7 million cars and was the seventh largest US auto insurer. The deal added nearly $3 billion of float.

## Insurance float: how it works and when it is valuable

- Float is "money we hold but don't own." It arises because premiums are prepaid and claims take time to be reported and resolved.
- Underwriting loss is the cost of float. An insurance business is profitable over time if its cost of float is below what the company would otherwise pay for funds, and has negative value if the cost is above market rates.
- Berkshire's float grew at 20.7% compounded from 1967. Average float was $17.3 million (1967 profit year), $237 million (1980), $1,637 million (1990) and $3,607 million (1995). The cost of float was less than zero in 1993, 1994 and 1995 (underwriting profits), against a 5.95% long-term government bond yield at year-end 1995.
- Profitability depends on three things: what assets earn, what liabilities cost, and leverage. Berkshire's liabilities cost very little because float came on advantageous terms; for many other insurers the cost exceeds the float's worth, making leverage a disadvantage.
- Warren's equity-equivalence argument: replacing $3.4 billion of float with $3.4 billion of equity would have meant more shares, equal assets and lower earnings, since the float actually threw off profits.
- The float he calls "free": nearly all of the table's float growth was developed internally, whereas GEICO's float was bought at a very substantial price.

## Super-cat and large-risk insurance: pricing discipline, volatility, and alignment

- Berkshire sells policies that insurers and reinsurers buy against mega-catastrophes. Warren expects large profits in most years and occasionally a huge loss, so measuring attractiveness takes many years. He cautions that three good years (1993-95) will be "at least partially offset" by some terrible year.
- Ajit Jain also writes one-off large-risk coverage: Mike Tyson's life (declining fight by fight), Lloyd's against over 225 "names" dying during the year, and the launch and one-year orbit of two Chinese satellites. Policies offered carried up to $1 billion of potential loss; the largest accepted coverage was $400 million.
- Why clients come to Berkshire: unmatched financial strength, the fastest quotes, and the largest limits. Competitors lay off much of their business via reinsurance, which reduces flexibility and reaction time.
- Warren and Charlie accept volatility: "we prefer a lumpy 15% to a smooth 12%." Most managers opt for smoothness, which leaves Berkshire a competitive advantage. They still monitor aggregate exposure so the worst case stays at a level they are comfortable with.
- Many primary insurers writing many small property policies face an uncapped aggregate risk that could blow through their reinsurance covers. Berkshire's worst case is large but capped.
- He will not knowingly write business at inadequate rates, even as super-cat prices weaken. Berkshire learned this in the early 1970s: "A bad reinsurance contract is like hell: easy to enter and impossible to exit." Berkshire "paid a heavy tuition" for his education in reinsurance, and GEICO had a similar disaster in the early 1980s.
- Alignment: Lloyd's problems show that people writing insurance must share the downside as well as the upside with those supplying capital. The joke he tells: the CEO saying things could be worse, "It could be my money." Warren's version: "At Berkshire, it's our money."
- Other insurance results: National Indemnity's traditional business had a combined ratio of 84.2 (three-year average 85.6, run by Don Wurster); the homestate operation under Rod Eldred had 81.4 (three-year 82.4); California workers' comp under Brad Kinstler lost renewals rather than accept inadequate rates and still earned an underwriting profit; Central States Indemnity under John Kizer grew premiums 23% and underwriting profit 59%.

## Convertible preferreds, mistakes, and the Gillette lesson

- Berkshire made five private convertible preferred purchases during 1987-91 (Champion, First Empire, Gillette, Salomon, USAir). They were valued mainly as fixed-income securities, with the conversion option as a "kicker."
- Warren admits he got three predictions wrong or only partly right. Returns "moderately" beat medium-term fixed income only because of one issue (Gillette). They did not produce returns like those from wonderful businesses. His claim that it was almost impossible to lose money on them gave him "well-deserved heartburn."
- Gillette was his biggest mistake of a kind never shown on financial statements. Berkshire paid $600 million in 1989 for preferred convertible into 48 million split-adjusted shares; buying common directly with the same money could have meant about 60 million shares, possibly at a 5%+ discount (the common then traded around $10.50). He estimates he would have been better off at year-end 1995 by about $625 million minus roughly $70 million of extra dividends. He chose preferred because he was "far too clever."
- Champion: he and Charlie have never had a conviction about the paper industry, and in 54 years of investing he cannot recall owning a paper producer's common stock. They converted ahead of a call and sold the shares back to the company at a modest discount, for a gain of about 19% after tax over six years.
- First Empire: to be called March 31, 1996, and Berkshire will convert and keep the common because he is "comfortable owning stock in well-run banks" and thinks highly of CEO Bob Wilmers.
- Salomon: he took the position expecting no special insight into investment banking and later served as interim chairman, giving the holding far more management time than its economic significance. The Dow doubled after the purchase, and the brokerage group did equally well, so he grades his decision to value the conversion option "very poor." The 9% dividend remains attractive, 20% of the issue is redeemed each year from 1995-99 ($140 million taken in 1995), and he thinks the conversion right may still prove valuable.
- USAir: results improved, but the company is a weak credit. The preferred was carried at 60% of par; unpaid dividends compound at 5% over prime.
- Net: leaving Gillette aside, the preferreds as a group earned after-tax returns no better than the medium-term fixed-income issues they replaced.

## Common stock holdings, "Rip Van Winkle" patience, and costly sales

- Year-end 1995 holdings (cost / market): Coca-Cola 100 million shares ($1,298.9 million / $7,425 million), Gillette ($600 million / $2,502 million), Capital Cities/ABC 20 million shares ($345 million / $2,467.5 million), GEICO ($45.7 million / $2,393.2 million), American Express ($1,392.7 million / $2,046.3 million), Wells Fargo ($423.7 million / $1,466.9 million), Freddie Mac ($260.1 million / $1,044 million). Total common stocks: cost $5,745.1 million, market $22,000.3 million.
- Five of the six top positions were left untouched in 1995, which he calls "Rip Van Winkle mode." The exception was American Express, where Berkshire raised its stake to about 10%.
- Disney: he first became interested in 1966, when its market value was under $90 million against about $21 million pre-tax earnings in 1965 and more cash than debt, "a company selling at only five times rides" (Pirates of the Caribbean cost $17 million). Buffett Partnership bought at a split-adjusted 31 cents per share and he sold in 1967 at 48 cents; the stock was $66 in 1996.
- Cap Cities/ABC: the Disney deal let holders swap for one Disney share plus $65 per Cap Cities share, with options for all stock or all cash. Berkshire sought stock. He had sold one-third of the Cap Cities holding two years earlier for $635 million versus the $1.27 billion those shares would bring in the merger.
- Capital Cities CEO Tom Murphy: "as fine an executive as I have ever seen." He cites Murphy's favoring owners when personal interests diverged and says he likes managers "whom I would love to have as a sibling, in-law, or trustee of my will." His advice: "If Murph should elect to run another business, don't bother to study its value - just buy the stock."
- The Disney-Cap Cities deal began with a chance meeting with Michael Eisner in Sun Valley on July 14, 1995, which Warren says is the deal's timing, not its logic: a deal that "makes so much sense" would likely have happened anyway.

## Operating-business trends, honest reporting, and the B-share recapitalization

- Charlie's dictum: "Just tell me the bad news; the good news will take care of itself." Warren applies it by reporting three declining businesses:
  - Shoes: a cyclical downturn, not a secular one; competitors earned marginal profits or worse, so Berkshire's relative position held or widened.
  - Buffalo News: still strong, but newspapers have "lost still another notch" from their once bullet-proof franchise and he expects gradual erosion over time.
  - World Book: facing CD-ROM and on-line competition; profitable but trending the wrong way, so it changed distribution, expanded electronic products and cut overhead.
- 1995 earnings: operating earnings $814.7 million pre-tax ($600.2 million after tax); total earnings across all entities $1,008.8 million pre-tax ($725.2 million after tax). Headquarters staff grew only from 11 to 12 despite 11,000+ new employees from three acquisitions.
- Recapitalization: Class A common remains; a new Class B has 1/30th of an A share's economic rights, 1/200th of the vote, no participation in the shareholder-designated charity program, and no conversion back to A. At least $100 million of B shares were to be offered for cash.
- Purpose: to make expense-laden "clone" unit trusts holding only Berkshire stock unmerchandisable. Warren fears brokers selling them to unsophisticated buyers drawn by Berkshire's record and publicity, a recipe for disappointed investors.
- Valuation candor: at $36,000 per share and a $43 billion market cap, "Berkshire is selling at a price at which Charlie and I would not consider buying it." He adds that over the past five years the stock "outperformed the business" and warns that this cannot persist.
- Shareholder philosophy: he wants shareholders of any size who are knowledgeable, share the long-term perspective and understand the limits imposed by a large capital base. He would ideally want the market price to track intrinsic value precisely, so every owner benefits in proportion to the business's progress.
- Tripling a $1,000 stake would require turning $43 billion into $129 billion (roughly General Electric's market cap), which Berkshire cannot come close to doing.
- Communication: Berkshire has no investor relations department and does not use analysts to disseminate "guidance." The annual meeting is its direct owner communication channel, and 1995's drew about 3,200 in the main room plus overflow, with Warren and Charlie answering questions for around five hours.
