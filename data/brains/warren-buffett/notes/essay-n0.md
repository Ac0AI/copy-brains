## Early Letters: Market Stance and Not Forecasting the Market

- Warren makes "no attempt to forecast either business or the stock market"; his effort goes to "finding undervalued securities."
- In 1957 he judged blue-chip stocks priced above intrinsic value and thought it very unlikely today's levels would "be thought of as cheap five years from now."
- Policy by market level: when the market is cheap, put capital in general issues (possibly with some borrowed money). When it rises, trim general issues as profits appear and increase work-outs.
- He warns that "widespread public belief in the inevitability of profits from investment in stocks will lead to eventual trouble," and that the longer such speculators stay, "the greater the reaction from it."
- In 1959 he wrote he would "rather sustain the penalties resulting from over-conservatism than face the consequences of error, perhaps with permanent capital loss, resulting from the adoption of a 'New Era' philosophy where trees really do grow to the sky."
- On long-run expectations (1961): over a long period the Dow will likely produce about 5% to 7% a year compounded from dividends plus price gain. Over the next ten years there will be a few years of +20-25%, a few of the same size negative, and a majority in between. He does not think the sequence matters to a long-term investor.

## The Three Categories: Generals, Work-Outs and Control Situations

**Generals (undervalued securities)**
- No say in corporate policy and no timetable for the undervaluation to correct.
- Historically the largest category, and "more money has been made here than in either of the other categories."
- Typically 5-10% of total assets in each of five or six generals, with smaller positions in another ten or fifteen.
- They are cheap because they lack glamour or an immediate catalyst, which gives "a comfortable margin of safety in each transaction," reinforced by diversification.
- They move with the Dow in a downturn; being cheap does not stop a stock falling.
- His timing of purchases was "considerably better than our timing of sales." He is content selling "at some intermediate level between our purchase price and what we regard as fair value to a private owner."

**Work-outs**
- A work-out depends on a specific corporate action (sale, merger, liquidation, tender, reorganization, spin-off) rather than a general rise in the stock. The risk is that "something will upset the applecart," not that markets fall.
- Typically 10-15 work-outs at once, at different stages. Returns, excluding borrowed money, usually fall in the 10% to 20% range.
- He uses borrowed money to offset a portion of the work-out book because of its safety, with a self-imposed borrowing cap of 25% of partnership net worth, used only against work-outs.
- Share of portfolio shifted from about 70-30 generals to work-outs at end 1956 to about 85-15 at end 1957. When work-outs were hard to find on good terms, he tried "to create my own work-outs" by building large positions in several undervalued securities.

**Control situations**
- Either control the company or take a very large position and influence policy. Measure them over several years; in a given year they may produce nothing.
- It helps for the stock to stay stagnant while being accumulated, and such holdings have little in common with Dow behavior.
- Sometimes a general is bought hoping it becomes a control situation. If the price jumps before a large stake is built, he sells at a profit instead.

## Accumulating a Position: Commonwealth Trust Example (1958)

- Commonwealth Trust Co. of Union City, NJ: intrinsic value about $125 per share on a conservative basis, earnings about $10 per share, no cash dividend (for good reasons), price about $50. Assets about $50 million.
- A larger bank owned 25.5% and had long wanted a merger. That was blocked for personal reasons, but evidence suggested it would not last forever.
- He listed three attractions: strong defensive characteristics, solid value building at a satisfactory pace, and evidence the value would eventually be unlocked, "although it might be one year or ten years." If ten, value might reach about $250 per share.
- Over about a year the partnership bought roughly 12% of the bank at an average of about $51, becoming the second-largest holder.
- "Obviously it was definitely to our advantage to have the stock remain dormant in price." During acquisition he wants the stock to do nothing or fall, so a fair proportion of the portfolio is always in a "sterile stage" that holds back relative results in a rising market.
- With about 300 holders and roughly two trades a month, general market activity barely affected the price. Competing buyers pushed it to about $65, where he stopped buying. Small orders move inactive stocks, which is why "leakage" about holdings must be avoided.
- He sold at $80 (market quote about 20% lower) because another situation offered a better price-to-value gap: at $80 vs. intrinsic value of $135 was not the same as $50 vs. $125.
- The replacement was about 25% of assets, and being the largest holder shortens the time to correct the undervaluation.
- He cautions that the year a situation like this realizes a profit is "to a great extent, fortuitous," so a single year is a poor basis for judging long-term results.

## Sanborn Map: A Control Investment Case Study (1960-61)

**The business and the mispricing**
- Sanborn published and continuously revised detailed city maps for fire insurers. Roughly 75 years of near-monopoly: profit every year, almost no recession sensitivity, no need for selling.
- Competition from "carding" cut after-tax map profits from over $500,000 a year in the late 1930s to under $100,000 in 1958 and 1959.
- Since the early 1930s retained earnings built an investment portfolio of about $2.5 million cost (roughly half bonds, half stocks); it was worth about $7 million against sales of about $2 million a year.
- In 1938 (Dow 100-120) Sanborn traded at $110 with about $20 per share of investments, implying $90 for the map business. In 1958 (Dow about 550) it traded at $45 with about $65 of investments, so the map business was valued at minus $20 and the portfolio at about 70 cents on the dollar.
- Reproducing the map information would have cost "tens of millions of dollars," and over $500 million of fire premiums were still written by mapping companies.

**Why it was stuck**
- Board problems: of 14 directors, nine were insurance-industry men who together held 46 of 105,000 shares (largest holding ten shares). Dividends were cut five times in eight years with no sign anyone proposed cutting salaries or director and committee fees.
- The strong portfolio masked the need to rejuvenate the map business.

**What the partnership did**
- Bought the widow's 15,000-share block, then built holdings (with associates) to about 24,000 shares. Together with two other dissatisfied holders the group had about 46,000 shares.
- Goal: separate the investment portfolio from the map business, realize its fair value, and rebuild map earnings (Booz, Allen & Hamilton recommended a similar plan).
- To avoid a proxy fight and time delay, a plan took out every stockholder who wanted out at fair value, paid in portfolio securities. The SEC ruled favorably on its fairness. About 72% of the stock (50% of 1,600 holders) was exchanged.
- Result: the map business kept over $1.25 million in government and municipal bonds, a potential corporate capital gains tax of over $1 million was eliminated, and remaining holders had slightly higher asset value, higher earnings per share and a higher dividend.
- At its peak this was 35% of partnership net assets, "an unusually large percentage," justified because the holding was partly an investment trust bought at a discount to asset value, with the partnership the largest holder.
- Takeaways: secrecy about portfolio operations matters, and control situations "may occur very infrequently."

## Dempster Mill: Valuing a Controlled Private Holding (1961)

- First bought five years earlier as a generally undervalued security; a block became available, he joined the board about four years in, and got majority control in August 1961. "Many of our operations are not exactly of the 'overnight' variety."
- Partnership owned 70%, associates about 10%, about 150 other holders; no real market for the stock.
- Business: farm implements and water systems, sales about $9 million in 1961, only nominal profits against capital because of poor management and a tough industry.
- Book value about $75 per share, working capital about $50 per share, controlling interest acquired at an average of about $28, valued at year end at $35. It was 21% of partnership net assets.
- Valuation principle: the year-end value should not be "what we hope it would be worth, or what it might be worth to an eager buyer," but what the interest would bring "if sold under current conditions in a reasonably short period of time." This matters because new partners buy in and old partners sell on that price.
- Control positions will not rise just because large blue chips do, and in a raging bull market they feel like a hard way to make money. He valued the insulation they provide more at high market levels.

## Performance Yardstick: Par, Time Horizon and the Dow

**Yardstick**
- The benchmark is the Dow-Jones Industrial Average including dividends. "I believe in establishing yardsticks prior to the act; retrospectively, almost anything can be made to look good."
- Three years is "a very minimal test," with the best test a period where the Dow ends near where it began. One year, and especially six months, is too short; his own thinking is "much more geared to five year performance."
- If performance is poor, partners should withdraw; he would look for another place for his own money.
- Target: average about ten percentage points per year over the Dow. With the Dow at 5-7% a year, he hoped for 15-17% a year.

**Relative performance by market type**
- Expected: better in declining or flat markets, average to lagging in strong bull markets. "A year in which we declined 15% and the Average 30% [is] much superior to a year when both we and the Average advanced 20%."
- "The important thing is to be beating par; a four on a par three hole is not as good as a five on a par five hole."
- Illustrative expectations: market down 35-40%, partnership down 15-20%; market flat, up about ten points; market up 20% or more, "struggle to be up as much." He wants partners to agree "down in the pit of your stomach."
- As the market rises, holdings become more conservative: a growing portion is kept at least partly insulated from market behavior.

**Results (partnership gain before limited-partner split vs. Dow with dividends)**
- 1957: 10.4% vs. -8.4%
- 1958: 40.9% vs. 38.5%
- 1959: 25.9% vs. 19.9%
- 1960: 22.8% vs. -6.3%
- 1961: 45.9% vs. 22.2%
- Cumulative 1957-61: 251.0% vs. 74.3%. Pro-forma limited partners' gain over the same period: 181.6%.

**Why the Dow is a tough competitor**
- Of 38 comparable mutual funds from Wiesenberger's survey for 1946-60, 32 did worse than the Dow and 6 better, none by more than a few points a year.
- Warren says this is not an indictment of fund managers; his own results on huge sums with activity restrictions would likely be no better. The point is that beating the Dow is no pushover.

**Luck in the short run**
- In 1957 three partnerships gained about 6.2%, 7.8% and 25% purely because of when money arrived. This "emphasizes the importance of luck in the short run."

## Conservatism, Risk and Size

**True conservatism**
- Buying long bonds was thought conservative and produced market depreciation and lost buying power. Buying blue chips "almost regardless of price-earnings ratios, dividend yields" is, to him, "fraught with danger."
- "There is nothing at all conservative... about speculating as to just how high a multiplier a greedy and capricious public will put on earnings."
- "You will not be right simply because a large number of people momentarily agree with you. You will not be right simply because important people agree with you."
- "You will be right, over the course of many transactions, if your hypotheses are correct, your facts are correct, and your reasoning is correct. True conservatism is only possible through knowledge and reason."
- Conservatism is tested by performance in down markets, not by whether a portfolio looks conventional. Through 1961 the partnership never had a realized loss over 0.5% of 1% (one half of one percent) of net assets, with realized gains to realized losses roughly 100 to 1 (noting this reflected a mostly rising market).

**Size**
- For passive positions in thin markets, larger funds hurt (buying 10,000 shares is much harder than 100); for broad-market stocks the penalty is slight.
- For control situations, larger funds help: "A 'Sanborn Map' cannot be accomplished without the wherewithal." Competition thins as the price of entry rises, and bigger companies tend to have less concentrated ownership.
- He saw the two effects as roughly offsetting, and said 1960 and 1961 results would not have been better on 1956-57 sums.

## Partnership Structure and Incentives

- Profit split in the 1961 merger plan: the first 6% a year to limited partners on beginning capital at market, then any excess one-fourth to Warren and three-fourths to partners. Shortfalls below 6% carry forward against future earnings but not back, and no clawback of amounts previously credited to him.
- Alignment: the merged partnership would hold his entire marketable-securities investing. He and his family were barred from buying other marketable securities, so "my results will have to be directly proportional to yours." His family's stake was about $1.8 million of $7.18 million in net assets at the start of 1962 (Susie and he $1,025,000; other relatives $782,600).
- Earlier arrangements had three versions (6% interest with 1/3 to him, 4% with 1/4, none with 1/6).
- Practical terms: monthly payments at 6% a year with an option to reinvest, borrowing up to 20% of an interest at 6%, year-end add or withdraw in round $100s, minimum for new partners $25,000, overhead expected under 0.5% of net assets excluding interest and the Nebraska Intangibles Tax (0.4% of market value of securities).
- Results are measured market to market (liquidation value), not on realized or tax results.
- Secrecy: no discussion of individual holdings, because small buying orders move thinly traded stocks and leakage hurts accumulation.
