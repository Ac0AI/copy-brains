## Operating results versus owner earnings: the "iceberg" of non-controlled holdings

- Warren judges single-year managerial performance by operating earnings divided by beginning equity, with securities valued at cost. In 1980 operating earnings were $41.9 million (vs. $36.0 million in 1979); return on beginning equity was 17.8% (vs. 18.6%).
- Berkshire's insurance subsidiaries hold large stakes (under 20%) in companies that pay out little of their earnings. Accounting only counts dividends from such holdings, so undistributed earnings are ignored.
- In 1980 the retained portion of "our" earnings in those companies exceeded Berkshire's total reported operating earnings. Conventional accounting lets less than half of the earnings "iceberg" show above the surface.
- He argues "our economic well-being is determined by their earnings, not their dividends."
- The value of retained earnings depends on what they are used for and the earnings they later produce, not on whether Berkshire owns 100%, 50%, 20% or 1%: "It's the act that counts, not the actors." "If a tree grows in a forest partially owned by us, but we don't record the growth in our financial statements, we still own part of the tree."
- He would rather have unrecorded earnings put to good use in a 10%-owned company by a management he didn't hire than recorded earnings put into dubious projects, even by himself.
- Replacing the equity portfolio with long-term tax-free bonds would lift reported operating earnings by over $30 million a year; the idea "tempts us not at all."
- Example: Berkshire's insurers own about 3% of Kaiser Aluminum and 1 1/4% of Alcoa, worth about $13 million of 1980 earnings. That gives a larger economic interest in aluminum than in nearly any controlled business (perhaps 25% should be deducted for tax if converted to value and dividends).

## Reported earnings versus real earnings, and what retained earnings become

- He explains that controlled businesses can have reported earnings worth far less than 100 cents on the dollar, because reinvested earnings may earn nothing close to a market return and assets deteriorate unless reinvested.
- Small fractions of businesses with extraordinary reinvestment opportunities can have retained earnings worth far more than 100 cents on the dollar.
- Long-term performance is measured including all realized and unrealized capital gains. Long-term return on equity considerably exceeds the average yearly return because retained earnings of non-controlled holdings are translated into market value gains.
- That translation is uneven, unpredictable in timing, and unlikely to be precisely dollar-for-dollar. "A silly purchase price for a block of stock in a corporation can negate the effects of a decade of earnings retention." With sensible purchase prices, market recognition of retained earnings almost certainly follows.
- Book value per share (insurance-held equities at market) rose from $19.46 to $400.80 in sixteen years, 20.5% compounded annually, "despite many mistakes." A competitive batting average is possible despite many managerial strikeouts.
- Warren's "commercial" on buybacks: if a fine business sells far below intrinsic value, repurchasing shares is the most certain and profitable use of capital. Corporate acquisitions usually require paying a full price or more, while the stock market's auction nature often lets well-run companies buy parts of themselves at under 50% of the price of acquiring equal earning power through a negotiated deal.

## Inflation as a tax on capital and the hurdle rate for owners

- "Only gains in purchasing power represent real earnings on investment." Hamburger test: forgo ten hamburgers, receive dividends that after tax buy two, then sell for after-tax proceeds that buy eight. There is no real income, however much the investment appreciated in dollars: "You may feel richer, but you won't eat richer."
- High inflation acts as a tax on capital that makes much corporate investment unwise, measured by whether owners get a positive real return. The required "hurdle rate" of return on equity has risen sharply.
- Example: with 12% inflation, a business earning 20% on equity and distributing it all to owners in the 50% bracket leaves them with about 98% of their original purchasing power. They would be better off with stable prices and earnings of a few percent.
- Explicit income taxes alone can never turn a positive corporate return into a negative owner return. The inflation tax is not limited by reported income. At 16% inflation, owners of the 60%-plus of corporate America earning less than that would have negative real returns even with no income taxes on dividends and gains.
- Taxes are levied on nominal, not real, income, so owners pay tax on what would be deficits in constant dollars.
- At the inflation rates of the time, he believed individuals in medium or high tax brackets should expect no real long-term return from the average American corporation, even reinvesting all after-tax dividends.
- Berkshire has no corporate solution; "Inflation does not improve our return on equity."
- Truly indexed capital means return on equity rises with the price level without extra capital (including working capital); earnings increases from added investment don't count. A thrifty worker saving half his paycheck is not indexed, and neither is a high-saving company with flat return on equity. Few businesses come close, and Berkshire is not one.
- He reports that his short-term forecasts of stock or bond prices are useless: "The forecasts may tell you a great deal about the forecaster; they tell you nothing about the future."

## GEICO: a franchise with a fixable problem, not a turnaround

- GEICO was Berkshire's largest non-controlled holding: 7.2 million shares, about 33% of the company, cost $47 million (about half invested in 1976, most of the rest in 1980), market value $105.3 million.
- Insurance regulators required the vote to sit with an independent party, so the stake didn't qualify for investee accounting. Dividends gave a little over $3 million a year in reported earnings, but Warren estimated his share of earning power at about $20 million, so undistributed earnings from this holding alone could equal about 40% of Berkshire's reported operating earnings.
- He is as comfortable with GEICO management retaining the estimated $17 million as if it were in his own hands. In two years GEICO's buybacks cut share equivalents outstanding from 34.2 million to 21.6 million.
- He calls GEICO "the best of all investment worlds": a very important, hard-to-duplicate business advantage plus a management skilled in both operations and capital allocation (Jack Byrne).
- Turnarounds: after tracking hundreds of cases, "when a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for poor fundamental economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."
- GEICO and American Express (after the 1964 salad oil scandal) are distinguished from true turnarounds. They were one-of-a-kind franchises with a localized, excisable cancer that needed a skilled surgeon. GEICO's low-cost design in a huge auto insurance market was intact, submerged under financial and operating troubles.
- Buying a similar $20 million of earning power in a first-class business would cost at least $200 million through a negotiated purchase of a whole company. Owning 100% adds options (leverage, changing managers, directing cash flow, selling), but buying minority pieces of already well-run businesses at prices far below the entire-business value suits Berkshire's insurance model.

## Insurance industry conditions: the bond-loss trap and "amateur night" reinsurance

- The industry combined ratio rose from 100.6 in 1979 to an estimated 103.5 in 1980, with underwriting losses expected to mount significantly in 1981 and 1982. Warren recommended Chubb's annual report and Barbara Stewart's October 1980 analysis of competitive dynamics.
- Insurers may carry bonds at amortized cost regardless of market value. Many held long-term bonds worth two to three times net worth at cost; some large property-casualty companies had nominal or negative net worth at market values.
- Selling bonds forces recognition of losses and cuts stated net worth, so insurers become frozen in their investment posture, perhaps for a decade or more. For example, unwillingness to book losses may block a sensible shift from tax-exempt to taxable bonds.
- Policyholder and claimant money funds the bonds. If premium volume shrinks, liabilities shrink and bonds must be sold, turning unrealized losses into realized ones.
- The two options for a weak insurer: (1) price to exposure ("a dollar of premium for every dollar of expense cost plus expectable loss cost"), lose business, and be forced to sell bonds; or (2) "asset maintenance" underwriting, keeping terrible business to hold assets and praying. Warren says the second will be chosen, so no better day for underwriting comes while many large insurers do it. "Right behind having financial problems yourself, the next worst plight is to have a large group of competitors with financial problems that they can defer by a 'sell-at-any-price' policy."
- He says Berkshire's net worth relative to premium volume was the strongest among large stockholder-owned property-casualty groups. Capital and flexibility give "more staying power and more options," but not immunity. He admits the company also lost sums in bonds "because your Chairman was talking when he should have been acting," and that its asset and liability maturities were mismatched.
- Reinsurance has easy entry, large premiums paid in advance, and delayed loss reports and payments. Early on "the morning mail brings lots of cash and few claims," a feeling like an innocent receiving a first credit card. High interest rates are turning the market into "amateur night." A super catastrophe could cause a "bloodbath." Berkshire stayed active but expected no premium growth.

## Underwriting discipline and operating managers

- National Indemnity (Phil Liesche, Roland Miller, Bill Lyons) had record underwriting margins relative to the industry on flat volume. Warren expected lower volume in 1981 but promised no complaints, layoffs or salary cuts, since the discipline set by founder Jack Ringwalt "if suspended, probably could not be fully regained."
- Cypress (Milt Thornton) runs a workers' compensation operation that far outpaces competitors: "admired and copied, but not matched." Frank DeNardo of the California workers' comp division died at 37; Warren praised him as someone who "instinctively thought like an underwriter."
- Homestate operations had serious problems; Insurance Company of Iowa lost money every year since 1973 and was folded into Cornhusker Casualty. Kansas (Floyd Taylor) was strong.
- Warren expected a significant decline in 1981 insurance volume and a poorer result, though better than the industry's.
- Textiles: Waumbec Mills was closed, and he called it a "costly mistake" not to face reality sooner. New Bedford dropped about a third of its looms and abandoned high-volume lines with no product differentiation, which could not earn adequate returns over a full cycle.
- Ben Rosner's Associated Retail Stores turned all of its excellent earnings into cash in a poor retail year; the 50th birthday was March 7, 1981.

## Financing, acquisition preferences and judging a seller

- In August 1980 Berkshire sold $60 million of 12 3/4% notes due 2005, not for an immediate need but because opportunities would come, possibly when credit is extremely expensive or unavailable: "we want to have plenty of financial firepower."
- Acquisition preferences: businesses that generate cash, not consume it. With inflation, many companies must spend all internal funds just to maintain physical volume; Warren calls such operations "mirage-like" and stays wary of earnings that never convert into "no-strings-attached cash."
- Qualifying businesses are hard to find: of hundreds of acquisitions reported each year, only a handful would interest him. Policy is ample liquidity, moderate and properly structured debt, and abundant capital strength, even though this slightly reduces return on equity.
- On Gene Abegg, founder of Illinois National Bank, who died at 82: you learn a lot about a person when you buy a business and he stays on as an employee. A seller knows the business intimately and has many chances to mislead through omissions and ambiguities, and attitudes can change after the check clears. Gene "shot straight 100% of the time," laid out the negatives upfront, later told Warren about undisclosed valuable items, worked harder for Berkshire than for himself after selling at 71, and never delayed reporting a problem. He kept a fiduciary attitude toward other people's money, with enough cash on hand in the 1933 bank holiday to pay all depositors in full.
