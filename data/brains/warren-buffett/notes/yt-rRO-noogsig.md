## Choosing people: character over grades

- Warren frames a thought experiment: you may pick one classmate and receive 10% of their lifetime earnings, and you must also short one classmate and pay 10% of theirs.
- The winner is not the top-grades student. The qualities that matter are self-made: integrity, honesty, generosity, willingness to do more than your share.
- The one to short is the person who fails in character: claims credit for things they didn't do, cuts corners, can't be counted on.
- He hires for three things: "intelligence," "initiative or energy," and "integrity." If someone lacks integrity, "the first two will kill you," because "you want them lazy and dumb."
- Character is a choice built from habits: "the chains of habit are too light to be felt until they're too heavy to be broken." The time to form good habits is a student's age.
- Ben Graham and Ben Franklin decided in their teens what kind of person they admired and behaved that way. Warren says anyone can become the person they would buy 10% of.
- Career advice: work for whoever you admire most rather than padding a resume. Taking a job that "looks good" now and doing what you like "someday" is "saving up sex for your old age."

## Circle of competence and why industry growth is not investment return

- Warren expects to make money only in businesses whose economics he can foresee 10 or 20 years out. Wrigley is the example: gum habits and brands (Doublemint, Spearmint, Juicy Fruit) won't change.
- Seeing that an industry will transform society does not tell you who wins. The auto industry had about 2,000 companies; roughly three survive, and Studebaker, Nash, Kelvinator and Hudson were all once in the Dow.
- Airlines: over 400 aircraft companies existed in the 1920s-30s, including in Nebraska. Warren says that if there had been a capitalist at Kitty Hawk "he should have shot Orville down." Aggregate airline earnings through 1991 were less than zero while passenger counts rose every year.
- TV sets and radios follow the same pattern: huge products, no lasting U.S. manufacturers.
- Coca-Cola, by contrast, now sells roughly 1.1 billion servings a day, 117 years after it began.
- On internet and tech: he enjoys using Amazon and the computer, but "unless I know who's going to win I'm not interested in investing."
- What matters is knowing the perimeter of your circle, not its size. He quotes Tom Watson Sr.: "I'm no genius, but I'm smart in spots and I stay around those spots."

## Intrinsic value and the bird in the bush

- Intrinsic value is all the cash a business will deliver between now and Judgment Day, discounted at the proper rate.
- A bond prints its cash flows; a stock certificate does not. The analyst's job is to turn the stock into a bond by estimating what it will pay out.
- Applied to Coca-Cola at roughly $110-115 billion: would you lay out that sum for what the company delivers over the next two or three hundred years? Volume, analyst recommendations and chart shapes are irrelevant; only cash received matters.
- The same logic applies to farms, apartment houses and oil in the ground: you lay out cash now to get more cash later, and the questions are how much, when, and how sure.
- Berkshire at about $105 billion: can it distribute enough cash soon enough to justify the price at current rates? If you can't answer that, you are gambling, not investing.
- Aesop's "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" is incomplete: how many birds are in the bush, when do they come out, and how sure are you? At 15% rates you need two birds in five years; at 3% you can wait 20 years.
- For many internet companies "there weren't any birds in the bush," so he stays away from them.

## Mistakes: cigar butts, omission, and the punch card

- Buying Berkshire Hathaway was a mistake. Following Ben Graham's quantitative approach, he bought it below working capital per share, but it was a lousy textile business and 20 years later he was still running one.
- The "cigar butt" approach finds a soggy butt with one free puff left. It can make money but not big money. Now: "I would rather buy a wonderful business at a fair price than a fair business at a wonderful price."
- "Time is the friend of the wonderful business" and "the enemy of the lousy business." Compounding only works in good businesses.
- Other errors: a $2,000 stake in a Sinclair filling station at age 20 (opportunity cost he puts at about $6 billion), and a 1989 US Air preferred purchase that went into the red once his check cleared. He jokes about calling an "800 number" to talk himself out of airline stocks.
- His biggest mistakes are omission, not commission: things within his circle of competence he passed on while "sucking my thumb." He cites Fannie Mae, which he says could have been bought for practically nothing about 20 years earlier, costing Berkshire at least $5 billion.
- The punch card idea: imagine only 20 financial decisions in a lifetime. You would think hard about each and make fewer, bigger, better ones. Dabbling, especially in bull markets and with online trading, is the temptation to avoid.

## When to sell and how he thinks about partners

- Early on he had more ideas than money (stocks at two or even one times earnings), so he sold his least favorite holding to fund the next compelling one. Now Berkshire has more money than ideas, generating at least $5 billion a year, "a hundred million bucks every week."
- Berkshire's wholly owned businesses (See's Candy, the Buffalo News) are not for sale regardless of price, even at three times value. He compares selling to selling one of his children for a big check.
- He sells only if he gets really discouraged with management or the economics of the business change in a big way, not because a stock looks high.
- He tells shareholders his approach up front, treating them as partners for life. His analogy: for a marriage that lasts, "you look for low expectations."

## Market cycles and temperament

- Per-capita U.S. GDP rose about 610% in the 20th century and grew in every decade, including the 1930s (up 13%) and the 1940s (up 36%).
- The Dow went from 66 to 71 between 1900 and 1921, then to 381 by September 1929, then fell to about 180 by 1948, then to near 1,000 by 1965, then went nowhere until 1982 (864 at the end of 1964, 865 at the end of 1981). It ended the century above 11,000.
- Of the 100 years, about 43.75 were huge bull markets and about 56.25 were stagnation, all during a growing economy.
- The cause is behavioral: investors look in the rearview mirror, see money made, and pile in, or see none and avoid stocks. They "get greedy when others get greedy" and fearful when others get fearful.
- Pension funds put 100%+ of new money into stocks in 1970 and a record-low 9% in 1978, when stocks were far cheaper.
- At 21 he couldn't sell Kansas City Life stock (under three times earnings, about a 35% earnings yield) even to the company's own agent.
- The edge: "it doesn't take brains, it takes temperament," the ability to detach from the crowd.
- Test for bubbles: ask whether you'd buy the whole business. A company selling for $10 billion must deliver several hundred million next year and growing after. Herb Stein: "anything that can't go on forever will end."

## Small business, scale and the Fed

- Scale helps in some industries, but small players often win. Sam Walton beat Sears despite worse credit, supplier access and real estate, by inspiring hundreds of thousands of people.
- Berkshire has about 112,000 employees but only about 13.8 people at headquarters, and keeps units small for nimbleness and responsiveness to customers.
- Nebraska Furniture Mart: Rose Blumkin arrived from Russia in 1921 speaking no English, started the store in 1937 with $500, undersold Brandeis in court, sold to Warren at 89 with no audit ("I'd rather have your word"), and worked until 103. Her store does about $325 million from one Omaha location. Her focus was entirely the customer.
- Similarly, Bill Child built a Utah furniture business from $250,000 a year to roughly $400 million by thinking only about the customer. Shaw went from unknown to about 40% of the carpet business.
- On the Fed: he admires Alan Greenspan, whom he knew from the Cap Cities board. He argues the Fed's brake is stronger than its gas pedal, citing Volcker breaking inflation and Japan's zero rates failing to restart growth.
- Currency and economics have "hundreds of variables" whose weights change, so history "doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes."

## Philanthropy and luck

- Warren plans to give 99%+ of his net worth to a foundation after he and his wife die, with few trustees chosen for high grade, because large boards homogenize to the lowest common denominator.
- He gives them no specific instructions: their judgment above ground will beat his instructions "from six feet underground." He wants big bets on intractable problems with no natural funding constituency, not an "eyedropper approach."
- He praises Bill Gates for treating giving rationally (saving the most lives per dollar, vaccines, AIDS in Africa).
- On luck: born in 1930, "the odds were 50 to 1 against me being born in the United States." Society, a capitalist market that pays well for his skill, made his wealth, so it should go back to society.
- On tax shelters: low-income housing credits are a congressionally intended benefit, which he distinguishes from aggressive shelters and evasion, which he says border on fraud.
