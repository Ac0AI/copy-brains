## Insurance float is Berkshire's core engine, and Warren values it far above book

- Property-casualty insurers collect premiums upfront and pay claims later. That "float" is money Berkshire holds and invests for its own benefit.
- Float grew from $39 million (1970) to $237 million (1980), $1.6 billion (1990), $27.9 billion (2000), $65.8 billion (2010) and $73.1 billion (2012).
- If premiums exceed expenses plus eventual losses, the underwriting profit means "free money" and "you get paid for holding it". Warren compares it to "taking out a loan and having the bank pay you interest."
- Berkshire had a $1.6 billion underwriting gain in 2012, its tenth straight profitable year, totaling $18.6 billion pre-tax over the decade.
- Competition pushes the industry as a whole to underwrite at a loss, which is what it pays to hold float. State Farm had underwriting losses in 8 of the 11 years to 2011. The industry's premiums were inadequate in 37 of the 45 years ending 2011, so its return on tangible equity falls well short of American industry's.
- Accounting deducts the full float as a liability, as if it had to be paid tomorrow. Warren says it should be viewed as a "revolving fund". If float is costless and long-enduring, its true liability value is dramatically lower.
- $15.5 billion of insurance goodwill sits on the books as the price paid for float-generating ability. Its cost has no bearing on its value: goodwill from prolonged underwriting losses is worthless, but Berkshire's is worth far more than carrying value.
- The value of float is "a huge reason" intrinsic value exceeds book value.
- Insurers' earnings are propped up by "legacy" bond portfolios whose high yields won't be available at reinvestment. Today's bond portfolios are "wasting assets".

## Four disciplines of sound insurance underwriting

Warren says a sound insurer must:
1. Understand all exposures that might cause a policy to incur losses.
2. Conservatively assess the likelihood of a loss and its probable cost.
3. Set a premium that on average delivers a profit after loss costs and operating expenses.
4. Be willing to walk away if the appropriate premium can't be obtained.

- Many insurers pass the first three and flunk the fourth because they can't turn away business competitors are eagerly writing. "The other guy is doing it, so we must as well" spells trouble in any business, "but none more so than insurance."
- Berkshire is far more conservative about risk than most large insurers. In a $250 billion industry mega-catastrophe (about triple any past loss), Berkshire would likely still record a profit because of its many earnings streams, while other major insurers would be far in the red.
- At GEICO, one percentage point of sustained improvement in persistency (policy renewals) adds more than $1 billion of intrinsic value. GEICO's market share rose from 2.5% to 9.7% since 1995 and premiums from $2.8 billion to $16.7 billion, without sacrificing underwriting discipline.
- Ajit Jain built a reinsurance business from scratch in 1985 to $35 billion of float with cumulative underwriting profit by insuring risks others lack the desire or capital to take.

## Capital allocation order: reinvest, acquire, repurchase, then dividends

Warren's priority for deploying earnings:
1. **Reinvest in current businesses.** Managers should seek ways to widen the moat: efficiency, territory, product lines. Berkshire spent a record $12.1 billion on fixed assets and bolt-ons in 2012, including $9.8 billion on plant and equipment. A conglomerate can "water the flowers and skip over the weeds."
2. **Acquisitions.** The test is whether Charlie and Warren can do a deal likely to leave shareholders wealthier per share. Warren admits "plenty of mistakes" and expects more, but the record is satisfactory.
3. **Repurchases.** These make sense only when the shares trade at a meaningful discount to conservatively calculated intrinsic value: "It's hard to go wrong when you're buying dollar bills for 80¢ or less." Berkshire raised its buyback limit from 110% to 120% of book in December 2012 after a large block became available at about 116%. "In repurchase decisions, price is all-important. Value is destroyed when purchases are made above intrinsic value."
4. **Dividends** come last.

- Capital allocation can be undermined by subconscious motivated reasoning: managers start with the answer they want and work backwards. Warren admits he did this in the textile business: "wishing makes dreams come true only in Disney movies; it's poison in business."
- Berkshire's building blocks for per-share value: improve subsidiaries' earning power, bolt-on acquisitions, participate in investees' growth, buy back stock at a meaningful discount, and an occasional large acquisition. It rarely, if ever, issues shares.
- Warren explains that the $4 billion Heinz common equity and $8 billion 9% preferred (with redemption premium and warrants for 5% of the common) shows he structures for downside protection and upside.

## Why Berkshire pays no dividend: the sell-off math

Warren's worked example: two equal owners of a business with $2 million net worth, earning 12% on tangible net worth and able to reinvest at 12%, with outsiders willing to buy at 125% of net worth.
- **Dividend plan** (pay out one-third of earnings): value and dividends grow 8% a year. After ten years, net worth is $4,317,850, each owner's shares are worth $2,698,656, and the next dividend is $86,357.
- **Sell-off plan** (retain everything, each sells 3.2% of shares a year for the same initial $40,000): net worth reaches $6,211,696, each owns 36.12%, and each stake is worth $2,804,425. That is about 4% more value and about 4% more annual cash than the dividend plan.
- Two further arguments:
  - Dividends impose one cash-out policy on all shareholders. With about 600,000 holders, many are net savers who prefer none. Selling lets each holder choose.
  - Taxes are worse for dividends, since all cash received is taxed, versus tax only on the gain portion of sales. Reinvesting dividends also means paying the 125% premium to buy shares back.
- Warren's own case: he gives away about 4.25% of his Berkshire shares a year, yet the book value of his holdings rose from $28.2 billion (2005) to $40.2 billion (2012).
- Dividend policy should be "clear, consistent and rational." Quoting Phil Fisher, you can run a hamburger restaurant or a Chinese one, but you can't switch capriciously and keep the fans of either.
- The approach holds only while the book-value buildup and market premium assumptions are reasonable. If they worsen materially, Berkshire will reexamine it.

## Intrinsic value, yardsticks, and what Berkshire's results should look like

- Warren can't give an exact intrinsic value. Book value is "a significantly understated proxy." Per-share investments rose 15.7% to $113,786 and per-share pre-tax non-insurance earnings rose 15.7% to $8,085. Since 1970 they have compounded at 19.4% and 20.8%, and the share price has tracked them. The emphasis is on building operating earnings.
- Book value grew 14.4% in 2012 and 19.7% a year over 48 years (from $19 to $114,214), but lagged the S&P in 9 of 48 years. In 8 of those 9, the S&P gained 15% or more. "We do better when the wind is in our face."
- Charlie and Warren won't change yardsticks. If they fail to outpace the S&P, management brings no value, since investors can buy a low-cost index fund.
- They expect to beat the S&P only by a small margin over time, doing relatively better in flat or down markets and worse in strong ones.
- Per-share results matter, not just growth. The five largest non-insurance businesses added $9.7 billion of annual earnings with only 6.1% dilution, from the BNSF share issuance.

## Owning wonderful businesses, accounting reality, and mistakes

- Charlie told Warren over 50 years ago that it's "far better to buy a wonderful business at a fair price than a fair business at a wonderful price." Warren still sometimes reverts to bargain-hunting with "tolerable to terrible" results, usually on smaller purchases. Large acquisitions have generally worked out well.
- Berkshire "much prefer[s] owning a non-controlling but substantial portion of a wonderful business to owning 100% of a so-so business." It can do this because it isn't limited to businesses it can operate. The Big Four (American Express, Coca-Cola, IBM, Wells Fargo) produced about $3.9 billion of Berkshire's look-through share of earnings in 2012, but only about $1.1 billion of dividends are reported. The other $2.8 billion of retained earnings is "every bit as valuable," funding buybacks and growth. Unrealized gains on the four were $26.7 billion.
- The manufacturing, service and retail group earned 16.3% after-tax on $22.6 billion of net tangible assets. Returns range from 25% to over 100% at the best units, 12-20% at good ones, and poor at a few owing to capital-allocation errors by Warren.
- Not all amortization is a real expense. Software amortization is real. Amortization of customer relationships under purchase accounting is not, and about 80% of the group's $600 million of amortization was treated as not real. Wells Fargo's roughly $1.5 billion "core deposit amortization" charge is not an expense in any sense except GAAP, since core deposits grow.
- Interest coverage should use pre-tax earnings/interest, not EBITDA/interest, "a commonly-used measure we view as deeply flawed." BNSF's 2012 coverage was 9.6x.
- GAAP forced a $700 million write-down on buying more of Marmon, even though the price implies about $12.6 billion for 90% against $8 billion carrying value, so intrinsic value exceeds book by at least $4.6 billion.

## Temperament, liquidity and optimism about American business

- Warren calls it a "terrible mistake" to dance in and out of stocks based on tarot cards, "experts" or business activity. "The risks of being out of the game are huge compared to the risks of being in it."
- The Dow rose from 66 to 11,497 in the 20th century (17,320%) despite four costly wars, a depression and many recessions. His first stock purchase was in spring 1942, amid Pacific war setbacks. Real GDP per capita more than quadrupled from 1941 to 2012.
- Berkshire didn't join CEOs crying "uncertainty": it invested a record amount and says "America's destiny has always been clear: ever-increasing abundance." He offers to take large, profitable projects CEOs have shelved out of short-term worries.
- Berkshire keeps "many redundant layers of liquidity" and avoids obligations that could drain cash, such as derivatives requiring collateral. That "reduces our returns in 99 years out of 100. But we will survive in the 100th while many others fail. And we will sleep well in all 100."
- Berkshire's long index put options (written 2004-2008, expiring 2018-2026) brought in $4.2 billion of premium to invest as float, with only index value at expiry counting and minimal collateral. Credit-default derivatives earned about $1 billion pre-tax.

## Buying local newspapers, and when to shut a business

- Warren still believes newspaper circulation, advertising and profits are certain to decline overall. Berkshire bought 28 dailies for $344 million anyway, because at that price the papers should meet its economic test.
- Newspapers once had primacy through a "virtuous circle" of readers drawing ads and ads drawing readers, and local monopolies made management quality nearly irrelevant. Help-wanted classifieds are down over 90% in 12 years.
- Local news in tightly-bound communities remains indispensable. Papers with comprehensive local content and a sensible Internet strategy should stay viable.
- Giving the paper away online while charging for print is a self-destructive strategy. Walter Hussman's Arkansas Democrat-Gazette adopted pay early and held circulation best. Cutting news content or publication frequency leads to "skimpy readership."
- Berkshire's six small dailies had flat 2012 revenue, and its two large papers lost 3%.
- Buy such papers only at "a very low multiple of current earnings." Berkshire will not continue "any business doomed to unending losses": it shut down one significantly unprofitable paper from the Media General bulk purchase after finding no remedy.
