## Why book value overstated 1998 progress

- Berkshire's net worth rose $25.9 billion in 1998, lifting per-share book value 48.3%. Over 34 years it grew from $19 to $37,801, or 24.7% compounded annually.
- Most of the 1998 gain came from issuing shares in acquisitions. Berkshire stock sells at a large premium to book value, so issuing shares for cash or in a merger instantly raises per-share book value without any earnings.
- Intrinsic value does not rise in these deals, because what Berkshire gives and gets is roughly equal. Warren and Charlie Munger stress that "it's the per-share gain in intrinsic value that counts rather than the per-share gain in book value."
- Intrinsic value still far exceeds book value.
- He tracks two components of value: per-share investments (including cash) and pre-tax operating earnings per share with all investment income excluded.
  - Investments per share: $53 (1968), $465 (1978), $4,876 (1988), $47,647 (1998), a 25.4% compound rate over 1968-1998.
  - Operating earnings per share: $2.87, $12.85, $145.77 and $474.45, an 18.6% compound rate over the same period.
  - Operating earnings per share fell 33.9% in 1998 on a pro-forma basis because of General Re's underwriting loss. Investments per share rose 25.2%.

## Size limits future returns; a 15% target

- Warren says future gains "will fall far short of those achieved in the past" because the $57.4 billion net worth, the largest of any American corporation, is too large for outsized returns. Anyone who believes otherwise "should consider a career in sales but avoid one in mathematics."
- He and Charlie will try to raise intrinsic value at an average of 15% a year, "a result we consider to be at the very peak of possible outcomes." Some years will fall short, including negative years.
- Compounding 15% for five years means adding about $58 billion of net worth. That requires "big ideas: Popcorn stands just won't do," so they hunt "elephants" even though markets are unfriendly to the search.
- He promises to keep at least 99% of his net worth in Berkshire.
- Capital allocation is a waiting game. "In allocating capital, activity does not correlate with achievement," and frenetic behavior in investments and acquisitions is often counterproductive. He and Charlie "mainly just wait for the phone to ring."
- Berkshire held more than $15 billion in cash equivalents at yearend. "Cash never makes us happy," but it is better in Berkshire's pocket than someone else's, and he saw nothing on the horizon to buy.
- He admits his 1998 portfolio moves cut the year's gain. Selling McDonald's was "a very big mistake," and Berkshire "would have been better off" had he "snuck off to the movies during market hours."
- Berkshire does not comment on specific holdings, since discussing them would make the shares worth less. He warns that media speculation about its investment moves is usually wrong.

## How to evaluate an insurer: float and its cost

- Three determinants of an insurance company's value:
  - the amount of float it generates;
  - the cost of that float;
  - most important, the long-term outlook for both.
- Float is "money we hold but don't own." Premiums arrive before losses are paid, sometimes years later, and the insurer invests the money in the meantime.
- Cost of float is the underwriting loss, if any. An insurer has value if its cost of float is below what it would otherwise pay for funds. It is "a lemon" if the cost exceeds market rates for money.
- Loss costs are estimates, so reserving errors can be huge, usually innocent but sometimes not. Outsiders can mostly only accept what is presented. Warren says Berkshire presents underwriting results conservatively because "virtually all surprises in insurance are unpleasant ones."
- Berkshire's average float:
  - $17 million in 1967;
  - $221 million in 1982;
  - $2.29 billion in 1992;
  - $7.09 billion in 1997;
  - $22.76 billion at yearend 1998, including General Re.
  - That is 25.4% compounded over 32 years.
- Berkshire's average cost of float over 32 years has been "well under zero," meaning a substantial aggregate underwriting profit. It is paid to hold a large, growing pool of money.
- Net float is a liability on the balance sheet, yet it has been worth more to Berkshire than an equal amount of net worth, as long as underwriting profit continues.
- Growth in float may be modest near-term. The reinsurance market is soft, and General Re is two-thirds of the total.

## General Re: why Berkshire is a better home for a reinsurer

- Berkshire completed the $22 billion acquisition on December 21, 1998. General Re owns General Reinsurance Corporation, the largest U.S. property-casualty reinsurer, and 82% of Cologne Re (including stock it has an arrangement to buy). Together they operate in 124 countries.
- Warren says Berkshire "can add absolutely nothing" to the managers' skills. He expects earnings a decade out to be materially higher than without the merger, because of the freedom Berkshire can offer.
- Reinsurers sell absorption of volatility, yet a public reinsurer is graded by owners and rating agencies on the smoothness of its own results. That pushes it to lay off business (retrocessions) or decline good business that adds volatility.
- Berkshire "happily accepts volatility" if it carries the expectation of higher profit. Its capital, "a Fort Knox," means lumpy earnings cannot hurt its credit ratings.
- "We prefer a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%."
- Berkshire will manage General Re's investment portfolio but not Cologne Re's, and will not be involved in underwriting. He asks the company to keep its underwriting discipline while retaining more business, broadening products and expanding geographically, especially internationally.
- After the merger, General Re dropped about 250 stock positions, incurring $935 million of taxes. This reflects a principle: "We don't back into decisions."

## GEICO: spend on growth when each dollar buys more than a dollar of value

- Warren's formula: "Combine a great idea with a great manager and you're certain to obtain a great result." The idea is low-cost direct auto insurance and the manager is Tony Nicely.
- Direct writing needs a large front-end investment and first-year business is unprofitable. Berkshire leaves that cost out of compensation so staff pursue new business aggressively.
  - 50% of bonuses and profit sharing is tied to earnings of the "seasoned" book (policies older than one year).
  - The other 50% is tied to growth in policyholders.
- Marketing spend rose from $33 million in 1995 to $143 million in 1998, and is at least $190 million planned for 1999. Telephone counselors grew from 652 to 2,162.
- The test: "We simply measure whether we are creating more than a dollar of value per dollar spent — and if that calculation is favorable, the more dollars we spend the happier I am." Companies focused on quarterly earnings would shy away from such spending.
- Results:
  - Voluntary auto policies in force rose from 2,949,439 (1997) to 3,562,644 (1998).
  - Profit margin was 6.7% in 1998, "better than we wished." Accident frequency and severity fell, so GEICO cut rates 3.3% and will cut more. The target margin is 4%, possibly lower.
  - Profit sharing was $103 million, or 32.3% of salary, for all 9,313 associates with over a year of service.
  - Market share rose from 3% to 3½%. About 40% of people who check rates save money. Shareholders get a special discount (usually 8%) in 40 states.
- Claims handling also counts. New York's Insurance Department found GEICO had the lowest complaint ratio of the five largest auto insurers, under half the average of the other four.

## Managing managers: "run it as if you own 100%"

- Warren says telling outstanding CEOs how to run their companies "would be the height of foolishness." Most would not work for Berkshire with much backseat driving, and about 75% are independently wealthy.
- Berkshire removes the ritual of the CEO job. Managers control their own schedules, with no board meetings, press interviews, banker presentations, analyst calls, or concern with financing, credit ratings or "Street" earnings expectations.
- The simple mission is to run the business as if:
  1. you own 100% of it;
  2. it is the only asset you and your family will ever have;
  3. you can't sell or merge it for at least a century.
- Managers should not let accounting considerations affect decisions. "We want our managers to think about what counts, not how it will be counted."
- This works because Berkshire's shareholders have the longest horizon in the public-company universe. A majority of shares are held by people who expect to die still holding them.
- Current results still matter, but not at the expense of building competitive strengths.
- Managers are motivated mainly by a vision of how far their business can go, not just pay. Example: Scott Fetzer earned a record $96.5 million after tax on $112 million of net worth with no leverage beyond a conservative finance-subsidiary debt.
- Corporate overhead is tiny: 12.8 people at headquarters, and about $3.5 million after-tax, under one basis point of assets managed.

## Executive Jet (NetJets): buying a franchise from a founder-operator

- Executive Jet Aviation sells fractional shares of jets and operates the fleet. Rich Santulli created the industry in 1986.
- An owner buys a fraction, say 1/8, which entitles them to 100 flying hours a year (averaged over five years), and pays a monthly management fee plus a fee per hour flown. A plane is available on a few hours' notice at 5,500 U.S. airports.
- Warren became a customer first. Frank Rooney, manager of H.H. Brown, recommended it, and Rich sold him a quarter of a Hawker 1000 (200 hours) in about 15 minutes. His family flew 900 hours on 300 trips and loved it, and he did a testimonial ad before there was any thought of buying the company. Rich called last May and they made a $725 million deal, half cash and half stock.
- The business has over 1,000 customers and 163 aircraft, including 23 "core" aircraft EJA owns or leases to guarantee service at peak demand. About 650 pilots train twice a year at FlightSafety International, another Berkshire company. In 1997 EJA took 31% of all corporate jets ordered worldwide.
- Competitive advantages:
  - Scale: planes positioned throughout the country improve service and cut dead-head costs.
  - Brand neutrality: it offers planes from Boeing, Gulfstream, Falcon, Cessna and Raytheon, while its two competitors are owned by manufacturers and sell only their own. Warren compares it to a physician who can recommend any medicine.
- Whole-plane ownership only makes sense at 350-400 hours a year, so Warren and Rich believe fractional ownership's potential "has barely been scratched."
- He sold the Berkshire plane and now flies on NetJets for business and personal trips.

## Accounting honesty: options, restructurings and merger games

- Options: existing rules ignore the cost of stock options in earnings. Warren calls that treatment "outrageous." Paying GEICO's $190 million ad bill in ten-year at-the-market options would plainly be a cost.
  - Options "if properly structured" can be a fine way to motivate managers but are usually "wildly capricious" in rewards, inefficient motivators and "inordinately expensive for shareholders."
  - When evaluating an option-issuing company, he and Charlie subtract what the company could have realized by selling options of like quantity and structure. This often cuts reported per-share earnings 5%, sometimes 10%, and has changed portfolio decisions.
  - He rebuts the usual objections: employee options are sometimes repriced (raising cost), and non-transferability lowers value to the employee but not cost to the employer, like a restricted company car.
  - For General Re, Berkshire replaced the stock option plan with a cash plan tied to operating results. The economics match, but the pro-forma showed $63 million more compensation expense.
  - His unanswered questions: "If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?"
- Restructuring charges: costs that belong to many years get dumped into one bad quarter, so Wall Street ignores it and later results beat expectations. He illustrates with golf scores of 140, 80, 80, 80, 80 instead of 91, 94, 89, 94, 92.
  - Special charges in 1998 totaled $72.1 billion across 1,369 items, versus $324 billion of 1997 earnings for the Fortune 500.
- Merger accounting: acquirers sometimes raise loss reserves at deal time to release them as "earnings" later. Berkshire leaves acquired reserves exactly as found. "If we are to disappoint you, we would rather it be with our earnings than with our accounting."
- He disagrees with the premise that a CEO's job is always to maximize the stock price, and says "bad accounting drives out good."
- He praises SEC chairman Arthur Levitt's campaign against "earnings management."
- Disclosure: Berkshire aims to give owners the information it would want in their position and to release it to everyone at once. It treats a B-share holder as equal to a large institution and refuses "earnings guidance," pointing to public documents.

## Taxes and Berkshire's tax position

- Berkshire and General Re owed $2.7 billion of federal income tax on 1998 earnings. Warren says Berkshire has prospered in America "as we would have in no other country," and is glad to write large checks rather than receive them.
- Capital gains get no special treatment for a corporation. It pays 35%, which is 75% higher than an individual pays on the same long-term gain.
- The dividends-received exclusion is 70% for most corporations but 59.5% for Berkshire's equity holdings, because almost all are in its insurance companies.
