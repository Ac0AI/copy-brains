## How Berkshire's insurance float works and why it is worth owning

- Float is "money that doesn't belong to us but that we temporarily hold." It arises because premiums are paid upfront while protection is delivered over a year or more, and because claims (asbestos is his example) can take many years to be reported, negotiated and settled.
- Berkshire's float grew from $20 million at the 1967 purchase of National Indemnity (NICO) to $46.1 billion at the end of 2004, through internal growth and acquisitions.
- Warren says "Float is wonderful – if it doesn't come at a high price." Its cost is the underwriting result: expenses and losses compared with premiums received.
- When there is an underwriting profit, float is "better than free" because Berkshire is "actually paid for holding other people's money." That happened in about half of Berkshire's 38 years in insurance.
- The property-casualty industry as a whole almost always runs an underwriting loss, which can make float expensive, "sometimes devastatingly so."
- In 2004 Berkshire earned an underwriting profit of $1.551 billion, so it was paid more than $1.5 billion to hold an average of about $45.2 billion. The profit split was GEICO $970 million, B-H Reinsurance $417 million, Other Primary $161 million and General Re $3 million. Float rose $1.9 billion.
- He expected 2005 pricing to be less attractive but still saw a decent chance of no-cost float again, absent a mega-catastrophe.
- On commutations (insureds unwinding reinsurance contracts), he agrees only when the economics favor Berkshire after weighing what it could earn on the money returned.

## Why insurance is a bad commodity business and how to get an edge anyway

- Insurers earn poor returns because the product is commodity-like: standard policy forms, many suppliers (including mutuals with limited profit goals), and customers who don't care who the insurer is. Warren's contrast: customers say "I need some Gillette blades" or "I'll have a Coke" but never "I'd like a National Indemnity policy, please." Price competition is fierce, "Think airline seats."
- He names two ways Berkshire gets durable advantage:
  - Discipline in pricing, which means being willing to lose volume (NICO).
  - Being the low-cost operator with a "foot-to-the-floor" strategy (GEICO).
- Reinsurance should not be a commodity because a policy is just a promise and promises vary in quality. At the primary level, state guaranty assessments cover 60% of industry volume (personal lines and workers' comp), so who makes the promise matters little. Reinsurance carries far more solvency risk: when a reinsurer fails, staggering losses hit the primary companies it dealt with, and GEICO lost tens of millions from careless reinsurer selection in the early 1980s.

## National Indemnity: pricing for profit and accepting shrinking volume

- NICO had no apparent advantage when bought in 1967. It was a specialist in commercial auto and general liability, little known, had no informational edge (it has never had an actuary), wasn't low-cost, and sold through general agents. Still, for almost all of 38 years it has been a star. Warren says without it, "Berkshire would be lucky to be worth half of what it is today."
- Its edge is a managerial mindset most insurers can't replicate. NICO "consistently priced to make a profit, not to match our most optimistic competitor." Written premiums fell from $366.2 million in 1986 to $54.5 million in 1999 even though billions of premium were available had it cut prices. In his words: "We never left customers – but they left us."
- Why other companies can't copy it:
  - The "institutional imperative" rejects extended volume declines. No CEO wants to tell shareholders business will keep shrinking.
  - Bad pricing doesn't show up for years, so optimistic reserving overstates earnings. He cites this as the self-deception that nearly destroyed GEICO in the early 1970s.
  - Fear of layoffs makes employees rationalize inadequate pricing to keep the organization and distribution system intact.
- Berkshire's fix is a promise that no one at NICO will be fired because of declining volume, however severe. NICO isn't labor-intensive and can live with excess overhead, but "it can't live with underpriced business."
- The no-layoff policy requires care about overstaffing in good times. Tom Murphy of Cap Cities taught Warren to treat a request to hire a $20,000 assistant as a $3 million decision, because the person will likely cost that much over a lifetime and is rarely dismissed. ("More people, more toilet paper.")
- Don Wurster, who has run NICO since 1989, "will accept a walk rather than swing at a bad pitch," like Barry Bonds. He has built $950 million of float that is almost certain to be negative-cost. With insurance prices falling, his volume will drop, and Warren says he will "applaud him ever more loudly."
- Warren believes extraordinary business ability is largely innate. NICO has had four CEOs since 1940 and only one graduated from college.

## GEICO: the low-cost model, a near-failure, and the turnaround

- History of low-cost insurers:
  - Around 1900 the industry was a cartel with "bureau" rates and high agent commissions.
  - State Farm (1922) used captive agents, lowered acquisition costs, and captured about 25% of personal lines.
  - Allstate (1931) followed the same model.
  - Leo Goodwin started GEICO in 1936 with $200,000 and eliminated the agent entirely, because most consumers know what they need in an auto policy. Warren calls this "a powerful insight."
- From 1936 to 1975 GEICO grew from nothing to a 4% market share, the fourth largest auto insurer. After Lorimer Davidson retired as CEO in 1970, successors under-reserved for losses. That gave faulty cost information, then inadequate pricing, and GEICO was near failure by 1976.
- Jack Byrne saved it with major price increases. Policyholders fled, share fell to 1.8% by 1980, and unwise diversification kept share at only 1.9% in 1993. Tony Nicely then took over, and GEICO should reach about a 6% share in 2005.
- 2004 benefits to each constituent:
  - Customers saved about $1 billion versus what they would otherwise have paid.
  - Employees earned a $191 million profit-sharing bonus averaging 24.3% of salary.
  - Berkshire got excellent returns.
- New Jersey re-entry:
  - GEICO left in 1976 and avoided New Jersey and Massachusetts as hard jurisdictions.
  - After 2003 reform legislation, it was licensed in August 2004.
  - It had 140,000 policyholders (about 4% of the market) by the letter, with word of mouth driving inquiries and a closure rate well above the national one.
- Warren claims GEICO offers the lowest price more often than any other national insurer serving all segments, though not for everyone. Berkshire shareholders get an 8% discount in most states.

## Catastrophe risk and being able to pay

- The largest insured loss to date is the World Trade Center at about $35 billion. Hurricane Andrew cost insurers about $15.5 billion in 1992. Four 2004 Florida hurricanes cost about $25 billion in insured losses, and Charley and Ivan could have done at least three times the damage had they landed slightly differently.
- Warren treats a $100 billion industry loss as a real possibility that many insurers call "unthinkable" and won't plan for. Berkshire's share would probably be 3% to 5%, and its investment and business earnings would comfortably exceed that: "When 'the day after' arrives, Berkshire's checks will clear."
- The 2004 hurricanes cost Berkshire $1.25 billion, yet the reinsurance operations did well.
- General Re: Joe Brandon restored underwriting discipline that "had lost its way." Good current results were offset by adverse development on years before he took over.
- Ajit Jain at NICO's reinsurance operation underwrites huge risks no one else will take, and Warren calls his value "enormous."

## Capital allocation, the "simple propositions" rule, and cash

- Business-owner mindset for managers: "Run your business as if it were the only asset your family will own over the next hundred years." After they fund their businesses, they send excess cash to Omaha for Warren to deploy.
- He admits he did that job poorly in 2004. He hoped for several multi-billion acquisitions, "struck out," and found few attractive securities, leaving $43 billion in cash equivalents, "not a happy position." He buys only at prices offering "a reasonable return on our investment."
- The "mono-linked chain" rule, drawn from the failed MidAmerican zinc recovery project (initiated 1998, operational 2002, written off in September 2004 with a $579 million loss):
  - If one key variable has a 90% chance of going your way, success is 90%.
  - If ten independent variables each have 90% odds, the chance of a winner is only 35%.
  - Mining prospects "tease" developers: every solved problem is followed by another.
  - His conclusion: "a chain is no stronger than its weakest link," so look for "mono-linked chains" and "stay with simple propositions."
- Debt: Berkshire typically shuns it but borrows to fund profitable interest-bearing receivables. Examples:
  - Clayton Homes: borrowing of $2 billion at the start of 2004 grew to $7.35 billion by January 2005, relent to Clayton at a one-point markup. Receivables should hover near $9 billion.
  - MidAmerican: Berkshire holds $1.478 billion of its junior debt at 11% interest, so partners aren't diluted when acquisitions need funding.

## How Warren views stocks, markets and investor mistakes

- He looks at holdings as "fractional ownerships in businesses," not stocks to trade on "chart patterns, brokers' opinions, or estimates of near-term earnings." This has been his approach since reading Ben Graham's The Intelligent Investor at 19, when "the scales fell from my eyes."
- The "Big Four" (American Express, Coca-Cola, Gillette, Wells Fargo):
  - Cost: $3.83 billion, bought between May 1988 and October 2003, with a weighted purchase date of July 1992.
  - 2004 results: Berkshire's share of their earnings was $1.2 billion, about 31.3% of cost, and distributions were $434 million, about 11.3% of cost.
  - His verdict: "satisfactory, though far from spectacular."
  - Market values diverged from business results. Market gains far outstripped business performance in the Bubble and the reverse followed.
- His admitted error: he "talked when I should have walked" during the Bubble, clucking about valuations but not acting. He also notes huge positions make it hard to dance in and out of holdings.
- Why investors do badly even in a good market (S&P averaged 11.2% over 35 years after the 1960s):
  - High costs from excess trading or expensive management.
  - Decisions based on tips and fads instead of "thoughtful, quantified evaluation of businesses."
  - A start-stop approach that enters after advances and exits after declines.
- His advice: "excitement and expenses are their enemies." If trying to time markets, "be fearful when others are greedy and greedy only when others are fearful."
- The index fund benchmark: shareholders can buy the S&P at very low cost, so unless Berkshire beats it in per-share intrinsic value, Charlie and he add nothing. 2004 book value rose 10.5% against the S&P's 10.9%.
- Book value vs intrinsic value: book is a slightly understated gauge of the long-term rise in intrinsic value. It matters less for single years because equities fell from an average 114% of net worth in the 1980s to under 50%.
- Realized gains are "meaningless for analytical purposes," and the timing of realizing gains does not depend on reporting preferences.

## Delegation, managers and Lou Simpson

- Warren and Charlie "truly hand over the baton." Lou Simpson manages about $2.5 billion of equities at GEICO, usually buying $200-$300 million positions in companies smaller than those Warren focuses on. Warren learns of the trades about ten days after month end and sometimes silently disagrees, "But he's usually right."
- Lou's record at GEICO equities from 1980-2004 was an average annual gain of 20.3% against 13.5% for the S&P, a 6.8-point edge. Warren says he is "a cinch" for the investment Hall of Fame.
- Press stories saying "Buffett buys" are usually wrong because the SEC filings are Berkshire's, and the buyer is often Lou.
- Manager examples he praises:
  - Kevin Clayton, a "prototype Berkshire manager," running Clayton Homes (11,837 employees versus 7,136 at purchase) after the Oakwood Homes acquisition.
  - Bill Child and Scott Hymas at R. C. Willey: Warren thought out-of-state expansion and a second Las Vegas store were mistakes, but each Las Vegas store does about 26% more volume than any other chain store. He jokes that "the opinion of someone who is always wrong has its own special utility."
  - The Bridges at Ben Bridge: same-store sales up 11.4% in 2004 and 8.8% average over a decade.
  - Gene Toombs at MiTek, building a "mini-Berkshire" through bolt-on acquisitions.

## Operating businesses: economics worth noting

- The manufacturing, service and retailing group earned 21.7% on average tangible net worth in 2004 (20.7% in 2003) with little leverage, but only 9.9% on carrying value because of premiums paid over net worth (goodwill). Pre-tax earnings were $2.481 billion on revenue of $44.142 billion.
- Cost pressure: MiTek's steel costs were up 100% year over year (665 million pounds a year), and Shaw faced more than $300 million in fiber cost increases. Shaw still earned 25.6% on tangible equity, though margins narrowed with a lag in price increases.
- FlightSafety is capital-intensive: 283 simulators cost $1.2 billion, so up to $3.50 of capital produces $1 of annual revenue. That demands high margins and utilization. Return on tangible equity rose to 15.1% from 8.4%. Founder Al Ueltschi's standard: a flunked pilot "belongs in the back of the plane, not the cockpit."
- NetJets:
  - Warren compares choosing a flight provider to picking a brain surgeon: "you simply want the best."
  - U.S. contracts grew from 3,877 to 4,967 (about 1,200 when Berkshire bought it in 1998), and it took about 70% of net new business among the four dominant firms.
  - Europe was costlier than expected (contracts rose from 364 to 693) but is essential, and a significant European loss is expected in 2005.
- Manufactured housing: sales of fewer than 135,000 homes in 2004, versus about 40% of 1995-99 volume levels, because of "irresponsible financing and naïve funders." Berkshire's funding gave Clayton an advantage once securitization dried up.
- Derivatives (Gen Re Securities wind-down):
  - Contracts fell to 2,890 from a peak of 23,218. "Like Hell, derivative trading is easy to enter but difficult to leave."
  - Marks are fuzzy over decades-long contracts and feed annual bonuses, so "phantom profits" get recorded.
  - Rapid growth in financial institutions can mask major problems, and the real test is performance in no-growth mode: "You only learn who has been swimming naked when the tide goes out."

## Currencies and the trade deficit

- Berkshire held about $21.4 billion in foreign exchange contracts across 12 currencies, a change for him: he never traded currencies before March 2002.
- His reasoning is not doubt about America, which he calls the strongest economy. It is that trade practices will pressure the dollar for years.
- Key figures: the 2004 current-account deficit was $618 billion, versus $263 billion in 1999 (the year before a Congressional study). Wealth is transferring abroad at about $1.8 billion a day, and foreigners own a net of about $3 trillion of the U.S.
- A decade out at the current pace, net foreign ownership would be about $11 trillion. At 5% that is $0.55 trillion yearly, about 3% of a roughly $18 trillion GDP, a "Sharecropper's Society."
- Budget deficit vs current-account deficit: the budget deficit only redistributes a pie Americans own, but persistent trade deficits send ownership abroad.
- He distinguishes "true trade" ($1.15 trillion of goods and services exchanged) from the $618 billion of "pseudo-trade," where wealth is exchanged for product.
- Caveats: "macro-economics is a tough game in which few people, Charlie and I included, have demonstrated skill," and it makes him uneasy that many pundits predict a weak dollar. He cites Keynes: "it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally." Berkshire manages as if he owned 100% of it, so a dollar-only policy wasn't right. A prompt policy fix would probably cost Berkshire losses on its contracts, but he'd welcome it since most assets are dollar-based.
- A 2004 profit example: Level 3 euro bonds bought in 2001 at 51.7% of par and sold at 85% in December 2004. About $85 million of the $163 million gain came from improved credit, and $78 million from the euro rising from 88 cents to $1.29.

## Governance, incentives and stock options

- Two reforms he regrets not adopting earlier: regular director meetings without the CEO present (he has sat on 19 boards), and a whistleblower line to him and the audit committee, useful in a 180,000-employee company.
- The three questions institutional owners should ask: is it the right CEO, is he overreaching on pay, and will proposed acquisitions create or destroy per-share value? He criticizes checklist "independence" tests.
- On independence, he cites Matthew 6:21 ("where your treasure is, there will your heart be also"). His $8 billion Coca-Cola stake dwarfs any business Berkshire does with Coke, so his loyalty is to Coke's owners.
- The Berkshire board standard: each director's family owns at least $4 million of stock, none acquired via options or grants, fees are a tiny portion of income, and there is no directors' liability insurance. "Board members travel the same road as shareholders."
- He argues the least independent directors are often those who get a big share of income from board fees. His example: a multi-billion acquisition offer above the long-run stock price was killed by directors earning about $100,000 a year, who then voted themselves a fee increase.
- Stock options should be expensed. He does not oppose options themselves, and says a successor's pay might include options with an appropriate strike price, a price escalation reflecting retained earnings, and a ban on quickly selling shares. CEOs resist expensing because it would make pay more rational and might lower the stock price.
