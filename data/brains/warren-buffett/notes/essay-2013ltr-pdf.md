## Float: the insurance engine and revolving fund

- Warren calls property-casualty float "money that doesn't belong to us but that we can invest for Berkshire's benefit." Insurers receive premiums upfront and pay claims later, sometimes decades later (certain workers' compensation accidents).
- Berkshire's float grew from $39 million (1970) to $237 million (1980), $1,632 million (1990), $27,871 million (2000), $65,832 million (2010) and $77,240 million (2013). It began with the 1967 purchase of National Indemnity for $8.6 million.
- Berkshire underwrote at a profit for 11 straight years, totaling $22 billion pre-tax ($3 billion in 2013), while float rose from $41 billion to $77 billion over that stretch.
- If premiums exceed expenses plus eventual losses, "we enjoy the use of free money – and, better yet, get paid for holding it." Competition usually pushes the industry as a whole into underwriting losses, which is "what the industry pays to hold its float." State Farm had an underwriting loss in nine of twelve years through 2012. Warren expects the industry to keep earning subnormal returns.
- Float "can be drowned by poor underwriting results," so daily focus is underwriting profit.
- Book value deducts float as a liability as if payable tomorrow. Warren says it should be viewed as a "revolving fund": about $17 billion was paid to more than five million claimants in 2013, while new business adds new float. If it is costless and long-enduring, the true value of the liability is "dramatically less than the accounting liability."
- Insurance goodwill of $15.5 billion on the books mostly reflects the price paid for float-generating ability. Its book cost has no bearing on true value; large, prolonged underwriting losses would make it worthless. Warren believes the economic value is far above carrying value, and it is a "huge reason" intrinsic value exceeds book value.
- Float may decline, but gradually (at most 3% a year), and Berkshire can never face immediate demands large relative to its cash. Property-casualty differs from certain life insurance in this respect.

## Four insurance disciplines and insurance as the sale of promises

- A sound insurer must (1) understand all exposures that might cause losses; (2) conservatively assess the likelihood and probable cost of loss; (3) set a premium that on average yields a profit after loss costs and operating expenses; (4) be willing to walk away if the right premium can't be obtained.
- Many insurers pass the first three and flunk the fourth. "The other guy is doing it, so we must as well" spells trouble in any business, "but in none more so than insurance." Tad Montross at General Re observes all four, and its float has been better than cost-free. General Re, which looked like a huge mistake soon after its 1998 purchase, "is now a gem."
- Warren defines insurance as "the sale of promises." Ability and willingness to pay, even in economic chaos, is all-important. Large insurers wanting to cede long-tail liabilities (asbestos) came to Berkshire; in 2007 Lloyd's transferred pre-1993 claims, with Berkshire's payments capped at a $15 billion policy limit.
- Ajit Jain's reinsurance unit insures risks others lack the desire or capital to take, yet Berkshire is "far more conservative in avoiding risk than most large insurers." If the industry suffered a $250 billion mega-catastrophe (about triple any prior loss), Berkshire would likely still show a significant profit and remain "awash in cash." Ajit built float of $37 billion from a standing start in 1985.
- Underwriting profit and float by unit, 2013: BH Reinsurance $1,294 million / $37,231 million; General Re $283 million / $20,013 million; GEICO $1,127 million / $12,566 million; Other Primary $385 million / $7,430 million. Total $3,089 million / $77,240 million.

## GEICO's cost-advantage moat

- Warren first encountered GEICO in January 1951 and was "blown away by the huge cost advantage" versus the giants. That efficiency persists and "is an all-important asset."
- "No one likes to buy auto insurance. But almost everyone likes to drive." The expense is major for families, savings matter, and "only a low-cost operation can deliver these."
- The cost advantage "creates a moat – an enduring one – that competitors are unable to cross" and let GEICO gain share every year. GEICO ranked seventh in 1996 and was number two in 2013, passing Allstate, because of "low prices and reliable service."
- Berkshire paid $1.4 billion over net tangible assets for the second half of GEICO in 1995. That goodwill stays fixed on the books, but Warren believes true economic goodwill is approaching $20 billion.
- Tony Nicely joined at 18, completed 52 years in 2013, and has been CEO since 1993.

## Intrinsic value, book value and buybacks

- Book value rose $34.2 billion in 2013 (18.2% per share), from $19 to $134,973 over 49 years, a 19.7% compounded rate. Per-share intrinsic value is what counts, but it is subjective; book value is "a rough tracking indicator."
- Intrinsic value "far exceeds" book value and the gap has widened, which is why the 2012 buyback authorization at 120% of book value makes sense: purchases benefit continuing holders when intrinsic value exceeds that level by a meaningful amount. No shares were bought in 2013 because the price never fell to 120%; "If it does, we will be aggressive."
- Two quantitative measures: per-share investments rose 13.6% to $129,253, and pre-tax earnings from non-insurance, non-investment businesses rose 12.8% to $9,116 per share. Since 1970 these grew 19.3% and 20.6% a year, closely matching the stock price over 43 years. Operating earnings get the strongest focus.
- Buying out minorities in Marmon and Iscar cost $3.5 billion and added about $300 million pre-tax earning power plus $800 million cash. Accounting booked it $1.8 billion below price paid, reducing book value, but that "instantly increased Berkshire's excess of intrinsic value over book value by the same $1.8 billion." He calls it "the surest sort of bolt-on."
- Expected underperformance: Berkshire should beat the S&P in down or modestly up years and fall short in strong years. It has underperformed in ten of 49 years, all but one when the S&P gained over 15%. "If we fail to do so [outperform over full cycles], we will not have earned our pay. After all, you could always own an index fund."
- Five building blocks: improving subsidiary earning power; bolt-on acquisitions; investee growth; repurchases at a meaningful discount to intrinsic value; an occasional large acquisition. Berkshire will rarely, if ever, issue shares, and will always hold at least $20 billion of cash equivalents and no material short-term obligations.
- Per-share growth over mere growth: the Powerhouse Five lifted annual earnings by $10.4 billion over nine years with only 6.1% dilution (from the BNSF share issuance).

## Capital allocation, acquisitions and the Heinz template

- 2013 deals: almost $18 billion for all of NV Energy ($5.6 billion via MidAmerican) and a major interest in H. J. Heinz. Both "will be prospering a century from now."
- Heinz structure with 3G Capital (Jorge Paulo Lemann): Berkshire is financing partner, buying $8 billion of preferred with a 9% coupon and features lifting returns to roughly 12%. Berkshire and 3G each bought half the common for $4.25 billion. 3G executives run operations. Unlike private equity, "Berkshire never intends to sell a share." One-time purchase and restructuring charges were $1.3 billion in 2013; 2014 earnings are expected to be substantial.
- Bolt-ons: subsidiaries contracted 25 deals costing $3.1 billion total (from $1.9 million to $1.1 billion). They "deploy capital in activities that fit with our existing businesses" and are run by existing managers: "no more work for us and more earnings for you."
- Subsidiaries spent a record $11 billion on plant and equipment, roughly twice depreciation; 89% in the U.S. BNSF spent $4 billion, double depreciation.
- Flexibility is an edge: willingness to buy either whole businesses or large passive stakes doubles the chances of finding uses for cash, whereas firms limited to acquisitions they can operate have fewer options.
- Partial ownership: "it's better to have a partial interest in the Hope diamond than to own all of a rhinestone." Warren prefers a substantial non-controlling stake in a wonderful company to 100% of a so-so one. Holdings in American Express (14.2%), Coca-Cola (9.1%), IBM (6.3%) and Wells Fargo (9.2%) were raised; each extra tenth of a percent in the four adds $50 million to Berkshire's share of annual earnings. Their 2013 earnings share was $4.4 billion, but only about $1.4 billion in dividends is reported; the $3 billion retained is "every bit as valuable." Unrealized gains in the four were $39 billion.
- Warren admits mistakes: some small acquisitions earn poor returns because he was "simply wrong in my evaluation of the economic dynamics." He expects more mistakes in buying businesses and stocks. In Energy Future Holdings bonds he bought about $2 billion without consulting Charlie, then sold for $259 million after $837 million of interest, a $873 million pre-tax loss: "Next time I'll call Charlie."
- Bank of America warrants (700 million shares for $5 billion, worth $10.9 billion at yearend, expiring September 2021) are effectively the fifth largest equity position.

## Accounting realism: amortization, depreciation, EBITDA and coverage

- Non-insurance units earned 16.7% after tax on about $25 billion of average net tangible assets with little leverage. Individual businesses range from 25% to well over 100% after tax on unleveraged net tangible assets, down to a few poor performers.
- GAAP treats all intangible amortization alike, but some intangibles truly deplete (software) while others (customer relationships from purchase accounting) "are clearly not real costs." Of $648 million of amortization, roughly 20% are "real." Warren tells investors to add back most reported amortization.
- "Every dime of depreciation expense we report... is a real cost." Of EBITDA as a valuation guide: "button your wallet." Interest coverage is measured as pre-tax earnings/interest, not EBITDA/interest, "a commonly-used measure we view as seriously flawed." BNSF's coverage was 9:1.
- Great businesses can be bad investments at excessive prices; Berkshire paid large premiums over net tangible assets but gets decent returns.

## Regulated, capital-intensive businesses: BNSF and MidAmerican

- Both carry heavy long-lived regulated assets partly financed by long-term debt not guaranteed by Berkshire; earnings far exceed interest needs even in terrible economies.
- BNSF carries about 15% of U.S. inter-city freight by ton-miles; a ton moves about 500 miles per gallon of diesel, with trucks using about four times as much fuel. 2013 revenue $22,014 million, pre-tax operating earnings $6,657 million, net earnings $3,793 million.
- MidAmerican serves retail customers in eleven states, holds 7% of U.S. wind capacity, and retained more earnings than any other U.S. electric utility. Renewables will have cost $15 billion when current projects finish. Net earnings were $1,636 million (Berkshire share $1,470 million). It ranked number one in a customer-satisfaction survey of 101 utilities (95.3% "very satisfied" versus 34.5% at the bottom).
- Warren relishes such commitments "as long as they promise reasonable returns" and trusts future regulation because governments need capital to keep flowing to essential projects.
- Powerhouse Five (MidAmerican, BNSF, Iscar, Lubrizol, Marmon) earned a record $10.8 billion pre-tax; smaller non-insurance units earned $4.7 billion (up from $3.9 billion).

## Investing fundamentals: the farm, NYU property and the know-nothing investor

- 1986 farm: bought 400 acres near Omaha from the FDIC for $280,000 after a farm-price bubble burst (price declines of 50% or more; five times as many Iowa and Nebraska banks failed as in the Great Recession). His son's estimates implied a normalized return of about 10% with no downside and upside from productivity. Twenty-eight years later earnings tripled and value is five times or more. He has visited twice.
- 1993 NYU-adjacent retail property bought from the RTC with Larry Silverstein and Fred Rose: roughly 10% unleveraged yield, undermanaged, and a tenant on a bargain lease paying about $5 a foot versus $70 for others, expiring in nine years. Earnings tripled; annual distributions exceed 35% of original equity, plus refinancing distributions of over 150%. He has never seen it.
- Lessons: you needn't be an expert, but recognize limitations, "keep things simple and don't swing for the fences," and answer quick-profit promises with a "quick 'no.'" Focus on future productivity of the asset; if you can't roughly estimate future earnings, "forget it and move on." Focusing on price change is speculation; "the fact that a given asset has appreciated in the recent past is never a reason to buy it." "Games are won by players who focus on the playing field – not by those whose eyes are glued to the scoreboard." Macro opinions and market predictions are "a waste of time" and "dangerous."
- Mr. Market lesson: if a moody neighbor shouted daily farm prices, you could only benefit. But stock owners let others' irrational behavior make them irrational; liquidity becomes "a curse." "A climate of fear is your friend when investing; a euphoric world is your enemy." In 2008 he never considered selling the farm or real estate, nor stocks, which are small participations in wonderful businesses.
- Charlie and Warren's stock process mirrors buying whole businesses: estimate an earnings range five or more years out; buy if the price is reasonable relative to the bottom of that range; otherwise move on. In 54 years they have never skipped a purchase because of the macro or political environment. Stay inside the "circle of competence."
- For non-professionals: "The goal... should not be to pick winners" but to own a cross-section of businesses via a low-cost S&P 500 index fund. The Dow went from 66 to 11,497 in the 20th Century. Accumulate over a long period and never sell when news is bad. Frictional costs from advisers and trading are "devoid of benefit" in aggregate: "ignore the chatter, keep your costs minimal, and invest in stocks as you would in a farm."
- His will directs the trustee for his wife to put 10% of the cash in short-term government bonds and 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund (he suggests Vanguard's). Berkshire shares go to philanthropy over ten years after his estate closes.
- Ben Graham: Warren bought The Intelligent Investor in 1949; the key chapters are 8 and 20. Before that he tried charting and tape watching. Graham's adage: "Price is what you pay, value is what you get." The book's postscript mystery stock bought in 1948 was GEICO.

## Optimism about America and public pension warnings

- Warren calls betting on U.S. prosperity "very close to a sure thing." "Who has ever benefited during the past 237 years by betting against America?" and "America's best days lie ahead." The BNSF purchase in 2009 was an "all-in wager on the economic future of the United States."
- Public pension problems stem from promised pensions that can't be funded, a "gigantic financial tapeworm." Investment policy matters; he points to his 1975 memo to Katharine Graham on pension promises, and expects mostly bad news on public pensions in the next decade.

## Nebraska Furniture Mart and Mrs. B

- Warren bought NFM on August 30, 1983 with a 1¼-page proposal, accepted by Rose Blumkin without changes, with no investment bankers or lawyers; unaudited statements didn't worry him because "her word was good enough."
- Mrs. B never went to school, emigrated from Russia without English, and worked until 103. From a 1946 net worth of $72,264 and $50 cash, plus the talents of the family, NFM grew to the two highest-volume U.S. home furnishings stores (about $450 million each yearly in Omaha and Kansas City, Kansas). The Texas store at The Colony will have 1.8 million square feet on 433 acres.
- Warren has visiting students start the day at NFM: if they absorb Mrs. B's lessons, "they need none from me."

## Managers, compensation of focus and shareholder treatment

- Operating managers run their businesses "as if they were the only asset owned by their families"; most have no financial need to work, and the joy of hitting business "home runs" means as much as a paycheck.
- Todd Combs and Ted Weschler each manage over $7 billion and outperformed the S&P 500 in 2013 and Warren's own picks; they have "Berkshire blood in their veins."
- Corporate office: 24 people handle regulatory filings, a 23,000-page federal tax return, shareholder inquiries and the annual meeting. Berkshire employed a record 330,745 people at yearend.
- Fair disclosure: financial information is issued late Fridays or early Saturdays so all shareholders get it simultaneously with time to analyze; Charlie and Warren do not talk one-on-one with large institutions or analysts. Meeting questions come from journalists, analysts (including a credentialed bear), and the audience, with no advance notice to Warren and Charlie.
