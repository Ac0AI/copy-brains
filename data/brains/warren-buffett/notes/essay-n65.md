## Why Warren measures himself against the Dow, and what the comparison shows

- Warren sets one yardstick in advance: the Dow including dividends, judged over at least a three-year window. His stated long-term goal is an average of ten percentage points a year ahead of the Dow, before the general partner's allocation. He calls it "Our Goal", not "Our Promise".
- He says anyone who manages money should have a standard of measurement, and that manager and client should agree on it beforehand.
- Fixed yardsticks keep "past successes" from clouding "judgment of current results" and reduce "ingenious rationalizations of inept performance". They also mean he gets blamed only for the right reason (doing worse than the Dow), not for having losing years.
- Partners who reject the standard "should not be in the Partnership".
- Partnership results (before the general partner's allocation), 1957-1966, with the Dow in brackets: 1957 +10.4% (-8.4%), 1958 +40.9% (+38.5%), 1960 +22.8% (-6.2%), 1961 +45.9% (+22.4%), 1962 +13.9% (-7.6%), 1963 +38.7% (+20.6%), 1964 +27.8% (+18.7%), 1965 +47.2% (+14.2%), 1966 +20.4% (-15.6%).
- Limited partners' compounded rate over 1957-1966 was 23.9% a year, against 11.4% for the Dow. Cumulatively that is 704.2% against 122.9%.
- He reports that his own and his family's money is almost entirely in the Partnership. In 1966 that was over 90% of net worth for his family.

## Why the big funds trail the Dow, and what conservatism means

- Warren tracks four big funds: Massachusetts Investors Trust, Investors Stock Fund, Lehman and Tri-Continental. They manage over $5 billion and roughly 600,000 shareholders own them. He treats their record as typical of most professional managers.
- Their annual compounded rates for 1957-1966 were 8.6%, 7.9%, 9.8% and 9.0%, against 9.7% for the Dow. The Dow is "no pushover as an index".
- He blames five causes:
  - group decisions ("close to impossible for outstanding investment management to come from a group of any size")
  - a desire to conform to other large organizations
  - an institutional setup where average is "safe" and independent action isn't rewarded
  - irrational diversification
  - inertia
- He calls his own pledge of consistent measurement the first job of any investment organization.
- Warren says neither conventional nor unconventional is conservative per se. "Truly conservative actions arise from intelligent hypotheses, correct facts and sound reasoning." "A public opinion poll is no substitute for thought."
- He draws a distinction. Funds meet one definition of conservatism, results that vary only slightly from average. He prefers the other: "subject to substantially less temporary or permanent shrinkage in value than total experience". The first he'd call "conventionalism".
- His test of conservatism is performance in down markets. Over the three down years 1957, 1960 and 1962, the Dow fell 20.6%, the funds fell 9.7% to 24.6%, and the limited partners gained 45.0%.
- Even Samson gets clipped. In H1 1966 an equal split of $100,000 across eight giants (GM, Standard of New Jersey, Sears, DuPont, U.S. Steel, Aetna, AT&T, Bank of America) lost 16.6%.

## Concentration and diversification

- Ground Rule 7 (from November 1965): "We diversify substantially less than most investment operations. We might invest up to 40% of our net worth in a single security under conditions coupling an extremely high probability that our facts and reasoning are correct with a very low probability that anything could drastically change the underlying value of the investment."
- His ideal would be 50 uncorrelated opportunities, each with an expected edge of 15 points over the Dow, 2% in each. Reality is that he has to work very hard to find a very few.
- A qualifying idea is one he expects to beat the Dow by at least ten points a year. Sizing between idea number one and number eight depends on the gap in expectation and on the chance of a poor relative result. Two ideas with equal expectation can warrant different sizes if one has a 5% chance of badly lagging and the other 1%.
- He sizes the limit so that the chance a single investment (or correlated group) makes the whole portfolio more than ten points worse than the Dow is tiny.
- He says a 100-stock portfolio "is not being operated logically". The hundredth stock can't reduce variance enough to offset the damage to expected return. He calls this the "Noah School of Investing - two of everything". Billy Rose: "You've got a harem of seventy girls; you don't get to know any of them very well."
- He accepts bumpier year-to-year results for better long-term performance, but not "substantial permanent capital loss".
- Only five or six situations in nine years went above 25% of the portfolio. In 1966 he had two over 25%, one a controlled company and one a large company he'd never take an active part in.
- 1965 was driven by five investments, with gains from about $800,000 to about $3.5 million. His five smallest general holdings were "lackluster".
- Borrowing stays small. Bank borrowings averaged well under 10% of net worth in 1966. A partner's line: "even five minutes is a long time if one's head is being held under water."

## The four investment categories

1. Generals - Private Owner Basis
   - Undervalued stocks, found on quantitative standards, trading well below value to a private owner.
   - He also likes good management, a decent industry and some "ferment", but "we demand value".
   - It's a "two strings to our bow" situation: market price can rise, or he can buy control at a bargain. Example: a 1965 tender offer paid $3,188,000 realized on one holding, of which $2,737,000 was economic gain for 1965 after $451,000 of unrealized gain in 1964.
2. Generals - Relatively Undervalued
   - Securities cheap relative to similar-quality securities, where private-owner value isn't meaningful (usually because of size).
   - He compares apples to apples and passes when he doesn't know the industry.
   - A hedging technique introduced in 1964-65 cuts the risk of a broad revaluation. Example risk: buying at 12 times earnings when peers trade at 20, then peers fall to 10.
   - The category is less solid for projection. In 1965-66 results rested on one or two holdings, one of which became his largest position and met the 40% rule.
3. Workouts
   - Securities with a timetable (mergers, sell-outs, reorganizations, spin-offs). He acts only on publicly announced events: "We wait until we can read it in the paper."
   - Risk is the deal failing (antitrust, stockholder disapproval, tax rulings). Gross profits look small, "like looking for parking meters with some time left on them".
   - Predictable returns and short holding periods give decent annual rates. Workouts cushion down years and lag in bull markets.
   - They returned 14.6% in 1962, 30.6% in 1963, 10.3% in 1964 and about 22.4% in 1966 on average capital of $7.67 million. This was once 30-40% of net worth but is unlikely to be again.
4. Controls
   - These come from the private-owner category, when a stock stays cheap long enough to build a big stake. He is active only when needed to optimize capital use.
   - Must have a built-in profit from the purchase price. Value then follows the business, not market quotes. He looks for wide margins and measures results over several years.
   - Dempster Mill's buyers are doing well, which he values. He rejects the "Bigger Fool Theory".

## Berkshire Hathaway and Hochschild, Kohn

- Buying of Berkshire began in November 1962 at $7.60 a share. Average cost was $14.86 after heavy early-1965 buying, which gave control in spring 1965.
- Net working capital alone was about $19 a share at end of 1965, before valuing plant and equipment. The company had slid from a 1948 peak of $29.5 million pretax earnings, 11 mills and about 11,000 workers to two mills and about 2,300 employees.
- He found excellent existing managers, notably Ken Chace, and brought in no outsiders. "It is well to have a diet consisting of oatmeal as well as cream puffs."
- Controlled holdings are valued as businesses, not at market. At end of 1965 he valued Berkshire halfway between net current asset value and book value, which meant current assets at 100 cents on the dollar and fixed assets at 50.
- In H1 1966 the Partnership, with two 10% partners, bought all of Hochschild, Kohn, a privately owned Baltimore department store. It was the first entire business bought by negotiation. About 10% of the Partnership's $50 million-plus is in HK, against more than three times that in one marketable security.
- He values HK at cost plus retained earnings, and says management quality mattered: he wouldn't have bought it with run-of-the-mill managers even at a lower price.
- He prefers the "iceberg approach" to disclosure.

## Temperament, forecasting, and staying in your lane

- Ground Rule 6: "I am not in the business of predicting general stock market or business fluctuations." He buys on what a company should do, not when. The market sets when he is right, his business analysis sets whether.
- He asks two questions of callers predicting declines. If they knew in February the Dow would hit 865 in May, why not say so then? And if not, how do they know in May?
- A private business is never bought or sold on a stock market guess. Market quotes should be used when they get silly in either direction, never allowed to dictate judgment. He points to chapter two of Graham's *The Intelligent Investor* as the most investment-relevant writing he knows.
- He expects poor years. If a 20-30% drop in equity value would cause distress, avoid stocks. He also warns that years of -20% or worse "should definitely be expected".
- He will not buy what he doesn't understand. He knows nothing of semiconductors or integrated circuits, and avoids deals where technology is crucial to the decision. His stance is "If you can't join 'em, lick 'em" and he will not follow fashion investing, where market action overrides business value.
- He also avoids deals where major human problems appear likely.
- He doesn't trust prophecy: it "reveals far more of the frailties of the prophet than it reveals of the future".
- He says short-term results under three years mean little, and that reporting more often than annually is foolish in a long-term business.

## Expectations, size, and the end of the easy years

- In January 1965 he expected the Dow to return about 7% a year over 20-30 years (versus 11.1% to that date), a ten-point average edge, and so about 17% before allocation and about 14% for limited partners. He labels this "conjecture".
- He says the 1957-1966 results can't be repeated: "They may well be achieved by some hungry twenty-five year old working with $105,100". Not by a thirty-six-year-old with $54,065,345.
- He now finds perhaps one-fifth to one-tenth as many good ideas as before, only two or three new ideas a year meeting his ten-point yardstick. Causes: a changed market, his increased size and more competition. Earlier, "a lesser effort produced literally dozens".
- Capital was about $43.6 million entering 1966. He decided to admit no new partners unless they brought something other than capital, while allowing existing partners to add.
- If the stream of ideas dries up, he promises to tell partners "honestly and promptly".

## Taxes, compounding, and the cost of fees and managers

- The goal is the largest after-tax compound rate, not the lowest taxes. "Means and end should not be confused."
- A portfolio should hold the most attractive securities at current prices, which means change and realized gains. Past performance, getting even, and the warm glow of a winner are irrelevant.
- The only ways to avoid tax are to die with the asset, give it away, or lose the gain. He maximizes gains and pays the tax.
- Swap funds (a tax-deferral pitch) did badly, he notes. Federal Street and Westminster, managed by advisers overseeing $2 billion-plus, returned 2.6% and 1.1% a year for 1962-64, against 9.8% for the Dow.
- Compounding: $100,000 at 4% / 8% / 12% / 16% becomes about $224,337 / $906,260 / $2,895,970 / $8,484,940 over 30 years. Small gains in annual rate matter a lot, so "every percentage point of investment return above average" has real meaning.
- Peter Minuit's $24 for Manhattan in 1626, compounded at 6.5%, would be about $42 billion today and about $205 billion at 7%.
- Advance payments from partners earn 6% to year-end, and advance withdrawals (up to 20%) cost 6%. He expects to earn better than 6% over the long term, since the general partner earns nothing unless returns exceed 6%.
