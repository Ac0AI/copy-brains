## Why earnings and book value must be judged against the capital that produces them

- Warren says an economic gain "must be evaluated by comparison with the capital that produces them." Berkshire's 1984 net worth gain was $152.6 million ($133 per share), which he calls "mediocre" because it was only 13.6% on the capital base.
- The 20-year compounded gain in book value was 22.1% a year ($19.46 in 1964 to $1,108.77 in 1984).
- The measure that counts is the gain in per-share intrinsic business value. It is subjective, so he uses book value as a useful but somewhat understated proxy.
- A growing capital base drags on returns. To earn even 15% a year for the next decade, Berkshire would need about $3.9 billion of aggregate profits. That takes "a few big ideas - small ones just won't do." He and Charlie Munger had none at the moment but had found that they "pop up occasionally."
- He regards any single year's realized capital gains or losses as meaningless. The aggregate of realized and unrealized gains over a period of years is "very important."
- On GEICO, the stock's carrying value barely moved in 1984 while its intrinsic value rose substantially. He would far rather have business value rise by X with market value falling than have intrinsic value rise only X/2 with market value soaring. "We look to business performance, not market performance. If we are correct in expectations regarding the business, the market eventually will follow along."

## Share repurchases: when they create value and how they signal management's character

- Buybacks at prices well below intrinsic value immediately raise per-share intrinsic value. Companies "often find it easy to get $2 of present value for $1." Corporate acquisition programs almost never do as well, and often fail to get $1 of value for each $1 spent.
- The second, subtler benefit is the signal. Repurchases when market value is well below business value demonstrate a management that acts for shareholder wealth rather than for expanding its own domain. Investors raise their estimates of future returns and prices move closer to intrinsic value. That repricing is rational: you should pay more for a business in the hands of a manager with demonstrated pro-shareholder leanings.
- The key word is "demonstrated." A manager who consistently skips clearly beneficial repurchases reveals his motives, however often he says "maximizing shareholder wealth." The market discounts assets lodged with him: "His heart is not listening to his mouth - and, after a while, neither will the market."
- Warren endorses only buybacks driven by price/value relationships. He calls "greenmail" repurchases "odious and repugnant," because the extortionist and the insiders both exploit an unconsulted third party, the innocent shareholder who funds the payoff.
- Berkshire's three largest holdings (GEICO, Washington Post, General Foods) all repurchased heavily at bargain prices. Exxon, the fourth largest and a recent position, also did. He likes owning businesses with excellent economics and shareholder-conscious managers.
- Tax and accounting detail on proportionate redemptions:
  - Berkshire sold shares back to GEICO (1983 tender, 350,000 shares, $21 million) and General Foods (1984, $21,843,601) so its ownership percentage stayed exactly unchanged (General Foods at 8.75%). The tax code treated these as dividends, taxed at the 6.9% inter-corporate rate.
  - He viewed the economic substance as dividend income. The receipts were far below the earnings retained for Berkshire's account.
  - To avoid a qualified opinion, Berkshire accepted the auditor's New York office view and recorded them as stock sales.
  - He considers such repurchases "at least equally favorable for shareholders who do not sell."

## Dividend policy and capital allocation: retain only if a dollar retained creates at least a dollar of market value

- He says dividend policy is "often reported to shareholders, but seldom explained," yet capital allocation "is crucial to business and investment management." A payout target such as "40% to 50% of earnings" is given without any analysis of why it suits owners.
- Not all earnings are equal. In asset-heavy businesses, inflation makes some reported earnings "ersatz" or "restricted." If distributed, the business loses ground in unit volume, competitive position or financial strength. Restricted earnings are seldom worthless to owners but "often must be discounted heavily." Consolidated Edison is his example of retention regardless of return: its stock sold at one-fourth of book value, so each retained dollar became 25 cents of market value, yet its slogan was "Dig We Must."
- For unrestricted earnings, the only valid reason to retain is "a reasonable prospect - backed preferably by historical evidence or ... a thoughtful analysis of the future - that for every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners." That requires incremental earnings at or above what investors can get elsewhere.
- Bond illustration: take a risk-free perpetual 10% bond with a cash-or-reinvest coupon option.
  - If market rates are 5%, take more bonds, which are worth well over 100 cents on the dollar.
  - If market rates are 15%, take cash.
  - Owners should reason the same way about retained earnings, estimating the likely return on reinvestment.
- Managers reason correctly at the subsidiary level (move cash from a 5%-return unit to a 15%-return unit) but often impose a historical or industry payout pattern on parent-company shareholders. They rarely supply owners the same analysis they demand from subsidiaries.
- A great core business can camouflage poor allocation. Returns on incremental capital look good because the core absorbs small amounts at very high returns, like a Pro-Am golf team's best-ball score hiding hopeless amateurs. Many companies burn retained earnings on high-priced acquisitions with "inherently mediocre economics." Better to retain only what the high-return business can use and pay out the rest or repurchase stock. Such managers "should be held to account for those allocation decisions."
- Dividends should be steady and reflect long-term expectations, not wiggle with each quarter. "If earnings have been unwisely retained, it is likely that managers, too, have been unwisely retained."
- Berkshire's own application:
  - It has earned well over market rates on retained earnings and so retains everything.
  - It would have been disastrous to pay out early earnings. The original cornerstone businesses had, respectively, survived but earned almost nothing (textiles), shriveled while incurring large losses (department stores), and shrunk to about 5% of their sales volume (trading stamps).
  - Only by redeploying into better businesses did it overcome those origins.
  - If prospective returns fall below the one-dollar-per-dollar threshold, it will distribute unrestricted earnings. It judges history with a five-year rolling average.

## Treating bonds as a "business": the WPPSS investment and the inflation warning

- Berkshire applies the same business-valuation lens to marketable stocks and to bonds. If it can buy small pieces of businesses with satisfactory economics at a fraction of per-share business value, "something good is likely to happen to us - particularly if we own a group of such securities." Academics reply that it works in practice but "will never work in theory."
- From October 1983 to June 1984 Berkshire's insurers bought Washington Public Power Supply System Projects 1, 2 and 3 bonds, even though the same issuer had defaulted on $2.2 billion of Projects 4 and 5 bonds.
- The math:
  - A $139 million yearend cost was compared with a $139 million operating business.
  - The bond "business" earned $22.7 million after tax in cash, a 16.3% after-tax return on unleveraged capital.
  - A comparable business at those earnings (about $45 million pre-tax) might sell for $250-$300 million, double what Berkshire paid.
  - Downside: a small risk the "business" is worth nothing within a year or two, and a possible interruption of interest.
  - Upside is capped at about $205 million face value, only 48% above cost.
- A 12% zero-coupon bond bought for $10 million grows to $300 million by 2015, the same as a $10 million business earning 12% on equity and retaining everything.
- Example of bond-buying errors: in 1946, 20-year AAA tax-exempt bonds yielded just under 1%, a "business" earning 1% on book that could never earn more, bought at 100 cents on the dollar, while good businesses were available near book value earning 10%-15% after tax. Ben Graham's line, from the last chapter of "The Intelligent Investor," which Warren calls by far the best investing book: "Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike."
- Inflation view:
  - He dislikes most long-term bonds, "as sound as a dollar," and views the long-term outlook for dollars as dismal.
  - He expects substantial inflation and sees a small but real chance of runaway inflation because of the huge deficit.
  - In runaway inflation a diversified stock portfolio would lose real value, but outstanding bonds would suffer far more. An all-bond portfolio carries an "unacceptable 'wipe out' risk."
  - Bond purchases must clear a special hurdle: decidedly superior to other business opportunities, so such occasions are rare.

## Temperament, concentration and unconventional decisions

- Warren says Charlie and he cannot get 50 such decisions a year; "in any given year we run a risk that we will look extraordinarily foolish." The intelligent-but-might-look-like-an-idiot decision is one most managers avoid, because success earns a pat on the back and failure a pink slip. "Failing conventionally is the route to go; as a group, lemmings may have a rotten image, but no individual lemming has ever received bad press."
- Berkshire's equation differs: with 47% of the stock, Charlie and he "don't worry about being fired" and are rewarded as owners, not managers, so they behave with Berkshire's money as with their own.
- They concentrate insurance-company investments in a few securities they have thought hard about and bought at attractive prices. This is appropriate only because of exceptional financial strength; most insurers could not withstand a big error. Billy Rose on over-diversification: "If you have a harem of forty women, you never get to know any of them very well." Concentration should produce superior long-term results, tempered by size, and a "really bad year" is inevitable.
- Doing nothing is hard. It had been over ten years since equity investments meeting both qualitative and quantitative standards were this difficult to find, and "we find doing nothing the most difficult task of all." He cites "masterly inactivity" as a strategy easier for historians to commend than for participants to follow.
- Secrecy on trading: Berkshire will not comment on its securities activity to anyone. In WPPSS it tried to buy nearly all it could from October 1983 to June 1984 but got under 3% of bonds outstanding; a few coat-tail riders "easily could have cost us $5 million." Any change in position would be disclosed only long after completion.
- Subsequent event: Berkshire agreed to buy three million Capital Cities Communications shares at $172.50, contingent on Capital Cities acquiring ABC. Warren calls Tom Murphy and Dan Burke "tops in both ability and integrity."

## Business quality: Nebraska Furniture Mart, See's and the dominant newspaper

- Nebraska Furniture Mart (Mrs. B, Rose Blumkin, age 91):
  - Sales rose $14.3 million to $115 million, from one Omaha store, the largest volume of any single home-furnishings store in the US.
  - Levitz ran a 44.4% gross margin and 35.6% operating expenses. NFM's margin is a bit over half that, with operating expenses about 16.5% of sales. NFM saves customers at least $30 million a year versus typical markups while earning excellent returns on capital.
  - The Blumkin family's four "secrets": (1) energy and application; (2) realistically define their area of competence and act decisively within it; (3) ignore enticing propositions outside it; (4) behave in a high-grade manner with everyone. Mrs. B's version: "sell cheap and tell the truth."
  - Buying 90% for $55 million: no audit, no inventory count, no receivables check, no title check. "We gave Mrs. B a check for $55 million and she gave us her word."
  - It all began with a $500 investment in 1937.
- See's Candies:
  - 1984 sales were $135.9 million, after-tax profit $13.4 million, 24.8 million pounds, 214 shops. Per-pound price was up only 1.4%, and 1985 prices were expected up 6%-7%.
  - Same-store volume fell 1.1%, creating cost pressure. Quantity is held level only by adding shops.
  - The Christmas four weeks produce 40% of volume and about 75% of profits.
  - Competitors add preservatives or freeze product to smooth production. See's refuses, "opting, in effect, for production headaches rather than product modification."
  - Success is credited to an exceptional product and an exceptional manager, Chuck Huggins.
- Buffalo Evening News (a dominant newspaper):
  - Total hours (excluding the newsroom) fell about 2.8% and overall costs rose only 4.9%. The 50.9% news hole (versus a typical 35%-40%) and a maintained newsroom staff are deliberate.
  - Advertising is usually 70%-80% of revenue and responds to useful circulation. Concentrated local circulation matters because "wasted" circulation raises costs without adding revenue. Penetration was number one on weekdays among the top 100 papers.
  - "The economics of a dominant newspaper are excellent, among the very best in the business world." Third-rate papers earn as well or better than first-class ones while dominant. Once dominant, the paper itself, not the marketplace, determines quality, so "management must impose its own" standards. Typical news-cost differences are about three points against pre-tax margins "often ten times that amount."
- GEICO: its low-cost auto and homeowners business has a "major, sustainable competitive advantage," which is "a rare asset in business generally" and almost non-existent in financial services. GEICO earned superior profit nowhere else but its core. Berkshire owned 36% and its share of GEICO's $885 million direct volume was $320 million. Lou Simpson has the "rare combination of temperamental and intellectual characteristics" for long-term investing, generating the industry's best returns "with below-average risk."

## Insurance economics: pricing cycles, reserving errors and financial strength

- Industry premiums must grow about 10% a year to keep the combined ratio stable (loss costs grow roughly 10% from volume, inflation and expanding judicial coverage). Since 1979 premiums rose 30% versus the 61% that 10% a year would give. The 1984 industry combined ratio was 117.7, not the 1979 level of 100.6.
  - Getting back to 100 would need five years of 15% premium growth. He expects several years above 10% followed by competitive pricing and combined ratios of 108-113.
  - Year-to-year change in industry premium volume is "the key index" to underwriting profitability.
- Berkshire's own 1984 combined ratio was a "humbling 134," the third year in a row far worse than the industry.
- Reserving errors:
  - Insurer statements are "at best, only a first rough draft of earnings and financial condition." The charge includes paid losses, estimates for reported-unsettled and incurred-but-not-reported losses, and revisions of prior years' estimates.
  - Example: a 1979 claim reserved at $10,000 but settled in 1984 for $100,000 charges $90,000 to 1984 earnings though it was a 1979 expense.
  - Berkshire reported underwriting results that were later corrected unfavorably each year since 1981: 1983 was reported as a $33.2 million loss and corrected to $51.0 million. 1984's reported $45.4 million loss was $27.6 million on 1984 business plus $17.8 million of 1983 correction.
  - Causes: juries and courts making the "deep pocket" pay, publicity about giant awards, and ceding insurers' mistakes in reinsurance, which Berkshire inherits since reserves are set on their data.
  - Consequences: management acted with less urgency, and Berkshire overpaid taxes on overstated earnings.
  - The "rented suit" story: a brother pays the funeral bill, then keeps getting $15 bills, because "We buried Dad in a rented suit." He uses it to illustrate lingering reserve liabilities, saying the record "should make us humble, and you suspicious."
- The "walking dead":
  - Insolvent insurers can be "broke but flush," because premium cash arrives long before losses are paid. Some take optimistic reserve views, and some hide losses, and external auditors cannot effectively police this. "The corpse is supposed to file the death certificate."
  - They keep writing business at almost any price, like an embezzler hoping the next batch covers earlier shortfalls, since penalties are no greater for a $100 million shortfall than $10 million.
  - Berkshire suffers from their sell-at-any-price competition and from state guarantee fund assessments when insolvency surfaces.
- Financial strength as a competitive weapon:
  - He argues the promise should be appraised "against the possibility of adversity, not prosperity." Berkshire believes it is almost certainly the strongest property/casualty operation in the country and intends to stay so.
  - This matters for structured settlements and loss-reserve assumptions, where claimants need confidence in decades of payments. Funds held against assumed liabilities grew from $16.2 million to $30.6 million.
  - Weakened competitors let Berkshire raise prices significantly in the last half of 1984 with little business lost. Its plan is to build insurance capital with retained earnings as industry premiums rose, probably over $15 billion in 1985 versus under $5 billion in 1983.

## What Berkshire wants to buy, and how it runs its annual meeting

- The standing "business wanted" ad lists:
  - Large purchases (at least $5 million of after-tax earnings).
  - Demonstrated consistent earning power; projections and turnarounds are of no interest.
  - Good returns on equity with little or no debt.
  - Management in place ("we can't supply it").
  - Simple businesses ("if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it").
  - An offering price.
- No unfriendly takeovers; complete confidentiality and an answer typically within five minutes. Cash preferred. Stock is considered only when Berkshire receives as much intrinsic business value as it gives.
- Annual meeting: Warren and Charlie answer every question about the business "no matter how long it takes," except matters where candor could cost money, mainly securities activity. He contrasts it with meetings that become "theatrics, spleen-venting and advocacy of issues."
- 97.2% of eligible shares took part in the shareholder-designated contributions program ($3,179,000 to 1,519 charities).
- He notes: "Managers who put their trust in capitalism seem in no hurry to put their trust in capitalists."
