## Berkshire's operating philosophy: partnership, per-share value, and owner orientation

- Warren describes Berkshire as a corporation in form but a partnership in attitude. He and Charlie Munger see shareholders as owner-partners and themselves as managing partners; the company is "a conduit through which our shareholders own the assets."
- All directors are major shareholders; for at least four of five, over 50% of family net worth is in Berkshire. "We eat our own cooking."
- The long-term goal is to maximize the average annual gain in intrinsic business value per share. Size is not the measure. He expects the rate to diminish as the capital base grows, but would be disappointed if it did not exceed that of the average large American corporation.
- Preferred route: directly own a diversified group of businesses that generate cash and earn above-average returns on capital. Second choice: own parts of similar businesses via marketable stocks held by the insurance subsidiaries. Price, availability, and insurance capital needs decide each year's allocation.
- "A managerial 'wish list' will not be filled at shareholder expense." Berkshire will not diversify by buying whole businesses at control prices that ignore long-term economics. It will only do with shareholders' money what it would do with its own.
- Candor: report the facts he would want to know if positions were reversed. "The CEO who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in private." Marketable-securities activity is discussed only as legally required, since good investment ideas are "rare, valuable and subject to competitive appropriation."

## Capital allocation rules: retention test, debt, share issuance, and not selling

- Retained earnings test: retention must deliver at least $1 of market value per $1 retained, assessed on a rolling five-year basis. It has been met so far; Warren notes it gets harder as net worth grows.
- Accounting consequences do not drive decisions. With similar acquisition costs, he prefers $2 of earnings not reportable by Berkshire (from partial stock holdings) over $1 that is reportable. Whole businesses often sell for double the pro-rata price of small portions. Unreported earnings are expected to show up in intrinsic value through capital gains.
- Debt: rarely used, and structured as long-term fixed rate when it is. "We will reject interesting opportunities rather than over-leverage our balance sheet." This conservatism has cost results but fits obligations to policyholders, depositors, lenders, and equity holders.
- Share issuance: only when Berkshire receives as much business value as it gives, across mergers, offerings, stock-for-debt swaps, options, and convertibles. Issuing shares means selling small portions of the company, which must not be done inconsistently with the value of the whole.
- Selling: no interest in selling good businesses at any price, and reluctance to sell sub-par ones that still generate cash with good managers and labor relations. This hurts returns, but "gin rummy managerial behavior (discard your least promising business at each turn) is not our style."
- On fixing poor businesses with capex: "The projections will be dazzling - the advocates will be sincere - but, in the end, major additional investment in a terrible industry usually is about as rewarding as struggling in quicksand."
- Dilution check: Berkshire had 1,146,909 shares outstanding versus 1,137,778 at the start of fiscal 1965, so owners at that time hold .99% for every 1% they held then, even as all of today's assets were added.

## Book value versus intrinsic business value, and how to judge performance

- 1983 book value rose from $737.43 to $975.83 per share (32%). Over the 19-year tenure, it grew from $19.46 to $975.83, or 22.6% compounded. Warren says that rate cannot be sustained at current size: "Those who believe otherwise should pursue a career in sales, but avoid one in mathematics."
- He does not take one-year figures seriously ("why should the time required for a planet to circle the sun synchronize precisely with the time required for business actions to pay off?"). He recommends at least a five-year test. Red lights should flash if the five-year average gain falls much below the return on equity of American industry in aggregate.
- Book value is an accounting concept recording contributed capital and retained earnings: "Book value tells you what has been put in; intrinsic business value estimates what can be taken out." Intrinsic value is future cash output discounted to present value.
- Analogy: two children whose college costs are identical have the same book value of education, but the future payoff could range from zero to many times the cost.
- At the start of 1965, book value overstated intrinsic value because it was all textile assets earning inadequate returns. Now intrinsic value exceeds book value because (1) some stocks are carried at lower of cost or market (a $70 million pre-tax excess, about $50 million after tax, at end-1983), and (2) several businesses have economic Goodwill far above the accounting Goodwill.
- Warren's own shift: 35 years earlier he was taught to favor tangible assets and shun Goodwill-dependent businesses, which caused "many important business mistakes of omission, although relatively few of commission." Citing Keynes: "The difficulty lies not in the new ideas but in escaping from the old ones." He now strongly prefers businesses with large enduring Goodwill and minimal tangible assets.

## Economic Goodwill, See's Candies, and inflation

- Economic Goodwill is the capitalized value of earnings on tangible assets far above market rates of return. Sources: consumer franchises where value to the buyer, not production cost, sets price; governmental franchises not subject to profit regulation (e.g., television stations); and an enduring low-cost-producer position.
- See's example: Blue Chip paid $25 million in 1972 against about $8 million of net tangible assets and about $2 million after-tax earnings. By 1983 See's earned $13 million after tax on about $20 million of net tangible assets, roughly 25%+ on tangibles with no leverage. Accounting amortization of the $17 million Goodwill (about $425,000 a year over 40 years) fell while economic Goodwill grew.
- Inflation comparison: a See's-type business needed only $8 million more capital to double earnings to $4 million, and could be worth about $50 million (over $3 of value per $1 invested). A mundane business earning 11% on $18 million of tangibles would need $18 million more and end up worth only about $36 million, a dollar-for-dollar result like a savings account.
- Conclusion: asset-heavy businesses are hurt most by inflation and earn low returns that barely fund their own inflationary needs. "During inflation, Goodwill is the gift that keeps giving." He calls the traditional wisdom of tangible-asset inflation protection "long on tradition, short on wisdom."
- Spurious accounting Goodwill from silly acquisition prices might better be labeled "No-Will."
- Two perspectives: (1) when analyzing operating results, ignore amortization and look at earnings on unleveraged net tangible assets; (2) when evaluating acquisitions, ignore amortization and view Goodwill at full cost, including the full intrinsic value of consideration given. "A good business is not always a good purchase - although it's a good place to look for one."
- See's operations: product quality is "sacred" and raw materials are bought at the finest quality regardless of price. Non-raw-material costs per pound rose faster than inflation, which had to be reversed. Same-store poundage fell 0.8% in 1983 (about 8% cumulative since 1979), so the 1984 price increase was kept modest, and profits were expected to be flat. Sales were $133.5 million with $13.7 million after-tax profit in 1983. Warren notes: "In candy, as in stocks, price and value can differ; price is what you give, value is what you get."

## Nebraska Furniture Mart and how Warren appraises a business

- Berkshire bought 90% of Nebraska Furniture Mart in 1983, with 10% left to family members in management and 10% optioned to young family managers. Mrs. Blumkin (90) stayed as Chairman and worked the floor seven days a week.
- She started in 1937 with $500, beat entrenched competitors who pressured suppliers, won Fair Trade lawsuits, and once sold carpet to the judge. The single 200,000 sq ft store does over $100 million in sales and sells more than all other Omaha competitors combined.
- Appraisal test: "how I would like, assuming I had ample capital and skilled personnel, to compete with it." He would "rather wrestle grizzlies than compete with Mrs. B."
- Why it works: brilliant buying, expense ratios competitors "don't even dream about," and passing savings to customers. "It's the ideal business - one built upon exceptional value to the customer that in turn translates into exceptional economics for its owners."
- Louie Blumkin is regarded as the shrewdest furniture and appliance buyer in the country. Warren emphasizes character and work ethic of the family above business ability, and says he had admired the business for decades before the deal closed quickly.

## Buffalo News: measuring newspaper franchise strength

- Warren considers a paper's penetration ratio (percent of households buying daily) the best measure of franchise strength. High penetration in the area retailers care about makes a paper an efficient buy for advertisers.
- The News ranked number one in weekday penetration among the 100 largest US papers and number three on Sunday. Average Sunday circulation was 376,000, against 270,000 for the Courier-Express in 1976.
- Three reasons: a stable population (low migration), a reputation for editorial quality and integrity, and a 50% news hole versus a high-30s average for comparable papers. Page for page, readers get over 25% more news, and Berkshire will keep it despite pressure for margins.
- The 1983 after-tax margin somewhat exceeded the 10% target, helped by a tax loss carry-forward and a newsprint cost drop he called an "unanticipated fluke" that would reverse.
- Preprint growth (revenue from $3.6 million to $8.1 million) is economically worse than ROP advertising: lower profitability, more competition, and less news space.
- He credits Henry Urban for not flinching through litigation and losses after the Sunday paper launched, an introduction many in the industry questioned. Without Sunday, the News would not have survived long term.

## Insurance, GEICO, and the long lead-time of underwriting

- Berkshire's own insurance operations had a 121 combined ratio in 1983 against an industry 111 (worst in a long time). GEICO wrote at 96 after a large voluntary accrual for policyholder dividends. Warren says his own decisions of a few years earlier produced the poor results: "I made some serious mistakes a few years ago that came home to roost."
- Insurance has long lead times: policy and personnel changes take years to show. That is how Berkshire made so much in GEICO, since the outcome could be foreseen well before it appeared.
- He expects poor industry experience for years, with the combined ratio averaging no better than 1981-83 levels. Given pessimism on inflation, industry premiums must grow about 10% a year just to stabilize loss ratios.
- GEICO: Berkshire owns roughly one-third, giving a $270 million share of premium volume, about 80% larger than its own. He credits "a truly exceptional business idea and an exceptional management," discipline on full loss reserves, and Lou Simpson as "the class of the field among insurance investment managers."
- New focus: reinsurance where the buyer's overriding concern is the seller's long-term creditworthiness, such as structured settlements (lifetime periodic payments) and loss-reserve transfers. Berkshire's financial strength is the selling point. It "front ends" no profit on these and expenses overhead currently.
- Tax detail: the special GEICO distribution (a proportional tender) was treated as a dividend, taxed at an effective 6.9% for corporations versus 28% on capital gains.
- Undistributed earnings of non-controlled holdings are expected to be reflected in market prices dollar-for-dollar over time. Berkshire's experience had been "far more than a dollar of market value gain for every dollar of earnings retained." Year-end common stocks: $566 million cost, $1.306 billion market, with GEICO at $398 million and R.J. Reynolds, General Foods, and Washington Post among top holdings.

## Stock splits, shareholder quality, and the cost of market activity

- Warren opposes splitting Berkshire's stock. The goal is a price "rationally related" to intrinsic value, which requires rational shareholders. "Manic-depressive personalities produce manic-depressive valuations."
- He attracts the right owners through consistent communication of business and ownership philosophy and then lets self-selection work, like an opera versus a rock concert audience. Over 95% of shares were held by those who were holders five years earlier, and the holding is typically at least double the size of the holder's next largest.
- A split would draw buyers who "prefer paper to value" (feeling wealthier with nine $10 bills than one $100 bill). "People who buy for non-value reasons are likely to sell for non-value reasons."
- "A hyperactive stock market is the pickpocket of enterprise." Example: a company earning 12% on equity with 100% annual turnover and 1% commissions each way loses 2% of net worth a year, or 1/6 of earnings, to transfer costs.
- At 100-million-share days and 15 cents per share each way, the "chair-changing tax" is about $7.5 billion a year, roughly the combined 1982 profits of Exxon, GM, Mobil, and Texaco. Investment management fees of over $2 billion equal the earnings of the five largest banks. "These expensive activities may decide who eats the pie, but they don't enlarge it."
- Berkshire's spread is about 2% with roughly 3% annual true turnover, so owners pay about 6/100 of 1% of market value yearly for transfer privileges.

## Acquisition criteria

- Warren repeats Berkshire's criteria: (1) large purchases, at least $5 million of after-tax earnings; (2) demonstrated consistent earning power ("future projections are of little interest to us, nor are 'turn-around' situations"); (3) good returns on equity with little or no debt; (4) management in place; (5) simple businesses ("if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it"); (6) an offering price stated up front.
- No unfriendly takeovers. He promises confidentiality and a fast answer, customarily within five minutes. Cash is preferred; stock only when Berkshire gets as much intrinsic value as it gives.
- Sellers are invited to check references from past deals: "For the right business - and the right people - we can provide a good home."
