## Why Warren expects Berkshire's operating earnings to hold up in 2023

- Q1 2023 operating earnings were a little over $8 billion. Operating earnings are Berkshire's GAAP earnings excluding realized and unrealized capital gains.
- Warren expects capital gains over time ("why would we own the stocks otherwise"), but stock prices "move around capriciously" in any quarter, year or even five-year period. He judges the businesses, not the quotes.
- The post-pandemic period was "about as extraordinary as I've seen in business since World War II": government money chased scarce goods, buyers did not wait for sales, and backlogs were full. That period has ended.
- He expects a majority of Berkshire's operating businesses to report lower earnings in 2023 than in 2022. Some managers over-ordered inventory that arrived just as demand cooled, and now need sales.
- Two items push the other way:
  - Investment income: about $125 billion sat in very short-term investments earning roughly 4 basis points (about $50 million a year). Berkshire had just bought $3 billion of T-bills at about 5.9%, which means roughly $5 billion a year on the same cash.
  - Insurance underwriting: it does not correlate with the business cycle. It depends on events like hurricanes and earthquakes, so on probability the year should beat 2022. One massive quake or a hurricane in the wrong place could change that.
- He cautions that "nothing is sure" in forecasts, and Berkshire pays little attention to markets or forecasts.

## Retained earnings, net worth and per-share ownership

- Berkshire retains all earnings, so operating earnings should be significantly higher in 5, 10 or 15 years. Warren calls this "no great triumph" because retention is what built the number from nothing. Historical growth rates cannot be repeated on today's capital base.
- Gaap net worth was about $504 billion at March 31. No other US company reports a larger number. This is not because Berkshire is the most valuable company; others used their cash to repurchase shares.
- The class A equivalent share count falls every year through buybacks. Owners' share of the businesses rises without them investing a dollar.
- Berkshire paid a dividend once, 10 cents a share in 1967, which Warren calls "a terrible mistake." He says he confessed to the board, and notes that retention produced the $500 billion of equity and over $30 billion of annual operating earnings.
- Cash and T-bills grew by roughly $6-7 billion in April. The pieces: about $400 million of stock buys, about $4 billion from net stock sales, and about $2.5 billion of operating earnings.

## Insurance float and Berkshire's underwriting position

- Float was $165 billion. It is money from premiums paid in advance, shown as a liability for unpaid losses, but it is held by Berkshire and has cost essentially nothing in most years.
- Warren compares it to "a bank with no employees, no interest and no ability to withdraw the money in a hurry." He says it finances assets the way stockholders' equity does, but without the cost. Equity is expensive; long-term debt can be cheap for a while but can become expensive or unavailable.
- Float grew from "a pittance" in 1986 to $165 billion under Ajit Jain, who joined that year.
- Berkshire's insurers hold about 26% of the net worth of all US property-casualty companies. Berkshire carries about four times the usual stockholder capital behind each premium dollar. It also receives $25 billion or more a year in earnings from non-insurance businesses, and it pays no dividend and has no big debt maturities in its insurance operations.
- Buyers of large insurance liabilities have no comparable counterparty:
  - Lloyd's used Berkshire to resuscitate its market.
  - On the 2016 AIG deal, Berkshire assumed up to $20 billion of liabilities for about $10 billion upfront. It is not required to put the $10 billion into matching bonds; it goes into the general pool of assets. Ajit says actual payouts are 96% of projections so far, funding cost is "significantly less than four percent," and Berkshire earned a fee of about $1 million in 2015 dollars. He calls himself cautiously optimistic since the game is not over.
  - Warren says deals of this size are ones where "there isn't any other stop."
- Property catastrophe reinsurance: Ajit passed on weak pricing at the January 1 renewal, kept capital in reserve, then wrote a lot at April 1 when prices rose. The portfolio is heavily weighted to Florida. A big Florida hurricane could cost up to about $15 billion across units; with no loss, the book makes several billion.
- Berkshire thinks of insurance exposure as about 5% of capital of a little under $300 billion. When Ajit asked to raise exposure from $13 billion to $15 billion, Warren said yes in under 30 seconds.
- Ajit rates Berkshire Hathaway Specialty (started with four people and now about 1,500 worldwide) as roughly $12 billion of float built with essentially no underwriting loss. Warren says it is the one insurer started in the last ten years that he knows to be an "overwhelming success." He says the many tech-labeled insurance startups lost huge sums and consumed capital, and he would not want to own any of them.
- Geico: Ajit says the combined ratio was about 93 in Q1, flattered by prior-year reserve releases and seasonally good Q1 auto results. He expects just under 100 for the year, aiming for 96 by end of next year, and prioritizes profitability over growth. The telematics gap is a technology problem: more than 600 legacy systems being consolidated to 15-16.
- On automakers selling their own insurance (GM, Tesla), Ajit says insurance at point of sale is convenient but needs driver data, and margins are thin (about 4%). Warren notes GM has done insurance for decades and says it is hard to improve on matching price to risk. He points to State Farm, a mutual that "took twenty points out of the cost" in the 1920s, as the one big idea in auto insurance.

## Capital allocation, buybacks and why Berkshire holds cash

- Warren's capital-allocation order: first do what the businesses need, then grow or buy more businesses, then consider dividends. Dividends are close to irrevocable ("you don't cut dividends"), so that decision is made carefully.
- On repurchases: if there is ample capital and the stock is below intrinsic value, it enhances value for the remaining owners. "It's no more complicated than if you and I and Charlie had a business and you wanted to sell your interest" for less than it is worth, without misleading you about it. Above intrinsic value, he says buying back is the wrong move whatever an investment banker says. Greg Abel understands capital allocation as well as Warren and will make these decisions in the same framework.
- Berkshire holds a lot of liquidity and avoids debt maturities and cashable insurance policies, so it never faces uncomfortable cash needs at inconvenient times. He frames the business model as being able to serve as a plus to the country in a crisis.
- Because Berkshire has no investor calls and makes no earnings promises, it can ignore Wall Street's quarterly expectations. Warren wants to own wonderful businesses "forever" and says "the world was made to order" for anyone thinking five, ten or twenty years out, since most others are short-term focused.

## Concentration and Apple

- Warren rejects the claim that Apple is 35% of Berkshire's portfolio. Berkshire's assets are the whole enterprise: railroad, energy, See's, insurance, plus the securities. He says Berkshire "can't own more than 100%" of anything, and Apple is one more business.
- Apple's own buybacks lift Berkshire's ownership with no spending. Shares outstanding were about 15.7 billion and falling, Berkshire owned about 5.6-6%, and each extra tenth of a percent adds about $100 million to Berkshire's share of earnings.
- He calls Apple a better business than any Berkshire owns outright, including the railroad. His reasoning: consumers will pay about $1,500 for a phone and would give up a second car (about $35,000) before the iPhone. He says he does not understand the phone itself but understands consumer behavior. Berkshire's Apple stake is larger than its energy business.
- He says he made a mistake selling some Apple shares a couple of years back for tax-year reasons.
- Charlie's view: broad diversification "is an insane idea" if you only have a few good ideas. Own your best ideas rather than your worst; "know the edge of your own ability."
- Berkshire lost the Taiwan Semiconductor position (about $5 billion sold) over location. Warren calls TSMC a superbly managed company and "one of the best" but says he would rather deploy capital in the US, and feels better about capital in Japan than in Taiwan.

## Finding opportunities: Japan, value investing and the market environment

- Charlie's description of the Japanese trading houses: five large, understandable companies earning roughly 14% on what Berkshire would pay, paying decent dividends, repurchasing shares, and owning many businesses Berkshire could understand. Berkshire could eliminate currency risk by financing in yen at about half a percent. "If you get 14 on one side and a half a percent on the other," it works. Warren says he bought for months before telling Greg, announced at 5%+ on his birthday, and now owns 7.4% of each, with a cap of 9.9% unless the company agrees. The yen issuance that was planned at 56 billion yen turned out to be about 164 billion yen. Warren says the position is ahead by "four or five billion" plus dividends, and Berkshire is the largest corporate borrower in Japan outside Japanese companies.
- Warren and Charlie both visited Japan; Greg joined for two days of meetings with the five companies.
- On whether value investing is harder now: Warren says to get used to making less, and that opportunities come from "other people doing dumb things," not from technology. He argues there are more big dumb things now because money is much easier to raise (for example, ten or fifteen dumb insurance startups in a decade). Small-capital investors have the advantage. He would "love to be born today" and start with little money.
- The T-bill market had a visible mispricing the week of the meeting (debt-ceiling doubts), which let Berkshire buy $3 billion at about 5.9%.
- Benjamin Graham: Warren says Graham did things for him without expecting anything in return. The Intelligent Investor (the 1949 book) has sold about 7.3 million copies and still ranks around #300-350 on Amazon, which Warren calls unmatched staying power. Charlie notes that over half of Graham's lifetime investment returns came from one stock, Geico, which Graham acknowledged; the Geico deal almost fell apart over a $25,000 difference in net worth.
- Warren's learning approach: he says he learns as he goes by watching consumer behavior across See's, apparel chains, auto dealerships (about 80), and Apple, and that early mistakes (department store business in 1966) taught him lessons. Charlie says he read "thousands and thousands of pages" of Moody's manuals in the past. Warren says he never looks at where managers went to school.

## Management, succession and Berkshire's long-term durability

- Greg Abel will succeed Warren as CEO and handle capital allocation. Insurance stays with Ajit under a deliberately separated structure. Warren declines to name the next layer of successors now because "a lot can change." He calls the idea of an "executive bench" largely "baloney": few people can run the largest US company by net worth, and the key is a top capital allocator plus strong operating managers.
- The board's new-CEO job would be hard to judge at a business as strong as Berkshire, because "if they don't show up at the office it'll keep working for a long time." Warren relays Tom Murphy: "The secret of business is to buy a good business, and it's okay to inherit one too."
- Warren has 99% of his net worth in Berkshire, and an estimated $100 billion or more of philanthropy rides on it.
- Hiring and loyalty stories:
  - Ajit walked into Warren's office on a Saturday in 1986 with zero insurance experience; Warren recognized him quickly. Warren says if he could pick the best ten insurance managers in the world, he could not replace Ajit.
  - Ben Rosner, who left school after fourth grade, ran the retailer Berkshire bought; Warren says he knew retailing in tough neighborhoods better than anyone. Warren says Rosner was the kind of person who could not be replaced when he died.
  - Paul Andrews of TTI chose Berkshire as the owner for his business because a competitor would fire his people and private equity would plan its exit.
  - Charlie, on Louie Vincenti at Wesco: Berkshire bought Wesco for tens of millions and it became worth two or three billion, in part because Berkshire did not meddle.
- Protecting against a takeover after Warren's death: Warren says Greg and the directors will get a long honeymoon, and with no dividends and a trillion-plus market value in 12-15 years, a raider needs enormous borrowing capacity. He wants Berkshire regarded "as a national asset rather than a national liability."
- Berkshire's decisions are rational rather than emotional in business, but Warren concedes loyalty to managers sometimes delays replacing them. Charlie adds that Berkshire changes managers far less frequently than other conglomerates, which helps results.
- For estate planning: Charlie and Warren advise being transparent. Warren will not sign a will until his three children have read it and made suggestions. He says kids learn values from how parents live, and recommends writing your own obituary and reversing it.

## Warren's outlook on risk, society and the US

- Warren will not bet against America. The country began with half of one percent of world population in 1790 and now has about a quarter of world GDP. He would pick the US and today as the best time and place to be born, and he points to personal examples like a recent root canal. He sees partisanship drifting toward "tribalism," which he says leads to mobs.
- Nuclear risk: he compares AI to the atom bomb, which could not be uninvented. Of AI he says "it can do all kinds of things, and when something can do all kinds of things I get a little bit worried." Charlie doubts the hype and prefers "old-fashioned intelligence."
- The US and China: Charlie says the tension was "wrongly created on both sides," and calls for more trade, citing Apple's China partnership as good for both. Warren says the two superpowers must understand the game and not push too far, comparing it to Cold War mutually assured destruction.
- Commercial real estate: the hollowing out of downtowns is "going to be quite significant and quite unpleasant." Buildings will remain, but owners will change. Warren notes the common standard that "a building is worth what you can borrow without signing your name." Rates have risen from the era of 2.5% loans; lenders will end up holding properties they do not want. Berkshire does not use commercial real estate heavily because it suits taxable investors better than corporations.
- Silicon Valley Bank: Warren says failing to cover all deposits would have been catastrophic. He says no official would go on TV and explain they were insuring only $250,000 and running the system.
- Energy transition: Greg says Berkshire Hathaway Energy has about $70 billion of known projects over the next decade, and the 2030 goal of cutting carbon 50% versus 2005. Pacific Corp's transmission plan started in the mid-2000s at about $6 billion and has cost about $7 billion for roughly a third of the build, with Greg describing multi-state transmission as the big bottleneck. Warren says World War II showed that the US can reorient industry quickly under unified authority, and doubts a peacetime 50-state system can do the same.
- BNSF: Warren says railroads will always have derailments (over 1,000 a year industry-wide) given weight, grades and 22,000 miles of track, and that BNSF must carry hazardous materials as a common carrier. Greg acknowledges BNSF breached a tribal-land easement on crude trains and says Berkshire is pursuing a resolution.
