## Book value growth, the 15% goal, and why results are lumpy

- Per-share book value rose 20.3% in 1992. Since present management took over 28 years earlier, it went from $19 to $7,745, or 23.6% compounded annually.
- Warren's stated goal is to grow per-share intrinsic value at 15% a year. He uses book value as a "conservative, but useful, proxy."
- He says the path cannot be smooth. Since 1979 GAAP has required insurers' stock holdings to be carried at market, so ordinary equity swings make annual results "gyrate."
- He lists three things to remember when comparing Berkshire with the S&P 500:
  - Early on, the textile business was a drag. More recently the operating businesses have usually earned well above the S&P.
  - Berkshire's returns are after corporate tax, while the S&P figures are pre-tax. An 18% S&P gain delivers a corporate holder well short of 13%. He calls this a structural disadvantage with "no antidote."
  - He and Charlie Munger are "virtually certain" the S&P's next-decade return will be far below the last decade's. They are "dead certain" that Berkshire's growing capital base will shrink its historical edge. Stocks "cannot forever overperform their underlying businesses."
- Short-term market forecasts are "poison" to be "kept locked up in a safe place, away from children and also from grown-ups who behave in the market like children."
- Volatility is accepted and welcomed because it comes from concentrated holdings and from super-catastrophe insurance. "A tolerance for short-term swings improves our long-term prospects." In baseball terms, the yardstick is "slugging percentage, not batting average."

## Look-through earnings and capital allocation

- Look-through earnings are: reported operating earnings, plus retained operating earnings of major investees not shown under GAAP, minus an allowance for the tax Berkshire would pay if those earnings were distributed. Warren thinks this portrays Berkshire's earnings better than the GAAP figure.
- 1992 look-through earnings were $604 million, against $516 million in 1991. The undistributed investee earnings were $298 million, less $42 million hypothetical tax, plus $348 million of reported operating earnings.
- For 15% intrinsic-value growth they must exceed $1.8 billion by 2000. He does not promise it. "When we allocate capital today, we are thinking about what will maximize look-through earnings in 2000."
- Long-term focus is no excuse for poor short-term results. "If plantings made confidently are repeatedly followed by disappointing harvests, something is wrong with the farmer." For some industries "there simply is no good long-term strategy."
- Be suspicious of managers who pump up short-term earnings with accounting maneuvers or asset sales. Be equally suspicious of those who miss for years and blame their long-term focus.
- Shares are issued only when Berkshire receives as much value as it gives. In a bank-merger anecdote, the acquiree's owner demanded a swap valuing his business at over twice the acquirer's and then asked that they never again make a deal "this dumb."

## Buying businesses: good businesses at fair prices

- Acquiring a business with excellent economics and managers he likes, trusts and admires is the most exhilarating activity. The search attitude should be like looking for a spouse: "active, interested and open-minded," but not "in a hurry."
- He criticizes managers who pay dearly to "kiss corporate toads" and then announce a big "restructuring" charge. "The CEO receives the education but the stockholders pay the tuition."
- His own early toads were cheap and still croaked. A golf pro's line, "Practice doesn't make perfect; practice makes permanent," led him to change strategy: buy "good businesses at fair prices rather than fair businesses at good prices."
- Central States Indemnity was bought in December 1992 as a prototype:
  - Berkshire bought 82%. The company insures monthly payments for credit-card holders who become disabled or unemployed.
  - Annual premiums are about $90 million and profits about $10 million.
  - It is run by Bill Kizer, a friend of 35+ years. The Kizer family keeps 18% and continues to run it.
- It resembles Berkshire's first insurance deal, National Indemnity, bought from Jack Ringwalt 26 years earlier. Both sellers chose Berkshire as the home for their company.
- Subsidiaries make small add-on acquisitions, which Warren calls "low-risk and high-return" since they extend managers already known to be outstanding. There were five in 1992. H. H. Brown bought Lowell Shoe, which has $90 million in sales and makes Nursemates.

## Value, growth, and the equity-investing creed

- The 1977 creed is unchanged except for one word. Buy businesses that:
  - (a) he can understand;
  - (b) have favorable long-term prospects;
  - (c) are operated by honest and competent people;
  - (d) are available at "an attractive price," previously "a very attractive price."
- He calls the value/growth split "fuzzy thinking." "The two approaches are joined at the hip." Growth is always a component of value, and its effect can be negative as well as positive. "Value investing" is redundant, since investing means seeking value at least equal to the price paid. Paying more in the hope of selling higher is speculation.
- Low price-to-book, low P/E and high dividend yield do not determine whether something is a value purchase. The opposite traits are not inconsistent with one.
- Growth helps only when incremental capital earns enticing returns, that is, when each dollar used to finance growth creates more than a dollar of long-term market value. The airline industry is his example: "The more the industry has grown, the worse the disaster for owners."
- Value is the discounted cash flows, in and out, over the remaining life of the asset, per John Burr Williams. The formula is the same for stocks and bonds, but with equities the analyst must estimate the "coupons," and management quality can swing them dramatically.
- Buy whichever investment is cheapest on that calculation, whether or not it grows, has smooth earnings, or looks cheap on P/E or book. When bonds calculate as more attractive, buy bonds.
- Best business: one that can employ large amounts of incremental capital at very high returns. Worst: one that consistently needs ever more capital at very low returns. Most high-return businesses need little capital, and their owners benefit if they pay out earnings or buy back stock.
- To avoid errors in estimating cash flows, he does two things:
  - Stick to businesses he understands, meaning simple and stable. "What counts for most people in investing is not how much they know, but rather how realistically they define what they don't know."
  - Insist on a margin of safety. If calculated value is only slightly above price, he is not interested. He calls this Ben Graham's principle "the cornerstone of investment success."
- "An investor needs to do very few things right as long as he or she avoids big mistakes."

## Portfolio activity, General Dynamics, and fixed income

- Holdings over $100 million at year-end 1992, with cost and market in millions:
  - Coca-Cola: $1,024 cost, $3,911 market
  - GEICO: $46 cost, $2,226 market
  - Capital Cities/ABC: $518 cost, $1,524 market
  - Gillette: $600 cost, $1,365 market
  - Freddie Mac: $414 cost, $784 market
  - Wells Fargo: $381 cost, $486 market
  - General Dynamics: $312 cost, $451 market
  - Washington Post: $10 cost, $397 market
  - Guinness: $333 cost, $300 market
- In 1992 Berkshire added to Guinness and Wells Fargo, more than doubled Freddie Mac, and started General Dynamics. "We like to buy. Selling, however, is a different story."
- General Dynamics began as an arbitrage. After announcing a Dutch tender for about 30% of its shares, Warren bought expecting a small profit by tendering. Then he studied CEO Bill Anders: a clear, rational strategy, urgency, and remarkable results. Warren dropped the arbitrage idea and became a long-term holder, buying 14% of the post-tender shares in a month. A tender swells trading volume, which helped him build the position.
- He has made arbitrage commitments like this perhaps half a dozen times in recent years, with decent returns.
- On fixed income:
  - Berkshire realized $80 million of gains from fixed income in 1992, and losses have been negligible: "thrills but so far no spills."
  - Negotiated preferred purchases have been inferior to secondary-market purchases, as he expected. The secondary market, "periodically ruled by mass folly," constantly sets a clearing price, and shares worth x have sold for 1/2x or less. Corporations and controlling holders pick when to issue, so they rarely offer bargains. "It's rare you'll find x for 1/2x here."
- He admits USAir was a mistake: "it was I who happily jumped into the pool; no one pushed me." He expected a tough industry but not "prolonged kamikaze behavior." A competitively beset business needs far more managerial skill, and the reward is "simply survival, not prosperity."
- He says he cannot forecast the economics of investment banking, airlines or paper.

## Insurance: float, cost of float, and super-cat

- Float is loss reserves, loss adjustment expense reserves and unearned premium reserves, less agents' balances, prepaid acquisition costs and deferred charges on assumed reinsurance. The cost of float is the underwriting loss. What counts is "the cost of funds developed from insurance."
- 1992 numbers:
  - Underwriting loss: $108.96 million.
  - Average float: $2,290.4 million.
  - Cost of float: 4.76%, against a 7.39% long-term Treasury yield.
  - In 21 of 26 insurance years Berkshire's float cost less than the government's rate. "If, on average, we didn't beat the Government's rate, there would be no economic reason for us to be in the business."
- Industry combined ratio was an estimated 114.8 in 1992, with about four points from Hurricane Andrew. A ratio of 106-110 typically means break-even once float income is counted. Below 100 is an underwriting profit.
- Andrew exposed weak reinsurance protection: "It's only when the tide goes out that you learn who's been swimming naked."
- Super-cat policies, sold to other insurers, usually trigger only when both of these happen:
  - the client's loss exceeds its retention;
  - industry-wide insured losses pass a minimum, usually $3 billion or more.
  - Many cover only a second or later event, or one type such as earthquake.
- Berkshire lost about $125 million to Andrew, roughly equal to its 1992 super-cat premiums, so the line lost only about $2 million. One policy would pay $100 million on a specified catastrophe. Warren targets a 10% margin over time but expects "enormously profitable or enormously unprofitable" years.
- On pricing and underwriting:
  - Warren warns that past experience cannot be simply extrapolated, citing possible global warming, growth of insured values in coastal areas, and rare events like the 1812 New Madrid quake.
  - Berkshire rejects more than 98% of the business it is offered, and Ajit Jain is "simply the best in this business."
  - "A fool and his money are soon invited everywhere."
- Super-cat premiums are recognized only when a loss occurs or the policy expires, because nine of the ten largest US insured losses came in the second half of a year.
- Berkshire was second in US property-casualty net worth, behind State Farm. Insurance is expected to be the main earnings source "for decades to come."

## Accounting honesty: stock options, retiree health, and overhead

- Stock options are compensation, so they are an expense:
  - "If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?"
  - Costs arise when something of value is delivered, not only when cash moves.
  - Imprecision is no excuse, since 747 lifespans, loan losses and insurance reserves are also estimates.
  - He offers to buy any restricted option from an executive who claims it is worthless, and to sell insurance in exchange for options on a company's stock.
  - He faults accountants and the SEC for letting executives "muscle" them.
- On post-retirement health benefits:
  - He calls the new rule requiring recognition of present-value liabilities a good change. Berkshire avoids acquiring companies with these liabilities.
  - In 1982 he nearly bought a company burdened by them. Another buyer appeared, the company went bankrupt, and workers' health promises became "largely worthless."
- Lincoln's riddle: "How many legs does a dog have if you call his tail a leg? Four, because calling a tail a leg does not make it a leg."
- Corporate overhead:
  - Berkshire's was $4.2 million in 1992, under 1% of operating earnings, with no legal, HR, PR, investor-relations or strategic-planning departments.
  - If a business spends 10% on headquarters while a peer spends 1%, shareholders lose about 9% of value. He sees no correlation between high corporate costs and good performance.
  - He admires the Wal-Mart, Nucor, Dover, GEICO, Golden West Financial and Price Co. models.

## Managers, owner policies, and temperament

- He prizes managers working past retirement age:
  - Rose Blumkin ("Mrs. B"), 99, works seven days a week. She sold her building to Nebraska Furniture Mart and signed a non-compete at 99.
  - Ralph Schey's Scott Fetzer earned a record $110 million pre-tax on only $116 million of equity, without leverage. It shrank inventory and fixed assets and sent Berkshire over 100% of its earnings in seven years.
- Operating managers' own charitable gifts go through the shareholder-designated program or their own money. Warren says if employees "wish to give to their alma maters... they should use their own money, not yours."
- Berkshire's shareholder-designated contributions were $7.6 million across 2,810 charities, with 97% of eligible shares taking part.
- No stock split. Warren believes the policy helps assemble shareholders who "think and behave like rational long-term owners." Trading frictions are minimal, and a split would modestly degrade the shareholder base.
- Aversion to debt, especially short-term. The 5½% zero-coupon convertible debentures were called because the rate stopped being attractive and the conversion option disadvantages owners. Of the $476 million, $405 million was redeemed in cash and 6,106 shares were issued.
- He accepts board seats, including at USAir, when large owners should "do their bit," though five outside boards is more than he thinks advisable for an active CEO.
