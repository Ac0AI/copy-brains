## How Warren values businesses, measures risk, and why he holds Coke-type franchises

- **Book value vs. intrinsic value.** Book value is "the capital, including retained earnings, that has been put into a business." Intrinsic value is "a present-value estimate of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life."
- At most companies the two are unrelated. At Berkshire, book value (14.3% growth in 1993, $19 to $8,854 over 29 years, 23.3% compounded) is a useful tracking device, though well below intrinsic value.
- Berkshire's long-standing goal is 15% annual growth in per-share intrinsic value. He says the growing capital base makes it "an ever-more difficult target to hit."
- Look-through earnings must reach about $1.85 billion by 2000 to hit the goal. He "paints first and shoots later" rather than centering the target on wherever the arrow landed.

## Market price vs. business value: Mr. Market and the voting machine

- Berkshire's market price rose 39% in 1993 while book and intrinsic value rose about 14%. Over time price and value "arrive at about the same destination," but in the short run they often diverge sharply.
- From 1991 to 1993, Coke and Gillette grew operating earnings per share 38% and 37%, but their stocks rose only 11% and 6%. What counts over time is the earnings performance of the businesses.
- Coke IPO'd in 1919 at $40 and fell more than 50% to $19.50 by end-1920. A single share with dividends reinvested was worth over $2.1 million at end-1993.
- He quotes Ben Graham: "In the short-run, the market is a voting machine... but in the long-run, the market is a weighing machine."
- A wildly fluctuating market means "irrationally low prices will periodically be attached to solid businesses." The true investor welcomes volatility and is "totally free to either ignore the market or exploit its folly."
- After buying, he would not be disturbed if markets closed for a year or two. He needs no daily quote on a 7% Coke stake, any more than on 100% of See's or H. H. Brown.

## Redefining risk: against beta, for business economics

- Risk, in dictionary terms, is "the possibility of loss or injury." He rejects the academic definition of risk as relative volatility ("beta"): "It is better to be approximately right than precisely wrong."
- Beta logic is absurd: Washington Post, bought in 1973 after a sharp drop, became "riskier" at the lower price, though someone offered the whole company at a vastly reduced price would see it as safer.
- The real risk is whether aggregate after-tax receipts, including sale proceeds, give at least as much purchasing power as the initial stake plus a modest interest rate.
- Five factors determine it:
  1. The certainty with which the business's long-term economics can be evaluated.
  2. The certainty with which management can be evaluated, both on realizing the business's potential and on employing its cash flows wisely.
  3. The certainty that management will channel rewards to shareholders rather than itself.
  4. The purchase price.
  5. The levels of taxation and inflation.
- These can't be pulled from a database, but like Justice Stewart on obscenity, "I know it when I see it."
- Coke sells about 44% of all soft drinks. Gillette has more than 60% of the blade market by value. Both have increased share, and brand power, product attributes and distribution give them "a protective moat around their economic castles."
- Their stocks have betas similar to run-of-the-mill companies with no advantage, which shows beta can't capture business risk.
- He quotes Peter Lynch: commodity-business stocks should carry the warning "Competition may prove hazardous to human wealth."

## Staying in the circle of competence and concentrating

- He can usually tell a "Barbie" (Monopoly, Barbie) from a "pet rock" (hula hoops) in consumer businesses. In many industries he and Charlie cannot, even after years of study.
- Fast-moving technology industries don't lend themselves to reliable long-term evaluation. They didn't foresee what would happen in TVs or computers 30 years ago. His stance: "Why search for a needle buried in a haystack when one is sitting in plain sight?"
- Charlie and he concluded that "it's just too hard to make hundreds of smart decisions." They need to be smart only a few times and will "settle for one good idea a year."
- Concentration may lower risk because it raises the intensity of thought and the comfort level required before buying.
- For a know-something investor who can find five to ten sensibly priced companies with durable advantages, conventional diversification "is apt simply to hurt your results and increase your risk." He can't see why anyone puts money into a 20th favorite rather than adding to top choices. Quoting Mae West: "Too much of a good thing can be wonderful."
- Wide diversification is right in two cases:
  - Risky-but-favorable bets such as arbitrage or venture capital. Act like a casino with a roulette wheel: want lots of action, but refuse any single huge bet.
  - The know-nothing investor who regularly buys an index fund can outperform most professionals. "When 'dumb' money acknowledges its limitations, it ceases to be dumb."

## Holding great businesses and the power of deferred taxes

- Berkshire's 1993 holdings were nearly unchanged from the prior year: Coke (93.4M shares, cost $1.02B, market $4.17B), GEICO (cost $46M, market $1.76B), Gillette, Cap Cities/ABC, Wells Fargo, Freddie Mac, Washington Post, General Dynamics, Guinness.
- Rule: hold "a small piece of an outstanding business with the same tenacity that an owner would exhibit if he owned all of that business." Understandable, durably wonderful businesses "are simply too hard to replace."
- CEOs would never sell a crown-jewel subsidiary, yet move around their personal portfolios on slogans like "You can't go broke taking a profit." He asks whether a CEO would tell his board that about a star subsidiary.
- In 1938 Fortune said investors looking at Coke were "looking too late." Coke sold 207 million cases then and about 10.7 billion in 1993 (50-fold). $40 invested in 1919 became $3,277 by 1938, and a fresh $40 in 1938 grew to $25,000 by end-1993.
- He keeps a buy-and-hold policy even as if tax-exempt, but notes taxes are due only on realization.
- Li'l Abner example: doubling $1 for 27.5 years, taxed at 35% each year, needs 7.5 extra years beyond 20 to reach $1M (about $22,370 after 20 years). One investment held through the same doublings yields about $200M pre-tax, or about $130M after a $70M tax. A single investment compounding internally beats a succession of investments at the same rate.
- Cap Cities: bought 3M shares in 1986 at $172.50, sold a third at $630 in 1993. After 35% capital gains tax, the realized profit was $297M, versus only $152M of hypothetically taxed look-through retained earnings over eight years. He views look-through earnings as conservative.
- Berkshire paid about $390M in 1993 federal income taxes (about $200M on operating earnings, $190M on gains). He has "absolutely no complaint," noting the market economy rewards them far more than others of equal benefit.

## Insurance float and super-cat underwriting

- Float is premiums prepaid plus funds earmarked for incurred-but-unpaid claims. Berkshire computes it as loss reserves, loss adjustment reserves and unearned premium reserves, minus agents' balances, prepaid acquisition costs and deferred charges on assumed reinsurance.
- Falling interest rates cut the value of float. The industry once broke even at a combined ratio of 107-111, but a company writing at the same ratio now has a "far less attractive business."
- Float should be valued at the risk-free long-term interest rate, and underwriting results separated from investment results when judging managers.
- Berkshire's cost of float is its underwriting loss or profit. Average float in 1993 was $2,624.7M, at a negative cost (a $31M underwriting profit), versus a 6.35% long-bond yield.
- He tempers enthusiasm because the profit partly reflects a quiet super-cat year. A year without losses doesn't validate the business:
  - An event that occurs 25 times a century, priced at 5-for-1 odds, gives many winning years and even a straight six or seven, but "you also will eventually go broke."
  - Berkshire thinks it gives about 3.5-for-1, but no one can calculate true super-cat odds, and it will take decades to know.
- Hurricane Andrew cost about $125M in 1992. A similar storm after expansion could cost $600M. Losses in a bad year could be three or four times 1993's earnings from super-cat. The LA quake would have been a different story at 7.5 instead of 6.8.
- Advantages: Ajit Jain, and a net worth ten to twenty times that of main competitors. Super-cat can't be diversified or laid off, so capital determines limits offered. The four largest reinsurers buy meaningful coverage from Berkshire, because "the test of a reinsurer is its ability and willingness to pay losses under trying circumstances."
- About $5B of new reinsurance capital has arrived. If prices deteriorate, he'll accept much-reduced volume rather than chase business.

## Acquisitions, managers, and why owners sell to Berkshire

- Dexter Shoe: founded in 1956 by Harold Alfond with $10,000; produces over 7.5M pairs a year with 77 retail outlets. Berkshire issued 25,203 shares. Combined shoe sales in 1994 are expected to exceed $550M, with pre-tax earnings possibly above $85M, and 7,200 employees.
- Berkshire's path was H. H. Brown (1991), Lowell (end of 1992), then Dexter, after Frank Rooney vouched for it. Frank's H. H. Brown earnings were up 35% over its 1992 high.
- No grand plan: "usually poison for a corporate giant's shareholders if it embarks upon new ventures pursuant to some grand vision." Berkshire focuses on "the economic characteristics of businesses that we wish to own and the personal characteristics of managers," then hopes to find both together.
- Pitch to private owners:
  - A tax-free swap into a diversified group of terrific businesses.
  - Easier gifting and liquidity.
  - Owner-oriented treatment, with no restricted stock or options for Warren, so his gain-loss equation matches other owners'.
  - Promises of no change of control or culture for decades.
  - Managers keep running the business: "we do not tell .400 hitters how to swing."
- He refuses marginal deals just because Berkshire is long on cash: "There's no use running if you're on the wrong road."
- Manager examples:
  - Mrs. B turned 100 and founded Nebraska Furniture Mart with $500 in 1937. Berkshire bought control without audits, real estate checks or warranties, because "her word was good enough."
  - Kay Graham at Washington Post: Berkshire paid about $10M in 1973 and now has a stake worth over $400M paying $7M a year in dividends. He judged her character and shareholder treatment on a dual-class company.
  - Don Keough and Roberto Goizueta took Coke from $4.4B to $58B of market value in under 13 years. His observations of Keough as a neighbor contributed to the record $1B Coke investment in 1988-89.
  - Susan Jacques went from a $4/hour job to president and CEO of Borsheim's in 11 years.

## Corporate governance and shareholder-oriented capital allocation

- Three board situations:
  1. No controlling shareholder: directors should act as if for a single absentee owner. They should be relatively few (ten or fewer), mostly outsiders, chosen for business savvy, interest in the job and owner-orientation (not prominence or diversity). They should evaluate the CEO without him present.
  2. Controlling owner who also manages (Berkshire now): directors can only persuade, and should resign if serious problems persist.
  3. Controlling owner not in management (Berkshire after Warren): the best structure. Directors take complaints to one interested owner, who can fix mistakes quickly.
- After his death the stock goes to Susie, or to a foundation, with no consequential stock sales needed. She and son Howard are on the board, and most other directors are significant owners.
- Philanthropy: "the company's money is the owners' money." Shareholders direct $10 per share ($9.4M in 1993, 97% participation, 3,110 charities) rather than the CEO. He has "yet to find a CEO who believes he should personally fund the charities favored by his shareholders."
- Overhead is under half of charitable contributions ($4.9M overhead, of which $1.4M is the corporate jet).
- Disclosure standard: give shareholders the information he and Charlie would want if positions were reversed, which is why he presents earnings by business excluding purchase-price accounting charges.
