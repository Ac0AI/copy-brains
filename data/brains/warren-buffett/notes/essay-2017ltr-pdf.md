## Berkshire's 2017 scorecard: net worth, the new GAAP rule, and how Warren measures progress

- Berkshire's net worth rose $65.3 billion in 2017, lifting per-share book value of both Class A and Class B by 23%. Over 53 years, per-share book value grew from $19 to $211,750, a 19.1% compounded annual rate.
- Only $36 billion came from Berkshire's operations. The other $29 billion came from the December rewrite of the U.S. Tax Code.
- A new GAAP rule requires net income to include unrealized gains and losses on stocks held. Berkshire owns $170 billion of marketable stocks (excluding Kraft Heinz), which can swing $10 billion or more in a quarter. Warren says this makes Berkshire's GAAP "bottom-line" "useless" for analysis.
- Realized gains are also random, "largely because we sell securities when that seems the intelligent thing to do, not because we are trying to influence earnings." He warns shareholders to ignore them.
- Warren and Charlie Munger focus on increases in "normalized per-share earning power." Berkshire publishes financials late Friday or early Saturday so owners have time to analyze before markets open Monday.
- Berkshire's four building blocks of value: sizable stand-alone acquisitions, bolt-on acquisitions, internal sales growth and margin improvement, and investment earnings from stocks and bonds.

## Buying businesses in a frenzy: criteria, why CEOs overpay, and aversion to leverage

- Criteria for stand-alone acquisitions: durable competitive strengths, able and high-grade management, good returns on net tangible assets, opportunities for internal growth at attractive returns, and a sensible price.
- Price blocked nearly every deal in 2017, as prices for "decent, but far from spectacular, businesses hit an all-time high."
- Why others overpay:
  - The CEO job self-selects for "can-do" types, and urging such a CEO to consider acquisitions is "a bit like telling your ripening teenager to be sure to have a normal sex life."
  - Subordinates cheer the bigger domain and pay that rises with company size.
  - Investment bankers smell fees ("Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut").
  - If history doesn't justify the deal, large "synergies" get forecast: "Spreadsheets never disappoint."
  - Cheap debt makes even a high-priced deal boost per-share earnings.
- Berkshire evaluates acquisitions on an all-equity basis and never factors in synergies, "nor do we often find" them. Exceptions to assigning debt to a business: Clayton's lending portfolio and the regulated utilities' fixed-asset commitments.
- On leverage: its aversion "has dampened our returns over the years. But Charlie and I sleep well. Both of us believe it is insane to risk what you have and need in order to obtain what you don't need." Both held this view 50 years ago running their partnerships.
- Guideline: "The less the prudence with which others conduct their affairs, the greater the prudence with which we must conduct our own."
- Warren expects occasional chances for very large purchases despite the drought.

## 2017 deals: Pilot Flying J and the bolt-ons

- **Pilot Flying J (PFJ):** Berkshire bought a 38.6% partnership interest. PFJ does about $20 billion in annual volume, is the nation's leading travel-center operator, and is run by the Haslam family (Jimmy Haslam manages 27,000 associates at about 750 locations). Berkshire contractually increases its stake to 80% in 2023; the family keeps 20%. Kevin Clayton's praise of the Haslams helped cement the deal.
- **Clayton Homes:**
  - Bought two conventional-home builders (Oakwood Homes, Harris Doyle); Warren expects 2018 site-built volume above $1 billion.
  - 2017 sales: 19,168 units through its own retail and 26,706 wholesaled.
  - Its 49% share of manufactured homes is about three times the nearest competitor, versus 13% in 2003.
- **Shaw Industries:** Acquired U.S. Floors, a luxury vinyl tile distributor. Sales grew 40% in 2017; Shaw's sales reached $5.7 billion with 22,000 employees.
- **HomeServices:**
  - Added Long and Foster, Houlihan Lawrence and Gloria Nilson, bringing agents to 40,950 (up 12,300).
  - It took part in $127 billion of "sides" in 2017 and is on track for only about 3% of U.S. home brokerage.
- **Precision Castparts:** Bought Wilhelm Schulz GmbH. Warren admits he doesn't understand manufacturing as well as brokerage, home building or truck stops, but trusts CEO Mark Donegan: "Betting on people can sometimes be more certain than betting on physical assets."

## Insurance float: how it works and how Berkshire got it

- Berkshire bought National Indemnity and a sister company in early 1967 for $8.6 million. It received $6.7 million of tangible net worth (invested in securities Berkshire would have owned anyway, "trading dollars"), paid a $1.9 million premium over net worth, and gained $19.4 million of "float."
- Float is money that belongs to others but that Berkshire holds and invests. Dividends, interest and gains belong to Berkshire, and so do investment losses.
- Float arises because premiums are paid upfront while losses emerge over the policy and often long after. Some claims (asbestos) surface and settle over many years, and some payments (lifetime workers' comp care) stretch over decades.
- "Long-tail" lines such as medical malpractice and product liability generate far more float than auto collision or homeowners. Berkshire specializes in jumbo reinsurance that assumes long-tail losses already incurred by other insurers.
- Growth of float ($ millions):
  - 1970: $39
  - 1980: $237
  - 1990: $1,632
  - 2000: $27,871
  - 2010: $65,832
  - 2017: $114,500
- Premiums reached $60,597 million in 2017, boosted by reinsuring up to $20 billion of AIG's long-tail losses for a $10.2 billion premium, "a world's record and one we won't come close to repeating."
- Berkshire is the country's second-largest p/c company by premiums and the leader in float. Float will probably grow slowly, and any decline would be at most about 3% in a year.
- Unlike bank deposits or life policies with surrender options, p/c float can't be withdrawn. That means no runs, which Warren factors into investment decisions.
- When underwriting profits occur, "our cost of float was less than zero."

## Catastrophe risk, financial strength, and liquidity

- Hurricanes Harvey, Irma and Maria (Texas, Florida, Puerto Rico): Warren guessed industry insured losses at about $100 billion and Berkshire's loss at $3 billion ($2 billion after tax), about 3% of the industry. He expects roughly that share of future U.S. mega-cats.
- The $2 billion net cost cut GAAP net worth by under 1%. Other reinsurers lost 7% to more than 15% of net worth. A slightly more easterly Irma path might have added another $100 billion of insured losses.
- Mega-cat odds: he puts the annual probability of a U.S. event causing $400 billion or more of insured losses at about 2%, rising over time with more and costlier structures in vulnerable areas. Berkshire's share might be about $12 billion, far below its non-insurance annual earnings, while "much – indeed, perhaps most – of the p/c world would be out of business."
- Reserves are "similar to a self-graded exam" (V.J. Dowling), and early loss estimates for mega-cats usually run low.
- After 14 straight years of underwriting profit ($28.3 billion pre-tax), 2017 had a $3.2 billion pre-tax underwriting loss.
- In insurance "there are no trade secrets, patents, or locational advantages. What counts are brains and capital."
- Liquidity: year-end cash and Treasury Bills were $116.0 billion (average maturity 88 days), up from $86.4 billion. In 2008-2009 Warren liked having "loads of Treasury Bills" so Berkshire never had to rely on bank lines or commercial paper. "Charlie and I never will operate Berkshire in a manner that depends on the kindness of strangers." This excess cash earns "only a pittance" and is above the level they want.

## Non-insurance operations and capital spending

- Excluding investment income, non-insurance businesses earned $20 billion pre-tax in 2017, up $950 million. BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy (90.2% owned) produced about 44%.
- Next five by earnings (Clayton, International Metalworking, Lubrizol, Marmon, Precision Castparts): $5.5 billion, versus $5.4 billion.
- Next five (Forest River, Johns Manville, MiTek, Shaw, TTI): $2.1 billion, versus $1.7 billion. All others: $3.7 billion, versus $3.5 billion.
- Depreciation was $7.6 billion against capital expenditures of $11.5 billion. Almost 90% of investment is in the U.S.: "America's economic soil remains fertile."
- Amortization of $1.3 billion is largely not a true economic cost, while BNSF (like all railroads) records depreciation well short of what upkeep requires.
- To substantially raise non-insurance earnings Berkshire needs "one or more huge acquisitions" and has the resources.

## Owning stocks as businesses: retained earnings and the voting/weighing machine

- Warren and Charlie view marketable stocks "as interests in businesses, not as ticker symbols to be bought or sold based on their 'chart' patterns, the 'target' prices of analysts or the opinions of media pundits." If the businesses succeed, the investments will, though he admits "I will make expensive mistakes." "In America, equity investors have the wind at their back."
- Dividends of $3.7 billion in 2017 understate true earnings. Under Principle 6, undistributed investee earnings should deliver at least equivalent capital gains over time, though the link is invisible short term.
- Quoting Ben Graham: "In the short run, the market is a voting machine; in the long run, however, it becomes a weighing machine."
- Biggest holdings by market value at 12/31/17 (cost basis in parentheses): Wells Fargo $29.3 billion ($11.8 billion), Apple $28.2 billion ($21.0 billion), Bank of America $20.7 billion, Coca-Cola $18.4 billion ($1.3 billion), American Express $15.1 billion ($1.3 billion). Total common stocks carried at market: $170.5 billion on a $74.7 billion cost.
- Kraft Heinz (325,442,152 shares) is carried at a GAAP $17.6 billion, with a market value of $25.3 billion and a cost basis of $9.8 billion.
- Todd Combs and Ted Weschler each manage over $12 billion independently of Warren (about $25 billion together, including over $8 billion of pension assets).

## Temperament, debt, and surviving the drawdowns

- Berkshire shares fell hard four times in 53 years:
  - March 1973 to January 1975: from $93 to $38 (-59.1%)
  - 10/2/87 to 10/27/87: from $4,250 to $2,675 (-37.1%)
  - 6/19/98 to 3/10/2000: from $80,900 to $41,300 (-48.9%)
  - 9/19/08 to 3/5/09: from $147,000 to $72,400 (-50.7%)
- This is "the strongest argument I can muster against ever using borrowed money to own stocks." Even modest borrowing risks a rattled mind, and "an unsettled mind will not make good decisions."
- No one can say when declines come: "The light can at any time go from green to red without pausing at yellow." Such drops offer extraordinary opportunities to those without debt. He cites Kipling's "If": "If you can keep your head when all about you are losing theirs…"

## The ten-year bet against hedge funds: index funds, fees, and the real meaning of risk

- Warren bet Protégé Partners on December 19, 2007 that an S&P 500 index fund would beat a group of five funds-of-funds (holding 200+ hedge funds) over ten years. Each side funded $318,250 of zero-coupon Treasuries, worth $500,000 at maturity. The prize went to Girls Inc. of Omaha.
- Results: the S&P 500 index fund returned 125.8% (8.5% a year). The funds-of-funds returned 21.7%, 42.3%, 87.7%, 2.8% and 27.0% (average annual 2.0%, 3.6%, 6.5%, 0.3%, 2.4%). The funds-of-funds trailed in each of the nine years after 2008.
- The expert side had every advantage: elite managers, freedom to switch managers, heavy incentives, and market behavior that was ordinary (about the 8.5% experts would have predicted). Fees averaging about 2½% of assets were paid every year. "Performance comes, performance goes. Fees never falter."
- In 100% of 43 ten-year periods since taking control of Berkshire, S&P gain years outnumbered loss years.
- The switch: by November 2012 the bonds sold at 95.7% of face with a yield of only .88%. Both sides sold them and bought 11,200 Berkshire "B" shares. Warren expected Berkshire to grow at least 8% a year even in a so-so economy, and agreed to cover any shortfall below $1 million. Girls Inc. received $2,222,279.
- Selling at over 100 times "earnings" (95.7/.88) was "a dumb – a really dumb – investment" relative to equities, which earn far more than 10% on equity, with S&P dividends of 2½% (about triple the bond yield) plus retained earnings and buybacks.
- Lessons:
  - Markets are generally rational but "occasionally do crazy things." Exploiting them needs no genius, only ignoring mob fears and enthusiasms, focusing on a few fundamentals, and "a willingness to look unimaginative for a sustained period – or even to look foolish."
  - Risk is the possibility that the goal of greater future consumption isn't attained. By that standard "risk-free" long bonds in 2012 were riskier than long-term stocks, since even 1% inflation erodes their purchasing power.
  - Stocks are far riskier than short-term bonds over a day, week or year, but a diversified U.S. equity portfolio gets less risky than bonds as the horizon lengthens, if bought at a sensible multiple of earnings relative to interest rates. Measuring risk by a portfolio's bond-to-stock ratio is "a terrible mistake" for long-horizon investors, and high-grade bonds "often" raise risk.
  - "Stick with big, 'easy' decisions and eschew activity." The 200+ hedge fund managers made tens of thousands of decisions, while Warren and Protégé made one.

## Shareholder relations, managers, and succession

- Warren treats all shareholders equally, with no one-on-one talks with large institutions or analysts. The image in his mind is "the shareholder of limited means who trusts us with a substantial portion of his or her savings."
- Financial data is released late Friday or early Saturday, and the annual meeting is on Saturday. Meeting questions come from three journalists, three analysts and the audience, with no multi-part questions and no advance notice to Warren and Charlie.
- The 2017 meeting webcast drew about 3.1 million real-time viewers, up 72%.
- Operating managers run their businesses "as if they were the only asset owned by their families." Most don't need the money, and the joy of "business 'home runs'" matters as much as pay. Any shares managers and directors hold came from open-market purchases or sales of their businesses to Berkshire, so they "stand in your shoes" with downside as well as upside.
- Headquarters is small but handles a 32,700-page federal tax return and 3,935 state returns.
- Succession: in early 2018 the board elected Ajit Jain (insurance operations) and Greg Abel (all other businesses) as directors and Vice Chairmen. Warren and Charlie focus on investments and capital allocation. Both men have been with Berkshire for decades, and "the character of each man matches his talents."
