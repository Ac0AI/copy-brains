## Compounding, owner-minded managers, and why Warren's job is attracting them

- Warren and Charlie Munger say they have only two jobs: attract and keep outstanding managers, and allocate capital.
- Most managers came with the companies Berkshire bought, already stars in their fields. Warren's main contribution is "to not get in their way." He compares it to managing a golf team where Jack Nicklaus and Arnold Palmer would get no swing directives.
- The highest compliment he pays a manager is that they "think like owners" and love the work. Several are independently wealthy and keep working for the thrill of outstanding performance.
- He endorses David Ogilvy: "If each of us hires people who are smaller than we are, we shall become a company of dwarfs. But, if each of us hires people who are bigger than we are, we shall become a company of giants."
- Span of control: with able managers of high character running businesses they are passionate about, a dozen or more can report to you "and still have time for an afternoon nap." One deceitful, inept or uninterested manager can overwhelm you. Charlie and Warren could handle double the number of managers, if they had the same rare qualities.
- Policy: work only with people they like and admire. Working with people who make your stomach churn is "like marrying for money - probably a bad idea under any circumstances, but absolute madness if you are already rich."
- 1986 results: net worth up $492.5 million, or 26.1%. Per-share book value rose from $19.46 to $2,073.06 over 22 years, 23.3% compounded. Net worth rose 10,600% while shares outstanding rose less than 1%.

## Capital allocation: why it matters more at Berkshire, and why 1986 was hard

- Capital allocation matters more at Berkshire than at most companies for three reasons: it earns more than average, it retains all earnings, and most operations need little incremental capital to stay competitive and grow.
- A business earning 23% and retaining everything is far more affected by today's allocation decisions than one earning 10% and paying out half. At a company adding only about 5% to net worth a year, allocation errors change the economics much more slowly.
- Berkshire's retained earnings, plus those of major investees GEICO and Capital Cities/ABC, must be deployed productively or "the economics of Berkshire will deteriorate very quickly."
- In 1986 Warren admits his performance lagged his managers'. He bought one business (Fechheimer, about 2% of net worth) and found no new ideas in marketable equities. The main moves were paying off debt and stockpiling funds.
- Size drag: Warren still hopes for 15% return on equity, but that requires net worth to grow $7.2 billion in ten years. That is only possible with "a few very big (and good) ideas."
- He expects roughly one operating-business acquisition every couple of years, and it must be large to matter.

## What Berkshire wants to buy: the acquisition "ad" and the Fechheimer case

- The 1986 purchase of Fechheimer Bros. came from the annual "ad" describing what Berkshire wants. Chairman Bob Heldman, a long-time shareholder, wrote on January 15, 1986.
- Fechheimer is a uniform manufacturer and distributor founded in 1842. It was sold in a 1981 leveraged buyout, with management keeping equity. Debt was paid down and equity value rose, and the venture capitalists wanted to sell. Berkshire bought about 84% on a valuation of $55 million for the whole business.
- The purchase resembled Nebraska Furniture Mart: sellers wanted money for other uses, the family wanted to keep running the business, several generations were active, and they wanted a buyer who "would not re-sell, regardless of price."
- Warren and Charlie had not visited Cincinnati, and See's manager Chuck Huggins, after 15 years, had never visited Omaha. Warren says they judge "the economic characteristics of the business - its competitive strengths and weaknesses - and the quality of the people," not plant inspections.
- The acquisition criteria, as restated:
  1. Large purchases (at least $10 million of after-tax earnings, raised from $5 million).
  2. Demonstrated consistent earning power. Future projections and turnarounds are of no interest.
  3. Good returns on equity with little or no debt.
  4. Management in place ("we can't supply it").
  5. Simple businesses ("if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it").
  6. An offering price stated up front.
- Terms: no unfriendly takeovers, complete confidentiality, an answer customarily within five minutes. Cash is preferred. Stock is issued only when Berkshire receives as much intrinsic business value as it gives.
- Not interested in: new ventures, turnarounds, auction-like sales, and "I'm-sure-something-will-work-out-if-you-people-get-to-know-each-other" deals.
- Also interested in large negotiated, non-controlling stakes (as with Cap Cities) when comfortable with the economics and the people's ability and integrity. The preference is for commitments of many times $50 million.

## Moats and franchise economics: Buffalo News, See's, NFM, GEICO

- GEICO's low costs: underwriting plus loss-adjustment expense was 23.5% of premiums in 1986 (24.1% in 1985). Many major insurers run about 15 points higher. Warren calls this gap "a kind of moat that protects a valuable and much-sought-after business castle," and says chairman Bill Snyder keeps widening the moat by cutting costs further.
- GEICO's combined ratio was 96.9 with 16% premium growth, and it repurchased shares, ending the year with 5.5% fewer outstanding.
- Lou Simpson's GEICO equity portfolio beat the S&P 500 every year from 1980 to 1986. 1986: 38.7% vs 18.6%. Warren credits buying "undervalued common stocks that, individually, were unlikely to present him with a permanent loss and that, collectively, were close to risk-free."
- The Buffalo News: a 50% news hole versus about 40% at comparable papers (30 ad pages become 30 pages of news instead of 20). It has the highest weekday penetration among the top 50 US papers and 83% Sunday penetration, against about 63% for the rival Courier-Express a decade earlier. Ad rate increases were well below industry levels. Warren says he would keep the news-hole ratio even if margins shrink.
- See's: over 12,000 tons sold a year. Same-store pounds were flat after six years of decline. It held margins with minimal price increases, and Warren expects only modest increases that match cost increases. Its "personality" comes from taste, moderate price, total control of distribution and service.
- Nebraska Furniture Mart: 1986 sales rose 10.2% to $132 million (from $44 million ten years earlier) from a single store whose reach widens because of "rock-bottom everyday prices" and the broadest selection. Mrs. B, 93, works seven days a week.
- World Book and Kirby: unit volume rose for four straight years. World Book is priced under 5 cents per page, and over half its salespeople are teachers or former teachers.

## Taxes and who captures the benefit: franchise strength decides

- The 1986 tax law cut the corporate rate from 46% to 34% by 1988. Warren asks whether companies keep the benefit or pass it to customers. His answer: it depends on franchise strength and whether profits are regulated.
  - Regulated utilities: tax changes flow into prices.
  - Price-competitive industries with weak franchises: the market "regulates" profits, so tax changes eventually affect prices more than profits.
  - Unregulated businesses with strong franchises: the company and shareholders keep the benefit. His analogy is the best local brain surgeon or lawyer, whose fees won't fall because the tax rate does.
- Shareholders' take on a dollar of per-share business value fell from 80 cents to 72 cents as the top personal capital gains rate rose from 20% to 28%.
- Corporate capital gains rose from 28% to 34%. This hurts because most of Berkshire's expected value gain comes from capital gains. Cap Cities, GEICO and Washington Post were over $1.7 billion (close to 75% of net worth) and yielded only about $9 million a year in income. Unrealized gains were $1.2 billion, and about $73 million would move from GAAP net worth to deferred taxes once the accounting rule changed.
- Insurer-specific hits: dividends taxed on 20% (up from 15%), tax-exempt interest on bonds bought after August 7, 1986 only 85% exempt, and reserves discounted for tax. Warren guesses these cut insurance earning power by at least 10%. A one-time "fresh start" benefit was expected at $30-$40 million.
- Repeal of the General Utilities Doctrine creates a double tax on corporate liquidations. Warren says this reduces what sellers can realize and that investors and managers have not yet fully grasped it.
- He doubts the scheduled 1988 rates are sustainable and expects higher tax rates or higher inflation within about five years, possibly both.

## Insurance: commodity pricing, float, and discipline

- Combined ratio: losses plus expenses over premiums. Below 100 is an underwriting profit. With float income, a combined ratio of about 107-112 typically produces break-even.
- Warren says industry revenues must grow close to 10% a year just to hold profitability, because social and judicial inflation (bigger verdicts, expanded coverage) outruns general inflation of 2-4%.
- Industry premium growth was 22.6% in 1986, and the combined ratio improved from 116.5 to 108.5. Quarterly premium growth slowed from 27.1% to 18.7%. He predicted that profits would be fleeting and that improvement would end by late 1988 or early 1989.
- The commodity cycle: profits come only under shortage. Insurers then raise capital, new entrants rush in, "capacity soars, competitive juices flow, and prices fade." Appeals for "statesmanlike" pricing are like asking corn growers to market with more statesmanship: "What's needed is not more statesmen, but less corn."
- Berkshire's approach: always willing to write at adequate prices, and its volume swings widely because competitors come and go. National Indemnity monthly volume went from $5 million (late 1984) to about $35 million (early 1986) to about $20 million and falling. No layoffs when volume falls, so employees don't chase volume out of fear.
- Combined ratio fell from 111 to 103. Warren admits three straight years of reserve underestimation, and calls "reserve strengthening" a euphemism for "correction of previous untruths."
- Warren says Berkshire's edges are attitude, capital, and developing personnel, plus a long-term edge in investing float. Insurance is "a treacherous business and a wary attitude is essential."

## Investing temperament, "owner earnings," and the cash flow fallacy

- When no stocks meet his tests, he doesn't buy. In 1986 he found none, and insurance money went into about $700 million of mediocre tax-exempt bonds (8 to 12 years), the least objectionable option. He stresses that this is not a market prediction: "we have no idea - and never have had" where the market goes.
- Fear and greed: "we simply attempt to be fearful when others are greedy and to be greedy only when others are fearful." Euphoria was prevailing. Because of transaction and management costs, stockholders as a whole must underperform the businesses they own. If American business earns about 12% on equity, investors must earn significantly less: "Bull markets can obscure mathematical laws, but they cannot repeal them."
- Long bonds are avoided over fear of higher inflation. Arbitrage is limited to a few large, publicly announced deals, never "betting on the come," as an alternative to Treasury bills with higher risk.
- Holdings at yearend 1986 included Capital Cities/ABC (cost $515.8 million, market $801.7 million), GEICO (cost $45.7 million, market $674.7 million) and Washington Post (cost $9.7 million, market $269.5 million). Warren expects to hold these three permanently, even if overpriced, just as he wouldn't sell See's or the Buffalo News at a price far above value. He favors a "'til-death-do-us-part policy" over the "portfolio restructuring" mentality.
- Owner earnings = reported earnings + depreciation, depletion, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus the average annual capital expenditures needed to fully maintain long-term competitive position and unit volume (plus any extra working capital needed). He calls it the relevant figure for valuation. The capex term is a guess: "I would rather be vaguely right than precisely wrong" (Keynes).
- Scott Fetzer example: Berkshire paid $315 million for net assets carried at $172.4 million, a $142.6 million premium. Purchase-price adjustments cut 1986 GAAP net income from $40.2 million (old basis) to $28.6 million (new basis) with identical economics and cash. Warren says Scott Fetzer's maintenance capex is close to the old depreciation of $8.3 million, so owner earnings are far better shown by the higher "old" figure. That is why Berkshire reports business-unit earnings before purchase-price adjustments.
- The "cash flow" fallacy: Wall Street cash flow adds earnings plus depreciation but ignores capital spending. Warren says it is meaningless for manufacturing, retailing, extractive companies and utilities, whose capex is always significant. Investment bankers use it to "justify the unjustifiable," such as junk bond debt service or foolish stock prices. For many businesses (the oil industry is the example) GAAP earnings overstate owner earnings.
- On accounting: it is the language of business and the starting point, but "accounting is but an aid to business thinking, never a substitute for it." The accountant's job is to record; the evaluation falls to investors and managers.
- Intrinsic value: business value is a "soft number," and two well-informed observers could differ by more than 10%. Book value has been a conservative proxy for Berkshire's business value.
