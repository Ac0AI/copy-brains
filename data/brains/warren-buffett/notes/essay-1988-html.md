## Valuing the Business: Intrinsic Value, Book Value, and the Growing-Capital Problem

- Warren says what counts is intrinsic business value, "the figure, necessarily an estimate, indicating what all of our constituent businesses are worth." Berkshire's intrinsic value significantly exceeds book value.
- 1988 net worth gain was $569 million, or 20.0%. Per-share book value went from $19.46 to $2,974.52 over 24 years, 23.0% compounded.
- He warns against extrapolating the past: doing so is like judging "a 42-year-old center fielder on the basis of his lifetime batting average."
- Headwinds he lists: a less attractive stock market, higher corporate tax rates on investment income, a richly priced market for acquiring businesses, and weaker industry conditions at Capital Cities/ABC, GEICO and Washington Post.
- The main problem is a growing capital base. Four years earlier a 15% annual return over the next decade needed $3.9 billion of profits; now it needs $10.3 billion.
- The offset is the "Sainted Seven" (Buffalo News, Fechheimer, Kirby, Nebraska Furniture Mart, Scott Fetzer Manufacturing Group, See's, World Book). They earned about 67% on average equity capital with no financial leverage.
- Their results come from an exceptional franchise plus exceptional management. His and Charlie's contribution "is to leave these managers alone." The open question is whether they can effectively deploy the cash these businesses generate.

## Accounting, GAAP, and What Owners Need to Know

- Warren treats GAAP "as a beginning rather than an end" of a CEO's duty to inform owners. A subsidiary manager who gave his boss only barebones numbers would be in hot water, so a CEO shouldn't do it to shareholders.
- Reporting should help financially literate readers answer three questions: (1) approximately how much is the company worth, (2) how likely is it to meet its obligations, (3) how good a job are managers doing given the hand they were dealt.
- Many managements see GAAP "not as a standard to be met, but as an obstacle to overcome." Earnings smoothing and the "big bath" quarter are "white lie" techniques. Some managers use GAAP to defraud: "It has been far safer to steal large sums with a pen than small sums with a gun."
- Consolidation is mandated, but he argues the more diverse the businesses lumped together, the less useful the statements. Charlie and he "constantly study" segment data and prepare consolidated figures only for outside requirements.
- Berkshire added supplementary four-segment statements: finance operations, insurance, manufacturing/publishing/retailing, and all other. They are meant to give owners information "in a form that we would wish to get it if our roles were reversed."
- Deferred taxes: Berkshire books 28% on $1.2 billion of older unrealized appreciation and 34% on $600 million built since. A 1990 rule would require 34% on everything, cutting earnings by $71 million. Neither rate reflects reality since Berkshire has no plans to sell the stocks holding its gains.
- Shareholders can take a "faith" approach (Warren and Charlie have most of their money in Berkshire, gains and losses are shared proportionally) or an "analysis" approach. "In our own investing, we search for situations in which both approaches give us the same answer."

## Managers, Family Businesses, and the Borsheim's Purchase

- Warren does not retire superstars by age. Mrs. B (Rose Blumkin) turned 95; she started Nebraska Furniture Mart at 44 with $500 and has been boss for 51 years. "Superb managers are too scarce a resource to be discarded simply because a cake gets crowded with candles."
- He is skeptical of newly minted MBAs: records look terrific but candidates are "too often short on personal commitment to the company and general business savvy."
- Examples of managers: Stan Lipsey at the Buffalo News improved margins even after Warren predicted they'd fall; the paper keeps its newshole near 50% (49.5% in 1988) regardless of margins. Chuck Huggins runs See's, which sold a record 25.1 million pounds and earned 90% of annual profit in December ($29 million of $32.5 million pre-tax). Ralph Schey runs 19 businesses at Scott Fetzer.
- Fechheimer's Heldman family made a fairly large acquisition that Warren and Charlie "okayed the deal without even looking at it." Furniture retailing and uniform manufacturing are not inherently attractive, so "only exceptional managements can deliver high returns."
- In early 1989 Berkshire bought 80% of Borsheim's, an Omaha jeweler run by the Friedman family (relatives of Mrs. B). He admits he blundered in 1983 by not asking Mrs. B "Are there any more at home like you?"
- The cornerstone is Mrs. B's creed, "Sell cheap and tell the truth." Fundamentals: single-store operations with huge inventories, daily top-management attention to detail, rapid turnover, shrewd buying, and very low expenses.
- Jewelry buyers cannot judge quality or price, so: "If you don't know jewelry, know the jeweler."
- Berkshire bought Borsheim's with no audit, inventory count or receivables check. Ike Friedman "simply told us what was so" and a one-page contract was signed.
- Warren and Charlie stay on the sidelines; the Friedmans keep operating as before.

## Insurance: Pricing Discipline, Float, and Industry Economics

- The combined ratio is losses plus expenses over premiums; below 100 means an underwriting profit. With float counted, a ratio of 107-111 typically breaks even. The 1988 industry combined ratio was 105.4.
- He expects industry incurred losses to grow about 10% a year, so underwriting losses will mount if premiums lag, and may be obscured by under-reserving.
- Industries with inadequate prices get blamed by customers, while highly profitable ones don't. Breakfast cereal earns more than double the return on capital of auto insurance, yet "not a peep" is heard on price hikes. Cereal makers sell at about five times book; most insurers sell near book.
- California's Proposition 103 would force auto rates below cost. Warren argues that below-cost prices must eventually be subsidized by taxpayers. Most insurers have commodity economics and little "economic goodwill" to lose, but GEICO, a low-cost producer with high returns, has a lot at risk, and so does Berkshire (44% owner).
- Berkshire "will not write policies at rates that carry the expectation of economic loss." Volume will stay low. Its 1988 combined ratio was 104 and reserve development was favorable for a second year.
- Low volume with high float means better profits than the industry: he expects float-to-premium at least three times a typical property/casualty company in 1989-1990.
- He expects to be "deluged with insurance business" at some point, likely after a catastrophe or when other insurers recognize they're under-reserved, as in 1985. Meanwhile: retain talent, protect capital, avoid major mistakes.

## Investing Approach, Holdings, and Bonds

- Criteria for choosing among stocks, bonds, cash and arbitrage: highest after-tax return by "mathematical expectation," limited to alternatives Berkshire thinks it understands. The goal is to maximize eventual net worth, not reported earnings.
- Holdings over $100 million at cost/market (000s): Capital Cities/ABC $517,500 / $1,086,750; Coca-Cola $592,540 / $632,448; Freddie Mac preferred $71,729 / $121,200; GEICO $45,713 / $849,400; Washington Post $9,731 / $364,126.
- Cap Cities, GEICO and Washington Post are permanent holdings. Coca-Cola and Freddie Mac were major 1988 buys that he expects to hold long. "Our favorite holding period is forever." He rejects "cutting the flowers and watering the weeds" (Peter Lynch's phrase).
- He concentrates in a few companies he understands: "Too much of a good thing can be wonderful" (Mae West). New commitments reflect long-term business views: "We do not have, never have had, and never will have an opinion about where the stock market, interest rates, or business activity will be a year from now."
- Long-term bonds: he stays averse until he's enthused about long-term stability in the purchasing power of money, which he doesn't expect.
- Exceptions: WPPSS bonds, bought around 17% after-tax yield when unrated and stigmatized, now rated AA-; carried at $247 million, worth about $352 million. He hopes to find "some other large stigmatized issue" misappraised by the market.
- Texaco bankruptcy bonds sold for about $22 million pre-tax profit. The $700 million Salomon 9% convertible preferred is mostly valued for its fixed-income traits; the conversion option may add value, but he has no insight into investment banking profitability.
- He sold about $100 million of tax-exempt bonds bought after August 7, 1986; the remaining $800 million is mostly grandfathered fully tax-exempt.

## CEO Accountability and Board Dynamics

- "The supreme irony of business management is that it is far easier for an inadequate CEO to keep his job than it is for an inadequate subordinate."
- A secretary who can't type 80 words per minute is gone quickly because the standard is clear. CEO standards are fuzzy or waived: "the boss shoots the arrow of managerial performance and then hastily paints the bullseye around the spot where it lands."
- The CEO's boss, the board, seldom measures itself or is held to account. Even if a takeover follows a hiring mistake, outgoing directors typically benefit. Board criticism of the CEO is viewed as "the social equivalent of belching."
- He praises his permanent-holding managers (Tom Murphy and Dan Burke, Bill Snyder and Lou Simpson, Kay Graham and Dick Simmons) for loving their businesses, thinking like owners, and showing integrity and ability.

## Arbitrage and Efficient Market Theory

- Berkshire's insurance units use arbitrage as an alternative to cash when they "have more cash than good ideas." It also "cools any temptation" to relax standards for long-term investments. 1988 pre-tax gain: about $78 million on average invested funds of about $147 million.
- Four questions for any arbitrage: (1) how likely is the promised event, (2) how long is money tied up, (3) what chance of something better (e.g. a competing bid), (4) what happens if the event fails.
- Arcata example: KKR offered $37.00 plus two-thirds of any extra government payment for redwood lands. Warren bought about 400,000 shares near $33.50 and later raised to 655,000 shares (over 7%), buying near $38.00, leaning toward "a whole lot" rather than "zero" on the redwood claim. KKR's financing wobbled, but a rival group paid $37.50 plus half of any recovery. Berkshire received $24.6 million on a $22.9 million cost (about 15% annualized over roughly six months), then about $19.3 million more ($29.48 per share) after the claim settled for $519 million.
- Rockwood cocoa example (age 24, Graham-Newman): the company distributed cocoa beans to shareholders at 80 pounds per share to avoid LIFO tax. Warren bought shares, sold beans, and profited with only subway tokens as expense. Rockwood stock rose from 15 to 100 around the tender.
- Berkshire's style: only a few, usually very large, publicly announced deals per year; no rumors or guessing takeover candidates; "What's the sense in getting rich just to stare at a ticker tape all day?" RJR Nabisco: 3,342,000 shares at year-end, cost $281.8 million, eventually a $64 million pre-tax profit.
- He expects to sit out 1989 arbitrage because of takeover-field excesses: "the less the prudence with which others conduct their affairs, the greater the prudence with which we should conduct our own."
- On efficient market theory: the market is frequently efficient, not always, and "the difference between these propositions is night and day." Graham-Newman's 1926-1956 unleveraged arbitrage averaged 20% a year; 1956-1988 returns at Buffett Partnership and Berkshire averaged well over 20%. The market returned just under 10%, so $1,000 becomes $405,000 at 10% versus $97 million at 20%.
- Caveat: arbitrage is not a guaranteed 20%. "An investor cannot obtain superior profits from stocks by simply committing to a specific investment category or style. He can earn them only by carefully evaluating facts and continuously exercising discipline."

## Shareholder Policy, Acquisition Criteria, and Dave Dodd

- Warren does not want to maximize Berkshire's share price. He wants it to trade in a narrow range around intrinsic value, since both big over- and undervaluation produce investor results that diverge from business results.
- He wants little trading and long-term owners with no timetable or price target: "At what other organization - school, club, church, etc. - do leaders cheer when members leave?"
- Acquisition criteria: (1) purchases of at least $10 million after-tax earnings, (2) demonstrated consistent earning power (no projections or turnarounds), (3) good returns on equity with little or no debt, (4) management in place, (5) simple businesses ("if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it"), (6) an offering price stated up front.
- No unfriendly takeovers. Confidentiality promised, an answer usually within five minutes, cash preferred, stock only when Berkshire gets as much intrinsic value as it gives. Favorite deal: the Blumkin-Friedman-Heldman mold, where owner-managers take cash but stay on as significant owners running the business. He also wants large non-controlling stakes and convertible preferreds, like Salomon.
- Warning: "if you advertise an interest in buying collies, a lot of people will call hoping to sell you their cocker spaniels."
- S&P raised Berkshire to AAA, which only 15 other U.S. industrial or property-casualty companies hold. He promises bondholders the same respect as shareholders and no "event risk."
- Warren credits David Dodd (died at 93; co-author of Security Analysis with Ben Graham) for principles that were "simple, sound, useful, and enduring." Berkshire's prosperity is "the fruit of their intellectual tree."
