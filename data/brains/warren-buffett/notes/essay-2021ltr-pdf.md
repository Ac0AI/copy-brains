## How Warren Defines What Berkshire Owns and Why He Picks Businesses, Not Stocks

- Warren says he and Charlie have the job of managing a portion of shareholders' savings, and he reports what he would want to know "if we were the absentee owner and you were the manager."
- He treats all shareholders equally: no discussions with analysts or large institutions, and important news is released on Saturday mornings so everyone can absorb it before markets open Monday.
- His primary duty for 57 years has been increasing the intrinsic value of Berkshire shares.
- The goal is "meaningful investments in businesses with both durable economic advantages and a first-class CEO."
- Stocks are owned for expected long-term business performance, not as vehicles for timely market moves. His line: "Charlie and I are not stock-pickers; we are business-pickers."
- He admits "I make many mistakes." The portfolio holds some extraordinary businesses, many good ones and a few marginal ones.
- Advantage of marketable stocks: occasionally it is easy to buy pieces of wonderful businesses at wonderful prices. That "shooting-fish-in-a-barrel" experience is very rare in negotiated deals and never happens en masse. It is also far easier to exit a mistake in the marketable arena.

## Honest Earnings, Taxes, and the Partnership With America

- Warren favors "old-fashioned" earnings: calculated after interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization and all forms of compensation. BNSF's record $6 billion in 2021 was measured this way.
- He warns that deceptive "adjustments" to earnings have become more frequent and more fanciful as stocks have risen: "bull markets breed bloviated bull."
- Berkshire owns and operates more U.S. infrastructure assets (property, plant and equipment) than any other American corporation: $158 billion at yearend 2021. This was never a goal, and "Berkshire always will be building."
- Berkshire paid $3.3 billion in federal income tax in 2021, against $402 billion of total U.S. corporate receipts, plus state and foreign taxes.
- Berkshire's textile history: after the 1955 merger of Berkshire Fine Spinning and Hathaway Manufacturing, net worth fell from $51.4 million to $22.1 million in nine years, partly from repurchases, ill-advised dividends and plant shutdowns. The company paid only $337,359 in income tax in that period, about $100 per day. The New England textile industry was in an "extended and non-reversible death march."
- In 1965 new management redeployed cash and steered essentially all earnings into good businesses. Reinvestment plus compounding "worked its magic." Berkshire now pays roughly $9 million daily in income tax.
- Warren credits operating in America for the result: "When you see the flag, say thanks." On March 11, 1942, he bought three Cities Service preferred shares for $114.75 (all his savings) when the Dow closed at 99: "Never bet against America."

## Insurance Float and Why It Is Berkshire's Core Advantage

- Berkshire bought National Indemnity in 1967 for $8.6 million. Float, money held and invested but not owned, grew from $19 million to $147 billion, including a small sum from life insurance. It rose $9 billion in 2021.
- Over 55 years the float has cost less than nothing: despite some years where losses plus expenses exceeded premiums, Berkshire earned a modest overall underwriting profit.
- Float is "very sticky." Individual funds come and go daily, but the total is immune from precipitous decline, so it can be invested for the long term.
- Float value is not reflected in GAAP earnings or net worth.
- Hiring Ajit Jain in 1986: asked about insurance experience, Ajit said "None." Warren replied "Nobody's perfect" and hired him. He calls it a lucky day and credits Ajit for much of the insurance value creation, 35 years on.
- Outlook: likely, but far from assured, that float can be maintained without a long-term underwriting loss. Warren is certain there will be some loss years, perhaps very large. Berkshire is built to handle catastrophes "as no other insurer," and will stay so after he and Charlie are gone.
- Why insurance suits Berkshire: the product never becomes obsolete, volume grows with economic growth and inflation, and integrity and capital always matter. Replicating Berkshire's operation would be almost impossible.

## The Four Giants: Insurance, Apple, BNSF and BHE

- Insurance: Berkshire effectively owns 100% of the insurers, with invested assets enlarged by the large capital backing their promises.
- Apple: Berkshire owns 5.55%, up from 5.39%, at no cost to Berkshire because Apple's buybacks shrank the share count. Each 0.1% of Apple's 2021 earnings equaled $100 million. GAAP counts only dividends ($785 million), while Berkshire's look-through share of earnings was $5.6 billion. Warren applauds Apple using retained earnings for repurchases and calls Tim Cook a "brilliant CEO."
- BNSF: "number one artery of American commerce," record $6 billion earnings, 143 million miles and 535 million tons carried in 2021. Moving that cargo by truck would sharply raise emissions. He predicts it will be a key asset a century from now.
- BHE: earned a record $4 billion in 2021, up over 30-fold from $122 million in 2000, when Berkshire first bought a stake. Berkshire owns 91.1%. It had no wind or solar in 2000 and is now a leader in wind, solar and transmission under David Sokol and Greg Abel. Warren notes it has reported its renewables plans every year since 2007 and has the management, capital and appetite for the large power projects the country needs.

## The Cash Pile, Interest Rates and Share Repurchases

- Berkshire held $144 billion in cash and equivalents (excluding BNSF and BHE), $120 billion of it in T-bills maturing within a year, financing about 1/2 of 1% of the public debt.
- Pledge: Berkshire (apart from BNSF and BHE) will always hold more than $30 billion in cash. The aim is to be "financially impregnable and never dependent on the kindness of strangers (or even that of friends)." Warren and Charlie "like to sleep soundly."
- The cash is not patriotism or a loss of preference for ownership. Warren has kept at least 80% of his net worth in equities since 1942, and 100% is his favorite. Berkshire's roughly 80% is due to failing to find companies meeting its long-term criteria. Cash-heavy periods "are never pleasant; they are also never permanent."
- Three ways to increase value: (1) raise earning power of controlled businesses by internal growth or acquisitions (internal opportunities now beat acquisitions on returns but are small relative to resources); (2) buy non-controlling stakes in good public businesses (little excites him now); (3) repurchase Berkshire shares.
- Interest rates: "Long-term interest rates that are low push the prices of all productive investments upward, whether these are stocks, apartments, farms, oil wells, whatever." Rates always matter for valuation.
- Buybacks: when price/value is right, this is "the easiest and most certain way" to increase shareholders' wealth. Over two years Berkshire repurchased 9% of shares outstanding at yearend 2019 for $51.7 billion, leaving continuing holders owning about 10% more of every business. Another $1.2 billion was bought by February 23, 2022.
- Buybacks only make sense at appropriate value: overpaying for Berkshire would destroy value just as overpaying for others would. Appetite is "price-dependent."
- Buybacks also raise float per share, which rose 25% in two years, from $79,387 to $99,497 per A share.
- A high-class, buy-and-keep investor base limits buyback opportunities (short-term speculators would create more volatility and more chances), but Warren and Charlie prefer these owners.

## The TTI and BNSF Story: Selling to the Right Home

- Paul Andrews founded Tex-Tronics (TTI) in 1971 with $500 after being fired by General Dynamics; first-year sales were $112,000. Today TTI sells over one million items, with $7.7 billion annual volume.
- In 2006, at 63, Paul worried about what would happen to those who depended on him if he died. He rejected a competitor sale (best "synergies" economics, but the buyer would cut TTI's CFO, legal and HR people and favor its own city) and a leveraged buyout (focused on an "exit strategy," ending in a reseller). He told Warren: "you are the only guy left." One meeting, one lunch, one deal.
- Results: employees grew from 2,387 to 8,043, earnings up 673%. Each year Warren suggested a raise and Paul replied "We can talk about that next year."
- Luck chain: John Roach's friendship led Paul to Omaha. In the fall of 2009 Berkshire's directors met in Fort Worth to visit TTI. Warren met BNSF CEO Matt Rose on October 22, 2009, the day BNSF reported weak third-quarter results during the Great Recession. The next day he suggested Berkshire was a better long-term home than being public and named his maximum price. Eleven days later the deal was announced. "The BNSF acquisition would never have happened if Paul Andrews hadn't sized up Berkshire as the right home for TTI."
- Lesson: Berkshire's appeal to founders is permanence and keeping people and places intact, not the highest bid.

## Work, Teaching and Choosing Your Shareholders

- Advice to students: seek employment in (1) the field and (2) with the kind of people you would select if you had no need for money. Find that and "you will no longer be 'working.'"
- Warren and Charlie started at his grandfather's grocery store and found job satisfaction only at Berkshire. They work with people they like and trust; the home office has "no jerks" and about one departure per year.
- Teaching clarifies thinking. Charlie's "orangutan effect": explain a cherished idea to an orangutan and you may leave behind a puzzled primate but exit thinking more clearly.
- Shareholders: Berkshire cannot select owners as a partnership could, but has an unusual corps of individuals and families with a "til death do us part" attitude. People comfortable with their investments "will, on average, achieve better results than those who are motivated by ever-changing headlines, chatter and promises."
- Long-term individual owners are the "partners" he and Charlie have in mind in every decision.
