## 1985 results and why Berkshire's returns will fall

- Net worth rose $613.6 million, or 48.2%, in 1985. Per-share book value went from $19.46 to $1643.71 over 21 years, 23.2% compounded.
- Warren says neither figure can be repeated. One cause is a market offering little opportunity: "we cannot find significantly-undervalued equities to purchase for our insurance company portfolios."
- The second and "far more telling" cause is size. "An iron law of business is that growth eventually dampens exceptional economics." No high-return company he knows of has kept earning 20% or more on equity for ten years while reinvesting all earnings once it passed $1 billion of equity. To sustain returns, such companies had to shed capital through dividends or buybacks.
- Berkshire needed $3.9 billion of profits over ten years to earn 15% annually. The figure is now $5.7 billion, up 48% in line with the capital base.
- Berkshire's advantages: no pressure from quarterly or annual figures, freedom to enter any business that makes sense, and "we love our work." Hitting 15% will still take far more luck than 23.2% did.

## Market price versus business value, and who the shareholders are

- Berkshire historically sold modestly below intrinsic value. The discount has disappeared and a premium occasionally appears. That helped holders but hurts new buyers, who must see the premium maintained to match the business's results.
- Warren prefers a price that consistently approximates business value, so all owners prosper as the business prospers. "In the end, investor gains must equal business gains." Long over- or undervaluation distributes the gains unfairly depending on how "lucky, shrewd, or foolish" each owner is.
- Berkshire's shareholders are almost all individuals, not institutions, and have been "rational, interested, and investment-oriented." Institutions with large staffs are not a stabilizing force: "stocks heavily owned and constantly monitored by institutions have often been among the most inappropriately valued."
- He uses Ben Graham's story of the prospector who yells "Oil discovered in hell" and then follows the crowd, to illustrate herd behavior among professionals.

## Reading earnings and selling securities

- Warren and Charlie focus on segment-level earnings and never prepare consolidated figures for internal use. Investors need segment data too.
- Realized gains are "meaningless as a measure of how well we have done in the current year." They are like a graduation: all growth since purchase is booked in the year of sale.
- The General Foods sale produced $338 million of the $488 million realized gain. Purchased in 1980 far below business value, it paid off through four factors: a bargain price, fine economics, an able shareholder-oriented management, and a buyer paying full value. Only the last produces reported earnings, but the first three are the key. "We devote our attention to attractive purchases, not to the possibility of attractive sales."
- Special distributions (Washington Post, General Foods) are sales of shares back to the company, sized to keep Berkshire's ownership percentage unchanged. They are taxed like dividends, which is favorable for corporate holders. Warren agrees only when management proposes it and the buyback is below intrinsic value.

## The textile failure: a business with bad economics

- Buffett Partnership bought control of Berkshire 21 years ago with $22 million net worth, all in textiles. The previous nine years produced $10 million of aggregate losses on $530 million of sales.
- Warren closed the business in 1985 and says he should be "faulted for not quitting sooner." He wrote in 1978 that textiles "should average modest cash returns relative to investment"; he was "very wrong." He "ignored Comte's advice" and "believed what I preferred to believe." The 250 textile mills closed since 1980 processed the same information "more objectively."
- Good managers (Ken Chace, Garry Morrison) and a cooperative union could not fix it. In a commodity business with world excess capacity, each firm's cost-cutting capex looked rational but neutralized itself as savings became industrywide price cuts, "just as happens when each person watching a parade decides he can see a little better if he stands on tiptoes."
- Burlington Industries spent about $3 billion of capex from 1964 to 1985 (more than $200 per share on a $60 stock). Its stock was about at its 1964 price after a 2-for-1 split, while the CPI more than tripled. "A textile company that allocates capital brilliantly within its industry is a remarkable textile company - but not a remarkable business."
- Lesson: "A good managerial record ... is far more a function of what business boat you get into than it is of how effectively you row." In a chronically leaking boat, "energy devoted to changing vessels is likely to be more productive than energy devoted to patching leaks."
- He won't close sub-normal businesses just to add a fraction of a point to returns, but won't fund one with unending losses in prospect.
- Machinery cost $13 million, had $866,000 book value and $30-50 million replacement cost. It sold for $163,122 gross, with a net below zero. Modern looms bought for $5,000 sold for $26 scrap. Book value and replacement value mislead; two paper routes or one See's store have more economic goodwill than that equipment.
- He cites Charlie's habit of studying mistakes: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die so I'll never go there."

## Incentive compensation and stock options

- NFM, See's and the Buffalo News grew pre-tax earnings from about $8 million to $72 million in 15 years while needing only about $40 million more capital. The average American business needs about $5 of added capital per $1 of added pre-tax earnings, which would have meant over $300 million.
- Earnings growth from retained earnings alone is no achievement. A savings account at 8% quadruples its earnings in 18 years. With a $100,000 account and a 25% payout ratio, it reaches $179,084 in ten years and earnings rise from $8,000 to $13,515.
- Ten-year fixed-price options let managers profit from retained earnings, ignore the carrying cost of capital, and are "blind to individual performance," so "a managerial Rip Van Winkle" could collect. Owners bear capital costs and downside; option holders do not. "I'll be happy to accept a lottery ticket as a gift - but I'll never buy one."
- Managers would never give outsiders such options. Warren says options should go only to those with overall responsibility, include a retained-earnings or carrying-cost factor, and be priced at true business value.
- Berkshire instead pays bonuses for results in each manager's own unit, regardless of Berkshire's stock price. Bonuses are uncapped, sometimes five times salary or more, and age and seniority don't matter. Many managers buy Berkshire stock and so "walk in the shoes of owners."

## Insurance: a commodity business and the lay-off game

- Premiums grew 20.9% in 1985 but the industry combined ratio was about 118. Losses grew 16% in 1984 and 17% in 1985, partly from reserve corrections and "judicial inflation."
- The profit upturn will be short. Commodity businesses profit only when prices are fixed or capacity is short, and in insurance capacity is "an attitudinal concept": capital can be raised instantly. About 15 insurers raised over $3 billion in 1985. Price-cutting will follow, and the fault will lie with the capital-raisers.
- Berkshire's strong capital is now a competitive advantage. Premium volume more than tripled. It is willing to risk $10 million of its own money on one event if the price is right and the risk is uncorrelated with others, while few insurers will risk half that.
- Large insurers wrote big gross lines but kept small net lines. One 1984 example: the issuer kept about 40% of $6 billion in premiums with an underwriting loss under $200 million (combined ratio well under 110), while reinsurers lost over $1.5 billion (over 140). Berkshire never played the "lay it off at a profit" game.
- A National Indemnity ad for policies with minimum $1 million premiums drew 600 replies and about $50 million in premiums. It is long-tail, so results are unknown for at least five years.
- Combined ratio improved from 134 to 111, still reflecting past under-reserving. Warren says "except for" should be excised from business vocabulary: count "the runs scored against you in all nine innings." "The real mistake is not the act, but the actor." Learn lessons from others' experience.
- Fireman's Fund: from September 1, 1985 Berkshire takes a 7% share of its business for four years, receiving $92.7 million of unearned premium and paying $29.4 million of expenses. Berkshire holds the float for investment but has no role in underwriting or specific investment decisions.

## Buying stocks and businesses: Washington Post, Cap Cities, Scott Fetzer

- Washington Post: bought in 1973 at no more than one-quarter of per-share business value (about $100 million market versus $400-500 million value). Warren says the edge was attitude from Graham, not insight, and he credits academics preaching efficient markets as an advantage. The stock fell 25% by 1974 before Kay Graham's buybacks and management paid off. A $10.6 million cost became $221 million, versus perhaps $40-60 million in a typical media stock. With a $1.8 billion valuation now, such returns are impossible.
- Cap Cities/ABC: 3 million shares at $172.50, a full price. Warren calls Tom Murphy and Dan Burke the best managers of any public company. Berkshire gave Tom the vote on its shares and accepted sale restrictions, as at GEICO and Washington Post, to shield management from "revolving-door capitalists." Warren says there will be "no surprises."
- Scott & Fetzer: about $320 million plus $90 million of existing debt, 17 businesses on $700 million of sales, World Book 40% of sales. Warren wrote Ralph Schey on October 10, met him October 22 and signed within a week.
- Warren's acquisition ad: (1) at least $10 million after-tax earnings, (2) consistent earning power, no turnarounds or projections, (3) good returns on equity with little debt, (4) management in place, (5) simple businesses, (6) a stated price. No unfriendly takeovers, a fast answer (customarily five minutes), and cash preferred. Berkshire also buys large non-controlling blocks only when the economics and the people are right.
- Berkshire has "no master strategy" and no planners: it waits for something sensible and acts. Beatrice was a short-term arbitrage "parking place for money" when Berkshire has "more money than ideas."
