## Retained earnings only make sense when the business earns above the passive-return "crossbar"

- Warren frames the case for equity as "value-added": equity should earn more than passive returns (bond interest) because it carries more risk and benefits from managerial skill.
- Decades earlier, a 10% return on equity made a "good" business, since long-term taxable bonds yielded 5% and tax-exempts 3%. American business then earned about 11% on equity and stocks sold at over 150 cents on the dollar of book value.
- In 1981, long-term taxable bonds yielded over 16% and tax-exempts 14%, while American business earned only about 14% on equity, before tax on the owner.
- Example for a 50% bracket investor: if a typical company pays out all earnings at 14% ROE, the return equals a 7% tax-exempt bond, which "might be worth fifty cents on the dollar."
- If all earnings are retained, earnings and price grow 14% a year, but a 20% maximum capital gains tax makes the net return worse than the passive after-tax rate.
- Conclusion: "companies achieving 14% per year gains in earnings per share while paying no cash dividend are an economic failure for their individual shareholders." The returns from passive capital outstrip the returns from active capital.
- Most American businesses were "bad" businesses economically, producing less after-tax for individual investors than the tax-exempt passive rate. American equity capital in aggregate produced no value-added.
- The crossbar rose faster than corporations jumped: ROE improved a few points in a decade, but passive rates rose much more. If inflation is tempered and passive returns fall, many "bad" businesses return to "good."
- Warren warns that old yardsticks (historic p/e ratios, valuation rules) are costly to retain when change is rapid: "facts do not cease to exist, either because they are unpleasant or because they are ignored."

## Retention policy: high-return businesses should retain, low-return businesses should pay out

- Rational policy: a company with high historic and prospective ROE should retain most or all earnings so owners earn premium returns on added capital. A low-return company should pay a high dividend so owners can redeploy capital.
- Analogy: a holder of a 5% bond doesn't use the coupons to buy more 5% bonds at 100 cents when similar bonds sell at 40 cents. "Good money is not thrown after bad."
- He cites the parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14-30): the high-earning servants keep and expand their capital, while the non-earning servant must hand his capital to the top performer.
- Inflation inverts this. It acts as a "gigantic corporate tapeworm": regardless of profits, more dollars are needed each year for receivables, inventory and fixed assets just to match the prior year's unit volume. The less prosperous the firm, the larger the share claimed.
- A business earning 8% or 10% on equity often has no leftovers for expansion, debt reduction or "real" dividends, so the low-return business must retain every nickel precisely because it is unattractive.
- Red flag: "Beware of 'dividends' that can be paid out only if someone promises to replace the capital distributed." Examples include dividend reinvestment plans with discounts that nearly force reinvestment, and issuing shares to Peter to pay dividends to Paul.
- Berkshire retains earnings "for offensive, not defensive or obligatory, reasons," yet only barely clears the after-tax passive crossbar with its historic 21% return. He admits its own value-added could turn negative.

## Non-controlled ownership earnings and buying fractions of businesses

- Berkshire's share of undistributed earnings in companies it doesn't control is excluded from reported operating earnings. Warren believes they will become tangible value "just as surely as if subsidiaries we control had earned, retained - and reported - similar earnings."
- The translation into market gains is irregular. Market values track business values well over long periods but "in any given year the relationship can gyrate capriciously." Companies that employ retained earnings poorly see low or negative recognition; those with high returns on added capital see far more than dollar-for-dollar.
- In 1981 these unrecorded earnings exceeded reported operating earnings. Four positions (GEICO, General Foods, R. J. Reynolds, Washington Post) alone were expected to contribute well over $35 million of undistributed earnings in 1982.
- Non-controlled holdings have better underlying economics than controlled businesses because the area of choice is far wider. "Small portions of exceptionally good businesses are usually available in the securities markets at reasonable prices," while whole businesses are rarely for sale, and then at high prices.
- Current approach: open-market purchase of fractional positions in companies with "excellent business franchises and competent, honest managements." Berkshire never expects to run them but expects to profit.
- If these don't deliver full value, the mistake lies in one of three things: the management joined, the future economics of the business, or the price paid. Misjudging future economics is the most common error. Example: he volunteered a rosy view on aluminum, then revised it "approximately 180 degrees" (Berkshire held Alcoa).
- Lack of control is often an economic plus because mistakes in marketable securities can be corrected far faster than in controlled subsidiaries.
- GEICO accounted for over half of the $124 million (about 31%) net worth gain in 1981 (cost $47.1 million, market $199.8 million). He cautions that market gains outstripped underlying business value gains, and "such market variations will not always be on the pleasant side."

## Why acquirers overpay: toads, princes and the 2X takeover

- Principle: "we would rather buy 10% of Wonderful Business T at X per share than 100% of T at 2X per share," regardless of the effect on reported earnings. Most managers prefer the reverse.
- Acquisition decisions aim at real economic benefit, not "managerial domain" or reported numbers. "Managements stressing accounting appearance over economic substance usually achieve little of either."
- Three usually unspoken motives behind high-premium takeovers:
  1. Animal spirits: leaders relish activity and challenge (Berkshire's pulse also quickens when an acquisition is in prospect).
  2. Size as yardstick: organizations measure and pay managers by size. A Fortune 500 manager knows his sales rank, not necessarily his profitability rank.
  3. The toad-to-prince fairy tale: managers assume their "kiss" will transform the target's profitability.
- Logic: investors can always buy toads at the going price. If they fund princesses paying double for the right to kiss the toad, "those kisses had better pack some real dynamite." Warren has "observed many kisses but very few miracles."
- Berkshire's own record: kisses on bargain toads "fell flat." Successes were princes when purchased, and some fractional interests in identifiable princes bought at toad-like prices.
- Warren won't pay a lot for what he is supposed to "bring to the party," because "we ordinarily don't bring a lot." He may pay a fairly fancy price for a business if he is reasonably confident of what he's getting, with prices that make sense even if the future resembles the past.
- In 1981 he came close to a major purchase but walked away because the price, given alternative uses of funds, would have left owners worse off: "The empire would have been larger, but the citizenry would have been poorer."
- Berkshire avoids small commitments: "If something's not worth doing at all, it's not worth doing well."

## The two categories of dazzling acquisition records

- Category 1: companies that bought businesses well adapted to inflation. Such businesses need two traits:
  1. Ability to raise prices easily, even with flat demand and unused capacity, without losing share or unit volume.
  2. Ability to absorb large dollar volume increases (often inflation-driven) with only minor additional capital.
- Managers of ordinary ability focused solely on these tests did excellently in recent decades. Few businesses have both traits, and competition to buy them became "fierce to the point of being self-defeating."
- Category 2: managerial superstars who can spot the prince disguised as a toad and peel away the disguise. Warren names Ben Heineman (Northwest Industries), Henry Singleton (Teledyne), Erwin Zaban (National Service Industries) and especially Tom Murphy (Capital Cities Communications), a "twofer" in both categories.
- These champions made few deals in recent years and often found repurchase of their own shares the most sensible use of capital.
- Warren says he does not qualify for Category 2, and Berkshire's actual Category 1 activity was "sporadic and inadequate." He invokes the Noah principle: "predicting rain doesn't count, building arks does."

## Property-casualty insurance: pricing discipline, the cycle and balance sheet strength

- Because policies average a little under twelve months and prices are frozen for the contract life, a year's premiums written fix about half of next year's premiums earned. Pricing mistakes must be lived with for an uncomfortable period.
- Warren forecast that 1982 would be the worst underwriting year in recent history, locked in by pricing behavior. Industry premium growth was 3.6% written in 1981 and the combined ratio 105.7 (versus 103.1 in 1980 and 97.2 in 1977).
- An industry volume gain of at least 10% a year was needed to stabilize losses, since insurers expect incurred losses to rise about 10% annually. Each percentage point below 10% accelerates deterioration.
- Cost drivers: monetary inflation and "social" inflation (courts and juries stretching coverage). Results had worsened despite good luck (hurricanes staying at sea, less driving).
- Why insurers underprice: carriers holding bonds carried at "nonsensically high prices" must keep cash flowing by selling policies at nonsensically low prices. They fear a volume drop more than an underwriting loss. Others follow because "it's difficult to price much differently than your most threatened competitor," compounded by worship of size over profitability and fear that lost market share can't be regained.
- He rejects the notion of a regular underwriting cycle with a constant midpoint. He expects very large underwriting losses to be the norm, and the best future years may look substandard against the past decade's average.
- Berkshire's response: sacrifice volume, keep underwriting superior to the industry, expect continued low volume, and preserve financial flexibility. "At some point, should fear ever prevail throughout the industry, our financial strength could become an operational asset of immense value."
- He credits managers Phil Liesche, Bill Lyons, Roland Miller, Floyd Taylor and Milt Thornton for swimming against the tide. GEICO, by "extreme and improving operating efficiency," is in a more protected position and is "a brilliantly run implementation of a very important business idea."

## Measuring performance, inflation, and shareholder-designated contributions

- Operating earnings were $39.7 million in 1981, 15.2% of beginning equity (securities at cost), versus 17.8% in 1980. The shareholder-designated giving program cut earnings by about $900,000.
- Over the 17 years under current management, GAAP book value rose from $19.46 to $526.02 per share, 21.1% compounded. Warren says this rate "is highly likely to drift downward" and hopes it stays significantly above the average large American corporation.
- Because GAAP ignores undistributed earnings of non-controlled companies, single-year return on equity is a weak measure. For long-term measurement, insurance-held equities are valued at market less a charge for taxes on unrealized gains.
- On inflation: he stays negative on the long-term trend ("Like virginity, a stable price level seems capable of maintenance, but not of restoration"). Apparently satisfactory long-term results are illusory as real investment results, and Berkshire "will continue to do a much better job of filling your wallet than of filling your stomach."
- Shareholder-designated contributions: owners, not officers, choose where corporate charitable funds go. 95.6% of 932,206 eligible shares responded, with no return envelope provided. $1,783,655 went to about 675 charities. The program runs only in years with adequate tax deductions (perhaps seven or eight years of ten).
- Practical lesson: shares held in nominee (broker) name missed the program, so Warren advises holding at least one share in your own name to be sure of receiving corporate news.
- Charlie Munger conceived the idea; he and Warren work "as partners in managing all controlled companies."
