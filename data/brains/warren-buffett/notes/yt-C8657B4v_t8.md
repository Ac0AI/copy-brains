## Warren's baseline: when to invest in a business, and why temperament beats IQ

- Warren's best investment was a book: Ben Graham's *The Intelligent Investor*, bought in 1949. Aside from what he paid for his two marriage licenses, he calls it the best investment he ever made.
- He says you need "the right framework" and a sound investing philosophy. Graham's approach is simple, and Warren adopted it immediately, which most people don't.
- With the right philosophy you find opportunities over 20, 30, 40 or 50 years, and most often in panics. He says stocks sell at silly prices from time to time, and most stocks at one time or another sell at very silly prices.
- Spotting a cheap stock doesn't take a high IQ. It takes a temperament willing to step up and act.
- On IQ: "if you've got 160 IQ sell 30 points to somebody else because you won't need it." He figured out early that you don't have to be that smart, but you must have the right temperament.
- He says you must ignore what other people say and look at the facts, asking whether a stock selling at X is worth 2X. When you don't find such a case, you do nothing.
- He started young: he bought three shares of City Service preferred at 11, in spring 1942, after saving $120 over five years. He read every investment book in the Omaha public library, and his father's books on Saturday mornings in the investment office.
- He says he has as much fun working at 83 as ever: "working with people I love doing what I love."

## Circle of competence and the no-called-strikes advantage

- Warren says the most important thing is to define which businesses you can reach an intelligent decision on and which are beyond your ability to evaluate. You don't need to be right about thousands of companies, only a few.
- Ted Williams, in *The Science of Hitting*, divided the strike zone into 77 baseball-sized squares. Swinging only at pitches in his sweet zone, Williams would hit .400. Forced to swing at low outside pitches in the zone, he'd hit .230. The key to hitting is waiting for the right pitch.
- Warren says investing is better than baseball because there are no called strikes. People can throw Microsoft or General Motors at him and he doesn't have to swing. He can watch thousands of companies and swing only at something he understands at a price he likes.
- "It's a terrible mistake to think you have to have an opinion on everything. You only have to have an opinion on a few things."
- His punch-card idea: if students got a card with 20 punches for all the investment decisions of their lives, they'd get very rich, because they would think hard about each one. Four or five right decisions would probably do it over time.
- He met Bill Gates on July 5, 1991, in Seattle. Gates said he needed a computer to do taxes and track his portfolio. Warren replied he had no income, Berkshire paid no dividend, and he held only one stock. He asked whether computers would change whether people chew gum, Gates said probably not, and Warren said "I'll stick to chewing gum and you stick to computers."
- He says there are all kinds of businesses he doesn't understand, but thousands of opportunities he does. He didn't try to predict that Amazon would win while ten others lost. He tips his hat to Jeff Bezos but says "I don't have to form an opinion on Amazon."
- He did form opinions on Bank of America and Coca-Cola.

## Valuing a brand: the Coca-Cola penny math, and products that travel

- Warren points out Coca-Cola has been around since 1886, and about 1.8 billion eight-ounce servings are sold every day.
- One extra penny on each serving is $18 million a day. Over a year that is about $6.57 billion.
- His question: is Coca-Cola worth a penny more than "Joe's cola"? He says yes, and he has about 127 years of history behind that view.
- He likes decisions of that kind: simple, based on a durable product with a long record.
- On Heinz, he looks at people pouring ketchup on hamburgers and potatoes and doesn't think that will change.
- He prefers products that travel well. Soft drinks and ketchup travel. Candy bars don't: Cadbury bars sell poorly in the US and Hershey bars sell less well elsewhere.

## The GEICO story: patience, relationships, and buying the rest

- At 20, Warren came to Washington on a Saturday because his Columbia professor Ben Graham was GEICO's chairman. The door was locked, a janitor let him in, and Lorimer Davidson spent four hours teaching him insurance. Warren says this help mattered enormously.
- He later sold 100 shares of GEICO to his aunt Alice, the first stock he ever sold.
- In 1976 GEICO was close to going broke after miscalculating its reserves. Warren came back and bought a third of the company in the market in a short period.
- By 1995 the company had repurchased shares, so Berkshire's third had become half. He visited Davidson, then 96 or 97, who held GEICO stock with a very low cost basis. Warren said an offer would trigger a big tax, whereas holding until death gives a stepped-up basis, so he wouldn't make it unless Davidson was comfortable. Davidson said "I've hoped for this all my life", and Berkshire bought the rest.
- Warren's lesson: "Nobody does it alone." He says "we all sit in the shade of trees that were planted by others," so it's obligatory to plant a few trees yourself if you've had good luck.

## How Warren finds deals: Iscar, Heinz and 3G

- On deals, he says he loves not knowing what he'll do next. Playing golf where every drive went in the hole would make him give up the game; the rough makes it interesting.
- Iscar (2006): He received a letter out of the blue from Eitan Wertheimer, about a page and a half, saying that if the family sold, Berkshire was the only buyer they wanted. Warren emailed back, they came to Omaha quickly, and Berkshire paid $4 billion for 80% of a company he had never seen.
- Eitan insisted he visit Israel. Warren said he doesn't even go to Council Bluffs, Iowa, but agreed to visit after the purchase. The plants turned out to be the greatest operation he'd seen. His line: if he had visited first, "I'd pay more money."
- Heinz: He got to know Jorge Paulo Lemann through a board (Gillette). Last December, on a trip to Boulder, Colorado, Lemann explained the Heinz idea. Warren said "count me in."
- Lemann's 3G does the heavy lifting and runs the business. Afterward he sent Warren a one-page governance description of how the partnership would work and a short description of a fair deal. Warren didn't change a word. "Those are the kind of people you like to associate with."

## Panics, human behavior and how the next crisis gets handled

- Warren says people will keep making the same mistakes, and it doesn't correlate with IQ. When greedy they build bubbles, like the housing bubble where a house worked like a margin account. When fearful, they all leave at once.
- He expects recessions periodically and occasional panics, though not all recessions come from panics. He says it will happen again in different circumstances, but "the human animal will keep behaving pretty much the way it has."
- His analogy: people think they're Cinderella at the ball. As the night goes on the music gets better and the drinks flow, they all think they'll leave at two minutes to twelve, and there are no clocks on the wall.
- On 2008: he says the panic was worse than anyone had seen and the country was "right on the edge of the cliff." He credits Bernanke, Paulson, Geithner and President Bush, who said "if money doesn't loosen up this sucker could go down."
- What stops a real panic is someone with the ability and the will saying "I'm going to do whatever it takes." He cites the Fed putting $85 billion into AIG, Paulson guaranteeing money market funds after about $300 billion left non-government funds in three days, and Bernanke's use of Section 13(3) and the Exchange Stabilization Fund.
- He doubts Bernanke and Paulson could do the same under Dodd-Frank, and says he isn't sure whether the new law is a plus or minus for the next panic. On derivatives and regulation, he says it is very hard to write rules that stop people acting foolishly after foolishness has been profitable for years.
- On the Fed's bond buying, he said the Fed had a $3.5 trillion balance sheet and called it "the greatest hedge fund in history," funded partly by currency that costs nothing and bank deposits at 25 basis points. He thinks it faces no pressure to deleverage and can pick its time, though it's an experiment at this scale.

## Optimism about America and the opportunity-versus-fairness tension

- Warren says America works: the Dow went from 66 to 11,497 in the 20th century despite two world wars, the Depression, the flu epidemic, the Cold War and the atom bomb. Stocks were around 100 when he bought his first in 1942, weeks after Pearl Harbor.
- Real GDP per capita has risen about sixfold since he was born in 1930. At 2% growth with 1% population growth, per-capita growth of 1% yields a 20% gain in a generation.
- He calls the baby born today in the US the luckiest person, probabilistically, ever. His advice: "don't ever worry about America."
- But he says the gains are unevenly shared. The Forbes 400's aggregate wealth went from about $300 billion to $2 trillion in 20 years, while median income in real purchasing power is about where it was in 1989.
- He argues markets get more specialized and will not pay well for a significant share of people, so government has to address it. He doesn't want equality of results but a satisfactory base level.
- His thought experiment: you are in the womb, a genie lets you design the economic, social and political system, but you then pull a random slip from a barrel of seven billion (male or female, black or white, strong or infirm, bright or not, US or Bangladesh). You'd want a system that produces lots of output, removes fear (of old age, health), and takes care of those who draw bad tickets.

## Giving it away: the Giving Pledge and swinging for the fences

- The Giving Pledge came from Warren, Bill and Melinda Gates, and a dinner hosted by David Rockefeller with about 16-18 people (including Oprah and Mayor Bloomberg) talking about how they developed their philosophy of giving. The pledge now has about 115 people, and Warren has been "dialing for dollars." The letters are on givingpledge.org.
- He tells 70-year-olds: do you think your decision-making will be better at 95 "with some blonde on your lap" or now?
- He says nobody in the pledge group has given up anything that affects how they live. He admires more the person who drops a dollar in the collection plate, since that costs them real utility, whereas his stock certificates have none for him.
- He and his wife planned in their 20s to give back once they had everything they needed; he assumed she would outlive him, but she died in 2004. He applies Adam Smith's specialization of labor: go to those best at giving money away, as he goes to an obstetrician or a dentist.
- He picked the Gates Foundation because they were younger, energetic, smart and share the principle that "every human life has equal value." They also work for nothing.
- He tells the foundations of his three children to swing for the fences: if everything they do succeeds, they're doing the wrong things, since the important problems are the tough ones.
- His estate: all of it must be spent within 10 years after the estate is closed. He doesn't believe in controlling things from the grave or in picking some great-great-grandchild because of the name.
- He backs Tom Cousins' East Lake model (Atlanta), a holistic mixed-income community rebuild now replicated in New Orleans, Indianapolis and about 11 more towns, because it's high-quality, energetic people putting in their own money.
