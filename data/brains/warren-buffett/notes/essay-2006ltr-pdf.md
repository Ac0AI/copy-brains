## Berkshire's 2006 Results and the Scale of the Business

- Net worth rose $16.9 billion in 2006, lifting per-share book value 18.4%. Since present management took over 42 years earlier, book value grew from $19 to $70,281, a 21.4% compound annual rate.
- Warren believes this is a record one-year gain for an American business, excluding merger effects. Exxon Mobil earns more, but pays most of it out as dividends and repurchases rather than building net worth.
- He admits luck helped: after hurricane losses in 2004 and 2005, "Mother Nature... went on vacation" and super-cat red ink turned "very black."
- GEICO under Tony Nicely: policies rose 42% (5.7M to 8.1M) from yearend 2003 to 2006 while full-time-equivalent employees fell 3.5%, a 47% productivity gain.
  - That kept GEICO a low-cost producer even as ad spend rose to $631 million (from $238 million in 2003 and $31 million in 1995).
- Berkshire had 217,000 employees and revenues approaching $100 billion. Warren and Charlie grew skeptical of big organizations, which become "slow-thinking, resistant to change and smug."
  - Only one of the ten largest non-oil companies by market cap in 1965 (GM, Sears, DuPont, Kodak among them) was still on the 2006 list.
- His job model: work through great managers who run their own shows. His only tasks are to cheer them on, "sculpt and harden our corporate culture," and make major capital-allocation decisions.

## Yardsticks for Intrinsic Value

- No single statistic captures value. Succession depth, for example, matters but can't be proven with numbers.
- Two statistics matter most:
  - Per-share investments (including cash, excluding the finance operation): $4 in 1965, $159 in 1975, $2,407 in 1985, $21,817 in 1995, $80,636 in 2006. That is 27.5% compounded from 1965, but 12.6% from 1995.
  - Per-share pre-tax earnings from non-insurance businesses: $4 (1965), $4 (1975), $52 (1985), $175 (1995), $3,625 (2006). That is 17.9% compounded from 1965 and 31.7% from 1995. Non-insurance earnings rose 38% in 2006.
- Investment growth slowed because Berkshire shifted from buying marketable securities to buying operating businesses, which accelerated earnings growth.
- Large further gains will require "major, and sensible, acquisitions," which won't be easy. Berkshire's advantage is increasingly being "the buyer of choice" for business owners and managers.

## Acquisitions: ISCAR, TTI and Tuck-Ins

- Warren began 2006 by closing about $6 billion of deals pending at yearend 2005: PacifiCorp, Business Wire and Applied Underwriters.
- ISCAR (Israel, cutting tools):
  - The deal began with a 1¼-page letter from chairman Eitan Wertheimer on October 25, 2005. It described generational-transfer concerns and said Berkshire would be "the ideal home."
  - A few hours in Omaha with Eitan, CEO Jacob Harpaz and CFO Danny Goldman convinced Warren they could be trusted to run the business with the same energy after a sale.
  - Berkshire bought 80% for $4 billion; the Wertheimer family kept 20%. Warren first had to learn tax and jurisdictional issues, having never bought a business based outside the U.S.
  - ISCAR sells small consumable tools used with expensive machine tools. "It's a business without magic except for that imparted by the people who run it": it makes money because it enables customers to make more money.
- TTI (Fort Worth electronic-components distributor):
  - Founder Paul Andrews, Jr., 64, owned about 80% and built sales from $112,000 to $1.3 billion over 35 years.
  - He saw how a founder's death disrupts a private company and wanted an owner he chose rather than a trust officer's or lawyer's auction.
  - He rejected a "strategic" buyer (which would chase "synergies" and dismantle his work) and private equity (which would load debt and flip it). He and Warren met the morning of November 15 and made a deal before lunch.
- Tuck-ins at Fruit of the Loom, MiTek, CTB, Shaw and Clayton:
  - Fruit bought Russell for about $1.2 billion including debt, and agreed to buy VF Corp.'s intimate apparel business, adding about $2.2 billion of sales and 23,000 employees.
  - Warren likes placing acquisitions under managers who have already proven themselves at Berkshire. MiTek's Gene Toombs made 14 acquisitions that beat his predictions.
- Berkshire still needs "elephants" to use its flood of incoming cash, so Warren and Charlie ignore "mice." He asks for acquisition leads "day or night."

## Insurance, Float and Super-Cat Risk

- Berkshire bought National Indemnity and National Fire & Marine from Jack Ringwalt on March 9, 1967 for $8.6 million.
  - Warren made the deal in minutes, waiving audit and due diligence so Jack had no chance to reconsider. Jack arrived late because he was hunting for a parking meter with time left, which told Warren he was "my kind of manager."
  - Float then was $17 million. At yearend 2006 it was $50.9 billion, with about $7 billion more coming from the Equitas contract.
- Float is "money we hold that is not ours but which we get to invest." Cost of float over time should be well below the industry's, "perhaps even falling to less than zero," but there will be bad years.
- 2006 underwriting profit was $3,838 million versus $53 million in 2005 (Gen Re $526M, B-H Reinsurance $1,658M, GEICO $1,314M, other primary $340M). Warren warns, "you won't soon see another like it."
- Super-cat: he doesn't know whether 2004-05 were aberrations or a warning about climate change, and says it would be "a huge mistake to bet that evolving atmospheric changes are benign." Katrina is nowhere near a worst case.
- Pricing discipline:
  - Berkshire is willing to lose $6 billion in a single event if paid appropriately for the risk.
  - It won't take even small exposures at prices that don't reflect loss probabilities: "Appropriate prices don't guarantee profits in any given year, but inappropriate prices most certainly guarantee eventual losses."
  - Rates fell as capital flooded super-cat, so Berkshire sharply cut wind exposure: "Be fearful when others are greedy, and be greedy when others are fearful."

## Equitas and Retroactive Reinsurance Accounting

- Lloyd's began around 1688 at Edward Lloyd's coffee house. Passive "names" had unlimited liability "down to the last cufflink," but three centuries of retained cufflinks made the warning feel perfunctory.
- Asbestos and environmental claims from the 1980s caused Lloyd's to implode. In 1996 Equitas received £11.1 billion to pay all pre-1993 claims, pooling the misery and cutting syndicate squabbling.
- Berkshire's deal: Equitas pays $7.12 billion in cash and securities, and Berkshire pays all future claims and expenses up to $13.9 billion, which is $5.7 billion above Equitas's own estimate. Warren and Ajit Jain judged "the odds are in our favor," and "should we be wrong, Berkshire can handle it."
- Accounting (laid out in debits and credits because Warren dislikes opaque footnotes):
  - No profit or loss is booked at inception. Underwriting losses follow annually as the Deferred Charges for Reinsurance Assumed (DCRA) asset is amortized.
  - Retroactive contracts always produce underwriting losses. They are worth it only if investment income on the cash exceeds those losses.
  - Annual DCRA charges had been about $300 million and will rise to about $450 million after Equitas. Other insurance operations must earn at least that in underwriting gains for float to be cost-free, a hurdle Warren expects to clear in most years.
- Berkshire is the reinsurer of choice because some obligations (lifetime indemnity and medical payments to injured workers) can run 50 years or more, and no one else offers equal certainty.

## Operating Businesses, Newspapers and Compensation

- The manufacturing, service and retailing group earned 25% on average tangible net worth with little leverage. Because many units were bought at premiums to net worth, the return on carrying value is 10.8%.
- Business-quality lessons:
  - "If you want to get a reputation as a good businessman, be sure to get into a good business." When fundamentals erode, talented managers only slow the decline.
  - Newspapers were once near-monopolies: for most of the 20th century they were the primary source of news and the easiest way to find jobs or grocery prices. The leading paper in a city took share of both readers and advertisers ("Survival of the Fattest") and gained pricing power in both. Warren warned of change in 1991. If cable, satellite and the internet had come first, newspapers "probably would never have existed."
  - The Buffalo News still has the highest market penetration of large U.S. papers, but profit margins will slide and "the days of lush profits... are over." Fixed costs are high, and non-economic buyers value local prominence. Berkshire will keep the paper unless it faces an irreversible cash drain.
- MiTek: Berkshire paid $420 million in 2001, lending $200 million at 9% and buying $198 million of stock at $10,000 a share. Fifty-five employees bought 2,200 shares for $22 million at the same price. Five years later, sales tripled and the stock was valued at $71,699 a share, and the debt was repaid.
- NetJets: revenue from flight operations is up 596% since 1998. Europe cost a cumulative $212 million pre-tax loss by mid-2006 before turning profitable. Worldwide pre-tax earnings were $143 million in 2006.
- Compensation:
  - Warren is a one-man compensation committee for about 40 CEOs, spending virtually no time on it; no CEO has voluntarily left in 42 years.
  - Incentives are tied only to results the CEO controls. A .300 hitter gets paid as one even if Berkshire performs poorly, and a .150 hitter gets nothing just because others did well. Berkshire's $61 billion of equities can swing 10% a year, and operating executives' pay shouldn't depend on that.
  - Excessive pay persists because CEO perks get copied ("All the other kids have one") and consultants pick favorable peer groups. Reform will come only if a few large institutional shareholders demand it.
- Real estate: slowing housing reflected weak lending, such as "optional" contracts and teaser rates. "A rolling loan gathers no loss," but unpaid payments add to principal, leaving borrowers a larger bill later.

## Investments, Currencies, Derivatives and Wall Street

- Warren expected Berkshire's major holdings to grow earnings 6% to 8% a year. In 2006 American Express, Coca-Cola, Procter & Gamble and Wells Fargo grew per-share earnings 18%, 9%, 8% and 11%.
- Currencies: direct foreign-exchange gains reached $2.2 billion since 2002, including $186 million in 2006, with the largest from the euro ($839.2 million). Berkshire nearly closed the position once interest-rate differentials turned negative in 2005, and shifted to foreign equities and U.S. companies with large foreign earnings.
- Trade deficit view:
  - He supports real trade, but the U.S. ran $0.76 trillion of "pseudo-trade" (imports with no exports exchanged, about 6% of GDP).
  - That means transferring assets or IOUs abroad, like a rich, self-indulgent family selling off assets to consume more than it produces. The U.S. "investment income" account turned negative in 2006 for the first time since 1915, creating "reverse compounding."
  - Americans will still live better in 10-20 years, but must ship part of production abroad as annual "tribute." He expects political backlash and thinks a "soft landing" is wishful thinking.
- Derivatives: Berkshire selectively writes a few, large contracts when they are mispriced; it had 62 outstanding, managed personally by Warren, with no counterparty credit risk, producing pre-tax profits in the hundreds of millions. This contrasts with the Gen Re derivatives wind-down: contracts fell from 23,218 to 197, with cumulative pre-tax loss of $409 million.
- The Gotrocks family and "2-and-20":
  - Investors keep paying ever-higher fees to "helpers" and holding unrealistic return expectations (pension plans use assumptions out of reach).
  - Under 2-and-20, a 10% gross return nets investors 6.4 points after 3.6 points go to the manager. On a $3 billion fund that is $108 million to the manager, while an index fund might have returned 15% for a token fee.
  - Adage: when someone with experience proposes a deal to someone with money, the person with money gets the experience and the experienced person gets the money.
- Walter Schloss:
  - Managed a partnership from 1956 to 2002, taking nothing unless investors made money, with one file cabinet, no secretary, and no inside information. His approach, per his son Edwin: "We try to buy stocks cheap."
  - Built his record across about 1,000 mostly lackluster securities, so a few big winners did not explain it. It could not be luck.
  - Efficient market theory was taught as scripture while Walter's record contradicted it, yet business schools made no attempt to study it. "If you are in the shipping business, it's helpful to have all of your potential competitors be taught that the earth is flat."

## Succession, Boards and Giving

- Berkshire has three outstanding CEO candidates, all much younger than Warren. The investment side is less prepared: Lou Simpson is only six years younger. Warren plans to hire a younger investor (possibly several candidates) as future chief investment officer.
- Qualities sought in an investment successor:
  - Genetic wiring to recognize and avoid serious risks, including never-before-seen ones. Models commonly used by financial institutions can't spot certain perils, and "a single, big mistake could wipe out a long string of successes."
  - Temperament: "Independent thinking, emotional stability, and a keen understanding of both human and institutional behavior is vital to long-term investment success."
  - Retention: Berkshire on a resume makes someone marketable, so they need a person like Lou Simpson, who stayed despite being able to earn far more elsewhere.
- Board criteria: owner-oriented, business-savvy, interested and truly independent. Fees of $150,000 to $250,000 undermine true independence. Warren says to select for business judgment and to ask "Does he think like an intelligent owner?" rather than "stocking Noah's ark." Susan Decker (Yahoo! CFO, age 44) joins; Kim Chace steps down.
- Philanthropy: most of Warren's shares go to five foundations. Proceeds from remaining shares are to be spent within ten years of his estate closing, a roughly 25-year horizon. He prefers prompt spending by capable, motivated people over perpetual foundations whose vigor may fade, because future rich donors can fund the best organizations through a "market test of ideas."
- Foundation sales won't hurt the stock: annual volume is only 15% of shares, and the foundations would add at most three points. Berkshire should sell near intrinsic value, neither at a large discount nor a premium. Dividend, buyback and issuance decisions follow the usual rule: what is likely to deliver the best result for shareholders over time.

## Overhead, Taxes and Culture

- Berkshire expected to pay about $4.4 billion of federal income tax on 2006 earnings, roughly half a day of U.S. government spending. The tax return ran 9,386 pages.
- World Headquarters has 19 employees in 9,708 square feet (plus 655 for Charlie in Los Angeles), with home-office payroll of $3,531,978: "We're careful when spending your money."
- Executives spend differently when it's not their own wallet. At Salomon, once free haircuts ended, one top executive cut his barber visits from weekly to every three weeks.
- Blue Chip Stamps (bought 1970): sales fell from $126 million to $25,920 in 2006, a reminder that a seemingly sure thing can fail.
- Warren feels fortunate: born in America, good parents, health, and a "business" gene that pays disproportionately, along with jobs they love. He is 76 and credits "Cherry Coke and hamburgers" for his health.
