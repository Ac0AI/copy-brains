## The "circle of confidence" and how Warren buys a business

- Warren operates inside a "circle of confidence." What matters is not its size but how well the perimeter is defined: "If you know where the edges are you're way better off than somebody that's got one that's five times as large but they get very fuzzy about the edge."
- He can understand simple businesses and not complicated ones, and he stays out of the rest. He does not try to pick winners among technology companies where "five guys are out in a garage someplace."
- His filter: a business he can understand, people running it he likes and trusts, and a price that makes sense relative to future economics. When he finds one he buys a lot and keeps it, because replacements are hard to find.
- He quotes Peter Lynch: "buy a business that's so good that any idiot can run it because sooner or later one will." He still wants a terrific manager on top of it.
- Any good investment idea can be put in one paragraph.
- Nebraska Furniture Mart example: Rose Blumkin arrived in the US unable to speak English, started the store with $500 after bringing over seven siblings and her parents from Russia, and was nearing 101. The store does about $230 million at one location, more than twice any other single-store furniture operation in the US, and earns roughly $22-23 million pre-tax. She cannot read, but computes room dimensions and prices in her head.
- Berkshire's total legal and accounting cost to buy the business was $1,400, on a one-page contract. There was no audit, and he never checked the real estate records on the 60 acres. "Anything she said was so."
- He does not need to be right on many companies: "I just have to be right on one decision a year."

## Predicting markets and treating stocks as pieces of businesses

- Warren never thinks about what the stock market will do. He asks what the business will do over time.
- Test: if Walt Disney had offered him 5% of a private company for $4 million in 1966, or Roberto Goizueta had offered him a slice of a private Coca-Cola, he would have evaluated the company, not the market.
- He ignores forecasts, even from Alan Greenspan: "I'm not going to trade something I know how to do and give up something I know how to do because of some opinion about something I don't know how to do." He does not predict the stock market, interest rates, or business cycles.
- His formula: "If I buy the right kind of business at the right price with the right people I'll do well over time."
- "In stocks it's very hard to know when something will happen and it's very easy to know what will happen." He may not know whether the market will be higher in two years, but there will be intelligent things to do, and "if you see one the thing to do is to do it."
- Buying 100% of See's Candy (1972) and buying 6% of Coca-Cola use the same analysis: how strong is the hold on customers, and can prices be raised over time. For See's he asked whether Russell Stover could knock them out of the box.
- Owning all of a business has small advantages over owning a piece: you can change management and control the allocation and distribution of earnings. But being in a good business with good managers matters far more. He would rather own a part of a great business than all of a mediocre one.
- Berkshire's edge is mental flexibility: it is not committed to steel, shoes, or insurance per se, and can move capital to wherever it makes sense.

## Moats, franchise value and durability

- Franchise test: if the store lacks your product and offers a substitute, will the customer walk across the street to buy yours, or pay a nickel more? If yes, it is a franchise, and it lives in people's minds.
- See's Candy sold at $1.95 a pound. The question was whether people would pay more as inflation went on. If a dominant share of customers perceive it as the best candy, the business does very well. Nobody takes the low bidder's chocolates home on Valentine's Day.
- Franchise value can come from taste, a mental association, service, or availability.
- His image: a moat around an economic castle. Two questions matter: how big is the castle, and how durable is the moat. Coca-Cola is a big castle; See's is a small castle, but still a castle with a moat.
- Moats are widened by service, employee training, and research, and you need one "to protect you from some guy that's going to come along and offer it a penny cheaper."
- You won't find it in carbon steel or integrated oil.
- Coca-Cola: 110 years old, 750 million eight-ounce servings a day, per-capita consumption rising in nearly every country, about 47% of soft drink consumption outside the US and 41% in the US, gaining share every year. Appeal is universal and the product is durable.
- Gillette: about 21 billion razor blades a year, 30% of them Gillettes but about 60% by value, with 90% share in some countries. About $20 a year per user, for a daily habit, and men rarely switch. Innovation in razors plus distribution plus mind position. Berkshire owned about 11% of Gillette (about two-thirds of its profits come from outside the US) and 7.7% of Coke (over 80% outside the US).
- He likes businesses where he knows what they will look like in 5, 10, 15 or 20 years.
- "You don't have to find very many." Ten good ideas in a lifetime is enough, and you could give half away.

## When to sell, how many bets, and mistakes

- Selling a truly great business because the price looks a little high is "almost always a mistake." He learned this slowly and says he hasn't fully learned it.
- Most fortunes were made in a few securities, held for a very long time, that the buyer understood.
- Investing has no called strikes. Unlike his department-store days, where he had to match every competitor move (air conditioning, 18-month terms, an escalator), an investor can wait for the pitch he likes, then swing big.
- The ease of trading (buy at 10:00, sell at 10:05) causes people to do things that don't make sense.
- He rarely sells his best holdings because replacements are hard to find.
- Cash makes him worse: "I make more mistakes when I have a lot of cash around." His decision acuity fades with idle cash, because there is more compulsion to do something.
- His largest loss at the partnership and Berkshire was about 2% of net worth. Being careful and doing few things limits errors of commission.
- The big mistakes are omissions:
  - He started to buy Fannie Mae around 1988 but didn't follow through; Berkshire made about $5 million, versus roughly $1.5 billion if he had.
  - Disney in 1966: the entire company was selling for about $80 million, debt free. Five percent cost $4 million. They had 300 acres at Anaheim with Disneyland drawing about 9 million people a year, a film library, and had just spent $17 million on the Pirates ride. He bought 5% and sold it about a year later for $6 million. It would be worth about a billion now.
- US Air (1989) he calls "temporary insanity." He had cash, no one misled him, and the manager was excellent. But airlines have revenues set by market forces and costs that are not. Since Kitty Hawk, the airline industry as a whole has returned less than zero to owners. He jokes that he keeps an 800 number to call and say "my name is Warren, I'm an airaholic."

## Hiring and the character test: buy 10% of a classmate

- Hiring at Salomon, Aug 1991: Salomon had about $150 billion in balance sheet assets, about $4 billion equity, with a large share of the other $146 billion due within weeks, and 8,000 employees. After the top management was forced out, Warren interviewed roughly a dozen candidates in one evening.
- He did not ask about grades, never looked at a résumé, and didn't ask about IQ, since everyone there was smart enough. He wanted Ben Franklin's qualities: willing to give others more credit than themselves, no corner-cutting, deliver what is promised and a little extra.
- He picked Deryck Maughan. The next day he tapped him on the chest ("you're it") and Maughan faced about 200 reporters for three hours with two minutes' notice. Maughan never asked what he'd be paid, worked 18-hour days for months, and showed uncanny judgment about what to bring to Warren.
- Classmate exercise: you get to pick one classmate and receive 10% of their lifetime earnings. You would not choose by grades, IQ, or looks, but by character: qualities anyone can acquire. Then imagine shorting 10% of another: the one who cuts corners and claims credit for others' work.
- Character determines output: some people have a 300-horsepower motor and get far less out of it; the best performers have the most efficient motors, not the biggest.
- Habits: Ben Graham wrote down at 12 the qualities he admired and disliked in others. These are habits you can choose. "The chains of habit are too light to be felt until they are too heavy to be broken," so form the right ones early. Decide to be the person you'd buy 10% of.
- Work only with people you like and admire: "like marrying for money... crazy if you're already rich." Go to work for whomever you admire most. Admired managers: Tom Murphy (Capital Cities), Roberto Goizueta and Don Keough (Coca-Cola, took market value from about $5 billion to about $60 billion in 11-12 years), Bill Gates.

## Shareholder base and communication

- Berkshire's A shares traded around $20,000-25,000 and Warren refuses to split the stock. A public company's owners are anyone with money, so the only tools are communications and policies. He wants partners with the same expectations, time horizon, and measures of success.
- Analogy: a sign reading "rock concert" draws one crowd and "ballet" another; both fine, but it's a mistake to advertise one and run the other.
- Not splitting screens out people who trade actively and buy because of a split or next quarter's earnings. Berkshire has by far the lowest turnover relative to capitalization on the NYSE, which he sees as a success. "If I was preaching at a church I would not measure my success by the turnover of the congregation."
- Writing as thinking: when he gets blocked writing the annual report, it's because he hasn't got the idea straight. "There's nothing like writing to force you to think."
- Footnotes: if he can't understand an accounting footnote, he assumes the company doesn't want him to, and "I won't invest in the company."

## Size, banks, foreign investing and inherited wealth

- Size is a drag. Starting with $10,000, one cheap stock mattered. Now a $100 million position that gains 50% (about $32.5 million after tax) moves net worth only about 0.3%. Berkshire's universe is very small and its returns cannot match the past.
- When he started he went through Moody's and Standard & Poor's page by page and found insurers and a utility trading at about two times earnings, but he could only put $5-50 thousand in.
- Banking consolidation: it will continue because managers like to grow and trade rankings by asset size, but he sees no great economies of scale beyond a point. He would rather own 30% of the market in Rockford, Illinois (where Berkshire had a bank earning 2% on assets after tax, conservative) than 15% nationally. Acquired banks' shareholders do better than acquirers'. Wells Fargo's First Interstate-style deal was an exception that worked.
- Foreign companies: he'd buy Coke if it were domiciled in London or Amsterdam, but slightly prefers US domicile for governance, tax, and accounting familiarity. He'll look anywhere, but US equities are a $5 trillion market, and if he can't make money there, going a few thousand miles away is wishful thinking.
- Inherited wealth: leave children "enough money so they can do anything but not enough so they can do nothing." Inherited wealth is "a lifetime supply of food stamps given through a trust officer instead of a welfare officer."
- He credits society for much of what he earned; in Bangladesh or Peru the same talent would earn far less. The market pays disproportionately for some talents (Mike Tyson, a .360 hitter) and little for teachers and nurses, and those richly rewarded owe society a large claim on the proceeds.
