## Earnings, GAAP, and why operating earnings count most

- Berkshire earned $42.5 billion under GAAP in 2020: $21.9 billion operating earnings, $4.9 billion realized capital gains, a $26.7 billion rise in unrealized gains, and an $11 billion write-down of a few subsidiaries and affiliates (all after tax).
- Warren says "Operating earnings are what count most," even when they are not the largest GAAP item. Berkshire's goals are to grow operating earnings and to acquire large, favorably-situated businesses. In 2020 it met neither: no sizable acquisitions, and operating earnings fell 9%.
- Per-share intrinsic value still rose through retained earnings and repurchasing about 5% of shares.
- Realized and unrealized capital gains "fluctuate capriciously" with the stock market. He and Charlie Munger still expect substantial capital gains from the holdings over time.
- Stock holdings ($281 billion at yearend) are treated as a collection of businesses. Under GAAP only dividends received count as income, so the earnings investees retain on Berkshire's behalf are invisible. Warren says "What's out of sight... should not be out of mind": those retained earnings fund expansion, acquisitions, debt paydown and buybacks, which raise Berkshire's share of future earnings.
- Some investees will disappoint and a few will over-deliver spectacularly. He expects the retained earnings of non-controlled businesses to produce equal or greater capital gains in aggregate, and over 56 years that has held.

## The Precision Castparts mistake

- The $11 billion write-down is "almost entirely the quantification of a mistake I made in 2016": he paid too much for Precision Castparts (PCC).
- No one misled him. He was too optimistic about PCC's normalized profit potential, and aerospace industry problems exposed the miscalculation in 2020.
- He still believes PCC is a fine company, the best in its business, and that it will earn good returns on its net tangible assets. His error was in the average amount of future earnings, and therefore the proper price.
- He calls it a big error and notes it is "far from my first error of that sort." CEO Mark Donegan is praised as a passionate manager.

## Why Berkshire is not a typical conglomerate

- Conventional conglomerates bought businesses only in their entirety. Problem one: most truly great businesses did not want to be taken over, so acquirers fished among so-so companies without durable competitive strengths.
- Problem two: they paid steep "control" premiums, then tried to solve overpayment by manufacturing an overvalued stock to use as currency. His illustration: "I'll pay you $10,000 for your dog by giving you two of my $5,000 cats."
- Promotion and "imaginative" accounting, sometimes crossing into fraud, pushed a stock to say 3x business value so it could offer a target 2x its value. He notes that illusions can last a long time, since Wall Street loves deal fees, the press loves colorful promoters, and a soaring price becomes its own "proof."
- Warren and Charlie want to own all or part of a diverse group of businesses with good economics and good managers, and whether Berkshire controls them is unimportant.
- A 20-year struggle with the textile operation he inherited, plus Charlie's influence, convinced him that a non-controlling stake in a wonderful business is "more profitable, more enjoyable and far less work than struggling with 100% of a marginal enterprise."
- Capital is deployed on three criteria: durable competitive strengths, the capabilities and character of management, and price. There are no points for "degree of difficulty" in business.

## The Big Four, insurance float and bonds

- Most of Berkshire's value sits in four businesses: property/casualty insurance (the largest, the core for 53 years, run by Ajit Jain, who joined in 1986), BNSF (100%), Apple (5.4%), and Berkshire Hathaway Energy (91%).
- The insurers operate with far more capital than any competitor worldwide. Together with cash flowing in from non-insurance businesses, this lets them follow an equity-heavy strategy that most insurers cannot, because regulators and rating agencies push them into bonds.
- Bonds are "not the place to be": the 10-year Treasury yielded 0.93% at yearend 2020, down 94% from 15.8% in September 1981. Germany and Japan have trillions of dollars of sovereign debt at negative yields. Reaching for yield through shaky borrowers is not the answer, and savings and loans destroyed themselves partly that way.
- Float is $138 billion: money that is not Berkshire's but is its to deploy in bonds, stocks or T-bills. It works like bank deposits (cash flows in and out, the total changes little), is likely to stay near this level for years, and on a cumulative basis has been costless. He adds that could change, but "over time, I like our odds."

## Buybacks and the Apple example

- In 2020 Berkshire repurchased the equivalent of 80,998 A shares for $24.7 billion, raising every shareholder's ownership of all businesses by 5.2%.
- Criteria: buy only when it raises intrinsic value per share for continuing holders and leaves ample funds for opportunities or problems. Repurchases are not appropriate "at simply any price."
- He criticizes American CEOs for spending more on buybacks when prices have risen than when they have tanked. Berkshire does the reverse.
- Apple: Berkshire bought from late 2016 to mid-2018, ending with just over one billion split-adjusted shares and 5.2% of Apple, at a cost of $36 billion. Since then it has received dividends averaging about $775 million a year and took in $11 billion by selling a small part in 2020.
- Despite that sale, Berkshire's stake rose to 5.4% at no cost, because Apple kept shrinking its share count. Combined with Berkshire's own buybacks, shareholders indirectly own 10% more of Apple's assets and earnings than in July 2018.
- "The math of repurchases grinds away slowly, but can be powerful over time."

## Entrepreneurs, small starts and "Never bet against America"

- Warren's examples of businesses started with little capital:
  - See's Candy (bought 1972): a century old. Rule: do not meddle, since for a non-essential consumer product "the customer is the boss."
  - GEICO: Leo and Lillian Goodwin started it in 1936 with $100,000 to sell auto insurance directly at lower prices. First full-year business was $238,288 in 1937; $35 billion in 2020. He was exposed to it 70 years ago and it was his first investment love.
  - National Indemnity: Jack Ringwalt started it in 1940 with $125,000, focusing on "odd-ball" risks the big insurers ignored. Berkshire bought it in 1967 on a handshake in 15 minutes, with no audit. It is now the only company willing to insure certain giant risks.
  - Nebraska Furniture Mart: Mrs. B started it in 1936 with $2,500. Net worth at end of 1946 was $72,264, with $50 in cash. By 1983 it was worth $60 million, when Berkshire bought 80%, again without an audit. It now owns the three largest US home-furnishings stores, each setting a sales record in 2020 despite six-plus weeks of closure.
  - Clayton Homes (founded 1956) and Pilot Travel Centers (founded 1958, bought a $6,000 service station): each now earns over $1 billion pre-tax. Berkshire owns 38% of Pilot, headed to 80% in 2023.
- Pattern he praises: owner-families who stay on to run the business, with satisfied sellers referring other sellers.
- His conclusion: "Never bet against America." American prosperity, though uneven in its progress toward "a more perfect union," has been "breathtaking."

## The Berkshire partnership and shareholder philosophy

- Directors must act in the best interest of the corporation and its stockholders. Customers, employees (360,000), lenders and communities are valued but have no vote on dividends, strategy, CEO selection or deals.
- Warren and Charlie ran partnerships from 1956 and 1962 respectively. Warren charged no fees or salary and took his share only above a 6% annual hurdle, with shortfalls carried forward. His own money and his family's were invested alongside. Investors correctly sensed "an extreme aversion to permanent loss of capital."
- Berkshire's 1983 first business principle: "Although our form is corporate, our attitude is partnership."
- He describes five ownership "buckets": himself (which will empty through philanthropy), index funds (automatic pilot), active institutional managers, individual traders, and over a million individuals who trust them with no intent to sell. Charlie and Warren feel special kinship with the last group.
- Berkshire does not court Wall Street analysts or institutions: "We already have the investors we want." He quotes Phil Fisher's restaurant analogy: a company must consistently serve either hamburgers and Coke or French cuisine, and Berkshire has served hamburgers and Coke for 56 years.
- Pledge: "We can and do... pledge to treat you as partners."

## Passive investing advice, fixed assets, BNSF and BHE

- Stock ownership is a positive-sum game. A monkey throwing 50 darts at the S&P 500 would do well if it never changed its picks. Wealth comes from productive assets, requiring "the passage of time, an inner calm, ample diversification and a minimization of transactions and fees." Investors' expenses are Wall Street's income.
- Berkshire owns $154 billion of depreciated US property, plant and equipment, more than any other US company (AT&T is next at $127 billion). He says this is not itself a triumph: the best returns come from businesses needing minimal assets, high margins and little capital to grow, though asset-heavy companies can still be good investments.
- BNSF and BHE earned a combined $4.2 billion in 2011 and $8.3 billion in 2020.
- BNSF: carries about 15% of US non-local ton-miles and owns 23,000 miles of track across 28 states. Since 2010 it has invested $41 billion, $20 billion more than depreciation. It has paid Berkshire $41.8 billion in dividends, keeping about $2 billion cash so it can borrow cheaply without a Berkshire guarantee. In 2020, despite a 7% volume decline, margin rose 2.9 points under Carl Ice; Katie Farmer became CEO.
- BHE: earnings grew from $122 million to $3.4 billion over 21 years, and it pays no common dividend, unusual for a utility. It is investing $18 billion (started 2006, completion 2030) to rebuild the western transmission grid for renewable power. Warren says the decision rested on trust in America's political, economic and judicial systems, and BHE is looking for other projects of similar size.
