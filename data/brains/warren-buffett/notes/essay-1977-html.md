## Return on equity, not EPS, measures managerial performance

- Warren says most companies define "record" earnings as a new high in earnings per share, but businesses routinely add to their equity base each year, so rising EPS alone proves little.
- A 10% increase in equity capital paired with a 5% increase in EPS is nothing noteworthy. Even "a totally dormant savings account" produces steadily rising interest earnings through compounding.
- He prefers return on equity capital as the measure of managerial economic performance. Exceptions: companies with unusual debt-equity ratios or important assets carried at unrealistic balance sheet values.
- 1977 example: operating earnings were $21,904,000, or $22.54 per share, a 19% return on beginning equity. That was slightly better than 1976 and above both Berkshire's long-term average and American industry's.
- Per-share operating earnings rose 37%, but beginning capital rose 24%, so the gain was "considerably less impressive than it might appear at first glance."
- Capital gains or losses realized directly by Berkshire or its insurance subsidiaries are excluded from operating earnings. Over the longer term, aggregate gains and losses still matter.

## Tailwinds versus headwinds: choosing the business

- Warren contrasts textiles with insurance. In textiles, "even very good management probably can average only modest results." In insurance, some mistakes can be made and overall performance can still be quite satisfactory.
- A lesson learned, and sometimes re-learned: "the importance of being in businesses where tailwinds prevail rather than headwinds."
- Textile operations had another very poor year. Berkshire had mistakenly predicted better results in each of the prior two years. Marketing and manufacturing problems persisted, some from industry conditions and some self-made.
- Why Berkshire stayed in textiles despite poor expected returns on capital:
  - The New Bedford and Manchester mills were among the largest local employers, with an older workforce whose skills were non-transferable.
  - Workers and unions cooperated on cost structure and product mix.
  - Ken Chace's work after the 1965 change of control generated textile capital that financed the acquisition and expansion of the insurance operation.
  - At least modest profits seemed achievable with effort and imagination.
- Single-year snapshots mislead. Berkshire Fine Spinning and Hathaway Manufacturing merged in 1955. In 1948 their pro forma combined after-tax earnings were almost $18 million, with 10,000 employees at a dozen New England mills (versus IBM $28 million, Safeway $10 million, 3M $13 million, Time $9 million).
- In the decade after the merger, sales of $595 million produced an aggregate loss of $10 million. By 1964 only two mills remained and net worth had fallen to $22 million from $53 million.

## Insurance underwriting: the pricing cycle and managerial discipline

- Berkshire entered insurance in early 1967 by buying National Indemnity and National Fire and Marine for about $8.6 million. Premium volume grew from $22 million in 1967 to $151 million in 1977, almost 600%, with no Berkshire shares issued.
- Growth came from National Indemnity's traditional liability lines, new startups (Cornhusker 1970, Lakeland 1971, Texas United 1972, Insurance Company of Iowa 1973, Kansas Fire and Casualty late 1977), cash purchases (Home and Automobile 1971, Kerkling/Central Fire and Casualty 1976, Cypress at yearend 1977), and added products, notably reinsurance.
- Mistakes were real: a surety operation started in 1969, Home and Auto's 1973 urban auto expansion into Miami, an unresolved aviation "fronting" arrangement, and California workers' compensation.
- The cycle: big rate increases in 1976 offset disastrous 1974-75 results. Because policies are typically one-year, repricing errors are fixed only at renewal, so the full earnings impact arrived in 1977.
- Costs in Berkshire's lines rise close to 1% per month from monetary inflation and "social inflation" (a broadening definition by society and juries of what is covered). Unless rates rise 1% per month, underwriting profits shrink. Rate increases had slowed sharply, and he expected margins to decline by the second half of 1978.
- Discipline: as markets loosen and rates become inadequate, Berkshire must accept reduced volume. "It runs counter to normal institutional behavior to let the other fellow take away business - even at foolish prices."
- National Indemnity's large volume gains came with excellent margins after competitors contracted or withdrew following 1974-75. Credit went to Phil Liesche, Roland Miller (underwriting) and Bill Lyons (claims).
- Reinsurance (George Young) had a combined ratio of 107.1, unsatisfactory but trending down, and generates unusually high investable funds relative to premium.
- Homestate operation (John Ringwalt): five companies, $23 million net premium versus $5.5 million three years earlier. All four full-year companies had combined ratios below 100; Cornhusker was 93.8 and had been below 100 in six of seven full years.
- Home and Auto (John Seward) was turned from red ink and possible extinction to sound, profitable and growing.
- Cypress Insurance (workers' comp, Milt Thornton) was bought for cash at yearend 1977, with about $12.5 million of volume not in 1977 results.

## Why people matter so much in insurance

- Warren says insurers sell standardized policies anyone can copy, and their only product is a promise.
- Licensing is not difficult and rates are an open book. There are no important advantages from trademarks, patents, location, corporate longevity or raw material sources, and little consumer differentiation to insulate from competition.
- Annual reports commonly stress the difference people make; sometimes true, sometimes not. But the nature of insurance "magnifies the effect which individual managers have on company performance."

## Insurance float invested: results and the long-term scorecard

- From 1975 to 1977, insurance investments at cost (excluding the Blue Chip Stamps stake) grew from $134.6 million to $252.8 million, driven by reserve growth from premium volume plus retained earnings.
- Pre-tax net investment income rose from $8.4 million in 1975 to $12.3 million in 1977. Realized pre-tax capital gains were $6.9 million, about a quarter from bonds.
- Unrealized stock gain at yearend 1977 was about $74 million, versus an unrealized loss of $17 million at the end of 1974. Warren says such single-date figures should not be taken too seriously.
- Most large positions will be held for many years, and "the scorecard on our investment decisions will be provided by business results over that period, and not by prices on any given day."
- He considers it as foolish to be mesmerized by near-term earnings when buying small pieces of a company as when buying a whole one.
- Largest holdings (cost / market, $000s): Washington Post Class B $10,628 / $33,401; GEICO convertible preferred $19,417 / $33,033; Interpublic $4,531 / $17,187; Capital Cities $10,909 / $13,228; GEICO common $4,116 / $10,516; Kaiser Aluminum $11,218 / $9,981; Knight-Ridder $7,534 / $8,736; Ogilvy & Mather $2,762 / $6,960; Kaiser Industries $778 / $6,039. Total equities: $106.9 million cost, $181.1 million market.

## How Warren selects stocks: buy pieces of businesses

- He picks marketable equities the way he would evaluate buying a whole business. Four criteria:
  1. A business he can understand.
  2. Favorable long-term prospects.
  3. Operated by honest and competent people.
  4. Available at a very attractive price.
- He ordinarily makes no attempt to buy for short-term stock price behavior. If business results keep satisfying him, he welcomes lower prices on stocks he owns as a chance to buy "more of a good thing at a better price."
- Pro-rata slices of truly outstanding businesses sometimes sell in securities markets at very large discounts to what they would fetch in negotiated whole-company deals. So "bargains in business ownership, which simply are not available directly through corporate acquisition, can be obtained indirectly through stock ownership."
- When prices are right, Berkshire will take very large positions with no intent to control, and no expectation of sale or merger, betting that excellent business results translate over time into market value and dividends for minority and majority owners alike.
- These positions may barely affect reported operating earnings at first. Capital Cities: $10.9 million invested in 1977; attributable earnings about $1.3 million, but only the $40,000 annual cash dividend flows into operating earnings.

## Non-control can beat control: the Capital Cities case

- Warren says Capital Cities has "both extraordinary properties and extraordinary management," skilled in both operations and the deployment of corporate capital.
- Buying such properties directly would cost roughly twice what Berkshire paid through the stock market, with no important advantages from direct ownership.
- Control would bring the opportunity and responsibility to manage operations and capital, but Berkshire could not match the management already in place.
- Conclusion: "we can obtain a better management result through non-control than control." He calls this unorthodox but believes it sound.

## Banking and See's Candies: high returns on little capital

- Illinois National Bank earns about three times the asset return of most large banks while paying maximum rates to savers and holding low-risk, highly liquid assets.
- Gene Abegg founded it in 1931 with $250,000; first full-year earnings were $8,782. No new capital has been added. Since Berkshire's 1969 purchase, $20 million in dividends have been paid. 1977 earnings were $3.6 million, more than many banks two or three times its size.
- Abegg, 80, asked that a successor be brought in; Peter Jeffrey became President and CEO March 1, with Abegg staying as Chairman.
- Blue Chip Stamps: Berkshire owned about 36.5% at yearend 1977. Blue Chip earned about $12.9 million from operations plus $4.1 million in realized securities gains.
- See's Candies (Chuck Huggins), bought by Blue Chip at the start of 1972, grew pre-tax operating earnings from $4.2 million to $12.6 million with little additional capital, in an industry with practically no unit growth.
