## Why he buys stocks as businesses, not tickers

- Warren says people would be better off thinking "I bought a business today, not a stock today." That frames the decision as owning a farm or apartment house for 10, 20 or 30 years.
- The only real question is whether the 10 or 20 year outlook for American businesses changed in the last 24 to 48 hours. He says it hasn't.
- He bought his first stock on March 12, 1941, and it fell about 2% that day. He has been a net buyer of stocks every year since he was 11, under every one of 15 presidents in his lifetime (7 Republicans, 7 Democrats after Hoover).
- Holdings are long-term: American Express for 20 years, Coca-Cola for 40 years.
- If a stock drops and you like the business, he says that is "good luck basically." A price quote 2% lower on a house you like "really doesn't make any difference" unless you have to sell.
- On an 800-point Dow drop (roughly 3%): "I like to buy stocks." He can't think of a 3% decline that shouldn't have been bought. Anything Berkshire was buying the previous Friday, it would buy more eagerly today.
- The Corona virus (the Feb 2020 selloff) makes no difference to his investments. Even if global growth were down 1% instead of 0.1%, he would still buy a business he likes at a price he likes.

## Valuation discipline: write down what you are buying and why

- He says you should be able to take a yellow pad and state: "I am buying the General Motors Company for $42 billion because..." (1.4 billion shares at $30 per share, used as an illustration).
- Example: American Express has about 815 million shares at roughly $126, so about $100 billion. The question is whether the company is worth more or less than $100 billion, not what the stock does tomorrow, next week or next month.
- Market prediction is not needed and not possible: "If you can predict the market, you don't need to read balance sheets." He says nobody he has met knows what the market will do.
- He can't predict what his businesses will earn in 6 or 12 months, but is confident American business will be "fabulously better" in 20 to 30 years.
- Example of buying at the bottom: in 1932 General Motors had 19,000 dealers, and in one month sold less than a tenth of a car per dealer. That was a terrific time to buy GM.
- He watches indicators like rail car loadings but says he doesn't use them for specific investment decisions. He believes he can make money predicting 10 years out, not next week.
- Trouble is always coming (1942, 1949, 2008). In 2008 he wrote in the New York Times that trouble was coming "but I said buy stocks." He would still say buy stocks "if you get enough for your money."

## Stocks versus bonds, interest rates and reaching for yield

- Edgar Lawrence Smith's 1924 book set out to show bonds beat stocks in deflation, but concluded the opposite: with equal yields, stocks win because retained earnings build additional value. Warren says Berkshire's value comes from retained earnings, not dividends.
- Ben Graham told him "you get more trouble with a good idea than a bad idea because the good idea works." The 1920s boom took Smith's idea past its limiting condition (bonds and stocks yielding the same) as stock prices ran up five or six times.
- Three basic alternatives: short-term cash (an option to act later), long-term bonds, or long-term stocks. At the time, stocks were cheaper than bonds.
- A 30-year bond at 2% means paying 50 times earnings for earnings that can't rise for 30 years. A 10-year at about 1.4% is roughly 70 times earnings with no growth for a decade.
- About $13 trillion was at negative rates worldwide. He says even Keynes and Adam Smith never addressed it, and "we don't know what that means." Interest rates are "the basis of all value."
- Cheap rates and loose lending (weak covenants, leveraged loans) raise the price buyers will pay for whole businesses, since they can borrow a large share of the price. That is part of why whole-company purchases looked expensive to him.
- Reaching for yield is "really stupid, but it's very human." Insurers and savers who need 3% but earn 1% should "quit giving 3%" or learn to live on 1%. "You should always adapt your consumption to your income," not the reverse.
- Reaching-for-yield excess can take longer than you think (like late-1990s tech), "but eventually you get to midnight and everything turns to pumpkins and mice."
- Low rates hurt life and annuity insurers that promised returns. Property-casualty insurers don't promise returns but still earn less on float.
- He notes the federal deficit was about 4.5% in good times and that the US benefits enormously from low interest costs. At 5% on $20 trillion the interest bill would be a trillion.

## Banks, Wells Fargo and incentive systems

- Banks are a good business if they "don't do dumb things on the asset side." The commercial banks Berkshire owns earn roughly 12% to 16% on tangible assets, versus a 2% long bond. He says a 3-4% return on tangible assets isn't likely.
- Steeper yield curves matter more than the absolute level of rates. American banks earn well at low rates; European and Japanese banks earn lower returns and use more leverage, which he dislikes.
- Bank of America buys back so much stock that Berkshire's ownership rises 7 or 8% a year "without us spending a dime," plus a dividend.
- He would not comment on why Berkshire sold down Wells Fargo (partly to stay under 10% to avoid Fed filings) or on specific positions, and says Berkshire doesn't give stock advice.
- Wells Fargo lesson: "dumbest incentive system you can think"; people "will do what they're incented to do," and Berkshire has made the same mistake. The bigger failure was ignoring the problem once management knew.
- Charlie Munger's version: "an ounce of prevention is worth a ton of cure." Attack problems immediately. Shareholders ended up paying billions for phony accounts that earned them nothing.
- Craft Heinz: it earns about $5 billion pre-tax after depreciation on about $7 billion of tangible assets with no working capital, so it's a valuable business. But "we paid too much" and it carries too much debt. He thinks it can pay down debt and keep the dividend.

## Running Berkshire: decentralization, conglomerate discount and capital allocation

- Subsidiary managers run their own businesses. He hasn't been to the See's Candies factory in at least 10 years and doesn't pick the box contents. BNSF will spend about $3.5 billion on capital and he approves none of it.
- What headquarters controls: dividend policy of subsidiaries and the movement of capital between them. See's, bought in 1972, has sent several billion to other businesses. Moving capital inside one company avoids the taxes of selling one stock to buy another.
- Managers also save about 25% of their time by never dealing with Wall Street or bankers, and they "never have to finance their businesses."
- Old conglomerates had a bad name for good reason: dirty pooling, touting stock and chain-letter behavior in the late 1960s. Berkshire has never wanted to issue shares or tout them. He notes index funds are also conglomerates of 500 businesses.
- Breaking up Berkshire wouldn't create value: it would be very tax-inefficient (the General Utilities doctrine ended in 1986), and the businesses have real synergies. Berkshire also uses less leverage than a piece-by-piece buyer would, by design, since it has insurance promises out 50 or 100 years.
- Shareholder base: "you get the shareholders you deserve." Run a hamburger stand or a French restaurant, but be clear which and don't mix. There are about 1,600-odd thousand A shares and he doesn't recruit new holders.
- He expects directors to resist "Wall Streeters bearing fees." All his shares go to charity over about 15 years after his death, and he wants them kept in Berkshire.
- GEICO: Berkshire bought control in 1995 at about 2.5% market share; it reached about 13.7%, premiums rose from about $2.5 billion to $35 billion, and it is number two to State Farm. He credits Tony Nicely. BNSF paid a $5 billion dividend last year against a $35 billion purchase price.
- Precision scheduled railroading: Hunter Harrison made customers adapt to the railroad and improved margins. BNSF's margins improved but less than peers; it gained share instead, and he says Berkshire could adopt the method if needed.
- On the underperformance of Berkshire against the S&P: size is part of it. He expects Berkshire to be neither in the top 10-15% nor the bottom of stocks over the next 10 to 15 years, outperforming in down markets, with only a small edge at a $550 billion market value.

## Cash, buybacks and reserve

- Berkshire holds more than $125 billion in cash and about $240 billion in stocks; roughly 80% is in equities counting operating businesses and about 20% in cash. He would rather have that 20% in good businesses, but whole companies were expensive.
- Cash exists for the "chief job": protecting people's money. Insurance obligations (workers' comp and auto claims) run out 50 years, and "every check clears under any circumstances." Berkshire holds Treasury bills, not commercial paper, and doesn't rely on bank lines. When people get terrified, "everything freezes."
- Buybacks: Berkshire bought $5 billion last year (about 1% of market cap). Blocks are hard to buy because holders are long-term. He invited holders with roughly $20 million or more to call his office, if Berkshire is buying at that price level; only blocks, and not tire-kickers.
- Banks and Apple can buy 4-5% or more a year without moving the market; Berkshire cannot.

## Apple, airlines and what he buys

- Apple is Berkshire's third largest business (about 5.6% owned). He doesn't think of it as a stock or a tech company but as "a consumer product company that uses technology," compared to See's Candies for its pervasive usefulness. "I should have appreciated it earlier."
- Corona virus effects he noted: Dairy Queen franchises in China, Apple's supply chain, and others (Johns Manville, Shaw). He will weigh where those businesses stand in 5 to 10 years, not the quarter.
- Berkshire owns close to 10% of each of the four largest airlines, the largest in Delta (roughly 11%). Buying one outright would be unlikely due to regulation and complications with American Express's bank-holding status. Past 15% in any company, Hart-Scott-Rodino rules apply.
- Whole-business purchases looked expensive because of cheap debt, so he put money into pieces of companies via stocks.
- He doesn't invest in cryptocurrencies, which "basically have no value and they don't produce anything"; they "can't mail you a check." He doesn't own any and never will. He also doesn't buy ETFs; a pension fund Berkshire doesn't manage bought them.
- On sustainable investing, he doesn't screen on it when choosing stocks.
- Newspapers: Berkshire is financing Lee; the Times, Journal and Post each sold their smaller papers, and he stayed in.

## Boards, directors and independence

- Berkshire looks for directors who are business-savvy, have a strong personal interest in Berkshire, and have integrity. They represent shareholders.
- He has served on 21 public company boards. Many "independent" directors are paid about $300,000 a year for a few days of work, and "who wouldn't want a job like that?" Headhunters and CEOs ask who "won't cause too much trouble." "To call them independent is ridiculous," and ignoring the compensation factor is "ridiculous."
- Succession: the board knows exactly what to do if he dies tonight. Greg Abel and Ajit Jain are vice chairmen and will take questions at the annual meeting.
- Todd Combs at GEICO: Bill Roberts took over less than two years earlier and asked to retire; Todd knows insurance, and the plan is to promote from within. Insurance is the only business he knows well. Underwriting needs rates that match risk (correlating risk and rate), such as pricing 16-year-old males differently from 80-year-olds.
- Most months neither Ted Weschler nor Todd Combs changes the portfolio. In many years leaving a portfolio untouched would beat tinkering.

## Charlie, politics and taxes (brief)

- Charlie Munger is "the best partner anybody could possibly have" after 60 years, with "the best 30 second mind in the world." Charlie dislikes EBITDA as an earnings measure, and Warren dislikes GAAP net income swings from marketable securities.
- Political views: he votes mostly Democratic but not as a "card-carrying" one. He'd vote for Michael Bloomberg over Sanders. He favors a much higher earned income tax credit so full-time workers aren't left behind, but says "I don't think we should have killed the capital system in the process."
- He wouldn't cast a presidential vote based on the market's next 30-90 days.
