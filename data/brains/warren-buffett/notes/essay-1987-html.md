## Per-share business value, not book value, is what counts

- Berkshire's net worth rose $464 million, or 19.5%, in 1987. Per-share book value grew from $19.46 to $2,477.47 over 23 years, a 23.1% compounded annual rate.
- Warren says the rate of gain in per-share business value is what matters, not book value. The two can be almost unrelated: LTV and Baldwin-United published year-end book values of $652 million and $397 million just before going bankrupt, while Belridge Oil sold to Shell in 1979 for $3.6 billion against a $177 million book value.
- At Berkshire, business value has moderately outpaced book value over the last decade, because it owns remarkable businesses run by remarkable managers.
- He ignores GAAP figures and focuses on the future earning power of both controlled and non-controlled businesses. He sets his own idea of business value, independent of the accounting values on the books for controlled companies and of the prices a "sometimes foolish" market puts on partially owned ones.
- GAAP quirk: controlled companies show full earnings in the income account but are never written up on the balance sheet. The three permanent stocks are marked to market on the balance sheet but only their small dividends reach income. They were worth over $2 billion and produced only $11 million of reported earnings, versus well over $100 million of look-through earnings.

## Judging managers by returns on underlying capital

- Berkshire's seven largest non-financial units (Buffalo News, Fechheimer, Kirby, Nebraska Furniture Mart, Scott Fetzer Manufacturing Group, See's Candies, World Book) earned $180 million before interest and taxes in 1987.
- Net interest expense was only $2 million, so pre-tax earnings on equity were $178 million on historical-cost equity of just $175 million. After-tax earnings of about $100 million equal roughly a 57% return on equity.
- Benchmark: per Fortune, only six of the 1,000 largest industrial and service companies averaged over 30% ROE in the prior decade. The best was Commerce Clearing House at 40.2%.
- Managers should be judged on returns on the underlying assets, not on what Berkshire paid. Those units are carried about $222 million above their historical accounting values, but "what we pay for a business does not affect the amount of capital its manager has to work with."
- Three inferences: business value is far above book value; so little capital is needed that the units can grow while sending almost all earnings out for redeployment; and the managers are extraordinary.

## How Warren and Charlie manage managers

- With such managers, Warren and Charlie "have little to do with operations." They hold no corporate meetings, set no corporate budgets and do no performance reviews. "What can we tell the Blumkins about home furnishings, or the Heldmans about uniforms?"
- Their contribution is "informed applause," based on long careers observing business performance and managerial behavior.
- They reject flexible operating budgets. At the Buffalo News the news hole is about 50% of the paper. Cutting it to a typical 40% would save about $4 million a year in newsprint, and "that interests us not at all," even if margins shrink.
- Neither cut essential activities when profits fall nor add unneeded people (a staff economist, a corporate strategist, an institutional ad campaign) when money is rolling in. "That kind of yo-yo approach is neither business-like nor humane."
- When a unit stumbles, they call a fixer. Harry Bottle was named "Man of the Year" for turning around Dempster Mill in 1962. In 1987 Charlie made him CEO of K & W Products after it stumbled in 1985-86; profits rose over 300% and receivables and inventories fell 20%.

## Boring franchises beat exciting change

- "Severe change and exceptional returns usually don't mix." Investors give the highest P/Es to exotic businesses promising feverish change, letting them fantasize about future profitability instead of facing today's realities.
- The best returns usually come from companies doing something quite similar to what they did five or ten years ago. Constant change brings many chances for major error and makes it hard to build a fortress-like franchise.
- Fortune's test: only 25 of 1,000 companies averaged over 20% ROE from 1977 to 1986 with no year below 15%. 24 of the 25 beat the S&P 500. Most used very little leverage ("Really good businesses usually don't need to borrow"), and most sold mundane, non-sexy products, apart from one high-tech company and several ethical-drug makers.
- Berkshire's managers get extraordinary results from ordinary things done exceptionally well. They protect their franchises, control costs, look for new products and markets that build on existing strengths, and don't get diverted.
- Examples:
  - Mrs. B (Rose Blumkin), 94, started Nebraska Furniture Mart 50 years earlier with $500. NFM sold $142.6 million in 1987, up 8%, and customers saved about $30 million versus buying elsewhere. Her maxim: "Sell cheap and tell the truth."
  - Chuck Huggins at See's: volume near 25 million pounds, about 85% of profit earned in December. When 14 candy items were cut, hundreds of customers complained; Chuck restored the two badly missed ones and answered every letter honestly.
  - Ralph Schey runs 19 Scott Fetzer businesses. Pre-tax earnings rose 10% in 1987 while average capital employed fell significantly.

## Mr. Market and investing as business analysis

- When buying stocks, Warren and Charlie act as if buying into a private business. They weigh the business's economics, its managers and the price, with no set time or price to sell. "We view ourselves as business analysts - not as market analysts, not as macroeconomic analysts, and not even as security analysts."
- Ben Graham's Mr. Market: a manic-depressive partner who quotes a buy-sell price daily. Transactions are strictly at your option, so his wild swings help you. "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. It is his pocketbook, not his wisdom, that you will find useful."
- "If you aren't certain that you understand and can value your business far better than Mr. Market, you don't belong in the game."
- Success comes from good business judgment plus insulating your thoughts and behavior from market emotions, not from "arcane formulae, computer programs or signals." Graham: "In the short run, the market is a voting machine but in the long run it is a weighing machine."
- Judge holdings by operating results, not price quotes. Delayed recognition can be an advantage, giving a chance to buy more at a bargain.
- Selling: sell when the market overvalues a business, or to fund a more undervalued idea. Don't sell just because of appreciation or holding time ("You can't go broke taking a profit" is perhaps "the most foolish" Wall Street maxim).
- Hold while the business's return on equity is satisfactory, management is competent and honest, and the market does not overvalue it.
- Goal: "an outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain price." Making "silk purses out of silk" works; sow's ears fail. Warren took about 20 years to learn this, after "bargains" like short-line farm implement makers, third-place department stores and New England textiles.
- Permanent holdings, which Warren would hold even if far overpriced: Capital Cities/ABC (3,000,000 shares, cost $517.5 million, market $1,035 million), GEICO (6,850,000 shares, cost $45.7 million, market $756.9 million), Washington Post (1,727,765 shares, cost $9.7 million, market $323.1 million). He prefers a return of X with people he admires over 110% of X with people he doesn't.
- Washington Post bought in 1973 at $5.63 per share earned $10.30 per share after tax in 1987. GEICO bought at an average $6.67 earned $9.01.

## Capital allocation and control versus minority stakes

- Control's first advantage: you allocate capital. Many CEOs rose through marketing, production, engineering or politics and never learned it, "as if the final step for a highly-talented musician was not to perform at Carnegie Hall but, instead, to be named Chairman of the Federal Reserve."
- It matters because a CEO retaining earnings equal to 10% of net worth will, after ten years, have deployed more than 60% of all capital in the business.
- Staffs, consultants and investment bankers more likely accentuate the problem than solve it. Plenty of unintelligent allocation explains the amount of "restructuring."
- Second advantage is tax: as a corporate holder, a given business result can deliver Berkshire results up to 50% better from an 80%-or-greater holding than from a lesser one.
- Management changes, "like marital changes, are painful, time-consuming and chancy," so the power to effect change is somewhat illusory.
- Offsetting benefit: the market occasionally sells non-controlling pieces of extraordinary businesses at prices far below those paid in control transactions.

## Insurance: commodity economics, float and discipline

- Combined ratio below 100 means an underwriting profit. With float, a ratio of 107-111 typically produces overall break-even, excluding earnings on shareholder funds.
- Industry revenues must grow about 10% a year to hold its own, because social and judicial inflation (courtroom costs, verdicts, expanded coverage) outruns general inflation. Premiums grew 22% in 1985-86 but only 8.7% in 1987; "the party is over." Policies run one year, so earnings lag price changes.
- Insurance is a commodity business: hundreds of competitors, easy entry, an undifferentiable product. Only a low-cost operator or protected niche sustains high profits, except in shortages. Typical managers hate shortages yet are the only time commodity businesses earn good returns, and they rush to add capacity (Disraeli: "What we learn from history is that we do not learn from history.").
- Berkshire differentiates in two ways: financial strength (which matters most when buyers remember Ben Franklin's line that "it is hard for an empty sack to stand upright") and total indifference to volume. It will write five times or one-fifth as much next year, pricing on exposure, not competition.
- Three conditions allow this: market share doesn't drive profitability ("survival of the fattest" doesn't apply); distribution channels are easily entered, so low volume this year doesn't preclude high volume next; idle capacity, mostly people, isn't intolerably costly.
- A broker who lost the business on price in 1987 bought more Berkshire stock instead. Berkshire's 1987 combined ratio was 105 (103 in 1986), but profits improved because it had far more float.
- Be suspicious of insurers' reported earnings. Long-tail lines (product liability, D&O) make reserves unknowable, and the dishonest exploit this ("long-tail insurance is heaven"). Warren proposes auditor opinion wording admitting they can't judge loss reserves.
- Tax changes hurt: the Tax Reform Act of 1986 and the 1987 Bill (dividends-received credit cut from 80% to 70%) penalize insurers owning stocks indirectly.

## Market turmoil, bonds, arbitrage, debt and acquisition criteria

- The Dow rose just 2.3% in 1987 despite a manic run and October crash. Warren blames professionals focusing on "what they expect other money managers to do in the days ahead." Portfolio insurance, with $60-90 billion poised on a hair trigger, is "an exotically-labeled version" of a stop-loss order. He compares it to selling a farm piece by piece when a neighbor sells lower.
- Volatility helps the true investor who sticks to his "investment knitting", unless forced by financial or psychological pressure to sell at bad times. Berkshire found little to buy and held no non-permanent, non-arbitrage stock position over $50 million.
- Bonds: about $900 million in medium-term tax-exempts as a parking place; aversion to long-term bonds because "bonds are no better than the currency in which they are denominated." He fears the trade deficit will pressure the US to inflate, though timing is unpredictable. Exception: Washington Public Power Supply Systems bonds, bought where he saw a specific edge (amortized cost $240 million, market $316 million, $34 million tax-exempt income).
- Arbitrage: few large, publicly announced deals, no greenmail situations; estimated average pre-tax returns of at least 25%, with bad experiences able to change that. Also bought Texaco bonds after bankruptcy (carried at $104 million, market $119 million) and $700 million of Salomon 9% convertible preferred (convertible at $38 after three years), based on trust in John Gutfreund.
- Debt: Berkshire wants to meet obligations with certainty and seeks "acceptable long-term results under extraordinarily adverse conditions, rather than optimal results under a normal range of conditions." Leverage risks what is important for returns that are unimportant.
- It finances in anticipation of need, since tight money creates the best acquisition chances. It raised $250 million of debentures at slightly over 10%, earning about 6.5% on the proceeds, costing about $160,000 per week. "If you want to shoot rare, fast-moving elephants, you should always carry a loaded gun."
- Warren never comments on rumors, true or false, since denying some and refusing comment on others would comment on all.
- Acquisition criteria:
  - Large purchases (at least $10 million of after-tax earnings)
  - Demonstrated consistent earning power (no projections or turnarounds)
  - Good ROE with little or no debt
  - Management in place
  - Simple businesses ("if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it")
  - An offering price
- No unfriendly takeovers. Complete confidentiality, and an answer "customarily within five minutes." Cash preferred, stock only when Berkshire gets as much intrinsic value as it gives.
