## How Warren views his investing record and succession at Berkshire (2011)

- Warren measures himself by the per-share rate of gain in intrinsic business value versus the S&P 500: "If it doesn't, we are overpaid at any price."
- Per-share book value rose 4.6% in 2011. Since 1964 it has grown from $19 to $99,860, a 19.8% compounded annual rate.
- Book value is a useful but "considerably understated" proxy for intrinsic value. It tracks business value roughly because the gap between the two does not swing wildly year to year.
- Book value should beat the S&P in a bad market year and lag in a strong one. Berkshire beat the S&P in all 42 five-year periods from 1965 on, and the 2007-11 period held. He expects the streak to snap if the S&P strings together five good years.
- Operating earnings matter most. Per-share investments rose 4% to $98,366, and pre-tax earnings outside insurance and investments rose 18% to $6,990 per share.
- Succession: the board's main job is to make sure the right people run the business and the next generation is identified. Warren says Berkshire's directors are the best he has seen on succession among the 19 boards he has served on.
- Todd Combs and Ted Weschler each handle a few billion dollars and have the "brains, judgment and character" to run the whole portfolio later. Each gets 80% of performance pay from his own results and 20% from his partner's.
- More than 98% of Warren's net worth is in Berkshire stock, all earmarked for philanthropy. He is comfortable with that concentration given the quality and diversity of the businesses.
- Warren's 2011 mistakes:
  - About $2 billion of Energy Future Holdings bonds, written down $1 billion in 2010 and $390 million in 2011. He "totally miscalculated the gain/loss probabilities," calling it "a major unforced error."
  - He predicted a housing recovery within a year and was "dead wrong."
  - He bought some small businesses that have poor returns. Charlie told him long ago, "If something's not worth doing at all, it's not worth doing well."

## Share repurchases and why Warren wants low stock prices

- Warren favors repurchases only when two conditions hold:
  - The company has ample funds for operating and liquidity needs.
  - The stock trades at a material discount to conservatively calculated intrinsic value.
- Offsetting dilution or having excess cash is not a justification. "Continuing shareholders are hurt unless shares are purchased below intrinsic value."
- "The first law of capital allocation – whether the money is slated for acquisitions or share repurchases – is that what is smart at one price is dumb at another." He recommends Jamie Dimon's J.P. Morgan letters for their focus on price versus value.
- Berkshire announced a buyback up to 110% of book value in September 2011 and bought only $67 million before the price moved above the limit. Warren says it will buy aggressively at or below that limit.
  - It will not "support" the stock, and bids fade in weak markets.
  - It will not buy if cash equivalents are below $20 billion.
  - He wants exiting partners fully informed of the value of what they sell.
- Buying a stock worth at least x for .9x or .8x is a sure way to make money for continuing holders. A director likens it to "shooting fish in a barrel, after the barrel has been drained."
- A net buyer of stock, directly or through an investee that repurchases shares, wants prices low. Warren compares those who cheer rising prices to a commuter who rejoices when gas rises because his tank holds a day's supply.
- IBM example:
  - IBM has 1.16 billion shares outstanding, of which Berkshire owns 63.9 million (5.5%). IBM will likely spend about $50 billion on buybacks over five years.
  - At an average price of $200, IBM retires 250 million shares, leaving 910 million, and Berkshire owns about 7%.
  - At an average price of $300, IBM retires 167 million shares, leaving about 990 million, and Berkshire owns 6.5%.
  - On $20 billion of fifth-year earnings, Berkshire's share is $100 million larger under the low-price scenario, and its stake might be worth about $1.5 billion more later.
- Warren says he once rejoiced when the market rose, until he read Chapter Eight of Graham's *The Intelligent Investor* on price fluctuations. "Immediately the scales fell from my eyes, and low prices became my friend."
- He admires IBM's financial management under Gerstner and Palmisano: debt used wisely, acquisitions made almost exclusively for cash, and aggressive buybacks.

## The big holdings as partnership interests in wonderful businesses

- Warren views holdings such as American Express, Coca-Cola, IBM and Wells Fargo as partnership interests in wonderful businesses, not securities to trade on near-term prospects.
- Ownership stakes at year-end 2011: American Express 13.0%, Coca-Cola 8.8%, IBM 5.5%, Wells Fargo 7.6%.
- Only dividends show in Berkshire's income statement. Had Berkshire held the 2011 positions all year:
  - Dividends from the "Big Four" would have been $862 million.
  - Berkshire's share of their earnings would have been $3.3 billion.
  - He believes the $2.4 billion not reported creates at least that much value by fueling future earnings gains and repurchases.
- He expects a decade from now the four could earn $7 billion attributable to Berkshire, with $2 billion in dividends.
- 2011 deals:
  - IBM: 63.9 million shares costing $10.9 billion.
  - Bank of America: $5 billion of 6% preferred with warrants to buy 700 million common shares at $7.14 before September 2, 2021.
  - He added $1 billion to Wells Fargo.
- On IBM he was "late to the party," as he was with Coca-Cola in 1988 and railroads in 2006. He had read IBM's annual report for over 50 years, but his thinking "crystallized" on a Saturday in March 2011. He quotes Thoreau: "It's not what you look at that matters, it's what you see."
- Bank of America: prior management made huge mistakes, but Brian Moynihan is cleaning them up and nurturing a large, attractive underlying business.
- Wells Fargo: earnings strong, assets solid, capital at record levels.
- Derivatives: Berkshire will not start major new positions because it shuns any contract that could require instant collateral posting. That conflicts with "redundant liquidity and unquestioned financial strength."
- The high-yield index default contracts brought in $3.4 billion of premiums against $2.6 billion paid, with about $2 billion of average float over their five years. Warren credits getting a premium commensurate with the risk.

## Insurance float and the four insurance disciplines

- Property-casualty insurers collect premiums now and pay claims later. The money held in between is "float," which Berkshire invests for its own benefit.
- Float history ($ millions): 1970: 39; 1980: 237; 1990: 1,632; 2000: 27,871; 2010: 65,832; 2011: 70,571. He doesn't expect it to grow much from here.
- If premiums exceed expenses and eventual losses, there is an underwriting profit and the float costs less than nothing.
- Berkshire had nine straight years of underwriting profit totaling about $17 billion.
- The industry usually loses on underwriting because of intense competition. State Farm lost on underwriting in eight of eleven years, and industry return on tangible equity has long trailed American industry.
- Valuation of float:
  - Book value deducts float in full as a liability, as if it had to be paid tomorrow. Warren says it should be viewed as a revolving fund, so true value is far lower than the accounting liability.
  - The $15.5 billion of insurance goodwill is the price paid for float-generating ability. Its value depends on sustained underwriting profit, not on cost.
  - This is a huge reason intrinsic value substantially exceeds book value.
- Four disciplines of insurance (General Re, under Tad Montross, follows them):
  1. Understand all exposures that might cause a policy to incur losses.
  2. Conservatively evaluate the likelihood of a loss and its probable cost.
  3. Set a premium that delivers a profit on average after loss costs and expenses.
  4. Be willing to walk away if the right premium can't be obtained.
- Many insurers pass the first three and fail the fourth. "The other guy is doing it so we must as well" spells trouble, especially in insurance. He illustrates independence with the joke about Albert, who says of the wrong-way car on the highway, "It's not just one car, there are hundreds of them."
- Risk posture: if the industry suffered a $250 billion mega-catastrophe loss (about triple anything it has faced), Berkshire would likely still record a moderate profit.
- Units:
  - BH Reinsurance (Ajit Jain): insures risks others lack the desire or capital to take. Started in 1985, it now has $34 billion of float. 2011 underwriting was a $714 million loss, and Charlie "would gladly trade me for a second Ajit."
  - General Re: float $19.7 billion, underwriting profit $144 million.
  - GEICO (Tony Nicely, 50 years of service): market share rose from 2.0% to 9.3% in 18 years. Premiums were $15.4 billion in 2011 versus $3.3 billion had share stayed flat. Low costs permit low prices.
  - Other primary insurers had $242 million of underwriting profit. Princeton Insurance, a med-mal writer, was acquired at yearend with over $600 million of float.
  - Total 2011 underwriting profit was $248 million, and total float $70.6 billion.

## Regulated capital-intensive businesses and the operating companies

- BNSF and MidAmerican Energy hold long-lived regulated assets funded partly by long-term debt not guaranteed by Berkshire. Earnings cover interest amply even in terrible conditions (BNSF interest coverage was 9.5x in 2011).
- BNSF carries about 15% of all U.S. inter-city freight ton-miles and is the largest "artery" of the economy's "circulatory system." It maintains 23,000 miles of track, 13,000 bridges and 80 tunnels. It invested $1.8 billion more than depreciation in 2011. BNSF 2011 revenue was $19.5 billion and net earnings $2.97 billion.
- Warren frames a "social compact": he quotes Franklin, "Keep thy shop, and thy shop will keep thee," and restates it as "Take care of your customer, and the regulator – your customer's representative – will take care of you."
- MidAmerican (89.8% owned) serves 2.5 million customers. It retains all earnings, unlike utilities that pay out most, and so funds about $6 billion in wind investment (3,316 MW by end of 2012) plus about $3 billion of solar projects. Net earnings were $1,331 million in 2011.
- Turnarounds: Northern Natural Gas was ranked last (43 of 43) when bought in 2002 and was second most recently.
- Goodwill of $20 billion is carried for BNSF and MidAmerican, but Warren thinks intrinsic value is far greater than book value.
- Non-insurance operating group: 2011 revenues were $72.4 billion and pre-tax earnings $5.04 billion. Returns on unleveraged net tangible assets range from 25% after-tax to over 100% at the best businesses, and 12-20% at good ones, with a few poor.
- Capital allocation mistakes he made: he "misjudged either the competitive strength of the business being purchased or the future economics of the industry."
  - He tries to look ahead ten or twenty years when acquiring a business, but "sometimes my eyesight has been poor."
  - He holds poor performers because of a commitment to sellers that Berkshire will keep their businesses "through thick and thin." He believes the goodwill this builds among prospective sellers offsets the cost. He will act promptly if a business is a long-term cash drain or labor strife is endemic.
- Housing-related companies earned $513 million pre-tax in 2011 versus $1.8 billion in 2006, and employment fell from 58,769 to 43,315. Warren expects a housing recovery because household formation now exceeds housing starts (about 600,000 annually) and sees unemployment dropping when it returns.
- Manager and business examples:
  - See's Candy: bought for $25 million, earned a record $83 million pre-tax in 2011, with $1.65 billion cumulative since purchase. Warren cites "buy commodities, sell brands" as a long-standing formula (Coca-Cola, Wrigley, See's).
  - Nebraska Furniture Mart: bought a 433-acre tract north of Dallas for what should be the highest-volume home-furnishings store in the country. The Omaha and Kansas City stores each exceeded $400 million of sales.
  - McLane: bought for $1.5 billion in 2003, with $2.4 billion of pre-tax earnings since.
  - NetJets: pre-tax earnings of $227 million after being his "number one worry" a few years earlier. Its focus on safety and service widens its "moat."
  - CTB: bought for $139 million in 2002, distributed $180 million, and earned $124 million pre-tax in 2011.
  - Iscar, Marmon (bolt-on acquisitions) and TTI also set records.
  - Lubrizol, bought September 16, 2011: CEO James Hambrick lifted pre-tax profits from $147 million to $1,085 million since 2004. Three bolt-ons cost $493 million.
- Berkshire's five largest non-insurance companies (BNSF, Iscar, Lubrizol, Marmon, MidAmerican) earned over $9 billion pre-tax in 2011 versus MidAmerican's $393 million seven years earlier. They should again set records in 2012, comfortably topping $10 billion if the economy doesn't weaken.
- Operating companies spent $8.2 billion on property, plant and equipment, about 95% in the U.S.
- Warren notes the Marmon accounting oddity (a $614 million GAAP write-off with no connection to economic reality), which widens the gap between intrinsic value and carrying value.
- Clayton Homes has stayed profitable through the housing depression by sticking to old-fashioned loan policies: "meaningful down payments and monthly payments with a sensible relationship to regular income."

## The three kinds of investments and why productive assets win

- Investing, as Berkshire defines it, is "the transfer to others of purchasing power now with the reasoned expectation of receiving more purchasing power – after taxes have been paid on nominal gains – in the future." More simply, it is forgoing consumption now to consume more later.
- Risk is not beta. It is "the reasoned probability of that investment causing its owner a loss of purchasing-power over his contemplated holding period." Assets can fluctuate widely without being risky, and a non-fluctuating asset can be laden with risk.
- Category 1: currency-based investments (money funds, bonds, mortgages, deposits)
  - Seen as safe, but "among the most dangerous of assets." Beta may be zero but risk is huge.
  - The dollar lost 86% of its value from 1965 to 2011, so $7 buys what $1 did. A tax-free institution needed 4.3% a year just to keep purchasing power.
  - For a taxpayer, rolling T-bills returned 5.7% a year over 47 years. At a 25% average tax rate, income tax took 1.4 points and the "invisible inflation tax" took the other 4.3 points, so real income was nothing.
  - "Right now bonds should come with a warning label," and he quotes Shelby Cullom Davis: "Bonds promoted as offering risk-free returns are now priced to deliver return-free risk."
  - Berkshire still holds T-bills for liquidity: a working level of $20 billion, absolute minimum of $10 billion. Unquestioned financial strength comes first.
- Category 2: assets that never produce anything (gold)
  - Value rests on an expanding pool of buyers who believe others will pay more later. Warren notes tulips in the 17th century, Internet stocks and houses. "What the wise man does in the beginning, the fool does in the end."
  - World gold stock is about 170,000 metric tons, a cube about 68 feet per side. At $1,750 an ounce it is worth $9.6 trillion ("pile A").
  - Pile B at the same cost: all 400 million acres of U.S. cropland (about $200 billion annual output), 16 Exxon Mobils (each earning over $40 billion a year), and about $1 trillion left over. "Can you imagine an investor with $9.6 trillion selecting pile A over pile B?"
  - "If you own one ounce of gold for an eternity, you will still own one ounce at its end."
- Category 3: productive assets (businesses, farms, real estate), which he prefers
  - Ideal assets deliver output that holds purchasing power in inflation while needing little new capital. Coca-Cola, IBM and See's Candy pass this double-barreled test.
  - Regulated utilities fail it because inflation forces owners to invest more to earn more, but they still beat nonproductive or currency-based assets.
  - He compares businesses to "cows" that give ever more "milk." Their value comes from capacity to deliver milk, not the medium of exchange. The Dow rose from 66 to 11,497 in the 20th century, plus dividends.
  - The first two categories peak in popularity at maximum fear. "Cash is king" was heard in late 2008, just when cash should have been deployed. "Cash is trash" was heard in the early 1980s, just when fixed-dollar investments were most attractive. "Investors who required a supportive crowd paid dearly for that comfort."
  - Berkshire's goal is to own more first-class businesses, preferably entire companies, otherwise via marketable stocks. Warren believes this category will be the runaway winner and by far the safest.
