## Charlie Munger and the shift from Graham to "wonderful businesses"

- Charlie Munger died November 28, 2023, 33 days before his 100th birthday. Warren first met him in 1959.
- In 1965, Charlie told Warren: "forget about ever buying another company like Berkshire. But now that you control Berkshire, add to it wonderful businesses purchased at fair prices and give up buying fair businesses at wonderful prices."
- Charlie also said: "abandon everything you learned from your hero, Ben Graham. It works but only when practiced at small scale."
- Warren followed this "with much back-sliding"; Charlie later partnered with him and "jerked me back to sanity when my old habits surfaced."
- Warren calls Charlie the "architect" of present Berkshire and himself the "general contractor" who carried out the day-by-day construction.
- Charlie never took credit and never reminded Warren of his mistakes.

## Who Berkshire writes for, and what "earnings" should mean

- Berkshire targets "lifetime" shareholders: investors who save to buy a farm or rental property, not those who buy lottery tickets or "hot" stocks.
- Warren pictures his sister Bertie as the reader. She is sensible, understands incentives and human weakness, and knows pundits should be ignored: "if she could reliably predict tomorrow's winners, would she freely share her valuable insights?"
- Shareholders are owed good and bad news directly from the CEO, not from an investor-relations officer serving "optimism and syrupy mush."
- GAAP net earnings were $90 billion (2021), ($23 billion) (2022) and $96 billion (2023). Warren calls this "worse-than-useless."
- The cause is the 2018 mandate to include unrealized capital gains and losses, which can exceed $5 billion a day.
- Berkshire prefers operating earnings, which exclude them: $27.6 billion (2021), $30.9 billion (2022), $37.4 billion (2023).
- Warren still expects capital gains to be a very important part of value accretion over decades. He says it is "more than silly" to judge investment value on earnings that include short-term market swings.
- Graham's line: "In the short run the market acts as a voting machine; in the long run it becomes a weighing machine."
- He has held a majority of his net worth in U.S. equities since his first stock purchase on March 11, 1942, when the Dow was below 100 (now about 38,000). Investors "need only sit quietly, listening to no one."

## What Berkshire buys, and why size now limits it

- The goal: own all or part of businesses with "good economics that are fundamental and enduring."
- Warren particularly favors the rare enterprise that can deploy additional capital at high returns in the future. Owning one and sitting tight can deliver wealth "almost beyond measure."
- Predicting winners is harder than it looks; those who claim to know are "self-delusional or snake-oil salesmen."
- He wants able, trustworthy managers but admits this judgment is hard. Quoting Hugh McCulloch (1863): "Never deal with a rascal under the expectation that you can prevent him from cheating you." Sincerity and empathy can be faked.
- Berkshire's GAAP net worth is $561 billion, versus $8.9 trillion for the other 499 S&P companies in 2022. That is nearly 6% of its universe.
- Doubling within five years is not possible, partly because Berkshire is highly averse to issuing shares.
- Only a handful of U.S. companies can move the needle, and they are picked over. Outside the U.S. there are essentially no meaningful candidates. "We have no possibility of eye-popping performance."
- Realistic aim: do a bit better than the average American corporation with materially less risk of permanent capital loss.
- Allegiance is to the country and shareholders: "your money is comingled with ours, it does not belong to us."

## Fortress balance sheet and the "not-so-secret weapon"

- Markets can seize up unpredictably (four months in 1914, a few days in 2001, September 2008). Berkshire can respond with huge sums and certainty of performance.
- Today's market participants are no more stable than before; markets show "far more casino-like behavior," and "the casino now resides in many homes."
- Wall Street's juices flow from feverish activity; "whatever foolishness can be marketed will be vigorously marketed."
- The rule that will not change: "Never risk permanent loss of capital." A couple of good decisions in a lifetime plus avoiding serious mistakes is enough, given the American tailwind and compounding.
- Berkshire pays no dividend, repurchases are 100% discretionary, annual debt maturities are never material, and it holds cash and T-bills far above conventional need.
- It relied on no commercial paper, bank lines or debt markets in 2008. "EBITDA" is a banned measurement; earnings are judged after interest, taxes and depreciation.
- Extreme conservatism is likely unneeded in most decades, like insurance on a fireproof-seeming building. Quotational shrinkage can't be avoided, but permanent damage can.
- "Berkshire is built to last."

## Patience in Coke, AmEx, Occidental and Japan

- Coca-Cola (1886) and American Express (1850) are each 4-5% of GAAP net worth. Both failed at unrelated expansion but succeeded in the base business, and their products "traveled" worldwide.
- Berkshire has not bought or sold either in over two decades. Its share of 2023 AmEx earnings exceeded the $1.3 billion cost. AmEx's dividend is expected up about 16% in 2024.
- Lesson: "When you find a truly wonderful business, stick with it. Patience pays, and one wonderful business can offset the many mediocre decisions."
- Buybacks raise ownership in everything Berkshire owns, but "All stock repurchases should be price-dependent. What is sensible at a discount to business-value becomes stupid if done at a premium."
- Occidental: 27.8% owned plus warrants giving the option to raise ownership at a fixed price for over five years. No interest in buying or managing it. Warren values its U.S. oil and gas holdings and says Vicki Hollub "knows how to separate oil from rock."
- U.S. output went from 8 million BOEPD (1975) to 5 million (2007) to over 13 million today after shale became feasible in 2011.
- Japan: stakes of about 9% in Itochu, Marubeni, Mitsubishi, Mitsui and Sumitomo, with a pledge not to exceed 9.9%. Purchases began July 4, 2019. Cost ¥1.6 trillion; yearend value ¥2.9 trillion; unrealized gain 61% or $8 billion in dollars.
- Berkshire financed most of it with ¥1.3 trillion of yen bonds, since neither he nor Greg Abel can forecast currencies. The yen gain was $1.9 billion.
- Warren likes that all five cut share counts at attractive prices, pay modest executive compensation, pay out only about 1/3 of earnings as dividends, and are reluctant to issue shares.
- Building positions at Berkshire's size is "like turning a battleship."

## BNSF and BHE: candid mistakes in capital-intensive businesses

- BNSF: 23,759 miles of main track, carried at $70 billion; Warren guesses replication would cost at least $500 billion. Capital spending above depreciation since the purchase 14 years ago totals $22 billion or more, over $1.5 billion a year.
- That gap means dividends to Berkshire fall short of reported earnings unless debt rises, which Berkshire won't do. Hence the return is acceptable but "a pittance on the replacement value," which is why BNSF was bought at a small fraction of replacement value in 2010.
- 2023 earnings fell more than expected as revenue dropped and Washington-set wage increases outran inflation goals. Margins have slipped relative to the five other major railroads, though he thinks the territory is second to none.
- BHE: Warren erred in expectations. Regulatory climate in a few states raises "the specter of zero profitability or even bankruptcy" (California's largest utility, Hawaii). Wildfire losses are estimated, final tally years away.
- The century-old pact of a fixed return on equity has been broken in some states. He calls failing to anticipate this "a costly mistake," shared with BHE's two partners.
- "Berkshire can sustain financial surprises but we will not knowingly throw good money after bad."

## Insurance float and underwriting discipline

- 2023 insurance underwriting earned $5.4 billion (vs. a $30 million loss) and insurance investment income $9.6 billion (vs. $6.5 billion). Records were set in sales, float and underwriting profit.
- P/C insurance is the core of Berkshire's well-being. Volume grew nearly 5,000-fold in 57 years, from $17 million to $83 billion.
- Key lesson: underwriters can be any type, "But they can't be optimists at the office." Surprises, even decades after short policies expire, are almost always negative.
- Inflation, monetary and "legal," is a wild card; charlatans make detection slow and costly.
- Ajit Jain, who joined in 1986, built the operation; before that Warren was "wandering in the wilderness." GEICO, from 1951, was an "unbelievably wonderful" experience.
- T-bill income finally paid well in 2023, boosting investment income, and P/C prices strengthened.

## The "Omaha Effect" and Bertie's example

- Greg Abel (non-insurance, ready to be CEO "tomorrow"), Ajit Jain and Charlie Munger all had Omaha ties; Berkshire itself moved to Omaha in 1970.
- Bertie was active (bonds, one-third in a mutual fund, frequent trading) for 20 years from 1956. In 1980, aged 46, she kept only the mutual fund and Berkshire and made no new trades for 43 years.
- She became very rich even after giving nine-figure philanthropic gifts, using only common sense. Warren's message: sit still with good holdings.
