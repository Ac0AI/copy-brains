## Intrinsic value, acquisitions and what Berkshire buys

- Warren writes the letter to give shareholders what they need to estimate intrinsic value. He calls the calculation "all-important" but "necessarily imprecise and often seriously wrong," and the more uncertain a business's future, the more likely the estimate is "wildly off-base."
- Berkshire is easier to value than most companies: relatively stable earnings streams, great liquidity, minimum debt.
- By 2005 Berkshire owned 68 distinct businesses, so consolidated statements no longer suffice. He groups them into four clusters (insurance, regulated utilities, finance, manufacturing/service/retailing) and asks whether Berkshire ownership adds value over owning the 68 directly. Shareholders must answer that themselves.
- Two value measures, per share (A shares):
  - Investments: $4 (1965) to $74,129 (2005), 28.0% compounded.
  - Pre-tax operating earnings: $4 (1965) to $2,441 (2005), 17.2% compounded (30.2% over 1995-2005).
- Be suspicious of growth rates: check why the start and end years were picked. A poor base year gives a "breathtaking, but meaningless" rate. 1965 was an abnormally good base year, so the 17.2% is not flattered.
- Book value rose from $19 to $59,377 over 41 years, 21.5% compounded; 2005 gain was $5.6 billion, or 6.4% per share.

## Acquisitions, stock issuance and "buy to keep"

- Existing businesses will produce only "modest growth"; satisfactory gains need major acquisitions.
- None of the 2005 deals involved issuing Berkshire shares. When a management buys a company with stock, its shareholders "are concurrently selling part of their interest in everything they own." He did this a few times and "on balance, my actions have cost you money."
- Berkshire has no "exit strategy": "We buy to keep." It has an "entrance strategy" of six criteria plus a price giving a reasonable return.
- 2005 deals:
  - Medical Protective (106-year-old malpractice insurer, bought June 30). Malpractice is a graveyard for many insurers, but Berkshire's underwriting discipline and financial strength help.
  - Forest River (RVs, $1.6 billion sales, 60 plants, 5,400 employees). A two-page fax arrived June 21, he made an offer the same afternoon he got figures, and they shook hands June 28. Owner Pete Liegl, 61, once sold to an LBO operator who meddled, left, watched the firm go bankrupt, and bought it back. Warren says he won't tell Pete how to run it.
  - Business Wire. President Cathy Baron Tamraz wrote after reading a WSJ piece; Warren liked "We run a tight ship... No secretaries or management layers here. Yet we'll invest big dollars to gain a technological advantage." Founder Lorry Lokey, 78, serves 25,000 clients in 150 countries.
  - Applied Underwriters (81% bought; payroll plus workers' comp). Founders Sid Ferenc and Steve Menzies keep 19%; 400 of 479 employees are in Omaha.
  - PacifiCorp via MidAmerican: Berkshire buys $3.4 billion of MidAmerican stock, supplemented by $1.7 billion of borrowing. Regulated utilities offer "the opportunity to deploy large sums at fair returns," not outsized profits.
- Bolt-ons also continue, such as Shaw spending about $550 million on two purchases.

## Insurance float, underwriting discipline and catastrophe risk

- Float is money Berkshire holds but doesn't own, arising from premiums paid up front and claims that take years to be reported and settled. It grew from $20 million (1967) to $49 billion.
- Float is "wonderful" only if cheap. Its cost is the underwriting result. Berkshire earned an underwriting profit in about half of 39 years, making float "better than free." The industry almost always runs an underwriting loss, which can make float "devastatingly" expensive.
- 2005 float was costless despite $3.4 billion of hurricane losses (Katrina about $2.5 billion; Rita and Wilma about $0.9 billion), thanks to GEICO and other units. Underwriting profit was $53 million versus $1,551 million in 2004; float rose from $46.1 billion to $49.3 billion.
- Mega-cat stance: because nobody knows whether storm frequency or intensity has changed, he follows Pascal's wager logic. Write mega-cat only at much higher prices and with aggregate exposure that wouldn't cause distress if storms get worse. Cut back in 2004 when prices didn't move. Berkshire keeps the ability and appetite to be the world's largest writer if prices are right.
- Reserving lesson: "Surprises in insurance are far from symmetrical. You are lucky if you get one that is pleasant for every ten that go the other way." Insurers react to looming losses with optimism. He added about $125 million to MedPro's reserves right after buying it.
- "Underwriting discipline trumps all other goals" is the attitude shared by all Berkshire insurers. Financial strength is itself a selling point, since doctors want claims that take years to settle not to boomerang if the insurer fails.
- Other primary insurers earned a $324 million underwriting profit on $1,270 million of volume.

## GEICO and low-cost compounding

- GEICO improved productivity 32% in two years: employment fell 4% while policy count grew 26%. Lower unit costs let it offer more value to customers.
- Policies in force grew 12.1%; U.S. private-passenger auto share rose from about 5.6% to 6.1%, each share point being $1.6 billion of sales. Underwriting profit was $1,221 million.
- Advertising went from $31 million (1996) to $502 million (2005), and he can't wait to spend more. It works because "more people can save money by insuring with us" than with any other national carrier, and quote-to-sale conversion hit a record.
- The simple formula (low costs, low prices, steady gains in profitable business) impressed him 55 years earlier; at 21 he wrote about it as "The Security I Like Best" when its market value was $7 million.
- Brand strength is not quantifiable but grew significantly.

## Moat-widening, management and CEO pay

- Every day each business's competitive position weakens or strengthens. Delighting customers, eliminating unnecessary costs and improving products and services builds strength; indifference and bloat erode it. Daily effects are imperceptible, cumulative effects "enormous."
- "Widening the moat" is essential. When short-term and long-term conflict, widening the moat must take precedence. Bad decisions made to hit earnings targets leave damage no later brilliance can fix; auto and airline managers inherit this.
- Berkshire's managers are passionate and usually ran their businesses before Berkshire arrived; Warren's job is to "stay out of the way."
- "It's hard to overemphasize the importance of who is CEO." Jim Kilts transformed Gillette after capital-allocation blunders (the Duracell acquisition cost shareholders billions without showing in accounting); Warren says he earned every penny of his pay. It's difficult to overpay a truly extraordinary CEO of a giant enterprise, but "this species is rare."
- The yardstick ignored in acquisitions: whether business value received equals what was given up.
- Fixed-price options: a CEO with ten-year fixed-price options is rewarded for skipping dividends and buying back stock or wasting retained earnings. Fred Futile example: $1 billion earnings on $10 billion of net worth, 100 million shares, p/e of 10, all earnings used for buybacks gives 158% appreciation and about $158 million to the CEO with no business improvement. Retained earnings deployed at a 5% return still pay him $63 million.
- Remedy: options with strike prices adjusted for retained earnings, which boards almost never issue.
- Comp committees ratchet up pay through comparative data ("all the other kids have one"). Hank Greenberg's rule for a .246 hitter asking for a bonus: "Ask for a uniform."

## Friction costs, the Gotrocks family and Dow math

- Owners in aggregate can earn only what their businesses earn, less frictional costs. One investor selling high means another buys high.
- Gotrocks parable: a family owning all American business earns about $700 billion a year. Brokers, then managers, consultants, and finally hedge/private-equity "hyper-Helpers" take slices. Frictional costs may reach about 20% of corporate earnings, so equity investors might earn only about 80% of what they would by doing nothing.
- Contingent-fee deals (heads the Helper takes winnings; tails the family loses and pays fixed fees) are especially costly.
- "Fourth Law of Motion: For investors as a whole, returns decrease as motion increases."
- The Dow went from 65.73 to 11,497.12 in the 20th century, 5.3% compounded; matching that would require about 2,011,011 by 2099.
- Portfolio outlook: expect "no miracles." Holdings (American Express, Coca-Cola, P&G, Wells Fargo, Moody's, Anheuser-Busch, Wal-Mart, etc.) aren't bargains; they may double in ten years as per-share earnings grow 6-8% a year and stock prices match.

## Derivatives, debt, currency and acting promptly

- Gen Re Securities: losses of $104 million in 2005 and $404 million cumulative while unwinding. Contracts fell from 23,218 to 741. One had a term of 100 years.
- Derivative danger: long or multi-variable contracts are hard to mark to market, invite "imagination" in valuation, and let two firms each book profit on the same trade, with compensation flowing from assumed numbers. Total derivatives are a multiple of 1998 levels; Berkshire is "a canary in this business coal mine." The time to fix levees was before Katrina.
- His mistake: he wasted years trying to sell the unit instead of closing it. "When a problem exists, whether in personnel or in business operations, the time to act is now." Charlie calls the dithering "thumb-sucking."
- Debt policy: Berkshire shuns debt except short-term repos on liquid government securities, borrowing against understood receivables (Clayton's manufactured-home loans, with a one-point markup; he prefers "better profits to better cosmetics" over securitizing), and MidAmerican's non-guaranteed utility debt, which is safe because of diversified, stable earnings.
- Why conservative: investors, directors and long-term insurance claimants depend on Berkshire, and he promised "total security, whatever comes." "A long string of impressive numbers multiplied by a single zero always equals zero."
- Currency bet: long-term positions are marked to market daily, costing $955 million pre-tax in 2005 (though $2.0 billion ahead overall). Rising U.S. rates create negative carry, so he favors foreign-currency-exposed equities. He sees the U.S. current-account deficit worsening and warns that the problem will address Americans "in an unpleasant way" if they don't address it first.

## Operating businesses, succession and temperament

- Non-insurance group: $46.9 billion revenue, $2.6 billion pre-tax earnings, 22.2% on average tangible net worth with little leverage; only 10.1% on carrying value because of goodwill from premium purchase prices.
- Examples: Fruit of the Loom's men's and boys' underwear share rose from 44.2% to 48.7% of mass-market sales. R. C. Willey's never-on-Sunday approach beat seven-day competitors (Boise up 21% after 10%). See's: Chuck Huggins' 34-year tenure produced more than tenfold profit growth in a slow industry.
- Admitted misses: NetJets U.S. fell far into the red and he was "dead wrong" saying it would earn money in 2005. Managed commitment to service and safety stays.
- Succession: operations "won't miss a beat" if he dies; three capable younger CEO candidates exist and the board has unanimously chosen a successor. Someone else will handle marketable securities, as at GEICO for 26 years. The board must be willing to tell him if he declines, and he wants them to, since all his shares go to philanthropy.
- Lucky-in-America mindset: he credits birth in America, good parents and a "business gene" and tap-dances to work.
