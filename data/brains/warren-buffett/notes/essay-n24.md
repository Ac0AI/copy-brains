## 1962 results and the case for a relative yardstick

- Warren measures the partnership against the Dow, not against zero. A year of -15% with the Dow at -25% is "much superior" to a year when both the partnership and the Dow advance 20%.
- First half of 1962: the Dow fell from 731.14 to 561.28, about -21.7% with roughly $11.00 of dividends. The partnership was -7.5%, a 14.2 point edge.
- Target: about a 1/2% decline for each 1% decline in the Dow. He calls that "a considerably more conservative vehicle for investment in stocks than practically any alternative."
- Full-year 1962: the Dow returned -7.6% and the partnership +13.9% (limited partners +11.9%).
- He warns that the 1962 edge was "distinctly abnormal." It came mostly from holding controlled assets and work-outs while the Dow fell. Had the Dow soared, "we would have been low man on the totem pole."
- His expectation: "much smaller advantages on average over the Dow."

## The Ground Rules (January 1963)

- No rate of return is guaranteed. Monthly withdrawals of one-half of 1% are just withdrawals; if earnings run under 6%, they are partly a return of capital.
- Gains and losses are market values, which may bear little relation to taxable realized results.
- Good or bad is measured against the Dow and leading investment companies, not by whether the year is plus or minus. "If we do poorer, we deserve the tomatoes."
- Three years is "an absolute minimum" for judging performance; he prefers five. If a three-year or longer period is poor, partners should look elsewhere, unless it covered a speculative bull-market explosion.
- He does not predict the stock market or business cycles. Anyone who thinks that is essential "should not be in the partnership."
- He promises three things:
  - Investments chosen on value, not popularity.
  - Permanent capital loss (not short-term quotational loss) kept to an absolute minimum through a wide margin of safety in each commitment plus diversification.
  - His wife, children and he have virtually their entire net worth in the partnership.
- By 1964 he asks every partner to reread the Ground Rules yearly and not to sign unless they agree "mentally and viscerally."

## Expectations: how the partnership should behave across market types

- Dow expectation: about 5% a year compounded (5-7% in the 1962 letter) from dividends plus price gains. "Anyone expecting substantially better than that from the general market probably faces disappointment."
- Yearly swings: a few years of +20-25%, a few of the same size negative, most in between. The sequence is "of no great importance for the long-term investor." Compounded results are the same however the years are shuffled.
- Target by market type:
  - Dow down 35-40%: the partnership down only 15-20%.
  - Dow roughly flat: up about 10 points.
  - Dow up 20%+: "we would struggle to be up as much."
- Goal: a ten-point annual edge over the Dow. With the Dow at 5%, he hopes for 15% a year. Ten points is "the very maximum" achievable over a long period.
- By 1964 he says his actual 17.7-point margin (22.3% a year versus the Dow's 10.0%, 1957-63) "is unattainable over any long period." He expects prolonged stretches of narrow margins and some years worse than the Dow.
- Size has not hurt: assets grew from $303,726 (1957) to $7.18 million (start of 1962) to $17.45 million (start of 1964). He says the portfolio could not be improved with $1 million or $5 million, and "our idea inventory has always seemed to be 10% ahead of our bank account."

## The three investment categories

**Generals (undervalued securities)**
- Bought at a large discount to value to a private owner, with no say in policy and no timetable.
- Typical positions are 5-10% of assets in each of five or six generals, with smaller stakes in 10-15 others.
- Selection is quantitative first, but "the qualitative is important": good management, a decent industry, some "ferment" among a dormant management or shareholders. "But we demand value."
- They move with the Dow. Cheap does not mean it cannot fall. They should be the star in sharply rising markets and lag in falls.
- Warren is content to sell at a level between cost and fair value rather than "the last nickel."
- He often rides the coattails of a dominant holder planning to redeploy assets, but is "careful whose coat we are holding."
- Where the company improves earnings and assets while the stock goes nowhere, he keeps buying week after week, perhaps for years. Either the market catches up or BPL gets control at a bargain.
- He is largest single holder in three companies by mid-1964. He calls the buying end "about 90% of this business."
- Borrowing against generals is "a very dangerous practice."

**Work-outs (corporate events with a timetable)**
- Mergers, liquidations, reorganizations, spin-offs and sell-outs, especially oil producers selling to majors. He acts only on publicly announced deals: "We wait until we can read it in the paper."
- Risk is that the deal falls through (antitrust, stockholder rejection, withheld tax rulings), not market direction.
- Typical returns are 10-20% a year before leverage, steady regardless of the Dow. They are a drag in bull markets and a cushion in bear ones.
- He holds five to ten at a time. Borrowing is acceptable against work-outs, with a self-imposed cap of 25% of partnership net worth. In 1962 he paid $75,000 of interest at about 5%, implying roughly $1.5 million borrowed.
- Short sales on a late-1962 deal removed market risk and locked in a 10%+ gross profit for a few months of tied-up money.
- Pattern: "the deals take longer than originally projected" and "the payouts tend to average a little better than estimates."

**Controls**
- Arise when a cheap general sits long enough that BPL accumulates a large stake, or via a large initial block. Measure over several years.
- Activism only when needed: "We do not want to get active merely for the sake of being active." Otherwise he lets others do the work.
- Built-in profit comes from the purchase price. Once control is achieved, value depends on the enterprise, not market irrationality.
- Willingness to take control gives "two-way stretch": price rises, or BPL keeps buying until the business itself vindicates the judgment. "If it looks at all close, we pass."
- Category mix is largely accident of availability, which is why a single year says little.

## Dempster Mill: buying below liquidation value and the asset-conversion case study

- Started as a general in 1956 at $18 with about $72 book value, $50 per share of it net current assets. Industry was tough and management unimpressive, but the numbers were attractive.
- His rule of thumb: buy 100 situations like this and perhaps 70 or 80 work out to reasonable profits in one to three years.
- BPL bought for five years at $16-25, then at $30.25 in August-September 1961 plus a tender offer at the same price, reaching over 71.7% (cost $1,262,577.27).
- Year-end 1961 valuation applied haircuts to a prompt-sale basis: inventory 60% of book (a 40% discount), receivables 85%, prepaid 25%, plant at estimated auction value. That gave about $35.25 per share.
- Old management did nothing in about six months. On April 17, 1962 he met Harry Bottle, offered pay tied to hitting goals, and Bottle was president six days later.
- Results by November 1962: inventory cut from $4.2 million to $1.6 million (about $550,000 written off), notes payable from $1.23 million to zero, adjusted value about $51.26 per share. By July 1963 inventory was under $1 million.
- Bottle's actions:
  - Administration and selling expense cut from $150,000 to $75,000 a month.
  - Factory overhead per direct labor hour cut from $6 to $4.50.
  - Five unprofitable branches closed and replaced with distributors.
  - Money-losing jobbed lines dropped.
  - Repair-part prices raised for roughly $200,000 of extra profit with almost no volume loss.
  - Freed capital put into marketable securities, a gain of over $400,000.
- At June 30, 1963 Dempster held securities worth $2,028,415.25; BPL's 71.7% share was $1,454,373.70 against a $1,262,577.27 cost. "Everything above ground (and part of it underground) is profit." He prefers this "fingers and toes method" to "any prayerful reliance that someone will pay me 35 times next year's earnings."
- Operating assets were sold in late 1963 at about book value; with the securities gain, realization was about $80 per share. Warren sold rather than keep paying corporate tax on a business that no longer needed its capital.
- Principle: "Never count on making a good sale. Have the purchase price be so attractive that even a mediocre sale gives good results. The better sales will be the frosting on the cake." And: "Our business is making excellent purchases, not making extraordinary sales."
- He values assets in controlled businesses on what a prompt sale would produce, not on potential. Management is paid with "big carrots" tied to results; he wants someone not "trying to figure how to get the fixtures in the executive washroom gold-plated."

## Texas National Petroleum: a typical work-out

- Union Oil of California bought TNP, announced April 1962. Warren ignored earlier rumors; he never acts on rumor, though entering at the rumor stage would have paid more.
- BPL bought $264,000 of 6.5% debentures (callable at 104.25), 64,035 shares (liquidation estimate $7.42 per share) and 83,200 warrants (about 13% of the issue, exercisable at $3.50, implying a $3.92 work-out).
- Risks assessed: stockholder disapproval nil (controlling holders negotiated it), title and legal risk near nil, no antitrust problem. The one risk was the IRS ruling on the production-payment financing, delayed because the University of Southern California was the payment holder.
- Company officials predicted closing in August or September; it closed October 31, 1962 after repeated delays.
- Returns: bonds about 20% annualized on $260,773 held under five months (capital gain $14,446 plus 6.5% interest); stock and warrants about 22% annualized (gain $89,304, positions up to $731,000). Both on all-equity capital.
- Warren says it is "bread-and-butter," not a triumph. A failed work-out causes a large market-value shrink, so errors must be few.
- BPL does not discuss current holdings, even with partners: "we must plead the 5th Amendment."

## Why institutions can't beat the Dow, and why conservatism is judged by results

- Each letter benchmarks BPL against the two largest mutual funds (Massachusetts Investors Trust, Investors Stock Fund) and two largest closed-end funds (Lehman, Tri-Continental). Together they managed over $4 billion of a roughly $28 billion industry.
- Through mid-1964 these funds compounded 9.4-10.1% a year versus 10.8% for the Dow. In the 1962 first half, all 17 large common stock funds studied by the Wall Street Journal trailed the Dow from its peak. Annual fees for the four exceeded $7 million.
- His point is not criticism. Within their institutional framework, "to behave unconventionally... is extremely difficult." Their value is convenience, diversification and insulation from bad techniques, not superior results.
- The duck analogy: "When the water (the market) rises, the duck rises; when it falls, back goes the duck."
- Hot "growth" funds that led in 1959-61 fell about 32.3% in the 1962 first half after attracting a rush of new money. Many more holders suffered the loss than had enjoyed the gains.
- Money managers meticulously grade companies' managements but not themselves. Standards should be chosen a priori, not retrospectively: "We started out with a 36-inch yardstick and we'll keep it that way."
- Conservatism is not blue chips at any price. Many who bought long bonds saw market depreciation and lost buying power. Buying blue chips regardless of price-earnings ratios is also dangerous: "There is nothing at all conservative... about speculating as to just how high a multiplier a greedy and capricious public will put on earnings."
- On independent thinking: "You will not be right simply because a large number of people momentarily agree with you. You will not be right simply because important people agree with you." Rather, "if your hypotheses are correct, your facts are correct, and your reasoning is correct. True conservatism is only possible through knowledge and reason."
- His test of conservatism is performance in down markets. In 1962 BPL had one realized loss of about 1.0% of assets; historically no realized loss above 0.5% of net assets and about 100 to 1 realized gains over losses (about 3 to 1 in 1962).

## Compounding and taxes

- Compounding illustrations: $100,000 grows to $259,374 / $672,748 / $1,744,930 at 10% over 10 / 20 / 30 years; at 15% it grows to $404,553 / $1,636,640 / $6,621,140. At 16% for 30 years it reaches $8,484,940, against $224,337 at 4%.
- Small rate differences "mushroom" over time, so a few points of edge over the Dow is "a very worthwhile achievement." Isabella's $30,000 at 4% would be about $2 trillion by 1962. Francis I's roughly $20,000 for the Mona Lisa at 6% would exceed $1 quadrillion.
- He cautions that sustaining very high rates is absurd. Realistic planning includes a -30% year and a Dow -40% year.
- Taxes: he is "an outspoken advocate of paying large amounts of income taxes, at low rates." Decisions should aim at "the most probable compounding of after-tax net worth with minimum risk."
- Low-basis stocks should not be held out of tax fear. "Any isolation of low-basis securities merely freezes a portion of net worth" at the Dow's compounding rate, which he calls "a nullification of investment management." A deferred tax liability "is just as real as the asset."
- BPL makes no moves to defer or accelerate taxes; the large realized gains in 1964 reflect positions sold, not performance.

## Partnership structure and alignment

- Limited partners receive the first 6% of annual profit; the general partner takes 25% of gains above 6%, with shortfalls carried forward. So a 14% gross year gives limited partners 12%.
- Partners can withdraw 20% before year-end at 6% interest and make advance payments at 6%. Warren pays 6% on advance payments even when bank loans cost 4% because he expects over 6% long term and sees it as future equity.
- His own stake: $1,377,400 (1963) and $2,392,900 (1964), with family adding $893,600 and $1,247,190. Associate Bill Scott and wife held $167,400, then $237,400. Warren says "we are all eating our own cooking."
- Auditors Peat, Marwick, Mitchell & Co. make two surprise securities checks a year.
- His position is "I can't promise results but I can promise a common destiny."
