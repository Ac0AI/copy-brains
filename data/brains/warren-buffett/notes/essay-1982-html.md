## Why 1982 "accounting" earnings understated Berkshire's economic earnings

- Warren reports 1982 operating earnings of $31.5 million, only 9.8% of beginning equity capital (valuing securities at cost). That compares with 15.2% in 1981 and a high of 19.4% in 1978.
- He blames three things: worse insurance underwriting, equity capital growing faster than the businesses Berkshire runs directly, and a growing commitment to partially-owned, nonoperated businesses.
- Accounting rules count only dividends from holdings below 20% ownership. Undistributed earnings of those companies are ignored.
- GEICO example: Berkshire owns about 35%, but because it assigned its voting rights it is treated as a sub-20% holding. Only $3.5 million of after-tax dividends hit reported earnings, while $23 million of Berkshire's share of GEICO's undistributed operating earnings was excluded.
- He notes the distortion cuts both ways. If GEICO had earned less but paid $1 million more in dividends, reported earnings would have been higher. If GEICO had earned $100 million more and retained it all, reported earnings would not have changed.
- He admits you should be suspicious of an executive abandoning a yardstick as results worsen: "most managers favor disposition of the yardstick rather than disposition of the manager." He compares it to drawing the bullseye around the arrow, and says he generally favors "pre-set, long-lived and small bullseyes."

## "Economic" earnings and the value of retained earnings

- Warren prefers "economic" earnings, which include all undistributed earnings regardless of ownership percentage.
- The value of retained earnings to owners depends on how effectively they are used, not on the size of one's ownership stake. A holder of 0.01% of Berkshire benefits fully from its retained earnings, just like a 20% owner.
- A 100% owner of many capital-intensive businesses can be credited with retained earnings "with painstaking precision" and still get minor or zero economic value.
- "Accounting numbers are the beginning, not the end, of business valuation."
- Undistributed 1982 operating earnings from GEICO, General Foods, Washington Post and R. J. Reynolds came to well over $40 million (he had predicted over $35 million). That exceeds Berkshire's total reported earnings, which included only $14 million of dividends from these companies.
- He cautions against false precision: realizing retained earnings through market value is subject to "very substantial, but indeterminate" taxation, and the translation into market value is uneven and unpredictable in timing.
- His job is to pick businesses where each retained dollar becomes at least a dollar of market value. Some investees turned a retained dollar into two or more dollars of market value, and these overachievers have so far offset the laggards ("St. Offset").

## Partial vs. total ownership and the market as an auction

- Partial stakes are bought in an auction market where prices are set by participants who sometimes behave like "an army of manic-depressive lemmings."
- This lets a value investor choose from nearly all major American corporations, many far better than any business that could be bought whole in a negotiated deal.
- Buying 100% of good businesses at reasonable prices is what "makes us dance," but it is "far more difficult" than buying fractional interests at attractive prices.
- He felt relief, not envy, at other companies' 1982 acquisitions: "managerial intellect wilted in competition with managerial adrenaline." He quotes Pascal on being unable "to stay quietly in one room."
- He says he almost starred in the "Acquisition Follies of 1982." A very large purchase Berkshire had firmly committed to fell through for reasons beyond its control, which he called the year's major accomplishment.
- The partial-ownership approach needs a moderately priced market. "A too-high purchase price for the stock of an excellent company can undo the effects of a subsequent decade of favorable business developments."
- In 1972, at the two-tier market mania peak, the insurance subsidiaries held only $18 million of equities (about 15% of insurance investments), versus about 80% in 1982. There were as many good businesses in 1972, but prices "looked absurd."
- Higher prices would make Berkshire's results look good temporarily but hurt its long-term prospects.

## Long-term performance, market value vs. intrinsic value

- 1982 net worth gain (insurance equities at market, less capital gains taxes payable) was $208 million, or 40% on a beginning base of $519 million.
- Over 18 years under present management, book value per share rose from $19.46 to $737.43, or 22.0% compounded annually. He warns it will fall: "Geometric progressions eventually forge their own anchors."
- Goal: a long-term return well above the average large American corporation, via partial or total ownership of well-situated businesses with price discipline.
- $79 million of the gain came from GEICO's higher market price. He says GEICO's market gain over two years exceeded its gain in intrinsic business value, which he expected as perception converged with reality.
- Market gains cannot favor Berkshire every year. In a down year net worth could shrink, and he would not be distressed: if the businesses still look attractive and cash is available, he would "simply add to our holdings at even more favorable prices."
- On GEICO management: "Let Jack Do It" (Jack Byrne) works as a corporate creed. Lou Simpson is called the best investment manager in property-casualty insurance.
- He jokes that "nostalgia should be weighted heavily in stock selection": the biggest unrealized gains were in Washington Post and GEICO, companies he connected with at ages 13 and 20. Cost vs. market: GEICO $47.1 million vs. $309.6 million; Washington Post $10.6 million vs. $103.2 million.

## Commodity economics and why insurance underwriting would not recover

- Industries with substantial overcapacity and an undifferentiated "commodity" product are prime candidates for poor profits, unless prices or costs are administered (by government, collusion or cartel).
- Differentiation works with candy bars (customers buy the brand) but not sugar. In such industries only a producer with a "wide and sustainable" cost advantage does consistently well.
- His equation: "persistent over-capacity without administered prices (or costs) equals poor profitability." Overcapacity corrects slowly, and the rebound breeds enthusiasm for expansion that recreates overcapacity: "nothing fails like success."
- Long-term profitability is set by the ratio of supply-tight to supply-ample years. In textiles, the last tight period "lasted the better part of a morning."
- Insurance capacity can be created instantly by capital plus an underwriter's willingness to sign his name, and the product is largely undifferentiated, so it is a "textbook case" of overcapacity plus commodity.
- Industry combined ratios averaged 99.0 from 1950 through 1970 because regulator-negotiated bureau rates acted as quasi-administered pricing. That system has been undercut by new capacity that competes on price.
- Industry combined ratio was 109.5 in 1982 (est.), up from 106.0 in 1981. He says it is a "best case" figure, since weak managements may use reserving maneuvers: "weak managements ... weak accounting" ("It's difficult for an empty sack to stand upright").
- Premium growth under 10% a year means worsening underwriting, since insured losses rise by roughly 10% or more.
- Capacity will shrink only after a "megadisaster," not mere poor profits. When it does, a few firms with capital, willingness to commit it and in-place distribution will find big opportunities.
- Berkshire's insurance underwriting fell from above average to modestly below average in 1982; the biggest swing was in National Indemnity's traditional lines. Underwriting loss was $21.6 million pre-tax versus a $1.5 million profit in 1981.
- GEICO is the "high-profit exception": a wide, sustainable cost advantage. Berkshire's 35% interest represents about $250 million of premium volume, more than its own direct volume. Cypress (Milt Thornton) and Kansas Fire and Casualty (Floyd Taylor) have never had an underwriting loss since joining.

## Issuing stock: never give more intrinsic value than you get

- Rule: "we will not issue shares unless we receive as much intrinsic business value as we give." Many managers issue "dollar bills in exchange for fifty-cent pieces."
- The trap: a seller of a whole business can demand full intrinsic value, but a buyer using its own stock gets only the market's valuation of it. If its stock sells at half of intrinsic value, it gives $2 to get $1. "Gold valued as gold cannot be purchased intelligently through the utilization of gold - or even silver - valued as lead."
- Rationalizations he rebuts:
  - "The target will be worth more": so will the business being traded away. If 2X is issued for X, the imbalance remains.
  - "We have to grow": existing owners' businesses shrink when shares are issued. His farm example: merging a neighbor's 60 acres with your family's 120 as equal partners grows your domain to 180 acres but cuts your family's ownership by 25%.
  - "51% stock and 49% cash for tax-free treatment": if full stock hurts, 51% hurts too ("a man is not charmed if a spaniel defaces his lawn... not a St. Bernard"). Sellers' wishes shouldn't set the buyer's interests.
- Three ways to avoid destroying value: (1) a true business-value-for-business-value merger, as intended for Berkshire-Blue Chip; (2) issue stock only when it sells at or above intrinsic value; (3) repurchase shares equal to those issued, turning a stock deal into a cash deal. He prefers repurchases that enhance wealth over "damage-repair" ones.
- He warns that projections can justify any price ("while deals often fail in practice, they never fail in projections"). Dilution should be judged on intrinsic business value, not near-term earnings per share.
- Describe a deal accurately: "Part of A sold to acquire B." Managers should ask whether they would sell 100% of their business on the same terms. His investment-company illustration: trading $2 of intrinsic value for $1 would never pass the SEC.
- "Double whammy": the merger itself destroys value, then the market rationally marks down a management with an anti-owner record. The highest price/value ratios go to managers who never issue shares on unfavorable terms.
- "We will not equate activity with progress or corporate size with owner-wealth."

## Acquisition criteria and how Berkshire is run

- What Warren wants to buy:
  - Large purchases (at least $5 million of after-tax earnings)
  - Demonstrated consistent earning power ("future projections are of little interest"; no turnarounds)
  - Good returns on equity with little or no debt
  - Management in place ("we can't supply it")
  - Simple businesses ("if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it")
  - An offering price
- Unfriendly deals are excluded. He promises confidentiality and a fast answer, usually within five minutes. Cash is preferred; stock only on the terms described above.
- Headquarters has six people (including Warren, Mike Goldberg, Joan Atherton, Gladys Kaiser, Verne McKenzie, Bill Scott), who "outproduce corporate groups many times their number." A compact organization lets them manage the business "rather than managing each other." He finds a phone call with Charlie Munger more productive than "a half-day committee meeting."
- Retiring managers Phil Liesche (National Indemnity) and Ben Rosner (Associated Retail Stores) ran their businesses with the care of 100% owners; "no rules were necessary" because it was in their character. Attracting such managers is why "you need not worry about Berkshire's future."

## Buffalo News example of a franchise gaining readership

- Sunday circulation of the Buffalo News reached 367,000 within six years of launching a Sunday edition, versus the rival Courier-Express's 272,000 as the only Sunday paper. That is a 35% gain, with little change in households.
- Only 14 U.S. cities have a daily paper with weekday circulation above the News. Warren credits Henry Urban, Stan Lipsey, Murray Light, Clyde Pinson, Dave Perona and Dick Feather.
- Earnings table notes: See's Candies pre-tax earnings were $23.9 million in 1982 (vs. $21.0 million), and Buffalo Evening News lost $1.2 million pre-tax.
