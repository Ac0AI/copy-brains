## Why Warren changed his goal to the lesser of 9% or 5 points over the Dow (October 1967)

Warren had pursued an average edge of ten percentage points a year over the Dow for eleven years. In October 1967 he lowered it to "the lesser of 9% per annum or a five percentage point advantage over the Dow." If the Dow averages -2% over five years, he hoped for +3%; if it averages +12%, he would hope for only +9%.

He gave four reasons:
- **Bargains disappeared.** Obvious quantitative bargains had dwindled sharply.
- **Hyper-reactive markets.** Intense interest in performance made markets hyper-reactive, and his analytical techniques had limited value against that.
- **Too much capital.** The base had grown to about $65 million against "a diminishing trickle of good investment ideas." He called this the least significant factor. He thought performance with one-tenth the capital "would be little better."
- **Personal motivation.** He wanted "a less compulsive approach" than when he was "younger and leaner."

He also said limited objectives should mean limited effort, and that the long-term downside risk would not be lower, only the upside potential. He told partners with better alternatives to take their money elsewhere, and about $1.6 million withdrew at yearend. Candor mattered to him: he disliked those who announce "phony proclamations" while pursuing other motives.

## Quantitative vs. qualitative investing

Warren described two schools:
- **Qualitative:** "Buy the right company... and the price will take care of itself."
- **Quantitative:** "Buy at the right price and the company (and stock) will take care of itself."

Money can be made either way, and every analyst blends the two. He placed himself mainly in the quantitative school.

His most sensational ideas, though, leaned qualitative, where he had a "high-probability insight." That "causes the cash register to really sing," but it is infrequent. On the quantitative side no insight is needed: "the figures should hit you over the head with a baseball bat." Big money comes from right qualitative calls; "the more sure money" comes from obvious quantitative ones.

He offered three possible causes of the disappearing statistical bargains:
- Constant combing of investments for twenty years, with no 1930s-style convulsion to spawn new bargains.
- Takeover bids becoming socially accepted, which focuses attention on bargain issues.
- Exploding ranks of security analysts.

Much of the 1964-66 performance came from a single idea. He noted that qualitative ideas are rare, like insights generally.

## Speculation, short time horizons, and refusing to change approach

Warren had long preached measuring performance, with three years as the minimum. The public shortened this to yearly, quarterly and monthly. He saw a self-generating cycle in which superior short-term results attract new money, which demands shorter time spans. The stock itself becomes "virtually incidental." He called the result "speculation on an increasing scale."

His commitments:
- He makes no attempt to guess the market and had "not the foggiest notion" whether the Dow would be at 600, 900 or 1200 a year later. Timing estimates are meaningless.
- He would not follow "fashion" investing, where anticipating market action overrides business valuations: "I will not invest my own money based upon such an approach – hence, I will most certainly not do so with your money."
- He would not abandon an approach whose logic he understands, even if it means "foregoing large and apparently easy, profits to embrace an approach which I don't understand... and which, possibly, could lead to substantial permanent loss of capital."
- He quoted Ben Graham: "Speculation is neither illegal, immoral nor fattening (financially)." In 1967 it was possible to get "fiscally flabby through a steady diet of speculative bonbons," while BPL "continue[d] to eat oatmeal."

In the July 1967 letter he wrote, "The tide continues to be far more important than the swimmers." In 1968 he criticized chain-letter stock promotions that relied on "bold, imaginative accounting," and "the hopeful, credulous and greedy, grasping for an excuse to believe." He mocked a manager's claim that securities "must be studied in a minute-by-minute program."

## BPL results and how the portfolio categories behaved

Overall results: 1967 BPL +35.9% (limited partners +28.4%) vs. Dow +19.0%. 1968 BPL +58.8% (limited partners +45.6%) vs. Dow +7.7%. Through 1968, BPL cumulative was 2610.6% (25.3% annually for limited partners) vs. 185.7% (9.1%) for the Dow. He called 1968 "a freak like picking up thirteen spades in a bridge game."

The four mutual and closed-end funds he tracked averaged only a fraction of a point a year better than the Dow over twelve years.

1967 categories:
- **Workouts** (securities with a timetable from announced sell-outs, mergers, reorganizations and spin-offs): worst year ever. $153,273 on $17,246,879 average investment, or 0.89%. "The streets were filled with upset applecarts."
- **Controls:** $2,894,571 on $20,192,776.
- **Generals, relatively undervalued:** $14,096,593 on $19,487,996, or 72%, driven largely by one security.
- **Generals, private owner:** $1,297,215 on $5,141,710.

1968 categories:
- **Controls:** $5,886,109 on $24,996,998.
- **Generals, private owner:** $21,994,736 on $16,363,100. His best category historically, with total profits "fifty times or more" total losses over twelve years. It came from one industry idea implemented several ways.
- **Generals, relatively undervalued:** $4,271,825.
- **Workouts:** $7,317,128 on $18,980,602. Warren concentrates workouts in a few situations a year, so yearly results vary more than with an across-the-board approach.

Caveats he gave: category returns are calculated on average investment, understating returns versus beginning-base methods, and each category is dominated by one or two investments, so conclusions are only tentative.

## Controlled businesses: valuation and why he kept them

- **Valuing controls.** Market price is irrelevant to valuing controlled companies. Berkshire was valued at 25 at yearend 1967 (market about 20) and 31 at yearend 1968 (market about 37). He would have done the same at market prices of 15 and 50. "Price is what you pay, value is what you get."
- **Why hold them.** He would keep up to 40% of capital in controlled businesses, limited by partnership liquidity needs, at an expected return below an aggressive stock operation. His reasoning: with people he likes, businesses he finds stimulating, and returns of 10-12%, it is foolish "to rush from situation to situation to earn a few more percentage points," or to trade pleasant relationships for "possible irritation, aggravation or worse."
- **Return on capital.** In 1967 Associated Cotton Shops (Ben Rosner) and National Indemnity (Jack Ringwalt) each earned about 20% on capital employed. Only 37 of the Fortune 500 manufacturers reached that figure.
- **Textiles.** The textile business earned too little, and he saw "no prospect of a good return" on those assets. Yet he declined to liquidate it: "I have no desire to trade severe human dislocations for a few percentage points additional return per annum." The business employed 1,100 people and needed no substantial new capital.
- **Reinvestment.** Capital from weaker segments was redeployed into insurance and banking. He valued Berkshire at about $43 tangible net assets per share. In 1969 he expected 10% annual intrinsic value growth for DRC and Berkshire.
- **Dividends.** Berkshire and DRC paid no dividends because of debt, policyholder and depositor protection, and good uses for capital.
- **Management.** He liked and praised the three excellent operating managers, all over sixty and "extraordinarily good," and judged the "second men" only partly knowable until they actually run a company.

## Closing the partnership (May-December 1969)

In May 1969 he announced his retirement. He had "flunked" the goal of lighter effort: "If I am going to participate publicly, I can't help being competitive." He would not "grope around, hoping to 'get lucky' with other people's money," nor "play a game I don't understand just so I can go out a hero." He expected breakeven for 1969, and the year ended slightly over 6%.

His reasons:
- Quantitative opportunities had "virtually disappeared."
- $100 million of assets meant commitments under about $3 million could not move results, which ruled out companies with less than about $100 million of stock at market value.
- The market was more short-term and speculative.

His workouts that year were "atrocious." He took full blame: "Our poor experience this year is 100% my fault," a misjudgment of a fast-developing government trend. He also refused to let what should happen socially color what he thinks will happen: "I never believe in mixing what I think should happen (socially) with what I think will happen."

Partner options at liquidation:
- Take cash.
- Keep proportional shares in Diversified Retailing and Berkshire Hathaway, valued unilaterally by him at fair value.
- Hold residual items (Blue Chip Stamps, small workout stubs).

He chose not to register the stock for a public distribution, so that sophisticated partners would have no edge over less sophisticated ones in a thin market. He expected private sales near yearend valuations. When Blue Chip's offering collapsed (the stock fell from 24 to 16-1/2 after announcement), he kept the block, preferring a better disposal.

On Berkshire and DRC he said: "I'm going to [hold]... and I plan to buy more." He called the risk of loss low "compared to most stocks" and said he thought about them "as businesses, not 'stocks'." He hoped their prices would follow business results rather than speculative enthusiasm, and said he would not "promote" the stocks.

## Bonds vs. stocks in 1969, and picking a manager

In October 1969 he judged that for the first time in his career there was "little choice for the average investor between professionally managed money in stocks and passive investment in bonds." His assumptions, for a 40% tax-bracket investor over ten years:
- **Tax-free bonds:** about 6-1/2% yield.
- **Stocks as a group:** about 9% pre-tax (3% dividends plus 6% gain), roughly 6-1/2% after tax. GNP growth of about 6%, stable profit share of GNP and stable earnings multiples cap long-term growth in aggregate valuation at about 6%.
- **Professional managers:** about half of stock money is professionally managed, so average results are the group average. Less than 10% might average 2 points a year over the group. Even the top 1-2% likely add no more than about 4 points, or about 9-1/2% after tax.
- **Conclusion:** passive tax-free bonds roughly equal professionally managed stocks and are only modestly inferior to extremely well-managed equity money. "Aggressively run" money (about $50 billion, perhaps 100 times a decade earlier) "just can't 'perform'."
- **Inflation:** it matters little to the comparison. "The best expectable after-tax rate of return makes the most sense."
- **Time horizon:** "In the long run, the market is a weighing machine – in the short run, a voting machine" (Ben Graham). He finds weights easier to judge than votes.

The decision should also reflect temperament: need for regular income, tolerance for principal swings, and the psychic pull of "juicy results."

On choosing Bill Ruane, he cited integrity, ability and availability to small partners. He noted Ruane's volatility (about -50% in 1962 and -15% in 1969 to date) and warned that any manager's results moderate as assets grow. The likely drawbacks lead to average results, "and average performance is just not that terrible a risk."

## Buying tax-free bonds: his practical rules (February 1970)

He offered to help partners buy bonds in March 1970, with no financial interest, then stop advising after March 31.

**What he buys:**
- Large revenue-producing public entities (toll roads, power and water districts).
- Industrial Development Authority bonds, e.g. a project leased to U.S. Steel, only as good as the lessee. Prejudice against them had kept yields above their credit quality.
- Public Housing Authority issues, effectively U.S.-guaranteed and AAA.
- State obligations.

**What he avoids:**
- Large-city bonds. "If I can't understand something, I tend to forget it." He wants to be paid for things he can handle and to be right on affirmative decisions.
- Illiquid issues with bid-ask spreads up to 15%. His targets had 2-5% spreads, acceptable only for long-term holders.
- Lots under $25,000.
- Corporate, convertible and short-term bonds.

**Call provisions.** He rejected bonds the issuer can call at a small premium: it is a long deal if it favors the issuer and a short one if it favors you. He said such bonds yield about the same as non-callable ones and called this "the height of foolishness," citing a 1969 Los Angeles Department of Water and Power issue. Deep-discount bonds callable at far above cost are fine.

**Maturity.** Maturity depends on:
- the shape of the yield curve;
- expectations for rates;
- tolerance for price swings.

He had been wrong on rates the year before. His reasoning: with a strongly positive curve and a 50-50 view on rates, long non-callable bonds have better odds than short ones. He chose 10-25 years. Illustration: a 5% bond falls to $791.60 if rates go to 7% with 19 years left, and rises to $1,288.10 if rates go to 3%.

**Discount vs. full coupon.** Discount bonds yield more gross but incur capital gains tax at maturity. He expected them to pay off after tax for most holders. The 1969 Tax Reform Act pushed banks out of discount tax-free bonds, which helps individuals. Expected after-tax yield on 20-year bonds was about 6-1/2%.

**Tax and borrowing:** do not own tax-free bonds while carrying general bank loans, because the interest deduction is at risk.
