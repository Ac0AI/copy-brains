## Alphabet as a business bet: capital intensity, returns, and who made the call

- Warren says he "initiated" the Alphabet position. Greg Abel approves it and "is the decider"; the two "talk all the time, every day."
- Berkshire's stake is over $31 billion including a $10 billion private placement. Warren ranks it "probably number five or six" among holdings, counting wholly owned businesses such as Burlington Northern.
- He does not like it "as well as at least four or five other businesses that we own."
- He says Berkshire chooses between marketable securities and whole companies "the same way." The only differences are minor ones, such as not being able to set dividend policy.
- His rule: "the important thing is to buy a good business, and to buy it on the right terms, and then get the right person to run it."
- On Alphabet's AI spending, he says the hyperscalers are "laying out hundreds of billions." That kind of capex is comparable to what a railroad does.
- He thinks a company that spends heavily is "more likely to be a winner, based on their record, than probably 90% or 95% of what will get merchandised through Wall Street."
- He says the other hyperscalers "don't have any choice." They are "playing a game, in many cases, that they don't want to play."
- On the idea that he never buys technology: "There are all kinds of games I don't understand, sure. Why should I expect to make money in all kinds of things I don't understand?"

## What makes a "good business": returns on capital versus the riskless rate

- Warren defines a good business as one where you can expect "high returns on capital over a long period of time."
- The benchmark is Treasuries. He can put huge sums in government bonds and collect "20 or 30 or 40 billion dollars a year," and he gets 5% on Treasury bills. A good business has to earn "a lot more than the returns on essentially riskless investments."
- His example is American Express. Most banks earn 13% or 14% on capital, while American Express earns "30% plus" without taking more risk.
- "The trick in investing is to find businesses that are going to earn high returns on capital for an extended period of time."
- The long period matters because "those doubles later on are the payoff of very good numbers."
- He ties this to Charlie Munger, who "pounded the idea" that a business isn't good because it does sexy things. It has to earn real cash that it could distribute within a short period.
- Better still are businesses that can reinvest that cash at high returns inside the business. He notes the harder case is businesses that earn high returns but can't deploy the excess capital.
- Coca-Cola is his example of a very good business. Berkshire has owned it about 45 years and has "not a thing to do with running" it.

## Durability, competition, and why moats get attacked

- "If you have a wonderful business, you are going to be subject to attack. So it's not a question of whether it was wonderful yesterday, it's the question of how long it is going to be wonderful."
- Data Documents, from 1958: his group started it after reading that IBM had been ordered in an antitrust suit to divest 50% of the capacity of its best business. He says it was among his most successful ventures and the opportunity "ran out after ten or fifteen years."
- A&P was the number one grocer in the 1930s and a top target of trust busters. "They had a very, very good hand, and that hand disappeared."
- Henry Ford owned the car business for 20 or 25 years with a vertically integrated model, got the Model T down to about $285, and kept cutting prices while raising wages. When he turned over to the Model A, General Motors "came racing by." Warren says "you've always got somebody shooting at you."
- On IBM: customers are "either happy customers or you don't have customers." Someone eventually offers a better way to meet the customer's objective.
- He prefers slow-changing product businesses to ones with fast technical competition: "If somebody came along with a better candy, that would have more predictive value to me" than a rival finding a better process that "a hundred of their competitors would sneak in the plant at night" to copy.
- People play for keeps in business. He recounts a Sinclair filling station he half-owned in Omaha in his early 20s. They set out to wipe out the competitor next door, which pumped 30,000 gallons a month to their 15,000. Two years later the numbers were the same and they closed.

## Apple, key-person risk, and trade secrets

- Warren still likes Apple, his largest position. He says he knows "more about Apple than I knew many years ago."
- On Tim Cook: "There was no move they could make that would replace Tim that I would have liked." His analogy is that if you have a Stradivarius playing for you, don't spend 300 years looking for another one.
- He says Apple has "very smart people all over the world trying to figure out how to make sure that Apple's future is as bright as its past."
- On Apple's lawsuit against OpenAI over trade secrets, he says most companies would love to steal trade secrets, though "they wouldn't love getting caught."

## Wall Street, quarterly thinking, and the gambling culture

- Wall Street "is only selling something." He can't recall a Wall Street report that gets into the internal rates of return a business is actually earning, and they ask "what will happen next quarter, and it's ridiculous."
- Compounding gets harder with size. The best long-term compounder was probably Rockefeller (oil and gas over 150 to 200 years), but Geico compounded faster in its early years: "it's easier to do when you're small."
- Opportunities arrive unevenly. Some times they are "thrown at you so fast you can't believe it," and at other times "you're very, very lucky if you find one thing in a couple of years."
- He thinks the latter should be normal, but humans love to gamble, so "there's more money in actually cultivating gamblers than there is in cultivating investors."
- Someone who bought Berkshire 40 or 50 years ago generated one commission, and the broker "should have spent the rest of his time telling the client don't do anything with it."
- Values are harder to find when everybody prefers gambling. He calls state lotteries "selling them a payout with a payout ratio of 60% or something like that" and "a cynical sort of activity."
- On the Dow: his first stock purchase came when it had just crossed 100, and it is now around 52,000. America has been a wonderful place to invest, and "the village idiot could have made it from that point forward."
- On the Fed: interest rates are gravity for asset prices in his past framing. He doesn't predict what Kevin Warsh will do but thinks he was "a good choice." He notes the Fed chair, unlike a president, is "not supposed to be looking at the next election."

## Succession, Greg Abel, and managing the end of control

- Warren says Berkshire is "my painting," and he can name fewer than ten people, perhaps fewer than five, he would trust to hand it to. With Greg Abel, "we've found him, and that becomes more evident by the day."
- He is "one hundred percent" confident in Abel, having "seen him in a lot of situations." He felt the same way about Munger and Tom Murphy.
- The remaining risk is mortality ("he is not immortal either"), and Warren has no list of backups. Berkshire's directors "like the concept of Berkshire Hathaway" and "would like to keep it going."
- Finding even one successor is hard: "nobody expects you to pick up 25 partners and have them all work out."
- The Class A shares he still holds were worth about $140 billion at the prior close. He now wants them disbursed to charity by the end of 2034, instead of ten years after his death, partly because he has faith in Abel and partly because of age and mortality, both his own and his children's.
- He says he wrote his will a couple of years ago and will not change it except for very important reasons, because he knew he "had my marbles when I wrote it." He has seen managers of Berkshire companies decline while assistants covered for them.
- Berkshire's head office has run with about 25 people, and he says it grew "ten for one" before the last few additions.

## Giving, luck, and passing wealth to the next generation

- Warren and Susie planned early that he would compound money at a better rate than society and she would give it away well. He did "wholesale" and she did "retail." He now says he may be "one of the ten luckiest" of 8 billion people, helped by the accident of being exposed early to what he was good at.
- He is increasing gifts to the Susan Thompson Buffett Foundation and to his three children's foundations. Each child's foundation rises about 50%, and the STB Foundation's payout is $4.5 billion this year. He gives nothing for now to the Gates Foundation, which he says received about $47 billion from him in total. He called Gates a friend since 1991 who remains in touch.
- He has never looked at his children's Form 990s and does not judge each action. He notes philanthropic bets can have "10% or 20% probability of success. It's not like investments."
- His children's foundations employ 11 to 25 people each, with expense ratios "far below that of institutions that are much better known."
- A rule he credits to someone else: give children "enough to do something, but not enough to do nothing."
- On the Giving Pledge: encourage giving, and let people choose what and when. He sees philanthropy as appealing to people's better instincts.
