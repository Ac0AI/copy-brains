## Warren on accounting measures: operating ROE, not EPS, and why market-value net worth distorts

- Warren measures a single year's operating performance as operating earnings (before securities gains or losses) divided by beginning shareholders' equity with all securities valued at cost. For 1979 this was 18.6% of beginning net worth.
- He argues measuring against equity with securities at market distorts the result: a large market decline shrinks net worth and makes mediocre operating earnings look unrealistically good, while strong investment success inflates the base and makes operations look worse.
- Insurance equity securities must now be carried at market value, which raises 1978 and 1979 net worth. Blue Chip Stamps still uses lower of cost or market, so the same stock bought at the same price by two subsidiaries can be carried at two different values.
- Earnings per share rose about 20% in 1979, but Warren calls it the wrong figure to focus on. Berkshire had far more capital in 1979, and its return on that capital fell short of 1978.
- "Earnings per share" rises constantly on a savings account or fixed-rate bond because interest is plowed back into the capital base: "even a 'stopped clock' can look like a growth stock if the dividend payout ratio is low."
- His primary test of managerial economic performance is a high earnings rate on equity capital employed, without undue leverage or accounting gimmickry, not consistent EPS gains. He thinks managements and analysts should de-emphasize EPS and its yearly changes.
- For long-term performance he does count realized and unrealized capital gains and uses market-value statements, since over years they matter as much as operating earnings. They are too capricious to judge a single year.

## Long-term results and the "investor's misery index"

- Book value per share went from $19.46 (September 30, 1964) to $335.85 at yearend 1979 with equities at market, a 20.5% compound annual gain. This was achieved with low financial leverage, low insurance premium-to-capital leverage, and no significant share issuance or repurchase.
- He notes the 1964 book value probably overstated intrinsic value, since the textile assets weren't worth 100 cents on the dollar either as a going concern or in liquidation.
- Warren warns that a 20% compounded return no longer guarantees a good real return. At 14% inflation, after-tax purchasing power gain is "very close to zero," because most of the remaining six points goes to income tax when converting gains to cash.
- He defines the "investor's misery index": inflation rate plus the percentage of capital the owner must pay in taxes (ordinary income tax on dividends, capital gains tax on retained earnings) to move the business's earnings into his pocket. When it exceeds the business's return on equity, the investor's real capital shrinks even if he consumes nothing.
- A 3% savings bond, 5% passbook account or 8% Treasury note were each turned by inflation into instruments that chew up purchasing power; a business earning 20% can do the same under inflation not much worse than prevailing.
- He has no corporate solution: high inflation won't raise Berkshire's returns on equity. A commentator noted 1964 book value bought about half an ounce of gold, and 1979 book value buys about the same half ounce. The rub: "government has been exceptionally able in printing money and creating promises, but is unable to print gold or create oil."
- External conditions affecting currency stability may be the most important factor in whether shareholders earn real rewards.

## Easy businesses versus turnarounds: the Waumbec lesson

- Warren says some businesses, like a network TV station, make it "virtually impossible to avoid earning extraordinary returns on tangible capital." Their assets sell at extraordinary prices (a thousand cents or more on the dollar), but "despite a fancy price tag, the 'easy' business may be the better route to go."
- He tried the other route with Waumbec Mills in Manchester, New Hampshire. The price was an extraordinary bargain by any statistical test: below working capital, with machinery and real estate effectively for less than nothing.
- It was still a mistake: "new problems arose as fast as old problems were tamed."
- His conclusion from operating and investment experience: "turnarounds" seldom turn, and the same energy and talent are better used "in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price."
- The textile business produces some cash but at a low rate relative to capital employed, which Warren attributes to the industry, not the managers.
- Some Waumbec pieces proved useful additions to the decorator line at New Bedford, the strongest franchise, and Manchester might run profitably on a reduced scale, but the original rationale did not prove out.
- Ben Rosner at Associated Retail Stores produces very large earnings relative to capital, realized in cash rather than receivables and inventories, in a low-growth market.

## Insurance underwriting discipline and the cycle

- The industry combined ratio rose from about 97.4% to 100.7% in 1979; Berkshire's fell from 98.2% to 97.1%. Warren forecasts the industry averaging around 105 over the next five years.
- He disagrees with the conventional view that rates firm in 1981. High interest rates encourage writing business at underwriting loss levels once regarded as unacceptable, so he expects competition to create "a new threshold of tolerance for underwriting losses."
- National Indemnity's section under Phil Liesche earned an $8.4 million underwriting profit on about $82 million of earned premiums. Warren praises him for actually cutting volume when business doesn't make sense: "if business makes sense, he writes it; if it doesn't, he rejects it."
- Berkshire does not lay off people because of volume swings from this policy; he'd "rather have some slack in the organization" than keep everyone busy writing money-losing business. This discipline came from founder Jack Ringwalt.
- Cypress (workers' compensation) followed the same rule of sticking with business it understands and wants regardless of volume, and turned out to be "an absolute gem."
- On reinsurance, he warns that capital inflows and softer prices on rising exposures may produce disastrous results for entrants, especially given the "long tail" that lets catastrophic loss experience fester undetected. Berkshire's reinsurance may shrink during the extended competition, since "it will be hard for us to be a whole lot smarter than the crowd."
- Insurance "tends to magnify, to an unusual degree, human managerial talent - or the lack of it," and has potential for terrible results in a single year, for example if auto accident frequency turns. Part of the 1979 reprieve was fewer accidents from changed driving habits after higher gas prices, a "dosage of serendipity" that won't last.
- He admits mistakes in the Homestate group, including data processing reorganization, and entered surety reinsurance late in the year, seeking long-term "partnership" relationships with quality client insurers.

## Bonds in an inflationary world: "Neither a short-term borrower nor a long-term lender be"

- The insurance industry lost an extraordinary amount in bonds. Warren suggests the accounting convention of carrying bonds at amortized cost may have contributed, since market-value recognition might have focused attention earlier on the dangers of very long contracts.
- He points to an inconsistency: insurers shifted auto policies from one year to six months because they couldn't forecast costs a year out, then invested the proceeds by "selling the money at a fixed price for thirty or forty years."
- The very long-term bond is "the last major fixed price contract of extended duration still regularly initiated in an inflation-ridden world." Elsewhere, long contracts are indexed or reviewable annually. Lenders "slept through an economic and contractual revolution."
- Berkshire's insurers have not been net buyers of straight long-term bonds for years and never bought 30- or 40-year bonds, but Warren calls this mild caution "an improper response": "You do not adequately protect yourself by being half awake while others are sleeping."
- Specific mistakes: buying fifteen-year bonds, and, worse, not selling them at a loss when his views crystallized. He also notes that trying to be clever with sinking funds and special issues repeated the textile error of fighting a tide.
- Net fixed-dollar commitments in recent years went into convertible bonds. The conversion option effectively shortens the average life, because Berkshire can end the contract by converting to stock at a time of its choosing. Some converts looked exceptionally attractive with the same earnings-retention logic as the equity portfolio.
- He still allows that he could be too negative: inflation is man-made and perhaps "man-mastered," and rates might already compensate. His unwillingness to fix a price now for a pound of See's candy or yard of Berkshire cloth in 2010 or 2020 makes him equally unwilling to price money for those years. Of the fifteen-year bonds he says Berkshire "will annually pay a price in terms of earning power."

## Equity holdings and retained earnings

- Berkshire's insurance equities (about $336.7 million market value vs $185.4 million cost at yearend 1979) performed well because the underlying companies did. Major positions included GEICO ($28.3M cost, $68.0M market), Washington Post ($10.6M cost, $39.2M market), Handy & Harman, SAFECO and Interpublic.
- Warren relies on retained earnings not reported in Berkshire's statements: he has faith that managements will turn a retained dollar into "a dollar or more of subsequent market value" for Berkshire.
- He expects portfolio underperformance relative to the market in 1980, "for the first time in recent years," but plans no changes to suit a specific year's market.
- He calls most holdings "splendid businesses, splendidly managed, purchased at highly attractive prices," and expects very large and growing underlying earning power from fractional ownership.
- On the planned sale of Illinois National Bank (required by the Bank Holding Company Act by end-1980), he warns the proceeds can't replace its earning power: "You simply can't buy high quality businesses at the sort of price/earnings multiple likely to prevail on our bank sale." The bank earned about 2.3% on average assets, over three times the average major bank.

## Shareholder communication and the right shareholder base

- About 98% of shares are held by people who were also holders at the start of the year, and perhaps 90% of owners have Berkshire as their largest holding. So the annual report builds on prior years instead of restating, and provides what Warren would want if roles were reversed. Quarterly reports carry no narrative because owners and managers have very long time horizons.
- He believes the CEO owes owners direct reporting: "You would demand that in a private company; you should expect no less in a public company." The report should not be handed to a PR consultant. Owners deserve the same scope, balance and candor Berkshire expects from its operating managers.
- Companies "obtain the shareholder constituency that they seek and deserve." Short-term focus attracts short-term shareholders, and cynicism toward investors is returned.
- He cites Phil Fisher's restaurant analogy: a restaurant targeting one clientele gets loyal returning customers, but one vacillating "between French cuisine and take-out chicken" gets "a revolving door of confused and dissatisfied customers." A company can't simultaneously attract owners wanting high yield, long-term growth and "stock market pyrotechnics."
- Managements that court heavy trading are in effect asking existing owners to leave, since new owners can't be added without losing old ones. Warren wants very low turnover among owners who "understand our operation, approve of our policies, and share our expectations."

## Management structure: centralized capital allocation, delegated operations

- Berkshire centralizes financial decisions "at the top (the very top)" and extremely delegates operating authority to key managers at each business. Headquarters uses about 1,500 square feet and "could just field a basketball team."
- He admits the approach "produces an occasional major mistake that might have been eliminated or minimized through closer operating controls," but says it removes layers of cost and speeds decisions. "Because everyone has a great deal to do, a very great deal gets done."
- Most importantly, it attracts and retains extraordinarily talented people "who simply can't be hired in the normal course of events" because working for Berkshire is "almost identical to running their own show."
- Warren notes sellers of businesses to Berkshire have almost without exception been honorable and fair. Thirteen businesses were acquired via negotiated cash purchases from private owners and six started, all from the original textile capital.
- Managers like Ben Rosner (76), Gene Abegg (82) and Louis Vincenti (74) keep achieving more each year. "We have placed much trust in them - and their achievements have far exceeded that trust."
