## Value comes from owned earnings, not reported earnings: "look-through" results

- Warren says GAAP earnings understate what Berkshire owns because investee profits are recorded only to the extent of dividends received. Example: Berkshire's 17% share of Capital Cities/ABC earnings was over $83 million in 1990, but only about $530,000 ($600,000 of dividends less roughly $70,000 of tax) hit reported earnings.
- His view: "The way they are accounted for is of no importance, but their ownership and subsequent utilization is all-important." He says he doesn't care whether auditors hear a tree fall in the forest; he cares who owns the tree and what's done with it.
- Retained earnings can be more efficient than dividends. When Coca-Cola repurchases shares, it raises Berkshire's ownership of "the most valuable franchise in the world" without the dividend tax Berkshire would pay if it received cash and bought shares itself.
- Look-through earnings method for 1990: take about $250 million (Berkshire's share of investee retained operating earnings), subtract $30 million (incremental tax had it been paid as dividends), and add the $220 million to reported operating earnings of $371 million. Result: about $590 million.
- Berkshire aims for 15% annual growth in look-through earnings and intrinsic value (Warren notes this gets harder as the equity base, then $5.3 billion, grows).
- Accounting numbers are "a beginning, not an end" in calculating true economic earnings. He warns earnings "can be as pliable as putty when a charlatan heads the company reporting them" and that some American fortunes were made by "monetization of accounting mirages."
- Appendix A reprints Ben Graham's 1936 satire "U.S. Steel Announces Sweeping Modernization Scheme" (plant written down to minus $1 billion, wages paid in option warrants, inventory carried at $1, a $1 billion contingency reserve), producing a pro-forma $49.80 per share against a reported loss of $2.76.

## Intrinsic value, book value and what shareholders actually earn

- Per-share book value grew from $19.46 to $4,612.06 over 26 years, 23.2% compounded. Net worth rose $362 million (7.3%) in 1990. Warren calls the long record "meaningless in forecasting future results."
- A shareholder's return matches the company's only if he sells at the same price-to-intrinsic-value relationship at which he bought. Example: buy at a 10% premium, intrinsic value compounds at 15%, sell at a 10% premium, and you earn 15%. Buy at a premium and sell at a smaller one and you do worse.
- Charlie Munger and Warren want Berkshire to trade consistently near intrinsic value and dislike "value-ignoring volatility." In 1989 book value rose 44% while the stock rose 85%; in 1990 book and intrinsic value rose slightly while the stock fell 23%.
- Intrinsic value is an estimate; Warren says he and Charlie might differ by 10% in appraising it. It substantially exceeds book value.
- Much of the excess value was created by the operating managers: "Our job is merely to identify talented managers and provide an environment in which they can do their stuff." His own contribution is compared (with the granddaughter and Beemer the Clown story) to a four-year-old who waves a wand and says "Gee, I'm really good at this."

## Excellent businesses run by excellent managers, in prosaic industries

- The non-insurance operations earned 51% after tax on average equity in 1990, which would have ranked about 20th on the 1989 Fortune 500. Leverage didn't produce it: facilities are mostly owned, and the small debt is basically offset by cash. On return on assets the group would rank in Fortune's top ten.
- The returns came from furniture retailing, candy, vacuum cleaners and steel warehousing, not from spectacular industries like cigarettes or network television. Warren's conclusion: "Our extraordinary returns flow from outstanding operating managers, not fortuitous industry economics."
- Managers deployed capital only with good reason. Net worth of these businesses rose just $47 million on $133 million of earnings, and over the past five years they sent well over 80% of earnings to Warren and Charlie for reallocation.
- Low operating cost is the competitive weapon at Borsheim's and Nebraska Furniture Mart:
  - Borsheim's operating costs run about 18% of sales versus about 40% at the typical fine-jewelry competitor. Sales rose 18% in 1990. Volume is believed to be second only to Tiffany's New York store. Mail assortments (about 1,500 in 1990) went to customers on an honor system with no losses from dishonesty to date.
  - Nebraska Furniture Mart: 1990 sales of $159 million, up 4%; operating costs 15% versus about 40% for Levitz and 25% for Circuit City. Low costs mean low prices, big volume and the broadest selection, so customers drive 130 miles from Des Moines.
  - "What works with diapers works with diamonds": he compares this to Wal-Mart's roughly 15% operating costs.
- Warren admits a "counterrevelation": after years of scorning corporate synergy claims, a See's candy cart at NFM outsold some See's stores.
- At See's, quality and service fanaticism (Chuck Huggins, 19 years running it) mattered. A 5% price increase lifted dollar sales when volume slipped; 263 customers rallied to save the Albuquerque store from a bad lease.

## Media businesses and the erosion of pricing power

- Warren was surprised by how vulnerable newspapers and media were to the early recession. He judges that media businesses will remain "economic marvels" relative to American industry but "considerably less marvelous" than he, the industry or lenders thought only a few years earlier.
- Their past excellence came from pricing power, not physical growth. Now ad dollars grow slowly, retailers using little media advertising have taken share, and the number of print and electronic ad channels has multiplied, dispersing ad dollars and weakening pricing power. That "materially" reduced the intrinsic value of Capital Cities/ABC, Washington Post and the Buffalo News.
- Buffalo News earnings fell 5% in 1990, with a steeper decline late in the year, and he promised they would fall substantially in 1991 as newsprint prices and labor costs rose. Despite the squeeze, he keeps the news hole at least 50% (52.3% in 1990): "Cutting product quality is not a proper response to adversity."

## Insurance: float, cost of funds and super-cat pricing

- Float is funds of others that insurers temporarily hold and can invest. A typical property-casualty insurer breaks even at a combined ratio of about 107-111 because of float income. Long-tail lines (malpractice) generate large float; crop hail generates almost none.
- Warren's preferred yardstick is underwriting loss divided by average float, a rough "cost of funds developed from insurance." A cost below what the government pays on long-term bonds means a good business. In 1990: about $1.6 billion average float ($1,637.3 million), a $26.65 million underwriting loss, cost of funds about 1.63%, versus an 8.24% long-term Treasury yield. Berkshire's float cost was below government bond yields in 19 of its 24 insurance years.
- Qualifications: true cost isn't known until claims settle decades later, and shareholders bear a tax penalty (about one percentage point added to the cost of float for the average insurer, and roughly that for Berkshire) because they fund the insurer and are taxed twice on its investment income.
- GEICO (48% owned) typically underwrites at a profit, so its policyholders effectively pay interest on the float; its edge is operating efficiency and careful risk classification.
- Pricing principle: long-tail lines may be profitable at 110-115 combined ratios, but pricing to those targets is dangerous. Aiming for 100 can itself lose heavily; "aiming for 110-115 is business suicide." Prices need a margin of safety against social trends that spring expensive surprises.
- Industry behavior: losses grow about 10% a year (11% over 25 years) while premiums lagged in 1990, so the 1991 combined ratio would rise about two points. "The most important thing to do when you find yourself in a hole is to stop digging." Berkshire would write huge amounts of business once managements got scared enough to raise prices.
- Super-cat reinsurance: Berkshire was likely the largest US seller. Illustrative contract: $10 million of coverage for roughly a $3 million premium, with $100 million of annual super-cat premiums leading to either a profit near $100 million or a loss well over $200 million. Combined ratio would be near zero or near 300%, concentrating rather than spreading risk.
- Warren's stance: "Charlie and I always have preferred a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%." He expects satisfactory results over a decade, terrible results in occasional years, and will write it only at prices commensurate with risk, letting volume fall when competitors get optimistic. He confesses insurance mistakes made before Mike Goldberg arrived: "there is no statute of limitations on stupidity."

## Buying Wells Fargo and thinking about banks and market prices

- Warren dislikes banking because with assets twenty times equity, small mistakes on a fraction of assets can wipe out much of the equity. Most bank losses came from the "institutional imperative": executives mindlessly imitating peers (follow-the-leader lending "with lemming-like zeal"). He wants only well-managed banks at fair prices, never poorly managed ones that look cheap.
- Wells Fargo purchase: Berkshire raised its stake to just under 10% (the most allowed without Federal Reserve approval) for $290 million, less than five times after-tax earnings and less than three times pre-tax earnings. The bank had $56 billion of assets, earned over 20% on equity and 1.25% on assets, and Carl Reichardt and Paul Hazen were "the best managers in the business." A 10% stake equated to buying a $5 billion bank outright for roughly twice $290 million, and without a Reichardt to run it.
- The stress test he applied: Wells earned well over $1 billion pre-tax after expensing over $300 million of loan losses. If 10% of its $48 billion in loans soured and lost 30% of principal (including foregone interest), the bank would roughly break even.
- Stock fell almost 50% within a few months in 1990 on real-estate and earthquake fears, which he welcomed since it let Berkshire buy more at "panic prices."
- Principle on prices: people who will be perpetual buyers should welcome falling prices, as with food or newsprint. "We will be buying businesses - or small parts of businesses, called stocks - year in, year out." Falling prices help, rising prices hurt. "It's optimism that is the enemy of the rational buyer."
- But unpopularity alone isn't a reason to buy; contrarianism is as foolish as following the crowd. "What's required is thinking rather than polling" (Bertrand Russell: "Most men would rather die than think. Many do.").
- Holdings table (12/31/90, cost to market): Capital Cities/ABC $517.5M to $1,377M; Coca-Cola $1,024M to $2,172M; GEICO $45.7M to $1,111M; Washington Post $9.7M to $342M; Wells Fargo $289M to $289M. He neither bought nor sold five of the six major holdings that year: "Lethargy bordering on sloth remains the cornerstone of our investment style."
- Qualities he admires in managers (Reichardt/Hazen like Murphy/Burke): mutual trust, paying good people well while hating excess headcount, attacking costs in good times and bad, and sticking to what they understand. He quotes Thomas Watson Sr.: "I'm smart in spots - but I stay around those spots."

## Junk bonds, debt and margin of safety

- Warren criticizes 1980s manufactured junk bonds. Salesmen claimed academic data showed higher yields had more than offset higher defaults, but the data came from "fallen angels" (once investment-grade bonds whose managers wanted to regain status), a different universe from new issues, like "checking the historical death rate from Kool-Aid before drinking the version served at Jonestown."
- The "debt as dagger on the steering wheel" thesis: huge debt makes managers careful, but a pothole causes a fatal crash: "a plan that requires dodging them all is a plan for disaster." Example: a Tampa TV station bought with so much debt that interest exceeded gross revenues.
- Beware past-performance "proofs": "If history books were the key to riches, the Forbes 400 would consist of librarians." Wall Street's enthusiasm for an idea is proportional "not to its merit, but rather to the revenue it would produce."
- He cites Graham's The Intelligent Investor: the three words are "Margin of Safety," still right 42 years after he read them.
- Berkshire never buys new-issue junk, but with the market in disarray bought RJR Nabisco bonds: $440 million at yearend 1990 (market value up more than $150 million after yearend), because the credit was better than perceived, asset sales were at good prices, equity was added and management was competent.
- He will look at any investment category so long as he understands the business and believes price and value differ significantly.

## Convertible preferreds, mistakes and commodity businesses

- Preferreds held: $700 million Salomon, $600 million Gillette (converting to 12 million common shares April 1), $358 million USAir, $300 million Champion International. At yearend 1990 he valued Salomon and Champion at about cost, Gillette somewhat above, and USAir substantially below.
- He calls USAir an "unforced error": he bought just as the airline hit industry and merger-integration (Piedmont) trouble. Lesson: "In a business selling a commodity-type product, it's impossible to be a lot smarter than your dumbest competitor."
- Valuation point: most of a convertible preferred's value comes from fixed-income characteristics. The security can't be worth less than as a non-convertible preferred and may be worth more with the conversion option; valuing it at the common-equivalent value is wrong.
- Gillette CEO Colman Mockler is described as a gentleman with "integrity, courage and modesty." Gillette's success came from devotion to quality and constantly improving a product already best in class.

## Acquisition criteria and how Berkshire treats sellers

- Criteria: large purchases (at least $10 million after-tax earnings); demonstrated consistent earning power (not projections or turnarounds); good return on equity with little or no debt; management in place; simple businesses ("if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it"); an offering price. No unfriendly takeovers; complete confidentiality; an answer customarily within five minutes; prefer cash, but will issue stock only when receiving as much intrinsic value as given.
- Favorite deal shape: the Blumkin-Friedman-Heldman mold, where owner-managers want cash for themselves or family but remain significant owners and keep running the business. Also interested in large, non-controlling negotiated blocks.
- Appendix B (seller letter): sellers sell only once, often under broker pressure, so mistakes are irreversible. Price matters but isn't the only factor, and a great business becomes more valuable over time, so the owner can wait and choose the buyer.
- Typical buyers are (1) industry operators who will eventually want "hands-on help," or (2) leveraged financial maneuverers who change accounting before flipping. Berkshire is different: it buys to keep, has no operating staff at the parent, runs businesses autonomously (managers often haven't visited Omaha or met each other), and has no one waiting for a chance to run an acquisition. It wants the operating family to keep 20%, since it needs 80% for tax consolidation.
- Warren's involvement is limited to capital allocation and selection and compensation of the top manager. Cash payment, no brokers, no financing contingency, no re-trading. He is candid that the seller would be no richer afterward; only the form of wealth changes.
- He also notes that advertising an interest in buying collies draws people hoping to sell cocker spaniels, and says of turnarounds and auctions: "When the phone don't ring, you'll know it's me."

## Succession and corporate-governance attitudes

- His wife Susan was nominated to the board. She would inherit his stock and effectively control the company, and shares his view that neither Berkshire nor its businesses should be sold just because a very high bid arrives. Both estate plans aim at preserving Berkshire's character and returning the fortune to society.
- If he died, none of his stock would be sold, a controlling shareholder and manager with similar philosophies would follow, and Charlie would sell the corporate jet, The Indefensible, adding $1 million a year to earnings.
- Charitable giving: 97.3% of eligible shares took part in shareholder-designated contributions ($5.8 million to 2,600 charities). He insists managers not use company money for personal-interest charities: "they should use their own money, not yours."
