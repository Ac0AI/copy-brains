## How Warren reads the Kraft Heinz write-down and what he paid for it

- Warren says Kraft Heinz is still a great business on tangible assets: it uses about $7 billion of tangible assets and earned about $6 billion pre-tax (after depreciation, before amortization).
- Berkshire and certain predecessors paid about $100 billion more than the tangible assets, so for Berkshire the business has to earn on roughly $107 billion, not $7 billion.
- "The business does not earn more just because you pay more for it." If Berkshire had paid $7 billion for it, the earnings would be the same.
- Warren says Berkshire overpaid for Kraft (not for Heinz) and calls the $15 billion write-down fair. He does his own write-downs in his head, and the market had already marked it down further.
- The full company was valued at roughly $71-72 billion: about 1.2 billion shares at around $42 billion of equity, plus about $31 billion of debt. At that price, with earnings not expected to grow in 2019, he sees better places to put money.
- Debt of about $31 billion is higher than originally projected to the rating agencies, because the company paid out a lot and kept little to retire debt. Cutting the dividend from $250 million to $160 million saves about $1.1 billion a year, which helps only slowly.
- He has never sold a share and has no intention of selling or buying more. He would be happy to still be in it in 5 or 10 years and is happy to be George Paulo's partner.
- He is not on the board, and is reluctant to trade partly because purchases and sales must be reported within two days.

## Packaged-goods brands versus retailers

- Warren says he and 3G misjudged the retailer-versus-brand fight. The retailers have gained ground and the weaker brands lose out; only the strongest brands can go toe-to-toe with Walmart or Costco.
- His example: Heinz dates to 1869 and Kraft to C.W. Post in 1895, with global distribution and a fortune spent on advertising, and sales of about $26-27 billion. Costco launched Kirkland in 1992 and did about $39 billion last year with only about 750 outlets, 50% more than all the Kraft Heinz brands combined.
- Private label is getting stronger and will keep getting bigger. Few new billion-dollar food brands are being created, and physical volume hasn't changed much, but the ability to raise prices has.
- He doesn't think the problem is Millennials changing habits, or under-investment in innovation: big food companies rarely produce new products that become big. Heinz ketchup has about 60% of its market and is very strong; Kool-Aid and Jell-O are old brands in secular decline.
- Amazon and Walmart fighting is "like the elephants fighting," and the mice get trampled. He doesn't see the retailers' position weakening.
- The 3G cost-cutting acquisition model works less well now because acquisition prices rose: "anything at a price can be good" and "if you pay too much you pay too much", especially if you borrowed to do it.

## Buying stocks and bad news on holdings

- "The stock market is there not to instruct me, it's there to serve me." When a holding falls on bad news, the question is whether the long-term valuation has changed. Bad news about a fundamentally good business can be a buying opportunity (he cites American Express in the sixties, the partnership's best investment).
- A stock does not know you own it. Paying $10, watching it fall to $8, and waiting to get back to $10 is "nuts"; likewise selling half after it doubles.
- He prefers prices to fall when he is a long-term holder and a company is buying back shares: Berkshire is better off if Apple is cheaper while it repurchases. Apple has said it will go to cash neutral, about $130 billion away, and he expects mostly buybacks. At $200 that retires about 650 million shares; at $150 about 900 million.
- He would be buying Apple at lower levels. His average cost is about $141 and he has never sold a share himself; Ted or Todd trimmed about 3 million shares to fund another purchase, and Warren was not involved in that. He owns about 5% and likes the idea of drifting past 6% as Apple shrinks its share count.
- He generally avoids going over 10% of a company, because it complicates life, and with banks it makes Berkshire a bank holding company. Some banks he owns at 9-plus percent, so he leaves room for a year or two of buybacks.
- On a $4 billion one-day loss on a $173 billion equity portfolio: it doesn't bother him. If you like a business and it is cheaper, you'd want to buy more, like paying less for hamburger.

## Why Warren likes banks and how he thinks about valuing them

- He added to JPMorgan, Bank of America, Bank of New York, PNC and US Bancorp. They are good businesses that are cheaper than other good businesses.
- JPMorgan earns roughly 15-17% on tangible equity, which is "incredible in the world of 3% bonds." His thought experiment: an FDIC-guaranteed deposit paying 15% by mistake and not redeemable would be worth well over 300 cents on the dollar, and more if the interest could compound.
- Bank of America's Brian Moynihan was the most underestimated bank executive in the country: he did what he said, beat his targets, and has repurchased shares smartly.
- Bank health: no FDIC-insured bank failed last year. The FDIC fund holds about $100 billion, all from banks, with no government money.
- Banks, like other US businesses, benefited from the tax cut, which could be reversed.
- He sold the short-lived Oracle position because he did not understand where the cloud business is going, after his IBM experience. He changed his mind on understanding it, not on valuation. He sold IBM at much higher prices than today and is not bothered that it has bounced.

## Stocks versus bonds, interest rates and finding deals

- Whether stocks are expensive "depends on interest rates." If 3% long bonds prevail for 30 years, stocks are incredibly cheap. He would give enormous odds that the S&P outperforms 30-year bonds over 30 years.
- Given a choice of a 10-year bond or the S&P 500 held 10 years, he would "buy the S&P in a second."
- He doesn't understand why rates are so low worldwide so long into a recovery, and calls it a world not described in classical economics. Japan since 1990 is the possible precedent, though Japan has a declining population and no energy resources.
- Warren does not trade on Federal Reserve statements. Berkshire always spends more on the railroad and utility than depreciation and doesn't try to time in and out. He thinks Jay Powell is a terrific chairman.
- Why Berkshire didn't deploy more in Q4: it had a possible large deal in mind, which didn't come together. He prefers buying a whole business to buying stocks. Berkshire had about $112 billion in cash (including the $20 billion floor), and he says it would love to use it.
- Whole-business buying is harder because of competition from private equity, which he calls "private debt to a great extent," borrowing six or seven times EBITDA (a metric he doesn't use). A $1 trillion pool leveraged with $2 trillion of debt could buy $3 trillion of assets, plus corporate buyers. Deals in the 1970s leveraged buyouts were far more attractive.
- In the 2008 panic Berkshire spent about $16 billion in three weeks (roughly Sept. 15 to Oct. 7 or 8), when nobody else was spending, plus had $3 billion committed to Dow. It tried to buy Constellation Energy.
- Size is his constraint: investing $5 billion in a company at 10% of market cap needs a $50 billion company, and a 50% gain adds only about half a percent to Berkshire. There are perhaps a hundred stocks that can move the needle. He also faces far more competition and far more available data than in 1951, when he dug through manuals and visited the Interstate Commerce Commission and the insurance commissions.

## Reporting Berkshire: operating earnings, market value and the "groves"

- Warren dropped book value as the lead metric because Berkshire is now overwhelmingly an operating company; market value is the more relevant measure over time. He kept both in the letter in a year when the change did not flatter him.
- He wrote the letter as if explaining to his sisters, Doris and Bertie, who hold nearly all their money in Berkshire, whether to sell their shares, since Berkshire may repurchase stock.
- He groups Berkshire's 80-90 businesses and close to 390,000 employees into "groves" (non-insurance businesses, equities, insurance, and cash and Treasuries) so the value of each can be judged, rather than looking at every tree.
- He won't name the diseased trees, for morale reasons; they are a tiny part of earnings and some are simply in industries in decline.
- BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy are the biggest redwoods, together earning about $8 billion after tax last year, about a third of operating earnings.
- Operating earnings were $24.8 billion, 41% above any prior year. GAAP earnings were about $4 billion because of a roughly $20.6 billion unrealized loss on the equity portfolio (new accounting rule: unrealized gains and losses now run through income) and a $3 billion Kraft Heinz write-down.
- He expects to make money on stocks over time but has no idea which years are up or down. Judge them over ten years. The $173 billion portfolio holds companies retaining earnings and shrinking share counts.
- Managers: Greg Abel and Ajit Jain each received about $18 million last year, mostly base salary with a discretionary bonus. Their pay is not deductible above $1 million, which he says doesn't bother him. Ted Weschler and Todd Combs each manage about $13 billion and are a tiny bit behind the S&P since arriving (they had clawback exposure on their pay), but are valuable on acquisitions and other work.

## Betting on America, politics and taxes

- Warren bought his first stock on March 11, 1942 (three shares of City Service preferred for $114.75). A million dollars in the S&P 500 over that time, reinvested, would be $5.3 billion with no need to read a headline or an annual report: "all you had to do was believe in America." Household wealth is about $108 trillion.
- Most people should own an S&P 500 index fund because most people do not know how to pick stocks, and aggregate investors by definition get average returns before fees. His instructions for his widow's money: 90% in an S&P 500 index fund, 10% in governments.
- On politics: he has invested under 14 presidents, 7 Republican and 7 Democrat. He says do not look at investing through a political prism, because a staunch partisan would have missed a great deal over 77 years.
- He believes the rich are under-taxed relative to the general population, and thinks the Earned Income Tax Credit is the best way to help decent people without market skills. He would not move to avoid higher taxes: America is where people want to come.
- The deficit is about $1 trillion (about 5% of GDP) in a good year; a 2-3% deficit would not push debt-to-GDP up.
- He is wary of public pension liabilities in some states; corporate pensions are less of a problem now.
- On tariffs: a 10% tariff has had some effect on Berkshire companies, mostly pushing prices up; 25% would force big adjustments. He hopes sense prevails in trade talks with China and generally expects two smart countries with a lot at stake to reach a rational outcome.
- On new tech IPOs: he dislikes them because the sellers pick the time to sell to you, whereas he likes picking his own time to buy. If a company is worth $50 billion, you should expect it to earn about $5 billion pre-tax within five years, and few do.
- On Bitcoin: it has no unique value and produces nothing, "a delusion basically." He has no knowledge of the marijuana industry.

## Timeless investing fundamentals

- Warren says the fundamentals won't change in the next 50 or 100 years: investing is laying out a dollar of purchasing power today to get more back in the future.
- You must learn how to value businesses, know which are within your circle of competence and which are outside, and know something about how markets operate. "You don't buy a business unless you can value it."
- Rose Blumkin, who founded Nebraska Furniture Mart, arrived in 1917 unable to speak English, started with $2,500 and built the country's largest home-furnishing store, and could not read or write. Berkshire bought about 80% of it in 1983 on a handshake and a one-page contract, valuing it at $60 million on a 100% basis, without an audit. He treated her word as good as the Bank of England's.
- He and Bezos and Dimon's health-care venture is long term, aimed at a medical system now costing about 18% of GDP versus 5% earlier. He calls it a tapeworm on the economy.
