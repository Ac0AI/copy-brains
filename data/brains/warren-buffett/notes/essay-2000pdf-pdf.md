## Where the money comes from: businesses over stocks, and why

- Warren says Berkshire would rather buy whole businesses than marketable stocks, and has said so since publishing its economic principles in 1983.
- Part of the reason is personal: he loves working with Berkshire's managers, who are "high-grade, talented and loyal," and finds their behavior "more rational and owner-oriented" than at many public companies.
- The bigger reason is tax. Owning 80% or more of a business is far more profitable, proportionately, than owning a minority stake.
- Wholly owned earnings of $1 million after tax:
  - Upstreamed dividends carry no tax.
  - Retained earnings add to the tax cost basis, so a later sale at $1 million above cost would bring no capital gains tax.
- A 10% stake in a business earning $10 million after tax, where Berkshire's $1 million share is taxed twice:
  - About $140,000 if distributed (a 14% rate on most dividends).
  - At least $350,000 if retained and later realized as a capital gain (usually about 35%, sometimes nearly 40%).
- His summary: the government is a "partner" twice on a stock investment but only once when Berkshire owns at least 80%.
- Warren said the equity portfolio looked only mildly attractive. Most holdings were "fully priced," with no "bargains," and he expected only moderate returns.
- The long-term outlook for equities in general was "far from exciting."
- Negotiated acquisitions were the most promising use of capital, but he said they "will at best provide us only reasonable returns." Really juicy results come only "when capital markets are severely constrained and the whole business world is pessimistic. We are 180 degrees from that point."

## Valuation: Aesop's bird in the hand, and why "growth vs. value" is a false split

- Warren values every asset bought for financial gain with one formula, which he attributes to Aesop in about 600 B.C.: "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush."
- Three questions fill it in:
  - How certain are you that there are birds in the bush?
  - When will they emerge, and how many?
  - What is the risk-free rate? Berkshire uses the yield on long-term U.S. bonds.
- Answering these gives the maximum value of the bush and the maximum number of birds in hand worth offering for it. "Don't literally think birds. Think dollars."
- The formula applies to farms, oil royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets and plants. The steam engine, electricity, the automobile and the Internet did not change it "one iota."
- Dividend yield, price-to-earnings, price-to-book and growth rates matter only as clues to the amount and timing of cash flows.
- Growth can destroy value if it needs early cash inputs larger than the discounted value of later cash.
- Warren calls "growth" and "value" styles contrasting approaches a display of ignorance: "Growth is simply a component... in the value equation."
- Precision is foolish. He works with a range of possibilities, and the range is often too wide to conclude anything.
- Occasionally even very conservative estimates show a price "startlingly low in relation to value." He jokes about this as the IBT, "Inefficient Bush Theory."
- An investor needs a general understanding of business economics and independent thinking, not "brilliance nor blinding insights."
- Berkshire keeps estimates conservative and sticks to industries where business surprises are unlikely to "wreak havoc on owners."
- He admits many mistakes, such as misjudging the future economics of "trading stamps, textiles, shoes and second-tier department stores."

## Speculation, the bubble and "value creation"

- Where no one can form a conviction about the birds, as with new businesses and rapidly changing industries, any capital commitment "must be labeled speculative."
- Warren defines speculation as focusing "not on what an asset will produce but rather on what the next fellow will pay for it." It is "neither illegal, immoral nor un-American," but Berkshire does not play: "We bring nothing to the party, so why should we expect to take anything home?"
- The line between investing and speculation blurs when most participants have recently won. "Nothing sedates rationality like large doses of effortless money."
- His Cinderella image: everyone plans to leave "just seconds before midnight," but "they are dancing in a room in which the clocks have no hands."
- A December 1999 Paine Webber-Gallup survey had investors expecting 19% a year over the next decade. Warren called that irrational, since for American business as a whole "there couldn't possibly be enough birds in the 2009 bush."
- On "value creation": value is "destroyed, not created, by any business that loses money over its lifetime, no matter how high its interim valuation may get." What actually happens is wealth transfer, often on a massive scale.
- Promoters "merchandising birdless bushes" moved billions from the public to themselves. For many, an IPO rather than profits was the real goal, and the "business model" was "the old-fashioned chain letter," with investment bankers as "eager postmen."
- Two lessons for investors:
  - Many on Wall Street, "a community in which quality control is not prized," will sell investors anything they will buy.
  - Speculation "is most dangerous when it looks easiest."
- Berkshire does not try to pick the few winners from an ocean of unproven enterprises: "We're not smart enough to do that, and we know it."

## How Berkshire buys businesses

- Warren's technique "is simplicity itself: We answer the phone." Owners and managers increasingly want to join Berkshire, and calls were coming more often.
- Cases from 2000:
  - CORT (rent-to-rent furniture): a stranger's fax on Nov 23, 1999 led to a meeting with the CEO on Nov 29. Warren saw the right ingredients: "a fine though unglamorous business, an outstanding manager, and a price... that made sense." Wesco paid about $386 million in cash.
  - Ben Bridge Jeweler: bought by phone, half cash and half stock, on a recommendation from Barnett Helzberg, who had sold Helzberg Diamonds in 1995. Same-store sales rose 9%, 11%, 13%, 10%, 12%, 21% and 7% over seven years. The family feared a jewelry chain would arrive with "ideas about synergy and cost saving," so Warren told Ed and Jon Bridge they would stay in charge.
  - Justin Industries (boots and brick): bought for $570 million in cash. Acme Brick makes over a billion bricks a year at 22 plants, about 11.7% of national output. 75% of Texans name Acme as a brick brand, against 16% for the runner-up.
  - Shaw Industries: the largest carpet maker, about $4 billion in sales, with Berkshire owning 87.3%. Founders Bob Shaw and Julian Saul kept at least 5%.
  - Benjamin Moore: after meeting the CEOs in late August, Warren and Charlie made a $1 billion cash offer on the spot. The deal closed in December.
  - Johns Manville: about $1.8 billion in cash. The LBO deal collapsed on Dec 8, Berkshire called the trust on Monday with an all-cash offer with no financing contingencies, and a contract was signed a week after the trustees voted tentatively.
- The eight acquisitions cost about $8 billion, 97% cash and 3% stock. They added about $13 billion in sales and 58,000 employees, with no debt incurred and Berkshire's share count up only 1/3 of 1%.
- Asbestos: Shaw's deal with an asbestos-burdened partner died when Warren refused to write an uncapped policy: "we would never issue a policy that lacked a cap."
- Why 2000 was a good buying year:
  - Managers and owners expected near-term slowdowns. Several targets will see earnings decline from 1999-2000 peaks. "The declines make no difference to us."
  - Junk bond markets dried up, so leveraged "financial" buyers could not borrow what they wanted and bid less aggressively. Berkshire analyzes purchases on an all-equity basis, "which means we became considerably more competitive."
- Berkshire is often "the buyer of choice." Warren values an owner who cares whom he sells to, because that signals honest accounting, pride of product, respect for customers and loyal associates. Owners who auction off their company "often dress it up for sale," especially "financial owners."
- Berkshire's pitch to the "painter" of a "business Rembrandt" is a permanent home. Promises such as keeping a business headquartered in Fort Worth or unmerged "can take those promises to the bank." "We'll leave the auctions to others."

## Managers: autonomy, retention and the lean home office

- At most large companies, talented divisional managers yearn to be CEO. At Berkshire, "our all-stars have exactly the jobs they want," so they focus on the long-term value of businesses they "own" and love.
- In 36 years, no manager of a significant subsidiary has voluntarily left for another business.
- Warren spends his time allocating capital rather than supervising. "I wouldn't be good at that anyway."
- The home office has 13.8 people, up one in a year when acquisitions more than doubled the workforce to about 112,000.
  - They cover a $40 billion revenue run-rate, more than 300,000 owners, a 4,896-page tax return, and checks to 3,660 shareholder-designated charities.
  - Charlie cites Franklin: "A small leak can sink a great ship."
- Ralph Schey ran Scott Fetzer for 15 years after Berkshire bought it in 1985 (he was then 61), and distributed $1.03 billion to Berkshire against a $230 million net purchase price. Warren puts him in Berkshire's Hall of Fame.
- Warren occasionally moves managers between businesses. Brad Kinstler went from Cypress to Fechheimer and is "hitting home runs." He says Berkshire should try it more often.
- Warren admits Dexter was a mistake: he overpaid in 1993 and compounded it by paying in Berkshire shares. All remaining Dexter goodwill was written off.
- Warren's example of the high cost of keeping production at home: the shoe businesses' attempt to keep most production in domestic factories "has cost us dearly."

## Insurance float: how to judge an insurer, and Berkshire's float

- Warren says to evaluate an insurer on three determinants:
  - The amount of float.
  - Its cost.
  - Most critical, the long-term outlook for both.
- Float is "money we hold but don't own," because premiums arrive before losses are paid, sometimes years earlier. The underwriting loss is the cost of float.
- An insurance business has value if its float costs less than the money it would otherwise have to raise. It is "a lemon" if float costs more than market rates.
- Caution: loss costs must be estimated, so insurers have "enormous latitude" in reporting results, and income statements and balance sheets "can be minefields." Surprises in insurance "almost always are unpleasant."

- Over the years the cost of float has been close to zero, with the worst year at 19% (1984). 2000 had a $1.6 billion underwriting loss, a 6% float cost.
- Two features distort Berkshire's results and are rare elsewhere:
  - Big loss-heavy contracts that other insurers offload, which penalize current earnings but give usable float for many years. Of the underwriting loss, $400 million (27.8%) in 1999 and $482 million (34.4%) in 2000 came from this business. "Pain-today, gain-tomorrow" is welcome when priced properly.
  - Retroactive reinsurance, which assumes past losses with a cap on exposure (always required). Under GAAP, a "deferred charges" asset is set up and amortized as annual underwriting losses for years, often decades. Warren still expects it to be "decent business."
- He expects float cost to be "very attractive" but rarely back to no-cost. Benefits depend on cost and "how effectively we deploy it."
- Ajit Jain, who wrote nearly all the retroactive business, brought in a $2.4 billion premium for a retroactive policy on a U.K. company. Other policies: disability cover on A-Rod's $252 million contract for the Texas Rangers, and the $1 billion Grab.com prize (a $170 million present value).
- Why Berkshire wins these: "Charlie and I... like any proposition that makes compelling mathematical sense, regardless of its effect on reported earnings." Other CEOs avoid the risk of a horrible quarterly number.
- General Re repriced or dropped severely unprofitable business, and Warren expects its float cost to fall materially if there is no mega-catastrophe in 2001.

## GEICO, a low-cost model, and learning from mistakes

- Warren was wrong that extra 2000 advertising would pay off. Inquiries did not rise proportionally, the conversion rate fell for the first time in years, and per-policy acquisition cost jumped.
- GEICO's new policies fell from 1,648,095 in 1999 to 1,472,853 in 2000. Policies in force rose to 4,696,842.
- His four explanations:
  - Frequency was pushed too hard, with diminishing returns: "The third ad in an hour on a given cable channel is simply not going to be as effective as the first."
  - Much low-hanging fruit may have been picked, though reluctance toward direct buying will ebb.
  - Stricter underwriting and rate increases cut conversion. Some prospects will switch to save $200 but not $50.
  - State Farm was slow to raise prices.
- State Farm lost 18% of premiums on auto in 2000 (including rebates), against 4% at GEICO. GEICO's float cost was 6.1%, and Warren estimates State Farm's at about 23%. The industry leader tolerating such a loss makes the economics hard for others.
- He urges business schools to study State Farm, because it succeeded without outside capital, stock options or lavish salaries.
- GEICO nonetheless has "much the better business model" with lower operating costs. In a commodity-like product, being the low-cost producer is "all-important," so GEICO will inevitably gain share. He first became enamored with it in 1951, as a 20-year-old student.
- Tony Nicely, GEICO's CEO, "never engages in wishful thinking," cut ineffective advertising, filed aggressively for price increases and reads loss reports daily. GEICO's profit-sharing targets only policy growth and the underwriting results of seasoned business, and it paid 8.8% ($40.7 million) despite the tough year.
- Post-mortems: "Agonizing over errors is a mistake. But acknowledging and analyzing them can be useful." Warren and Charlie have almost never seen a candid post-mortem of a failed decision, especially an acquisition, in boardrooms. The exception is The Washington Post Company, which reviews acquisitions three years later. Elsewhere, blunders are buried in "nonrecurring" restructuring charges, and "CEOs invoke the concept of the Virgin Birth."

## Honest reporting, look-through earnings and what Warren distrusts

- Full reporting means giving owners what he and Charlie would want if positions were reversed: the important facts on current operations, plus the CEO's frank view of long-term economics. They have "no interest in pictures of personnel, plants or products," are "very suspicious" of vague accounting, and don't want public-relations prose.
- EBITDA makes them "shudder: does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?" FlightSafety spent $272 million on simulators in 2000 and will spend a similar amount this year. Depreciation is a real cost, "every bit as real as payroll or raw materials."
- Reported earnings understate economic progress, so Berkshire uses look-through earnings:
  - Reported operating earnings, plus
  - its share of investees' retained operating earnings, less
  - an allowance for the tax that would be paid if those earnings had been distributed.
- 2000 look-through earnings were $2,415 million: $740 million of undistributed investee earnings, minus $104 million of hypothetical tax (at 14%), added to $1,779 million of reported operating earnings.
- Fair reporting means telling all owners at the same time. Berkshire posts financials on the Internet after the close on Friday, so shareholders have the weekend before markets open.
- He praises SEC Chairman Arthur Levitt for ending selective disclosure to analysts and large holders ("hints, winks and nods"), which gave speculators an edge. Reform came "because of coercion rather than conscience," which Warren says is shameful.
- He calls it "deceptive and dangerous" for CEOs to predict growth rates.
  - A 15% annual EPS growth rate is sustained by very few large companies. He would bet a "very significant sum" that fewer than 10 of the 200 most profitable companies in 2000 will reach it over 20 years.
  - Predictions corrode CEO behavior: uneconomic operating moves, then accounting games that snowball from fudging into fraud. "More money... has been stolen with the point of a pen than at the point of a gun."
  - Charlie and Warren are wary of CEOs who woo investors with fancy predictions, since they may be prophets, congenital optimists or charlatans.
- On media rumors: a Wall Street Journal and CNBC report said Berkshire was buying Conseco and Finova bonds. Berkshire had bought Finova debt, in far smaller amounts than reported, and had never bought Conseco. Conseco stock still jumped on heavy volume. Warren's point is that outlets should label such items "rumors" rather than hiding behind phrases such as "sources say."

## Long-run goals, compounding and Ben Graham

- Berkshire's 2000 net worth gain was $3.96 billion, or 6.5% per share. Over 36 years, book value per share grew from $19 to $40,442, or 23.6% compounded annually.
- Warren and Charlie aim to beat the S&P 500 modestly over time, and note that a small annual edge, sustained, becomes a large long-term gap. The plan needs a few good businesses each year, existing businesses gaining in value, and no material increase in shares outstanding.
- Intrinsic value, not book value, is the real yardstick, and he believed intrinsic value gained moderately more than book.
- Berkshire holds "awash in liquid assets" and is "eager and ready for even larger acquisitions."
- Dec 31, 2000 stock holdings (market value): Coca-Cola $12,188 million (cost $1,299 million), American Express $8,329 million, Gillette $3,468 million, Wells Fargo $3,067 million, Washington Post $1,066 million; total common stocks $37,619 million against $10,402 million cost.
- In 2000 Berkshire sold nearly all its Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae shares, took 15% positions in several mid-sized companies, bought a few high-yield bonds ("the category is not labeled junk without reason") and added to high-grade mortgage-backed securities.
- Ben Graham: Warren entered his Columbia class exactly 50 years earlier. In the prior decade he had "loved" analyzing and trading stocks but got average results. "A few hours spent at the feet of the master proved far more valuable... than had ten years of supposedly original thinking." His debt to Graham is "incalculable."
