## Retained earnings: why stocks compound (Edgar Lawrence Smith, Keynes)

- Warren credits Edgar Lawrence Smith's 1924 book *Common Stocks as Long Term Investments* with changing how investors think. Smith began by trying to prove stocks beat bonds in inflation and bonds beat stocks in deflation, and his facts failed to sustain that theory ("the record of a failure").
- Keynes's review, which Warren quotes, is the key insight: well-managed industrial companies retain part of their profits and put them back into the business, so "there is an element of compound interest operating in favour of a sound industrial investment."
- Before Smith, buyers of shares treated them as short-term gambles on market movements, and "gentlemen preferred bonds." This was odd, since Carnegie, Rockefeller and Ford had built vast wealth by retaining huge portions of earnings.
- Warren's conclusion: "Mr. Smith got it right." Retained earnings of investees are "certain to be of major importance in the growth of Berkshire's value."

## Berkshire's 2019 results and why GAAP net income misleads

- GAAP earnings were $81.4 billion: $24 billion operating earnings, $3.7 billion realized gains and $53.7 billion from higher net unrealized gains (all after tax).
- A GAAP rule imposed in 2018 requires unrealized gains and losses on equity holdings to flow through earnings. Warren and Charlie Munger disagree with it. Before 2018, unrealized gains were excluded and unrealized losses counted only if "other than temporary."
- 2018 showed the problem: unrealized gains fell $20.6 billion and GAAP earnings were about $4 billion. Going to $81.4 billion in 2019 was a "crazy 1,900% increase in GAAP earnings," while equity holdings averaged about $200 billion and intrinsic value grew steadily.
- His advice: focus on operating earnings (little changed in 2019) and ignore quarterly and annual investment gains or losses, realized or unrealized. He still expects the equity holdings to deliver major gains "in an unpredictable and highly irregular manner."

## Capital allocation: reinvest first, then buy good businesses

- Top priority is reinvesting in businesses already owned. Over the past decade depreciation charges totaled $65 billion versus $121 billion of internal investment in property, plant and equipment.
- Acquisition criteria: (1) good returns on the net tangible capital required, (2) able and honest managers, (3) a sensible price.
- He prefers buying 100% of a business, but major acquisitions meeting the criteria are rare. More often "a fickle stock market" offers large non-controlling stakes in qualifying public companies.
- Accounting difference: earnings of controlled companies (over 50%) flow straight into reported operating earnings. For non-controlled stocks only dividends count; retained earnings "are working hard and creating much added value" but are not recognized.
- Top 10 holdings: Berkshire's share of dividends was $3,798 million versus $8,332 million of retained earnings. Examples: Apple 5.7% owned, $773M dividends vs $2,519M retained; Bank of America 10.7%, $682M vs $2,167M; American Express 18.7%, $261M vs $998M; Coca-Cola 9.3%, $640M vs $194M.
- He expects realized gains from the group to at least equal, and probably beat, the earnings retained, though "sometimes, alas, retentions produce nothing." Realized gains are taxed at the then-current rate (21% federal at the time).
- Buybacks matter because they enlarge Berkshire's share of an investee's future earnings.

## Acquisition lessons: Tom Murphy's advice and the marriage analogy

- Tom Murphy's advice: "To achieve a reputation as a good manager, just be sure you buy good businesses."
- Warren's record is uneven: some acquisitions disappointed, more than a few were disasters, a reasonable number exceeded hopes.
- Acquisitions are like marriage: a joyful wedding, then reality diverges from expectations. It is "usually the buyer who encounters unpleasant surprises," and it is "easy to get dreamy-eyed during corporate courtships."
- Mitigating dynamic: poor businesses tend to stagnate and need an ever-smaller share of capital, while good businesses grow and absorb more capital at attractive rates. Winners become a growing share of total assets.
- Extreme example: the original textile business, which in 1965 required nearly all of Berkshire's capital and was a huge drag on returns, but by the early 1980s used only a tiny portion after Berkshire acquired a spread of good businesses.
- Today most capital sits in controlled businesses earning good-to-excellent returns on net tangible assets.
- Earnings are assessed after interest, depreciation, taxes, non-cash compensation and restructuring charges, "all of those pesky, but very real, costs that CEOs and Wall Street sometimes urge investors to ignore."

## Insurance float and underwriting discipline

- Property/casualty insurance has been the engine of Berkshire's growth since 1967, when it bought National Indemnity and National Fire & Marine for $8.6 million. National Indemnity is now the largest P/C company in the world by net worth.
- Model: premiums arrive upfront and claims are paid later (sometimes over decades), creating "float" that insurers invest for their own benefit.
- Float: $39M (1970), $237M (1980), $1,632M (1990), $27,871M (2000), $65,832M (2010), $122,732M (2018), $129,423M (2019).
- Any future decline would be gradual, at most 3% a year, and Berkshire's contracts can never produce immediate demands on cash that matter. If premiums exceed expenses and eventual losses, Berkshire enjoys "free money" and gets "paid for holding it."
- Industry float is worth far less than it was: insurers hold mostly high-grade bonds, and yields on float fell from roughly 5-6 cents per dollar to 2-3 cents (or less). Reaching for lower-quality bonds or illiquid "alternatives" is "dangerous."
- Berkshire has more flexibility thanks to its capital, cash and non-insurance earnings.
- Underwriting profit in 16 of the last 17 years; the exception was 2017 (pre-tax loss $3.2 billion). Cumulative pre-tax gain over the 17 years was $27.5 billion, $400 million of it in 2019. Underwriting discipline is "a religion, Old Testament style."
- He warns that such a streak will not repeat: risks can take decades to surface (asbestos), and a mega-catastrophe, possibly a cyber attack, will come. Berkshire will take big losses but it "will not come close to straining our resources" and will want to add business the next day.
- GUARD Insurance (Wilkes-Barre, PA): bought in late 2012 for $221 million, roughly net worth. By 2019 premiums were $1.9 billion, up 379%, and float was up 265%, with a satisfactory underwriting profit.

## Berkshire Hathaway Energy and reinvesting everything

- Berkshire bought 76% of BHE in 2000 and now owns 91% with Walter Scott Jr. and Greg Abel. BHE has never paid Berkshire a dividend and has retained $28 billion, unlike utilities that often pay out as much as 80% or more of earnings. "The more we can invest, the more we like it."
- Iowa residential rates were 8.8 cents/kWh in 2000 and have risen under 1% a year, with no base rate increases promised through 2028. Rival investor-owned utility rates were 61% higher (soon 70%).
- By 2021 BHE expects about 25.2 million MWh of wind generation in Iowa versus customer needs of about 24.6 million MWh, i.e. wind self-sufficiency. Three of its five largest customers are now high-tech giants.
- BHE could handle utility projects needing $100 billion or more of investment.

## Equities versus bonds, and temperament

- Fifteen largest holdings: $248 billion market value on $110 billion cost (Apple $73.7B, Bank of America $33.4B, Coca-Cola $22.1B, American Express $18.9B, Wells Fargo $18.6B). Kraft Heinz is excluded because it is equity-accounted: carried at $13.8 billion versus $10.5 billion market value.
- He views these holdings not as "stock market wagers" to be dropped over analyst downgrades, earnings misses, Fed actions, politics or economists' forecasts, but as partly owned companies earning more than 20% on net tangible equity, weighted, without excessive debt.
- By contrast, 30-year Treasuries yielded 2.5% or less. Forecasting rates "has never been our game," and pundits "reveal, by that very behavior, far more about themselves than they reveal about the future."
- If current rates and low corporate tax rates persist, equities will almost certainly beat long-term fixed-rate debt.
- Warning: anything can happen tomorrow, including market drops of 50% or more. Still, "The American Tailwind" plus compounding make equities the better long-term choice "for the individual who does not use borrowed money and who can control his or her emotions. Others? Beware!"

## Boards, acquisitions bias and director independence

- Warren has served on 21 public company boards over 62 years. The core board job is unchanged: find and retain a talented, honest CEO devoted to the company for a business lifetime. "When directors get it right, though, they need to do little else."
- Audit committees "remain no match for managers who wish to game numbers," encouraged by earnings "guidance" and the urge to "hit the number." In his limited experience, number-gaming came from ego more than money.
- Compensation committees rely on consultants, making pay plans complex and proxies "mind-numbing."
- Executive sessions without the CEO are a real improvement.
- Acquisitions: he has never seen a CEO who craves a deal bring in an informed critic, himself included. He suggests a board hire two advisors, one pro and one con, paying the winner ten times a token sum. Wall Street benefits from deals: "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut."
- Independence: director pay of $250,000-300,000 for about six meetings a year is 3-4 times median household income, which subtly shapes behavior. Non-wealthy directors seeking a second board need their CEO's endorsement, and CEOs "don't look for pit bulls. It's the cocker spaniel that gets taken home."
- Directors with fortunes tied to the company are often called not independent, while fee-dependent ones are. He prefers directors who bought shares with their own money; one laggard company had eight directors who never had.
- Berkshire seeks business-savvy, owner-oriented directors with a strong specific interest in the company, guided by "thought and principles, not robot-like 'process.'" Competence is narrow: "if you are Bobby Fischer, you must play only chess for money."

## Buybacks, taxes, succession and Berkshire's durability

- Buyback rule: only if (a) Charlie and Warren think the stock sells below intrinsic value and (b) ample cash remains afterward. Intrinsic value is imprecise, so they feel no urgency to buy "$1 of value for a very real 95 cents." In 2019 they spent $5 billion to repurchase about 1% of the company. They want share count to fall over time, but "will not prop the stock at any level."
- Berkshire paid $3.6 billion of current federal income tax in 2019, about 1.5% of the $243 billion corporate total. Fifty-five years earlier it paid nothing, and retaining nearly all earnings benefitted both shareholders and the government.
- Five reasons for confidence about life after Warren and Charlie: (1) assets spread over varied businesses earning attractive returns, (2) controlled businesses housed in one entity with lasting economic advantages, (3) finances managed to withstand extreme external shocks, (4) skilled, devoted top managers for whom the job is more than pay or prestige, (5) directors focused on owners and on a rare culture.
- Warren holds 99% of his net worth in Berkshire stock, has never sold shares (apart from a 1980 exchange for Illinois bank shares, plus gifts and donations), and has no plans to. His will tells executors and trustees not to sell, and absolves them of liability for the concentration. Each year they convert A shares to B and distribute them to foundations that must deploy grants promptly, a process he estimates will take 12 to 15 years.
- He holds that this beats the "safe" fiduciary course of selling into Treasuries, and relies on directors being tested by "Wall Streeters bearing fees."
- Going forward, Ajit Jain and Greg Abel get more exposure at the May 2, 2020 annual meeting and can be asked questions directly.
