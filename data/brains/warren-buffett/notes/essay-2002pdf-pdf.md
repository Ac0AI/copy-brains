## Float: the core of Berkshire's insurance economics

- Float is "money we hold but don't own": premiums arrive before losses are paid, sometimes years earlier, and the insurer invests the money meanwhile.
- Warren evaluates an insurer on three things: (1) the amount of float, (2) its cost, and (3) "most critical of all, the long-term outlook for both."
- The cost of float is the underwriting loss. An insurance business has value if its cost of float over time is below what the company would otherwise pay for funds. It is "a lemon" if the cost exceeds market rates for money.
- Falling interest rates turned formerly tolerable underwriting losses into burdens that push many insurers deep into the lemon category.
- Berkshire's cost has often been below zero (it was paid to hold other people's money). It is not automatic: 2001 cost 12.8% (about half from World Trade Center losses), and 1983-84 were worse.
- Float grew from $20 million (1967) to $1.5 billion (1987), $7.4 billion (1997) and $41.2 billion (2002), up $5.7 billion that year. 2002 float cost about 1%.
- 2002 float by segment: General Re $22.2 billion, Other Reinsurance $13.4 billion, GEICO $4.7 billion, Other Primary $0.9 billion.
- 2002 absorbed $1.75 billion (about 4.6% of float) in charges: a $1.31 billion General Re reserve correction and the "desirable" annual retroactive-insurance charge.
- Warren warns that cheap float needs a caveat: 2002 had no megacatastrophe, and float cost will spike when one arrives.

## Underwriting discipline, reserving and catastrophe aggregation

- Three rules for low-cost float: (a) "underwrite with unwavering discipline"; (b) reserve conservatively; (c) avoid aggregating exposures so that a supposedly "impossible" event threatens solvency.
- General Re broke all three after the 1998 merger. Warren admits: "I was dead wrong." He had studied it for decades and saw no slippage at merger time, but it was grossly mispricing business and had accumulated nuclear, chemical and biological risk that would have been fatal in a large terrorist attack. The World Trade Center loss alone would have threatened an independent Gen Re.
- The fix under Joe Brandon and Tad Montross: write only properly priced business "whatever the effect on volume"; judge themselves only on underwriting profit, not size; cut the NCB aggregation to a tolerable level.
- On reserving: "any insurer that has no idea what its costs are is heading for big trouble." Casualty managers tend naturally to under-reserve, and overcoming that takes a particular mindset that has "nothing to do with actuarial expertise." Reinsurers find it harder than primary insurers. Warren promises it is the top priority but cannot guarantee success.
- Financial strength matters most in reinsurance. Gen Re's three largest competitors were downgraded from AAA, leaving Gen Re alone at AAA across the board. One major reinsurer had all but stopped paying valid claims.
- "'Cheap' reinsurance is a fool's bargain." Paying today for a promise decades away is dangerous unless the reinsurer is the strongest around.
- GEICO case: in 1981-83 it wrote $3,051,000 of commercial umbrella and product liability premium and ceded $2,979,000 to reinsurers, keeping $72,000. Losses from the line total $94.1 million (about 130,000% of net premium), including $90.3 million of uncollectable reinsurance receivables.

## Retroactive insurance and tolerating volatility

- In retroactive insurance, Berkshire assumes another insurer's obligation to pay up to a specified cap for losses already incurred, often decades old, in exchange for a large upfront premium that is less than expected losses.
- Why Warren accepts the gap: payments are capped, and Berkshire uses the money for a decade or more until losses are paid. About 80% of the $6.6 billion of asbestos and environmental reserves come from capped contracts.
- Accounting: premium and reserve are booked immediately, with the difference recorded as an asset, "deferred charges, reinsurance assumed" ($3.4 billion at yearend 2002), then amortized through income. These charges ($440 million in 2002) create an intentional, desirable underwriting loss.
- Ajit Jain's reinsurance unit built $13.4 billion of float from a standing start in 1986 with about 20 people, and posted a $534 million 2002 underwriting profit after a $428 million retroactive charge.
- Warren states that Berkshire assumes far larger risks than any other insurer, so single events can swing quarterly or annual results. He is untroubled: "As long as we are paid appropriately, we love taking on short-term volatility that others wish to shed. At Berkshire, we would rather earn a lumpy 15% over time than a smooth 12%."
- Smaller insurers grew float 38% and earned a $32 million underwriting profit (4.5% of premiums). California workers' comp had reserving problems, so Warren will keep it small until it is fixed.

## Acquisition criteria and why Berkshire "buys to keep"

- Three-legged "entrance" strategy: good-to-great economic characteristics, great managers, and a sensible price. There is no exit strategy, unlike LBO and private-equity operators: "we buy to keep." That makes Berkshire usually the first, and sometimes the only, choice for sellers and their managers.
- 2002 purchases: Albecca/Larson-Juhl (custom picture frames), Fruit of the Loom (about 33.3% of US men's and boys' underwear), CTB (poultry, hog, egg and grain equipment) and Garan (children's apparel, Garanimals). The last two earn over $60 million pre-tax combined, in tough industries but with competitive strengths that earn decent returns on capital.
- The Pampered Chef: Doris Christopher started in 1980 with $3,000 borrowed against a life insurance policy (the only capital ever put in), did $50,000 in year one, and now does over $700 million a year through 67,000 consultants. Warren says it took him "about ten seconds" to decide to partner with her and CEO Sheila O'Connell Cooper.
- Non-insurance earnings: a decade earlier, pre-tax non-insurance earnings were $272 million a year; by 2002 Berkshire earned that amount monthly.
- Warren says Berkshire's "income" of about $500 million pre-tax in 2002 was helped by two favorable breaks: no megacatastrophe, and bond-market conditions favoring its finance strategies, which will fade within a year or two.

## Managers: hands-off management and capital allocation

- No subsidiary CEO has left Berkshire for another employer in 38 years, though many have no financial need to work. Six managers are over 75, and Warren hopes for more: "It's hard to teach a new dog old tricks."
- His job: "stay out of their way and allocate whatever excess capital their businesses generate. It's easy work."
- The Eddie Bennett parable: a batboy who joined the pennant-winning White Sox (1919), Dodgers (1920) and Yankees (1921), then collected a $700 World Series share in 1927. Lesson: "to be a winner, work with winners." Warren hands bats "to many of the heaviest hitters in American business."
- Cost-consciousness is cherished at Berkshire. Warren's model is the widow who placed the obituary "Fred Brown died, golf clubs for sale."
- Shaw's earnings grew from $292 million to $424 million on productivity gains and expense control, even though carpet prices rose only 1%. Berkshire's home and construction businesses earned $941 million pre-tax.
- MidAmerican Energy (80.2% equity, 9.9% voting due to PUHCA) bought Kern River (about 900 million cubic feet a day, $1.2 billion expansion) and Northern Natural Gas (16,600 miles). Once the expansion is done, MEHC will carry about 8% of US gas. Warren funded it with $1,273 million of junior debt and $402 million of common-equivalent stock.
- HomeServices became the second largest US residential brokerage, taking part in $37 billion of transactions (up 100%), and is a cyclical business Warren is happy to keep buying into at sensible prices.

## Derivatives as "financial weapons of mass destruction"

- Warren and Charlie view derivatives as "time bombs, both for the parties that deal in them and the economic system," and as "financial weapons of mass destruction, carrying dangers that, while now latent, are potentially lethal."
- Accounting problem: parties book large profits and losses without any cash changing hands. With no real market, firms use mark-to-model, which can degrade into "mark-to-myth." Marking errors are not symmetrical; they favor traders chasing bonuses and CEOs wanting to report impressive earnings.
- Illustration: General Re Securities held 14,384 contracts with 672 counterparties after ten months of winding down, and its $6.5 billion of receivables remained. It lost $173 million pre-tax in 2002. Berkshire could not sell it and is liquidating.
- Parallels with reinsurance: both are "like Hell, easy to enter and almost impossible to exit," and both generate earnings that are often wildly overstated because they rest on estimates that are not exposed for years.
- Systemic risks: collateral triggers on credit downgrades can spiral a firm into a liquidity crisis; counterparty receivables form a daisy chain that correlates in a crisis; risk is concentrated among a few dealers who trade with one another. LTCM in 1998 required a Fed-orchestrated rescue. Total-return swaps "make a joke of margin requirements."
- Principle: when a chain-reaction threat exists, "it pays to minimize links of any kind." Berkshire wants to be "a fortress of financial strength" for owners, creditors, policyholders and employees.

## Investing: patience, price discipline and junk bonds

- Equities: Warren did little in 2002. Major holdings (Coca-Cola, American Express, Gillette, Wells Fargo, Moody's, Washington Post) had higher earnings and lower valuations, yet he was not inclined to add because they were not yet undervalued. Total common stocks: $9,164 million cost, $28,363 million market.
- He calls the market's excesses "The Great Bubble" and warns that "the hangover may prove to be proportional to the binge."
- His hurdle: "Unless... we see a very high probability of at least 10% pre-tax returns (which translate to 6½-7% after corporate tax), we will sit on the sidelines." In 61 years of investing, about 50 offered such opportunities. "Occasionally successful investing requires inactivity," even with short-term money earning under 1% after tax.
- Berkshire-managed equity gains outweighed losses about 100 to one over 38 years, because Warren buys conservatively financed businesses with strong competitive strengths run by able, honest people at sensible prices.
- Junk bonds and loans: commitments rose sixfold to $8.3 billion. Both stocks and junk bonds require a price-value calculation and scanning hundreds of securities for the few with good reward/risk, but junk issuers are marginal, overleveraged, in low-return industries, and sometimes run by managers whose interests oppose the debtholders'. Warren expects occasional large losses there.
- Berkshire's book value grew from $19 to $41,727 per share (22.2% compounded) over 38 years. He cautions the 32.1-point outperformance in 2002 is "aberrational" and that the goal is only a few points of average annual advantage.

## Corporate governance, audit committees and honest accounting

- Directors should behave "as if there was a single absentee owner," removing mediocre CEOs regardless of likability and slapping the hands of overreaching ones. The failure came from "boardroom atmosphere," not weak laws.
- Real independence requires being business-savvy, interested and shareholder-oriented, not just formally independent. Warren served on 19 public boards over 40 years with about 250 directors, and most lacked at least one of these qualities. He admits he too stayed silent when collegiality trumped independence.
- Mutual fund boards are the 62-year case study: they have only two key jobs, choosing the best manager and negotiating the lowest fee, and have failed at both. A director whose income depends on board fees will not offend the CEO.
- The fix is concentrated owners: twenty or fewer large institutions could reform a company by withholding votes. The acid test is CEO pay. Directors should shout "Less!" for anything short of exceptional performance.
- Berkshire practice: pays directors a pittance, provides no D&O insurance so directors share in any disaster, and will choose directors who hold millions of dollars of Berkshire stock they bought themselves.
- Audit committees "can't audit." Warren's four questions for auditors, with answers reported to shareholders: Would the auditor have prepared the statements differently? Would an auditor-investor have received what he needs to understand performance? Would the auditor run internal audit differently as CEO? Is the auditor aware of any actions that shifted revenues or expenses between periods? Ask at least a week before earnings.
- Option expensing: Arthur Andersen's standards group backed expensing in 1992 and was overruled by rainmaker partners; FASB voted 7-0 for it and the Senate voted 88-9 against; 498 of 500 S&P companies chose not to expense.
- Three tips for investors: beware weak accounting ("There is seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen"); EBITDA treats depreciation as not a real expense, though the cash is paid up front; unintelligible footnotes signal untrustworthy management; and be suspicious of managers who trumpet earnings projections, since "Managers that always promise to 'make the numbers' will at some point be tempted to make up the numbers."
