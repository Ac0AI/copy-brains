## Early life, partnerships and why Warren followed his hero

- Warren always ran small businesses; the best was a pinball machine company (Wilson Coin Operated Machine Company, named after his high school). Barbers wanted newer flipper machines costing about $350, while obsolete machines cost $25. He stalled by saying he'd "take it up with Mr. Wilson," a mythical partner.
- He did not want college; his dad did. He attended Wharton two years, finished at Nebraska in three, and was rejected by Harvard after a 10-minute interview.
- He found Columbia in a library catalog, saw Graham and Dodd taught there, and wrote Dean Dodd: "I thought you guys were dead."
- He later worked for Ben Graham, his hero, for about a year and a half. He had read Graham's books so many times he "could have recited the words... better than he could have," so it was more inspiration than new learning.
- He left New York for Omaha because of family: two kids, grandparents alive, uncles and aunts, and a more pleasant life than commuting from White Plains. He rented a house for $175 a month and bought his current house in 1958.

## Starting the partnerships

- On returning to Omaha he had about $175,000 and thought that was all he'd need for life.
- In May 1956 seven people put in $105,000 and Warren put in $100, giving $105,100. He handed them a short document called the "ground rules."
- Between May 1956 and January 1, 1962 he started 10 more partnerships. With no secretary or accountant, each purchase meant 11 tickets, 11 checks, 11 sets of books and 11 tax returns, all done himself, and he took physical delivery of stocks because it was other people's money.
- On January 1, 1962 he merged all 11 into Buffett Partnership and ran it through 1969, then dissolved it. At that point it held about $105 million; roughly $70 million was cash to distribute and the rest was in three stocks, mostly Berkshire Hathaway, distributed pro rata.

## Why the results came: businesses at sensible prices

- His answer for success: studying companies, sticking to principles, and buying "decent businesses at sensible prices" with "good people to run them."
- At Berkshire the emphasis shifted over time from marketable securities to buying whole businesses.
- He is not against unfriendly deals ("there are managements that should be replaced") but Berkshire isn't interested in them.
- He screens inbound deal calls fast: he can usually tell within two or three minutes whether a deal will happen, because Berkshire's criteria are clear and there are "a half a dozen filters."

## Washington Post: assigning yourself the right story

- The Post went public in 1971. In 1973, with the Nixon administration (through Nixon pal Bebe Rebozo) challenging the licenses of two Florida TV stations, the stock fell from $37 to $16.
- At $16 with about 5 million shares, the whole company sold for about $80 million, including the newspaper, four big TV stations, Newsweek and other assets, with little debt. Warren put its intrinsic value at $400-500 million.
- Berkshire bought most of its stock at the equivalent of about a $100 million market value. The business was worth four or five times the price, and Nixon "wasn't going to put him out of business."
- His advice to Bob Woodward, who asked what to do with his new money: "Investing is just about assigning yourself the right story." Imagine an editor asking what the Post is worth: you'd interview TV and newspaper brokers and owners and value each asset. "That's what I do."
- He only writes stories he can write. He can't value glamorous businesses that don't make money, but he can value something like an electric utility.
- He reads annual reports like novels and does the math in his head: "If you need to carry something out to four decimal places, forget it." Opportunities are fewer now than then.

## How Berkshire's big deals actually get done

- **Precision Castparts (~$37 billion):** about $32-33 billion in cash plus about $4 billion of assumed debt. He met the CEO by accident when the CEO was calling on shareholders (a colleague already held the stock). After hearing him speak for 30 minutes, he had the colleague call the next day to say Berkshire would supply a cash bid if the CEO wanted one. He says that if he'd been out playing golf, it never would have happened.
- **No investment bankers for analysis.** Berkshire will pay commissions when a banker is involved in a deal, but doesn't hire them to analyze. In one deal he offered $35 a share; the target's banker spent a week and then pleaded for a higher price to justify the bill. Warren gave $35.05 so they "got the last nickel out of me."
- **Israeli company:** a letter arrived unsolicited from an Israeli company; Berkshire bought 80% for $4 billion and later the remaining 20%, without Warren visiting Israel.
- **BNSF:** the railroad business "had a bad century" but was rationalized and modernized, and is "not a great business but... a good business." In fall 2009 Berkshire already owned a fair amount. On a Thursday he said Berkshire would pay $100 per share if directors were interested; the board checked over the weekend and a contract was signed the following Sunday.

## Compounding, the U.S. economy and giving

- He calls the U.S. the best place he knows to invest: "nobody has sold America short since 1776." With about 2% growth and just under 1% population growth, per-capita GDP adds roughly $18-19,000 per person (about $75,000 for a family of four) in one 25-year generation. Compounding an already prosperous economy means people live far better in 20 years.
- He says his life has been "a product of compound interest."
- He held off giving much away during the intermediate decades because he felt he was compounding fast enough to give away "billions instead of millions" later. His first wife died in 2004, ending the plan for her to handle his giving, so he chose to give to the Gates Foundation rather than do the work himself. He declined to have his name on it.
- He believes anyone making millions a year should pay at least 30% combined payroll and income tax, and says everyone in his office does except him.

## Bill Gates, Berkshire's culture and advice for young investors

- He met Gates through Meg Greenfield's invitation and Gates's mother, Mary; they talked about 11 hours straight. He bought 100 shares of Microsoft just to keep track of the young founder.
- The first Berkshire annual meeting drew 12 people, including his aunt and uncle; now about 40,000 attend. He never imagined the scale and says he "put one foot in front of the other."
- Money has no utility to him; time does. He gets "to do every day what I love with people that I love," and likens Berkshire to a painting with an unlimited canvas and no finish line.
- Advice: "look for the job that you would want to hold if you didn't need a job." Marrying the right person matters more than earning X versus 120% of X. Don't worry about making the most money this week or next month. He offered to work for Graham for nothing, "and I meant it."
