## Picking people to bet on: the 10% classmate exercise

Warren borrows Pete Kiewit's hiring test: look for integrity, intelligence and energy, and if a person lacks the first, "the latter two would kill them." Dumb and lazy is better than smart and energetic without integrity.

His exercise for students:
- Imagine buying 10% of one classmate's lifetime earnings (no rich fathers allowed). You would not pick the highest IQ, best grades, or even the most energetic.
- You would pick the person with leadership qualities: generous, honest, gives others credit for ideas.
- Then imagine going short 10% of another classmate. That person is egotistic, greedy, or slightly dishonest and cuts corners.
- None of the good qualities is forbidden to anyone. They are behavior, temperament and character, and all are achievable.
- Habits harden: "the chains of habit are too light to be felt until they're too heavy to be broken." Young people can choose their habits now. Ben Graham and Ben Franklin each picked the people they admired and copied them.
- "You already own 100%" of yourself, so be the person you would buy 10% of.

## Careers, money and living well

- Take the job you would take if you were independently wealthy, not one that "looks good on your resume." Warren compares delaying a job you love for resume value to "saving up sex for your old age."
- He offered to work for Graham for free after Columbia; Graham said he was overpriced. He kept pestering him, sold securities in Omaha for 3 years, and kept sending ideas until he got the job.
- Chasing 10X or 20X wealth leads people to borrow money or cut corners they will regret.
- He argues that his life differs little from a student's except for travel (his private plane). He loved his work as much when he made a thousand dollars as he does now.
- On luck, the "ovarian lottery": before birth, imagine designing society without knowing which of 5.8 billion balls you draw (race, sex, health, IQ, country). He says you would want a system that produces abundant goods and services but does not abandon people who drew the wrong ball for it.
- His own ball: he is well wired for a market economy that pays him to allocate capital. Stranded on a desert island, the rice grower would be the most valuable person.
- If he lived again he would do almost the same, and work only with people he likes. Taking a deal with someone who makes his "stomach churn" is "like marrying for money."

## Long-Term Capital Management: why smart people go broke

- Warren was involved in the 1998 rescue. He bid $250 million for the net assets plus $3.75 billion on top ($3 billion Berkshire, $700 million AIG, $300 million Goldman Sachs), with a short fuse because a fixed bid on about $100 billion of moving securities is risky. The Fed-orchestrated bank deal won instead.
- The 16 principals had enormous IQs, 350-400 years of combined experience in their field, and most of their very large net worth in the fund.
- Lesson: "to make the money they didn't have and didn't need, they risked what they did have and did need." That is foolish at any IQ or odds.
- He compares it to a gun with a thousand chambers and one bullet: no payment justifies pulling the trigger once.
- "You only have to get rich once" (Walter Gutman's title). Going from $100 million to $110 million versus $120 million changes nothing, while ruin means disgrace and losing other people's money.
- He rejects reliance on math models: beta "doesn't tell you a damn thing about the risk of the stock," and sigmas do not measure the risk of going broke. History does not give the probabilities of future financial events.
- Blind spots arise where you know a great deal about one thing. He cites Henry Kaufman: those going broke are the ones who knew nothing and the ones who knew everything.
- Warren never borrows money, even when he had $10,000, because it made no difference to his happiness.
- If he wrote a book it would be "Why Smart People Do Dumb Things."

## Businesses he likes: understandable, with a moat and honest management

- "I like businesses I can understand." That cuts the field by about 90%, and a huge universe remains.
- He wants a valuable castle, an honest, hardworking and able duke in charge, and a wide moat. He wants businesses hard for competitors to attack.
- Moat examples:
  - GEICO: low cost, since people must buy auto insurance and judge mostly on price.
  - Coca-Cola: its moat is wider than 30 years ago.
  - Kodak: once as strong as Coke, but it let Fuji narrow the moat (for example by letting Fuji into the Olympics).
- "Share of mind" beats share of market. Kodak, Disney, See's and Coke own a favorable image in customers' heads. A parent choosing a video for the kids buys the Disney one without evaluating alternatives.
- His test: "Give me a billion dollars" (or ten billion) and ask how much you could hurt Wrigley or Coca-Cola. He believes you could not, so those are good businesses.
- He instructs his managers to "widen the moat." A See's clerk smiling at the last customer in a long line before Christmas widens it; a snarl narrows it. You cannot see it daily, but it shows up over ten years.
- He avoids tech businesses whose state in 10 years he cannot see (Oracle, Lotus, Microsoft), even though he calls Gates the best businessman he has met. Chewing gum is predictable; the internet will not change how people chew gum.
- On valuing internet companies: if he taught that exam, he would flunk anyone who gave a value, because he doesn't know how to do it.
- His test for ownership: be happy owning it if the stock exchange closed for 5 years, as you would with a farm or apartment house. "You're not buying a stock, you're buying a part ownership in a business." This is the most fundamental thing Graham taught him.

## Pricing power and the See's Candy purchase

- 1972: See's sold 16 million pounds at $1.95 a pound, earning about 4 million pre-tax (two bits a pound). Berkshire paid $25 million, and the business needed little capital.
- Analysis: Warren and Charlie judged whether there was untapped pricing power, i.e. whether the $1.95 box could sell for $2 or $2.25. An extra 30 cents on 16 million pounds is about 4.8 million on a $25 million price. They hired no consultants; their consulting was buying a box of candy.
- They saw that Californians had a favorable image of See's, like the Valentine's gift that earns a kiss.
- Gifts are not price-dependent: a husband will not hand his wife a box and say "this year I took the low bid."
- He raises prices on December 26th. This year See's will sell about 30 million pounds and earn about $60 million (roughly $2 a pound), with the same formulas and still no capital needs. About $55 million comes in the three weeks before Christmas.
- Coke is global happiness; he says RC Cola could never build that image worldwide with any budget.

## Coca-Cola, Gillette and McDonald's

- Coke's international growth will outpace the US for 20 years, so the Asian crisis is a short-term drag. Timing is tricky, but "you can figure out what will happen. You can't figure out when." Focus on what.
- Cola has no taste memory: you can drink one at 9, 11 and 3 and the 5 o'clock one tastes as good. Most foods "accumulate on you" (See's employees allowed unlimited candy eat the usual amount after a week). So people drink five a day.
- Prices: in 1936 he bought six bottles for a quarter and sold them at a nickel each. Now a 12 oz can costs not much more than 20 cents, only twice the per-ounce price of 1936.
- More than 1 billion 8 oz servings a day. Berkshire owns 8%; an extra penny per serving would be about $10 million a day for Coke, $800,000 a day for Berkshire.
- Coke went public in 1919 at $40, fell to $19 a year later, and survived the Depression, WWII and sugar rationing. One share bought at $40 with dividends reinvested is worth about $5 million. Being right about the business overrides timing.
- Procter & Gamble is a very good business, but he feels surer about Coke's unit growth and pricing power over 20-30 years.
- McDonald's has the strongest position in fast food, particularly abroad, but is tougher than Coke: people don't eat there daily, it doesn't win adult taste tests, and it leans on promotions (beanie babies). He prefers products that sell on their own, like Gillette's Mach 3. For Gillette: "a couple billion men with their hair growing on their faces" overnight.
- Berkshire owns Dairy Queen.

## Circle of competence, mistakes and how he analyzes deals

- Define your circle of competence. "The important thing is not how big the circle is. The important thing is staying inside the circle." Thirty companies is enough if you know which ones.
- He buys with little analysis. He bought General Re (about $18 billion) without visiting the headquarters, and bought Executive Jet after owning a quarter share in the program for 3 years. H.H. Brown shoes came together in about 5 minutes after a phone call, because he knew Frank Rooney and the basic economics.
- His price rule: if the seller's price makes sense he buys; otherwise "I was happy the day before, so I'll be happy the day after without owning it." He does not negotiate much.
- Early research: "scuttlebutt" per Phil Fisher. He asked every CEO in an industry which competitor's stock he would buy and which competitor he would shoot with a silver bullet, then pieced the answers together.
- Cigar butts: Graham-style buying of stocks well below working capital gets "one free puff" and then must be repeated. "Time is the friend of the wonderful business. It's the enemy of the lousy business." A windmill company bought at a third of working capital made money once but not repeatedly.
- His biggest mistakes were omissions: businesses he understood and did not buy, such as healthcare stocks during the Clinton plan fight and Fannie Mae in the mid-80s. These cost billions and don't show up in GAAP.
- Commission mistakes: US Air preferred and Salomon were attractive securities in businesses he would not own the common stock of. Buying because the terms look good, when he doesn't like the business, is a mistake. He jokes that he has an 800 number for "aeroholics."
- Mistakes happen when he holds cash and hangs around the office; Charlie tells him to go to a bar.
- His first stock purchase, 2,000 dollars of his 10,000 dollars into a Sinclair station, lost money, and he puts the opportunity cost at about 6 billion.
- He prefers learning from others' mistakes and does not look back.
- Every purchase must be explainable in the mirror. A cocktail-party tip or a chart is not a reason.

## Macro, diversification, valuation and temperament

- Focus on what is "important and knowable." Macro views are important but not knowable; Coke's business is knowable. Berkshire never buys or avoids a business over macro predictions. If Greenspan and Rubin whispered their plans in his ear it would not change what he pays. If he had avoided See's in 1972 over price controls, he would have missed $25 million turning into $60 million a year.
- Wall Street is stimulating; "Wall Street makes its money on activity. You make your money on inactivity." Brokers are like doctors paid per pill change. Thinking in a room alone beats being around constant price shouting. He aims for one good idea a year, then holds it.
- Buy with no price target. He didn't buy See's planning to sell at $50 million.
- Diversification: for non-professionals, he favors an index fund with very low cost and no trading. For someone willing to evaluate businesses, diversification is a mistake: "If you really know businesses, you probably shouldn't own more than six of them." "Very few people have gotten rich on their seventh best idea." Walter Schloss diversifies widely ("Noah, he's got two of everything") and does fine.
- Net savers should want lower prices: buyers of stocks over the next 10 years should hope stocks go down, like a hamburger eater. Graham's Intelligent Investor chapters 8 (market fluctuations) and 20 (margin of safety) are, to him, the two most important investing essays ever written.
- The stock doesn't know you own it, so feelings about your cost or the price are not reciprocated.
- Berkshire pays no dividend because each retained dollar has become more than a dollar, and he's wary of size: Berkshire's returns would be much higher at 1/100th of the size, and with roughly $75-80 billion to deploy he can only focus on very big companies.
- Japan: a 10-year yen loan at 1% means the hurdle is low, but he found few wonderful businesses there because returns on equity were low, and he avoids currency risk.
- Earlier returns: American business earning near 20% on equity is why large caps did so well, which he is skeptical will last.
- Real estate: he sees REITs as having high operating expenses and double-tax-like drag. Illiquid land valuations are often silly because you cannot sell 20% of the property at the quoted price. He would look at very big real estate deals.
- Utilities: he understands demand but not who will earn the profits after deregulation.
- Berkshire has 12 people at headquarters in 3,500 square feet, and about 45,000 employees. Partner Charlie Munger and he have never had an argument in 40 years.
- He judged turnover as a measure of shareholder quality: he wants Berkshire holders who plan to hold forever, like a church where the same people fill the seats each Sunday.
