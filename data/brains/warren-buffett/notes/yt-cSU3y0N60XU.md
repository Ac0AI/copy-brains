## Buying car dealerships: what makes a good business

- Warren defines a good business as "one that earns a high rate of return on tangible assets." The best ones earn that return and also grow, but even non-growers are good businesses if they earn high returns on tangible assets.
- Price still matters: pay too much and "you can turn a good business into a bad investment." Pay an appropriate price and you can do all right.
- His early mistake was buying a bad business at a really cheap price. It "took me about 20 or 30 years to figure out that wasn't a good idea."
- Car dealerships, run well, can be very good businesses. There are almost no receivables, inventory is financed through floor plan, and real estate can be leased (Berkshire owns about 95% of its own real estate, so the capital tied up is still small).
- Van Tuyl Group, which Berkshire bought in October 2014, has 78 dealerships averaging over $100 million in volume each. That volume allows narrow margins while still earning a high return on capital.
- There are over 17,000 dealerships in the US, down from roughly 30,000 40 or 50 years ago. The average dealer now does far greater volume even though the country and the number of nameplates have grown. [Note: transcript reads "177,000" and "30,000"; the dealer count is left out as ambiguous.]
- Berkshire buys such businesses "to own a 100 years," so the question is whether they make money in 2024 or 2044, not what the Fed or politics does this year.

## Why big banks became a merely good business

- Banks earn on assets, not on net worth. New rules require more net worth per dollar of assets, so with constant earnings on assets, returns on net worth fall.
- Fifteen or ten years earlier the better banks were "ungodly profitable" thanks to high assets-to-net-worth ratios. Some cheated with even more assets than regulators allowed, such as Citigroup's off-balance-sheet vehicles (the CIVs).
- Warren says a very profitable business "has been turned into a good business if executed well."
- Funding is cheap: he says Wells Fargo has roughly a trillion dollars of deposits costing around 10 basis points.
- Banks get into trouble on the asset side, not the liability side or the expense side. They "start copying what their dumb competitors are doing."
- He quotes John Stumpf of Wells Fargo: "I don't know why we keep looking for new ways to lose money when the old ones work so well."
- Copycat behavior is a danger in every business. When someone says "we want to do this because the other guy is doing it," Warren tells managers to "go back to square one and come up with a better reason."
- Example: a well-known property-casualty insurance CEO pitched a life insurance acquisition with weak reasons, then threw up his hands and said, "all the other kids have one."

## The stock market and the "zone of reasonableness"

- Warren once used total market capitalization to GDP (later dubbed the "Buffett indicator") in a Sun Valley talk reprinted in Fortune about 15 years earlier. It showed how extreme things had become when the ratio went from a historical norm of around 40% to a very high level. [Note: he said "170% or something"; he was not precise.]
- It was never meant as fine-tuning. He says the market is not like pi: "there's a range of reasonableness," and most of the time stocks trade within it.
- That range shifts as earnings accumulate and interest rates change.
- He has spoken publicly about the market being outside the range only about five times in his life. The most recent was early October 2008, when he said stocks were cheap in a New York Times op-ed. He expects maybe one definitive call every five or ten years.
- "Anybody thinks they can pinpoint it, it's crazy."
- At the time of this interview (October 2014), he says stocks are in the zone of reasonableness.
- You don't need precision: "if you buy good businesses at reasonable prices and hold them you're going to make a lot of money," and that holds for stocks as a group and for individual companies.

## Ignore macro and politics when making business decisions

- Warren and Charlie Munger have made stock and business decisions together for 55 years. They have never made decisions differently based on which party is in power, whether rates would rise or fall, or labor negotiations.
- "You don't want to give up what you know how to do because of opinions which you don't know whether are right or not and which are going to be transitory in any event."
- Each day you "do whatever makes the most sense." What matters is "whether we get a good business with good management at a sensible price."
- On Washington gridlock he is an optimist: "I always say buy stock in a business that's so good that an idiot can run it because sooner or later one will." The country has been through a civil war and much worse, and "the game isn't over."
- His concern is a situation where a significant portion of people involved "don't even really want to succeed, they want the other person to fail."

## The economy and America's long-run growth

- Since 2009 he sees steady growth of around 2% across Berkshire's 70-plus businesses (railroad freight loadings, autos, and so on). It neither accelerated nor decelerated much. Autos were better than he expected and housing worse.
- The 2008 crisis was unlike other postwar recessions: people were scared, and Treasury bill rates went negative, meaning investors accepted less than they would get "putting money under your mattress."
- Population grows a little under 1% a year, so 2% real growth means over 20% per capita real GDP gain in 20 years. Real GDP is about $54,000 per person, so that is roughly $10,000 more per person in one generation.
- Real GDP per capita has increased six-fold in his lifetime (born 1930). It isn't because Americans are smarter or work harder: "it's because we have a system that unleashes human potential."
- Housing: he expects a catch-up because household formation, which fell in the recession, resumes. "I have a lot of faith in hormones." Berkshire's Clayton Homes will build about 30,000 homes that year, and he is wrong on timing so far.
- On mortgages: a 30-year fixed mortgage is a one-sided instrument. If rates fall you refinance; if they rise you keep your low rate. "I can't get that one-sided an instrument at Berkshire."

## When to sell: recognizing bad businesses and mistakes

- Warren says you usually throw in the towel too late. He bought into textiles in 1965 and left about 20 years later, "about 20 years too late." People tend to hold on to justify old decisions.
- A clear sign of a bad business: a good manager and bad results. "When a management with a reputation for excellence encounters a business with a reputation for bad economics, it's the reputation of the business that remains intact."
- Textile example: Berkshire made over half of men's suit linings in the US and was named Sears' supplier of the year, but when it asked to raise prices a quarter of a cent per yard, Sears said ten others would sell at the old price. No customer asks for a particular lining, so there was no connection to the consumer.
- Dexter Shoe: he paid over $400 million in Berkshire stock, it started losing money right after purchase, and it went to zero because of foreign competition. That stock would now be worth about $5 billion. He had forgotten that shoes would no longer be made in the US.
- If a good person runs a business and it isn't making money, "you're in the wrong business" and you must face it quickly.

## How to think about stock prices and holding or selling

- Warren loves it when things he owns go down: it means he can buy more for less, like finding something cheaper at the grocery store.
- People wrongly treat a falling price as a referendum on themselves. "The stock doesn't care what you paid" and "doesn't even care that you own it." Waiting to get back to your cost before selling makes no sense.
- The only question is whether you can get more for your money somewhere else, though you shouldn't ask it that frequently.
- Investors have an advantage over Andrew Carnegie, who could not shift from steel to retailing. With negligible commissions you can rearrange your holdings at a moment's notice.
- "There is nothing about the price action of the stock that tells you whether you should keep owning." What matters is what you expect the company to do versus the current price, compared with other businesses you understand equally well.

## Crisis anecdotes: Lehman, Barclays, and AIG in 2008

- During the Lehman weekend, Bob Diamond of Barclays called Warren asking what he would charge to guarantee counterparties' contracts for 30 to 60 days until a Barclays shareholder vote. Diamond wanted the indemnity unlimited, which Warren says was "not a great word in the fall of 2008." Warren asked for a faxed offer, but none arrived. He only heard the voicemail months later, in July 2009.
- On AIG, he reviewed property-casualty materials for about an hour and told AIG's advisers he couldn't come up with a number that would help. He needed to know there were no cross-guarantees with AIG Financial Products.
- Tim Geithner later asked whether AIG's value supported an $85 billion Fed loan. Warren said nobody could know that day, but if AIG had staying power, the underlying subsidiaries would probably bring $85 billion in a normal market. They have since proven worth much more.
- In his view, without the New York Fed's money AIG "would have been in bankruptcy within 48 hours." Nobody else would have lent on terms half as onerous.
- 2008 was "like the fog of war," with inaccurate and biased reports from every front. He credits Paulson, Bernanke, Geithner and President Bush for doing the right thing, even if what led up to the crisis can be criticized.
