## How Warren treats mistakes, honesty and reporting to owners

- Warren frames the annual letter as a "report," which implies more responsibility than mandated data. The goal is to communicate the way he would want a CEO to communicate if he were a passive investor trusting someone with his savings.
- He follows advice Tom Murphy gave him 60 years ago: "praise by name, criticize by category."
- He admits mistakes openly: misjudging the future economics of a business (a capital allocation error, in either marketable stocks or 100% acquisitions) and misjudging the ability or fidelity of managers. Fidelity disappointments hurt beyond the money, "a pain that can approach that of a failed marriage."
- A decent batting average in personnel decisions is all that can be hoped for. The cardinal sin is delaying correction of mistakes, what Charlie Munger called "thumb-sucking." Problems "cannot be wished away. They require action, however uncomfortable."
- He used "mistake" or "error" 16 times in his 2019-23 letters, while many huge companies never use either word. He is wary of boards where "mistake" or "wrong" are forbidden words, since that taboo implies managerial perfection.
- If you start fooling your shareholders, "you will soon believe your own baloney and be fooling yourself as well." Greg Abel shares this creed.

## Pete Liegl and Forest River: buying a business and choosing a manager

- On June 21, 2005, Warren got a letter from an intermediary about Forest River, an Indiana RV maker 100% owned by Pete Liegl. It gave the data and the price Pete expected, and said Pete specifically wanted to sell to Berkshire. Warren liked the "no-nonsense approach."
- He checked with RV dealers, liked what he learned, and met Pete in Omaha on June 28.
- Pete wanted to keep running the business but wanted financial security for his family. Warren accepted Pete's valuation of leased real estate without appraisal.
- Compensation: Warren told Pete to name it and he would accept (an approach he says he does not recommend for general use). Pete asked for $100,000 a year ("I wouldn't want to make more than my boss") plus 10% of earnings above the company's current level.
- Warren added that significant acquisitions would bring an adjustment for the additional capital employed. He never defined "appropriate" or "significant," and the vague terms never caused a problem.
- Over the next 19 years Pete "shot the lights out" and no competitor came close. Pete died in November 2024, still working at 80, and contributed many billions to Berkshire shareholders' wealth.
- Takeaways: not every business is easy to understand, and few owners are like Pete. Warren has had many pleasant surprises in both business potential and manager ability and fidelity. A single winning decision (GEICO as a business, Ajit Jain as a manager, finding Charlie Munger as a partner) can make a "breathtaking difference over time." "Mistakes fade away; winners can forever blossom."

## Hiring CEOs: schooling doesn't matter, talent is innate

- Warren never looks at where a CEO candidate went to school. "Never!"
- Great managers come from famous schools, but plenty (like Pete) benefited from less prestigious schools or not finishing. Bill Gates left to get underway in an exploding industry.
- Ben Rosner, a retailing genius who ran a business for Warren and Charlie, never went past 6th grade.
- Warren believes in lifelong learning but observes that "a very large portion of business talent is innate with nature swamping nurture." "Pete Liegl was a natural."

## 2024 performance and how Warren measures earnings

- Berkshire did better than Warren expected in 2024 even though 53% of its 189 operating businesses reported lower earnings. A large gain in investment income came from higher Treasury Bill yields and a much larger T-bill position.
- Insurance earnings rose sharply, led by GEICO. Todd Combs spent five years reshaping GEICO, improving efficiency and modernizing underwriting. Warren calls it "a long-held gem that needed major repolishing."
- Railroad (BNSF) and utility (Berkshire Hathaway Energy) earnings improved in aggregate, but "much left to accomplish." Berkshire raised its utility ownership from about 92% to 100% for roughly $3.9 billion ($2.9 billion cash, the rest in B shares).
- Operating earnings were $47.4 billion in 2024 versus $37.35 billion in 2023. Components for 2024: insurance underwriting $9.02 billion, insurance investment income $13.67 billion, BNSF $5.03 billion, BHE $3.73 billion, other controlled businesses $13.07 billion, non-controlled businesses $1.52 billion, other $1.4 billion.
- Warren emphasizes operating earnings over GAAP earnings because they exclude realized and unrealized gains and losses on stocks and bonds, which swing wildly year to year. Berkshire's horizon for such holdings is "almost always far longer than a single year," often decades, and long-term holdings "sometimes make the cash register ring like church bells."
- Figures are after depreciation, amortization and tax. "EBITDA, a flawed favorite of Wall Street, is not for us."

## Reinvestment and the record tax payment

- Warren took control of Berkshire 60 years ago and calls it his mistake: the price looked cheap, but the northern textile business was headed for extinction. Charlie spotted the error immediately. In 1965 Berkshire paid no income tax, a "blinking yellow light."
- In 2024 Berkshire made four payments to the IRS totaling $26.8 billion, about 5% of all corporate income tax paid by corporate America, plus sizable payments to foreign governments and 44 states. Cumulative federal income tax paid exceeds $101 billion.
- This was possible because shareholders took only one dividend in 1965-2024: $101,755, or 10¢ per A share, on January 3, 1967. Warren says he cannot remember why he suggested it and it "now seems like a bad dream."
- Sixty years of continuous reinvestment built taxable income. Illustration: a $1 million check to the Treasury every 20 minutes through leap-year 2024 would still have left a balance owed at yearend.
- Berkshire is far more "youthful" than in 1965, since old age itself is not lethal to companies. Berkshire could only have achieved its results in America, whereas America would have succeeded without Berkshire.

## Where the money is: controlled businesses versus marketable stocks

- Berkshire owns equities "in both hands." One hand: control (at least 80%, generally 100%) of 189 subsidiaries, worth many hundreds of billions, including a few rare gems, many good-but-not-fabulous businesses and some laggards. "We own nothing that is a major drag, but we have a number that I should not have purchased."
- Other hand: small stakes in about a dozen very large, highly profitable businesses such as Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola and Moody's, many earning very high returns on the net tangible equity needed to operate. These were valued at $272 billion at yearend (down from $354 billion). Outstanding businesses are rarely sold whole, but fractions can be bought on Wall Street and occasionally at bargain prices.
- Berkshire is impartial between the two and deploys capital where it can do best. Often nothing looks compelling; very rarely it is "knee-deep in opportunities," and Greg has shown he can act then, as Charlie did.
- Trade-offs: marketable stocks are easier to exit after a mistake, but Berkshire's size means a year or more to build or sell a position, and minority holders cannot change management or control capital allocation. Controlled companies allow those decisions, but mistakes are harder to dispose of. Berkshire almost never sells controlled businesses unless facing "unending problems," and some owners seek Berkshire for that steadfastness.

## Why Berkshire stays in equities, not cash

- Despite a large cash pile that commentators noted, the great majority of shareholders' money remains in equities, and "that preference won't change." The non-quoted controlled businesses are worth far more than the marketable portfolio.
- Berkshire will forever deploy a substantial majority of money in equities, mostly American (many with significant international operations), and "will never prefer ownership of cash-equivalent assets over the ownership of good businesses."
- Reason: paper money can lose its value if fiscal folly prevails, and fixed-coupon bonds give no protection against runaway currency. Businesses and people with desired talents usually find a way to cope with monetary instability. Lacking special talents, Warren has relied on equities and American business success throughout his life.
- On capitalism: it requires citizens to save and then deploy savings wisely and imaginatively. It has faults and abuses (scoundrels and promoters, failed capital deployed against competition or disruption), but American savings delivered output beyond any colonist's dreams. He urges the government to keep a stable currency and look after those who got "the short straws in life."

## Property-casualty insurance and float

- P/C is Berkshire's core business and has a rare model: payment comes upfront and the true cost emerges later, sometimes 30+ years (asbestos exposures from 50+ years ago are still being paid). Most companies know their product cost before selling.
- Risk: an insurer can lose mountains of money before the CEO and directors notice. In long-tail lines (medical malpractice, product liability) it can report large but fictitious profits for decades, especially if the CEO is "an optimist or a crook." Short-tail lines such as crop or hail insurance report losses quickly.
- Berkshire invests the "float" while generally delivering a small underwriting profit, estimating for "surprises." It prices to absorb rising losses (think wildfires), takes lumps unemotionally and contests runaway verdicts, spurious litigation and fraud.
- Pricing discipline: "We must never write inadequately-priced policies in order to stay in the game. That policy is corporate suicide." Berkshire must shrink when prices are inadequate. It is "not a business for optimists." Mike Goldberg's line: "We want our underwriters to daily come to work nervous, but not paralyzed."
- Contract length: writing ten-year policies for hurricane, tornado and wildfire risk would be "madness," but one-year terms are generally manageable. Auto insurers moved from one-year to six-month policies, which reduced float but allowed smarter underwriting. Insurance growth depends on rising economic risk (300 million US vehicles versus none 135 years ago).
- Advantages: no private insurer can take on as much risk as Berkshire, it can absorb extreme losses financially and psychologically, and it does not depend on reinsurers, giving a durable cost advantage.
- Numbers: over two decades, $32 billion of after-tax underwriting profit, about 3.3 cents per dollar of sales after tax, while float grew from $46 billion to $171 billion. Float has a reasonable prospect of being costless.
- Incentives: Greg, the directors and Warren have very large investments in Berkshire relative to compensation, and Berkshire uses no options or other one-sided compensation. "If you lose money, so do we." This encourages caution but does not ensure foresight.

## Japan: five trading houses and yen financing

- Berkshire began buying ITOCHU, Marubeni, Mitsubishi, Mitsui and Sumitomo in July 2019, after looking at their financials and being "amazed at the low prices of their stocks." Admiration has grown since.
- Warren and Greg like their capital deployment, managements and attitude to investors: they raise dividends when appropriate, buy back shares when sensible, and their top managers are far less aggressive in pay than US counterparts.
- Holdings are for the very long term, with Berkshire committed to supporting boards. Berkshire originally agreed to stay below 10% of each; the companies agreed to moderately relax the ceiling, so ownership will likely rise somewhat.
- At yearend, aggregate cost was $13.8 billion and market value $23.5 billion.
- Berkshire funds this partly with fixed-rate yen borrowings (no floaters), seeking roughly currency-neutral exposure with no view on exchange rates. GAAP requires recognizing yen gains and losses in earnings: $2.3 billion cumulative after-tax gains at yearend, $850 million in 2024.
- Expected 2025 dividend income from the Japanese holdings is about $812 million versus about $135 million of yen interest cost.
