## Announcing the succession: Greg Abel becomes CEO at year end

- Warren reveals at the end of the 2025 meeting (60th annual) that he will recommend to the board, meeting the next day, that Greg Abel become CEO of Berkshire at year end. Two of the 11 directors (his children Howard and Susie) know; the rest will hear it first at the board meeting.
- The board will digest it and act at the following meeting, a few months later. He expects them to be unanimously in favor.
- Warren would "still hang around" and could be useful in periods of great opportunity or trouble, but "Greg would have the tickets" and the final word on operations and capital deployment.
- He thinks the board would be more willing to give Greg authority on large acquisitions if they knew Warren was around.
- He has "zero" intention of selling a single Berkshire share; it will be given away gradually. Keeping every share is "an economic decision" because he thinks Berkshire's prospects are better under Greg than under him.
- Berkshire has a reputation as an asset, not a liability, when government is in trouble, and he sees that as hard to have.
- On what makes Greg suited: Greg described his first meeting with Warren during the MidAmerican deal. Warren went straight to the derivatives on the balance sheet and asked what the underlying risk was. Later he asked how much money MidAmerican was making during the energy crisis. The answer was no more than before, because the derivatives supported the business and were not speculative.

## Cash pile, patience and acting fast

- Berkshire holds about $335 billion in Treasuries. Warren would rather have about $50 billion, but opportunities don't arrive on a schedule.
- He would spend $100 billion if something made sense, was understood, offered good value, and carried no worry of loss. Berkshire came close to spending $10 billion not long before.
- Investing $50 billion a year until cash reached $50 billion "would be the dumbest thing in the world." Berkshire has roughly $40 billion a year coming in.
- "Things get extraordinarily attractive very occasionally." The long-term trend is up, but nobody knows what the market or business will do next week. He has never found anyone worth listening to on the subject.
- Charlie said they made most of their money from eight or nine ideas over 50 years. Warren says if you think you can get an idea a day, "forget it."
- Passive investors can make a few simple investments and sit. Berkshire chose to be opportunistic and has made money by not being fully invested at all times.
- Patience and speed both matter: be patient waiting for the call, but act that same afternoon when a deal makes sense. "You have to be willing to hang up after 5 seconds and you have to be willing to say yes after 5 seconds." You can't be filled with self-doubt.
- Example: in 1966 he bought a business (Associated Cotton Shops) from Ben Rosner's partner's widow for $6 million. It had $2 million in cash, a $2 million Philadelphia property, and earned $2 million a year pre-tax. Rosner said he'd stay only until December 31; Warren told Charlie that if he quit, "you can throw away every book on psychology I've ever read." Rosner stayed on for years.
- Greg adds that being patient means doing the reading and work in advance so Berkshire is ready to act quickly.
- Warren says there's a big difference between a few good ideas and the market's spread of opportunities. In 2008-09 he said lots of things came along; he thinks something big will come within 5 years and that probability rises with time, "like death."

## Market temperament and volatility

- Berkshire shares have fallen 50% three times since he took over, with nothing fundamentally wrong with the company each time.
- The Dow was 381 in September 1929 and fell to 42. When he was born (August 30, 1930) it was 240, and it later hit 41. The 2025 drop was "nothing" by comparison.
- "If it makes a difference to you whether your stocks are down 15% or not, you need to get a somewhat different investment philosophy because the world is not going to adapt to you."
- He expects a "hair curler" in the next 20 years. Surprises get bigger as systems get more sophisticated.
- The market is a good place if you have the right temperament and terrible if you get frightened when it falls and excited when it rises. "You got to check [emotions] at the door."
- He doesn't have emotions about stock prices: "those decisions get all the way to my brain." If Berkshire fell 50% next week, he'd see "a fantastic opportunity."
- Berkshire's best deals come when people are most pessimistic. "Everything isn't properly appraised," and the more other people misjudge, the better the opportunity.
- Quarterly earnings are never a factor in decisions. He never raised a board discussion about how a move would affect annual earnings, and he warns that focusing on a number tempts managers to play with the numbers.

## Insurance, GEICO and float

- Berkshire's insurance advantages can't be replicated. Float is free money as long as the underwriting is profitable; cost of float including life insurance was negative 2.2% in the latest period, "like running a bank where people leave their money with you and you pay a minus 2.2%."
- Warren paid $50 million for half of GEICO in 1976. Berkshire now owns 100%, and 50% of the roughly $2 billion GEICO earned in Q1 is $1 billion, about 20 times the original investment in a quarter. GEICO also provides $29 billion of float.
- Ajit Jain says Todd Combs cut the workforce from over 50,000 to about 30,000 (a reduction of 20,000), worth at least $2 billion a year. GEICO caught up on telematics and matching rate to risk, and has posted a combined ratio starting with an 8 for seven straight quarters.
- Warren on how GEICO began in 1936: Lorimer Davidson (Warren says "Lauren"/"Larmer"), who came from USAA, bet that government employees were better-than-average drivers and priced to make money from the first year. Warren visited GEICO on a Saturday in January 1951, pounded on the locked door until a janitor let him in, and spent hours with Davidson, which changed his life.
- Auto insurance in 1950 cost about $40 a year; now it can reach $2,000 or more. Deaths per 100 million miles fell from about six to a little over one, yet premiums rose about 50 times. Homeowners' premiums in Nebraska doubled in 10 years after inflation and are still unprofitable.
- Self-driving cars: insurance will shift from driver error toward product liability. Ajit and Greg note accidents may fall, but repair costs per accident will rise with the technology.
- Warren said he'd rather have Ajit making property-casualty insurance decisions with $100 billion for 10 years than the top AI product. Berkshire is dabbling in AI but waiting to see; "we are not very good at being the fastest or first mover."
- Berkshire exited the private equity-style life and annuity space. Ajit says PE firms use leverage and credit risk that works only while the economy is good and spreads are low, and regulators could get cranky. Warren notes PE firms copy Berkshire's float model but without the CEO having all his money in the company forever.
- Berkshire and Zurich and Chubb announced a joint operation to write very large risks.

## Japan, currency and the dollar

- Warren found the five Japanese trading companies by reading a thick handbook of Japanese companies, and bought them for about a year at "ridiculously low prices." Berkshire is near the 10% limit and the limit is being relaxed somewhat.
- He expects to hold them for 50 years or forever and won't sell: "we will not be selling any stock." Greg meets them a couple of times a year and hopes to do large things with them globally.
- Berkshire has about $20 billion in these positions, but he'd rather have $100 billion: "size is an enemy" of performance.
- He sets no policy on Japanese rate moves. Berkshire borrows in yen to match yen assets because funding is cheap and the holding is permanent, the first time it has done that, but it is not a policy.
- He says "turn every page" is one important ingredient in investing, and the people who do it won't tell you what they find.
- On the dollar: governments tend to debase currencies over time and no system has beaten that. Fiscal policy is "what scares me" about the US. The only large currency bet Berkshire made was after his Fortune article, when it was long 12 currencies for a couple of years and made several billion dollars. He wouldn't rule out doing it again.
- A country attracting investors should develop a reputation for a solid currency; Berkshire avoids anywhere with a real chance of runaway inflation.
- Fiscal deficits: a roughly 7% gap versus perhaps 3% that is sustainable. "If something can't go on forever, it will end" (Herbert Stein). He says it's a job he doesn't want but should be done.

## Balance sheets, capital intensity and the cathedral and casino

- Warren spends more time on balance sheets than income statements. He likes to look at a balance sheet over an 8-10 year period first, since it's harder to play games with. Wall Street pays little attention to them.
- Charlie's rule: don't take a position until you can argue the opposing case better than the person who holds it.
- Asset-light businesses are better: "it's always better to make a lot of money without putting up anything than by putting up a lot of money." Coca-Cola's syrup business takes little capital; bottling takes real money. Apple has needed almost no capital and shrunk its share count. The Magnificent 7 are now more capital-intensive with AI spending, and he doesn't know whether that holds.
- Property-casualty insurance needs capital only as a guarantee fund, so the float can fund low-capital businesses.
- Money managers get rich by getting an override on other people's capital. If shareholders had paid 1% to manage Berkshire's assets last year, it would have been about $8 billion. He and Charlie put all their own money in and shared the losses.
- US capitalism is "a magnificent cathedral" attached to "a massive casino." The risk is the casino overtaking the cathedral, since people like the bells and drinks.
- Buybacks: Berkshire does none unless shares are almost certainly underpriced on conservative valuation. A 1% tax on buybacks means Berkshire pays more than a seller gets, and costs Apple about $1 billion a year on roughly $100 billion of repurchases.
- Earnings notes: Q1 underwriting income was down sharply from an exceptional prior year, with prices down and risks up. Of 49 non-insurance businesses tracked closely, 21 were up and 28 down.

## Utilities, wildfires and the BHE write-down

- Warren says the mistake with PacifiCorp was not carving it into seven state companies; it came as one aggregate. Some states are now "rat poison," as Charlie would say, for utility investors.
- Value of Berkshire Hathaway Energy fell from roughly $87 billion to $48.8 billion in two years because of societal trend and a real estate ruling, not just PacifiCorp's liabilities. The Scott family sale and the Greg sale happened at different times. He says BHE "is worth considerably less money than it was two years ago."
- Greg on wildfire risk: it can be reduced, not eliminated. Berkshire hardens assets, and now de-energizes lines when fires approach, which went against how employees were trained to keep lights on. Berkshire cannot be the "insurer of last resort."
- The 2020 Oregon fire is the largest, about 60% of claims; Berkshire says it started from a lightning strike outside its territory.
- Warren on Berkshire's role: it won't take your money and attempt unsolvable problems; "it's easier to do stupid things with other people's money than your own."
- Grid: America needs a government-private partnership similar to the interstate highway system or World War II production, because 48 jurisdictions can't coordinate. Berkshire has capital and knowhow but no workable model yet; it's a "clear and present use of hundreds of billions."
- Greg: Berkshire respects state policy. In Iowa it invested $16 billion in renewables and retired five of ten coal units, and still needs the other five to keep the system stable.

## Management, hiring and life lessons

- Greg's capital allocation approach: protect Berkshire's reputation, keep a "fortress" balance sheet and never depend on a bank, and use operating cash flow and float. He looks first at investing in existing businesses, then at buying whole companies, then at owning pieces through equities. He only invests if he can judge economics 5, 10 and 20 years out and understands the risk, whether it's 100% or 2% of a company.
- Greg manages with more engagement than Warren did: questions, linking managers (such as GEICO's tech transformation to others), while keeping autonomy. Warren says some managers need help and some should be left alone.
- Warren on culture: managers' behavior is contagious downward; if a boss games things, employees do too, and it's hard to rebuild. Quality falls more easily than it rises.
- Best manager he knew was Tom Murphy Sr., who "got the potential out of other people." Others he cites: Sandy Gottesman, Walter Scott, Bill Scott. Berkshire "held on to the human assets."
- Advice for young people: associate with people better than you; you go in the direction of those around you. Look for a job you'd hold if you didn't need the money. Be careful who you work for. Don't ignore a job's culture. Find the "sound" you're built for; he found investing at 7-8 years old. Spend time reading and you'll learn.
- A teacher trick: a Warren interview method with CEOs was to ask which competitor's stock they'd own if stranded on a desert island for ten years, and why. He asked it of coal companies 70 years ago. He used an hourglass for a 10-minute meeting.
- Setbacks: look at the bright side; he says people born now are very lucky.
- Warren says the most important trait is not getting rich once; "you only have to get rich once." Never do anything that risks what you've built, and avoid participating in stupid things around you.
- His reading as a boy: his dad's books on Saturdays, then every investment book in the Omaha library. He credits Ben Graham and David Dodd.

## Trade, tariffs and America

- Warren's 2003 Fortune import certificates idea aimed to balance imports and exports, not tariffs as weapons. Charlie thought it "too Rube Goldberg."
- He says trade can be an act of war, and he thinks balanced, cooperative trade benefits everyone. "Trade should not be a weapon."
- In a world with 8 nuclear nations, some unstable, it's unwise for 300 million people to crow while billions are envious. "The more prosperous the rest of the world becomes, it won't be at our expense."
- He says the US's luckiest day for him was the day he was born. America has changed constantly and fixed things slowly (the Constitution's early flaws, the 19th Amendment in 1920), and he says not to get discouraged.
- Health care: he says it's about 20% of GDP and the JPMorgan/Amazon/Berkshire venture couldn't dent it. "The tapeworm won," because the system is entrenched and only government can change it.
- On DOGE and government waste: bureaucracy is contagious, and government has no checks. The real issue is currency and the fiscal gap.
- Ben Franklin question: Warren would tell Franklin to keep thinking, avoid lecturing the world, and build a system that doesn't invent things that can destroy the planet. Fundamental to a stable society is a currency that doesn't get debased.
