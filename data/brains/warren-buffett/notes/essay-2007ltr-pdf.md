## How Warren defines a great business: the moat and the "savings account" test

- Warren and Charlie look for a) a business they understand, b) favorable long-term economics, c) able and trustworthy management, and d) a sensible price tag.
- He likes to buy the whole business or, where management is a partner, at least 80%. When control purchases of quality aren't available, he is happy to buy small stock-market portions of great businesses: "It's better to have a part interest in the Hope Diamond than to own all of a rhinestone."
- A great business needs an enduring "moat" protecting excellent returns on invested capital. Competitors will repeatedly assault any "castle" earning high returns, so a formidable barrier is essential.
  - Low-cost producer moats: GEICO, Costco.
  - Powerful worldwide brand moats: Coca-Cola, Gillette, American Express.
  - Business history is full of "Roman Candles", companies whose moats proved illusory.
- He rules out industries prone to rapid, continuous change. "Creative destruction" is good for society but precludes investment certainty: "A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all."
- He also rules out businesses that depend on a superstar manager. "If a business requires a superstar to produce great results, the business itself cannot be deemed great." A medical partnership led by the area's best brain surgeon loses its moat when the surgeon goes; the Mayo Clinic's moat endures even though you can't name its CEO.
- He wants long-term competitive advantage in a stable industry. Organic growth is a bonus; without it, he takes the earnings and buys similar businesses elsewhere. "There's no rule that you have to invest money where you've earned it."
- Three kinds of "savings accounts":
  - **Great:** pays an extraordinarily high rate that rises over the years.
  - **Good:** pays an attractive rate that is also earned on added deposits.
  - **Gruesome:** pays an inadequate rate and requires you to keep adding money at those returns.

## See's Candy: the prototype dream business

- See's operates in an unexciting industry: per-capita U.S. consumption of boxed chocolates is low and flat, many once-important brands disappeared, and only three companies earned more than token profits over 40 years. Warren believes See's accounts for nearly half the industry's earnings.
- Purchase in 1972 (by Blue Chip Stamps): $25 million for a company with $30 million of sales and under $5 million of pre-tax earnings. Capital required was $8 million, so it earned about 60% pre-tax on invested capital.
- Capital needs were low because the product sold for cash (no receivables) and the production and distribution cycle was short (little inventory).
- Results by 2007: sales of $383 million and pre-tax profit of $82 million, with $40 million of capital needed. Volume grew from 16 million to 31 million pounds, only about 2% a year.
- Only $32 million was reinvested since 1972. Cumulative pre-tax earnings were $1.35 billion, all sent to Berkshire except that $32 million, and redeployed into other businesses.
- Typical companies growing earnings from $5 million to $82 million need about $400 million of capital. Earning $82 million on $400 million is not shabby, but it is "vastly different" for the owner from See's. Others in this camp: Microsoft, Google.
- Durable advantage was built by the See's family over 50 years and strengthened by Chuck Huggins and Brad Kinstler. Brad, moved from running Fechheimer and Cypress Insurance, raised See's profits more than 50% in two years.
- Warren calls himself a "one-price" guy, citing See's, where he nearly walked from the deal.

## Great vs. good vs. gruesome: FlightSafety and airlines

- **FlightSafety (good, not great):** it has a durable advantage ("Going to any other flight-training provider than the best is like taking the low bid on a surgical procedure"), but growth demands heavy reinvestment.
  - 1996 purchase: $111 million pre-tax operating earnings, $570 million net fixed assets.
  - Since then depreciation totaled $923 million, capex $1.635 billion (mostly simulators costing over $12 million each; 273 in use).
  - 2007 pre-tax operating earnings were $270 million, up $159 million on an incremental investment of $509 million: "good, but far from See's-like".
  - Its "put-up-more-to-earn-more" profile is the norm for corporations, including Berkshire's regulated utilities.
- **Gruesome:** "The worst sort of business is one that grows rapidly, requires significant capital to engender the growth, and then earns little or no money. Think airlines."
  - Durable advantage has been elusive since the Wright Brothers; a farsighted capitalist at Kitty Hawk "would have done his successors a huge favor by shooting Orville down."
  - Investors poured money into a bottomless pit, "attracted by growth when they should have been repelled by it."
  - Warren's own error: buying U.S. Air preferred in 1989. The dividend stopped soon after; he got lucky selling in 1998 for a hefty gain; the company then went bankrupt twice.

## Warren's confessed mistakes: See's near-miss, the TV station, Dexter

He stresses these were "unforced errors"; no consultant, board or banker pushed him.
- **Almost blowing See's:** the seller asked $30 million and Warren was adamant at $25 million. The seller caved. Otherwise the $1.35 billion would have gone to someone else.
- **Passing on the Dallas-Fort Worth NBC station:** Tom Murphy of Capital Cities offered it for $35 million (a forced divestiture tied to a newspaper purchase).
  - TV stations were See's-like: little capital needed, good growth, cash-rich.
  - Murph, a friend he admired, "whispered 'buy'", but Warren didn't listen.
  - In 2006 the station earned $73 million pre-tax, at least $1 billion cumulatively since, with a capital value of about $800 million.
  - His explanation: "my brain had gone on vacation and forgot to notify me."
- **Dexter (worst deal to date):** a shoe business bought in 1993 for $433 million in Berkshire stock (25,203 A shares).
  - The competitive advantage vanished within a few years.
  - Paying in stock compounded the error: the cost to shareholders was about $3.5 billion, not $400 million. He gave away 1.6% of a wonderful business, then valued at $220 billion, for a worthless one.
  - He expects more mistakes. Quoting Bobby Bare on acquisitions: "I've never gone to bed with an ugly woman, but I've sure woke up with a few."
- General Re (merged 1998): he missed that its Tiffany reputation "outlived its factual underpinnings"; the 1998 company wasn't the 1968 or 1978 one.

## Managers, deal-making style and the Marmon purchase

- **Berkshire's managers:** many have no financial need to work (they sold for large sums) and run their businesses because they love it. They have the job they want for the rest of their working lives, unlike managers elsewhere who see a division as a way station. Their scorecard is long-term performance, not getting Warren's job: "a here-today, here-forever mindset." He calls this rare, hard-to-replicate structure a real advantage.
- **Hiring:** Warren and Charlie "are not big fans of resumes"; they focus on "brains, passion and integrity."
  - Susan Jacques started at Borsheims 25 years earlier as a $4-an-hour saleswoman and became CEO in 1994 without a managerial background: "She's smart, she loves the business, and she loves her associates. That beats having an MBA degree any time."
  - Cathy Baron Tamraz, who began as a cab driver, grew Business Wire's earnings.
- **Marmon:** the largest cash purchase in Berkshire's history, signed Christmas Day 2007.
  - Berkshire buys 60% now for $4.5 billion and nearly all the rest within six years at a price tied to an earnings formula.
  - Marmon: about 125 businesses in nine sectors, $7 billion of sales, about 20,000 employees; Union Tank Car and its Canadian counterpart own 94,000 leased rail cars with an original cost of $5.1 billion.
  - Price was set from Marmon's financial statements alone, with no advisors and no nit-picking. "With the Pritzkers, as with Berkshire, a deal is a deal": the seller could trust the business, and Berkshire would close on the dot however chaotic the markets.
  - Warren's interest began in 1954 when Jay Pritzker's cocoa-bean tender for Rockwood Chocolate impressed him.
  - Byron Trott of Goldman Sachs, "the rare investment banker who puts himself in his client's shoes," facilitated it.
- **Richline:** a small jewelry-supplier combination (Dennis Ulrich) bought below Berkshire's normal earnings threshold; Warren bet on the manager building a large, high-return operation.

## Insurance, float and underwriting discipline

- Berkshire's value has two buckets: investments ($141 billion at yearend, excluding finance and utility operations) and earnings from non-insurance, non-investment businesses (66 companies).
- Float of $59 billion funds the investments. It is "free" as long as underwriting breaks even (premiums equal losses plus expenses). Underwriting is volatile, but Berkshire has been profitable over its history, and Warren expects breakeven or better, making the investments an unencumbered source of value.
- Per-share investments grew from $4 (1965) to $90,343 (2007), 27.1% a year, but the pace slowed as funds went into operating businesses. Per-share pre-tax earnings of non-insurance businesses grew from $4 to $4,093, 17.8% a year, accelerating to 23.5% over 1993-2007.
- Warren warns the past record "can't be duplicated or even approached" because the asset base is too large.
- Insurance results: float grew from $18.5 million (1967) to $58.7 billion (2007); underwriting profits were $3.4 billion in 2007 and $3.8 billion in 2006.
- He warned that 2007 benefited from a second straight catastrophe-light year, that prices were falling, and that industry margins would fall about four points in 2008. He expects lower insurance earnings for a few years.
- GEICO: the widest moat of any Berkshire insurer. Market share rose from 2.5% (1995) to 7.2%, while ad spend rose from $31 million to $751 million; it is expanding into motorcycles (2.1% to 6%), ATVs, RVs and commercial auto. CEO Tony Nicely has been with the company since age 18.
- General Re (CEO Joe Brandon, with Tad Montross) restored underwriting discipline and holds $23 billion of float. Ajit Jain's reinsurance unit takes one-of-a-kind mammoth transactions; the Equitas deal moved capped liabilities for a single $7.1 billion premium.
- Reserving is an "honor system": the $56 billion loss reserve (about $3 billion discounted) is a guess that is "sure to be wrong." Payments can run 50 years or more. He tells the "rented suit" funeral story about hidden, unknown liabilities and says he believes reserves are adequate but has "been wrong several times in the past."

## Evaluating investments, sales and derivatives

- Warren measures stock holdings by the same two tests as owned businesses, not by yearly price moves: (1) improvement in earnings, allowing for industry conditions; (2) the more subjective test of whether the "moat" has widened.
- In 2007 American Express, Coca-Cola and Procter & Gamble grew per-share earnings 12%, 14% and 14%; Wells Fargo's slipped, though he believes its intrinsic value still rose slightly.
- Start-ups are "not our game": Amex and Wells Fargo date from 1850 and 1852, P&G from 1837, Coke from 1886.
- **PetroChina sale:** bought 1.3% in 2002-03 for $488 million, valuing the company at about $37 billion; Charlie and Warren thought it worth about $100 billion. By the second half of 2007 the market valued it at $275 billion, roughly their view of its worth against other giant oil companies, so they sold for $4 billion and paid $1.2 billion of tax.
- **Derivatives:** 94 contracts.
  - 54 contracts cover defaults in high-yield bond indices; premiums received were $3.2 billion, losses paid $472 million, and the worst-case further payout is $4.7 billion.
  - Index put options on the S&P 500 and three foreign indices have 15- or 20-year terms, exercisable only at expiration (2019-2027); premiums received were $4.5 billion against a recorded $4.6 billion liability.
  - He sees both as profitable in aggregate and notes Berkshire holds the money, so there is no counterparty risk.
  - Mark-to-market accounting may swing quarterly earnings by $1 billion or more; he and Charlie won't mind, trading short-run earnings volatility for long-run net-worth gains, as in catastrophe insurance.
- **Currencies:** the only direct position in 2007 was the Brazilian real; direct currency positions earned $2.3 billion pre-tax over five years. In 2001-02 Berkshire bought €310 million of Amazon 6 7/8 bonds due 2010 at 57% of par (cost $169 million), which were priced as junk but weren't; "you can occasionally find markets that are ridiculously inefficient." About $118 million of the $246 million gain came from the falling dollar.
- Trade deficit view: a falling dollar hasn't fixed it (U.S. deficit with Germany rose from $36 billion to $45 billion as the euro rose; with Canada from $50 billion to $64 billion). Roughly $2 billion a day of "force-fed" dollars goes abroad. He favors reducing the imbalance sooner rather than later, without singling out countries or industries.

## Housing and lending bubble; pension and accounting games

- **Lending folly:** "weakened lending practices" and the belief that house prices would rise forever (HPA) made borrower income and cash equity seem unimportant. "You only learn who has been swimming naked when the tide goes out." Clayton Homes' $11 billion portfolio of 300,000 borrowers performed in line with prior years.
- **Pension return assumptions:** the average S&P company assumed 8% (2006). With about 28% in bonds and cash earning no more than 5%, the rest must earn 9.2% net of fees.
  - The Dow rose from 66 to 11,497 in the 20th century, only 5.3% a year. Matching that this century would require the Dow near 2,000,000 by 2099; a 10% annual return would imply about 24,000,000.
  - Dividends are about 2%; subtracting costs of 0.5%, equities would give about 7% even with 5.3% appreciation.
  - CEOs choose high assumptions to report higher earnings, with the cost landing after they retire. Charlie's line: "If you've hit three balls out of bounds to the left, aim a little to the right on the next swing."
- **Active vs. passive:** all investors earn the average return minus costs; index investors have very low costs, so active investors, with high transaction, management and advisory costs, must lose as a group. "The passive group – the 'know-nothings' – must win." "Beware the glib helper who fills your head with fantasies while he fills his pockets with fees."
- **Stock options:** CEOs lobbied the Senate to 88-9 to override FASB's push to expense options; only 2 of the S&P 500 chose the preferred route to expensing in six years, and some backdated grants.
- **Public pensions:** huge promises, inadequate funding, long fuse; politicians promise early retirement and generous COLAs that will be hard to keep.

## Berkshire's operating model, succession and temperament

- Warren and Charlie view Berkshire's four sectors (insurance, regulated utilities, manufacturing/service/retail, finance) as separate businesses because their balance sheets differ.
- Manufacturing, service and retailing earned 23% on tangible net worth with minor leverage, but 9.8% on carrying value because of large premiums paid, reflected in goodwill.
- MidAmerican: Berkshire owns 87.4% with Walter Scott, Dave Sokol and Greg Abel; major moves are made only when all are unanimous. Warren agreed to buy at $35.05 a share in 1999 (he had decided the business was worth $35.00, then "caved" to give the bankers a nickel); 2007 earnings were $15.78 a share, or $15.01 normalized, so he was glad he wilted.
- Housing-linked units (Shaw, Acme Brick, Johns Manville, MiTek) saw pre-tax earnings fall 27%, 41%, 38% and 9%. Warren calls the setbacks minor and temporary since competitive positions and CEOs remain strong.
- Tuck-in acquisitions (Shaw, MiTek, Acme) continue.
- Succession: three internal CEO candidates are identified and the board knows its pick; four candidates for the investment role have been identified, all wealthy or well-off and motivated by more than compensation.
- Warren's attitude: he and Charlie, 84 and 77, say they "tap-dance to work" because they love the work and are helped by talented associates.
