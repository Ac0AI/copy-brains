## How Warren treats stocks as businesses and why Apple is his largest holding

- Warren and Charlie looked at marketable equities as businesses, whether the stock was Dairy Queen, Coca-Cola, American Express or Apple. They cannot buy 80-90% of every wonderful company, but they evaluate each stock as a business.
- He makes no attempt to predict markets or pick stocks. He spent years doing the wrong thing, fascinated by stocks, charts and moving averages, until he read *The Intelligent Investor*.
- The Graham lesson: treat the market as something there "to serve you," not to instruct you. Warren says the approach is "so simple that it's almost deceptive," and that investing needs the right mindset rather than ever more math or physics.
- Barring something dramatic that changes capital allocation strategy, Berkshire will keep American Express, Coca-Cola and Apple, and Apple will remain the largest common stock holding.
- He calls American Express "a wonderful business," Coca-Cola "a wonderful business," and Apple "an even better business." He says return on tangible equity at Coca-Cola, American Express and Apple makes utility returns look like "a whole different game."
- He is comfortable building cash under current conditions, given what is available in equity markets.

## Taxes, the fiscal deficit and why selling some Apple was acceptable

- Warren says he does not mind paying taxes, and Berkshire pays 21% federal on the gains it realizes in Apple. That rate was 35% not long ago and 52% in the past.
- The government effectively owns a percentage of Berkshire's earnings and can change that percentage any year. He thinks current fiscal policy cannot continue and that higher taxes are quite likely.
- Berkshire sent over $5 billion to the US Treasury last year. He says that if 800 other companies did the same, no other person in the country would need to pay federal taxes of any kind. He hopes Berkshire stays in "the 800 club" and moves up.
- He frames the tax payment as appropriate for a company in a country that has been so generous to Berkshire's owners, and says shareholders shouldn't mind that Berkshire sold some Apple.
- Treasury market size is not his worry. He says he doesn't worry about the quantity of US debt but about the fiscal deficit, which the media ignores while fixating on the Fed. The risk is inflation and the future value of the dollar, as Paul Volcker understood. He sees no real alternative to the dollar as a reserve currency.

## Berkshire's economic goal, retained earnings and the cash pile

- Berkshire's goal is to increase operating earnings and reduce shares outstanding. Warren says that is simple to describe, "not quite so simple to pull off."
- Operating earnings are the figure Berkshire uses because they strip out wild market swings. First-quarter operating earnings were $11.2 billion, helped by higher yields on short-term holdings. Investment income was certain to rise for the year.
- The earnings history he showed: $24 billion pre-pandemic, then a pandemic dip, then $27 billion, $30 billion and $37 billion. These figures are after depreciation, amortization and taxes. Warren says that works out to a little over $100 million per day to deploy, including weekends.
- Shareholders' equity was $574 billion (he also cites $571 billion) at March 31. Berkshire has retained earnings since taking control, apart from one 10-cent dividend decreed in about 1968 while he was "in the restroom."
- Compounding on a long runway is the mechanism, "without any Miracles." JP Morgan, second on equity, had $327 billion at year end and about $338 billion at quarter end. But it pays dividends and repurchases shares, so it does not plow back as Berkshire does.
- Cash and Treasury bills were $182 billion at quarter end, and he expected about $200 billion by the end of the next quarter. He says nobody at the table knows how to use it effectively right now. It would sit there at 5.4% or at 1%.
- "We only swing at pitches we like." He would not swing because he had passed on the last two pitches.
- Buying back stock adds slightly to value at current levels, but Berkshire can't repurchase in size because holders don't sell. The investors in the room don't think about selling, and "the people who check the price daily have not made the money" that those who forgot about it have.
- Large sums are hard to deploy. He says he could earn high returns on $10 million, and that something like Japan would have been better at a smaller size. He is not on "a hunger strike" and would do another Japan-style deal if one appeared.

## Japan, Costco, BYD and investing abroad

- Warren says his primary investments will always be in the United States. He expects anything really big to be American, and says he doesn't pick up on other cultures well.
- The US went from about half a percent of world population to well over 20% of world output in a short time. He feels less likely to make a major mistake with your money in the US than in many other countries.
- Coca-Cola is the preferred soft drink in something like 170-180 of 200 countries, which he calls almost unmatched. American Express has strengthened dramatically over 20 years.
- The Japan commitment, made five years ago, was "overwhelmingly compelling." Berkshire bought as fast as it could over a year. He feels extremely good about the position.
- Charlie pounded the table with him twice, once for BYD and once for Costco. Warren says he should have been more aggressive in Costco. It was not fatal, but Charlie was "right big time" on both.
- Canada is comfortable: Berkshire would put big money there if an idea met its standards. He mentioned that a deal in a financial institution in Canada was done in a couple of days.
- India could be great, but the question is whether Berkshire has an advantage. He said a more energetic management could pursue it, since Berkshire's name is known worldwide.

## Recognizing a big investment: Apple, Geico and Occidental

- Warren says Charlie and he decided very fast, but only after years of thinking about the parameters that enable the quick decision.
- Consumer behavior knowledge came from mistakes and small businesses. Berkshire's Baltimore furniture chain failed, but taught him how people behave. See's Candy was a study in consumer behavior, too. He did not learn how to run a department store or make candy.
- With Apple, he watched customers at the furniture store refuse to leave without one, even at prices with no profit, and he came to see it as "one of the great products maybe the greatest product of all time." People would give up a $30-35,000 second car before their iPhone, which costs a twentieth as much.
- He credits Tim Cook as a partner to Steve Jobs who did one thing extraordinarily well.
- The mental process resembles a "perceptive mass" crystallizing: many observations and knowledge then produce action, and "you can prepare yourself for it happening tomorrow."
- Geico: in 1950 Lorimer Davidson taught him in four hours on a Saturday what auto insurance was.
- Occidental: the original decision came over a weekend, then he kept buying as he learned more about the assets, the operations and the CEO's capital allocation. Berkshire holds options to buy more and intends to keep it. He does not know the price of oil.
- Paramount: he was 100% responsible for the decision, and Berkshire sold it all and lost a fair amount of money. He says he's smarter about the entertainment business now, but he paid for that knowledge.
- Warren sold shares for several reasons, including needing the money, but that rarely applies to Berkshire.

## Insurance: float, pricing and underwriting discipline

- Insurance is the most important business at Berkshire. Ajit Jain joined in 1986 and Warren says Berkshire won't find another, but the structure he built means the business will still be very good.
- Contracts run mostly one year, so Berkshire can reprice or exit each year. Ajit said the one-year horizon is what lets Berkshire stay in climate-exposed lines. Prices need to go up a lot, and regulators who block exits make that harder. Warren says climate change "makes our business bigger over time" provided risks are priced correctly: "if there was no risk there'd be no insurance business."
- On hurricane risk, Warren prefers one very smart underwriter like Ajit over a thousand analysts of water currents.
- Inflation done right helped the risk bearer. Geico had about 175,000 policies at about $40 a car (roughly $7 million of volume) in 1950, and now writes about $40 billion.
- Geico's edge: lowest costs, with the underwriting expense ratio below 10%. It lagged in matching rate to risk and data analytics, and Ajit expected it to be among the best by end of 2025. Warren says it isn't a survival or profitability threat, and Geico had about 16 million policies without losing policyholders in March.
- Cyber insurance: Warren and Ajit see aggregation as the problem. A single event could link thousands of policies, and there is little data on loss costs. Ajit told underwriters to assume every cyber policy loses money, whatever the price. Warren said everyone will rush into fashionable lines because agents like them, and "as Charlie would say it may be rat poison."
- Florida: lawyers and fraud push losses up, and storms add more. Berkshire grew exposure last year and nothing bad happened. Ajit expects rates to rise and a workable balance in time.
- On autonomous cars, Warren says fewer accidents would lower insurance volume but be good for society. Ajit noted repair costs per accident have skyrocketed.

## Capital allocation, succession and management

- Greg Abel will be CEO, and capital allocation, including the common stock portfolio, should rest with him. Warren says knowing businesses means you understand common stocks, and he would not split it among many managers. Greg said the capital allocation principles of today will continue.
- Greg sees equities as investing in a business, whether 1% or 100%, judged on economic prospects, sustainability, and what it looks like in 10 years, with excess cash in Treasuries to keep the "Fortress of a balance sheet."
- Warren says most of the job of the directors is picking the right CEO. The CEO must not be someone who wants to retire at 65. The other 1% is correcting a wrong pick, which is very hard.
- Managers now prefer to call Greg and Ajit, and Warren gets close to zero calls. He says Greg sees more of the managers, handles weak performers and delivers hard news well, as Charlie and he did not.
- Warren expects huge sums to be needed instantly maybe once every 10 years. In 2008-09 others had money but were paralyzed. Berkshire acted, and the government treated it as an asset. He wants Berkshire seen as an asset, not a supplicant.
- Tom Murphy was the best business manager he knew. The key is buying the right business: a great manager can't fix a terrible one, which is why he stayed too long in textiles, partly liking Ken Chace.
- Pilot: a couple of directors had doubts, but Warren says "all's well that ends well." He praises Adam Wright's story and the business, with over 800 locations on interstates averaging 10-12 acres.
- Tech Data: Berkshire bid in 2019 and the original bidder raised, and "we never make the same offer twice." Distribution is a decent business, not one to dream about, as TTI (Paul Andrews) shows. Berkshire won't pursue small acquisitions itself.
- Railroads: Warren bought BNSF in 2009 and says a 100%-owned rail is more tax-efficient than partial stakes. Greg said BNSF margins trail the other five Class I railroads because costs were not reset to demand after 2022. Fixes include yards, locomotive fleet and labor allocation. Railroads are essential and could not be built today.

## Utilities, energy and regulatory risk

- Berkshire Hathaway Energy had severe earnings disappointment last year. Warren says utilities are asked to earn modest returns, and "whether you earn X or go broke is not an equation that works." He said Berkshire would invest $100 billion or more but will not "throw good money after bad."
- Greg: demand will double in Iowa by the mid-2030s and triple in Nevada, requiring a working regulatory compact. After wildfires, Berkshire changed its culture to prioritize de-energizing lines and speed shutoffs. Utah caps non-economic wildfire damages and created a fund, which Greg called the gold standard.
- Warren expects Utah to treat Berkshire fairly. Solar will not be the only source. Batteries run about four hours, and gas is needed for reliability, so Nevada's last two coal units retire and are replaced by a new gas unit.

## Temperament, heroes and life lessons

- Warren advises thinking about how you'd like your obituary to read, then choosing your path. "Find the job you would like if you didn't need a job." He urges being kind: "you can be kind."
- He values a partner who never lies. Charlie never told him a half-lie or shaped a story. Warren says to surround yourself with trusted people, as he has since his 20s.
- Pick the right heroes by values and motivation. His father loved him even when he broke rules (the runaway-from-home story).
- He tells shareholders not to beat themselves up over the past. He believes the future can be changed and that "if you're lucky in life make sure a bunch of other people are lucky too."
- Luck matters: he was born male and American, and says anyone claiming to have done it all alone is delusional.
- Shareholders defer consumption for decades and then give it away. Ruth Gottesman gave $1 billion of Berkshire stock to Albert Einstein College of Medicine, making tuition free.
- Estate: he leaves his wife 90% in an S&P 500 index fund and 10% in short-term government bonds. He urges everyone to have a will (four presidents died intestate).
- Starting small: he'd read every page of manuals like Moody's, becoming deeply interested in obscure companies. With $1 million one could earn 50%, but "you have to be in love with the subject."
- AI: he compares it to the nuclear genie. He saw a deepfake of himself that he could not distinguish, and says scamming will be the "growth industry of all time."
