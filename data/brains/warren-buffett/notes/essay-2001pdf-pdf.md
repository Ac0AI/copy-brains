## Relative performance, and the "Ground Rules"

- Warren has judged results against general securities experience since forming his first partnership on May 5, 1956. His "Ground Rules" said: "Whether we do a good job or a poor job is to be measured against the general experience in securities."
- The benchmark started as the Dow Jones Industrials and moved to the S&P 500 once that index became widely used. In 2001 Berkshire beat it by 5.7 percentage points, even though book value fell 6.2% (a $3.77 billion loss in net worth).
- Per-share book value grew from $19 to $37,920 over 37 years, a 22.6% compounded annual rate. Intrinsic value grew somewhat faster than book value over that span.
- He answers "you can't eat relative performance" this way. If you expect the S&P 500 to deliver reasonably satisfactory results over time, small annual advantages over it must be rewarding for long-term investors. See's loses money in the summer months, yet it is a profitable business across the year, and variable absolute returns can work the same way.
- Another Ground Rule still holds: "I cannot promise results to partners."
- He expects Berkshire's future record to fall well short of the past. Valuations were lower then, and he worked with far less money. A good $10 million idea (Washington Post in 1973, GEICO in 1976) once moved the needle. Today ten such ideas, each tripling, would add only ¼ of 1% to net worth. Berkshire now needs "elephants."

## Owner-orientation, incentives and shareholder abuse

- Warren and Charlie promise that shareholders' results will parallel theirs. They take no cash compensation, restricted stock or option grants that would make their results better than shareholders'.
- He keeps well over 99% of his net worth in Berkshire. He and his wife have never sold a share and don't intend to.
- He is disgusted by CEOs and promoters who caused billions in shareholder losses and left rich. Some urged investors to buy while quietly dumping their own shares. These leaders see shareholders "as patsies, not partners."
- His illustration of the attitude: a CEO at a party is asked what he wants and answers, "Reprice my options."
- At MiTek, 55 managers bought 10% of the company, each putting in at least $100,000 of cash. Many borrowed to do it.
  - Warren says these managers are real owners, unlike option holders. They face downside as well as upside, incur a cost of capital, and can't "reprice" their stakes. "What they paid is what they live with."
- All 2001 acquisitions were paid in cash, so existing shareholders gave up no interest in the companies they already owned. The formula: raise the value of the excellent businesses already owned, add businesses of similar quality, and issue shares "only grudgingly."
- Berkshire completed 37 years without any operating-business CEO choosing to leave for another employer.

## How Warren finds and buys businesses

- Deals often start with the seller's own letter or call.
  - MiTek: its CEO, Gene Toombs, mailed Warren a "connector plate" (a part used in roofing trusses) along with a letter. A one-minute call showed Warren he was "our kind of manager" in "our kind of business." A cash offer to the U.K. parent followed, and a deal came quickly.
  - Larson-Juhl: owner Craig Ponzio called on December 3rd. Warren had never heard of the company, a maker of custom picture frames. Ponzio had grown sales from $3 million to $300 million since buying it in 1981. He was "straightforward in describing the business, cared about who bought it, and was realistic as to price."
  - The Larson-Juhl deal was agreed in 90 minutes in Omaha and signed within ten days. The company serves about 18,000 framing shops in the U.S. and leads in Canada and much of Europe.
- XTRA (truck-trailer leasing): Warren approached it after Julian Robertson announced he was liquidating Tiger Fund. The deal came about 18 months later, when Robertson decided to sell. Warren calls trailer leasing cyclical but expects "decent returns over time."
- Fruit of the Loom was bought out of bankruptcy.
  - It failed from too much debt and poor management. Warren's offer carried no financing conditions, though it had to stay open for months.
  - He insisted on one unusual condition: John Holland, who had run it in its best years and returned after bankruptcy, had to stay as CEO. In Warren's view, "John and the brand are Fruit's key assets." Holland cut employment from a bloated 40,000 to 23,000.
  - Warren had earlier bought the company's public and bank debt at about 50% of face value. Interest on the senior debt kept being paid, so he earned a current return of about 15%. He expected to recover roughly 70% of face value, which indirectly lowered his purchase price for the whole company.
- The 1955 P&R deal shows the kind of bargain he admires. Union Underwear (Fruit of the Loom) cost $15 million, and it held $5 million of cash and earned about $3 million pre-tax. Of the remaining $12.5 million owed, $9 million was paid in non-interest-bearing notes, payable from half of earnings above $1 million.
- He quotes Horace, from the frontispiece of *Security Analysis*: "Many shall be restored that now are fallen and many shall fall that are now in honor." His appreciation for it keeps growing. Acme Boot, once the world's largest bootmaker, ended up as remnants bought by H.H. Brown for $700,000.

## Insurance economics: float, cost of float, underwriting

- Three things determine an insurer's value: (1) the amount of float it generates, (2) its cost, and (3) most critical, the long-term outlook for both.
- Float is "money we hold but don't own." Premiums arrive before losses are paid, sometimes years earlier. An underwriting loss is the cost of float.
- An insurance business has value if its cost of float over time is below what the company would otherwise pay for funds. It is "a lemon" if the cost exceeds market rates.
- Berkshire's float rose from $20 million in 1967 to $35.5 billion in 2001 (GEICO $4,251 million, General Re $19,310 million, Other Reinsurance $11,262 million, Other Primary $685 million).
- Berkshire's float cost was below zero in about half its years. In 2001 it was 12.8%, the worst since 1984, after 6% in 2000.
- With short-term funds earning under 2%, float costing 4% is no longer tolerable. Every insurance operation except retroactive reinsurance must now earn an underwriting profit to count as a good business. Retroactive reinsurance has desirable economics even though it runs an underwriting loss of about $425 million a year.
- GEICO made a $221 million underwriting profit in 2001, so Berkshire was paid to hold $4.25 billion of float. Premiums rose 6.6%. Policies in force fell 0.8%.
- Warren and GEICO's CEO would like to spend more than the $219 million marketing budget but "cannot see how to do so effectively." GEICO's operating costs are far below its competitors'.
- The other primary insurers wrote $579 million of premium (up 40%), grew float 14.5% to $685 million, and made a $30 million underwriting profit.

## The three underwriting rules, and Warren's 9/11 mistake

- Size and brand name don't decide an insurer's profitability. Underwriting discipline does. The winners stick to three rules:
  1. Accept only risks you can properly evaluate (your circle of competence) that carry an expectancy of profit after weighing remote loss scenarios. Ignore market share, and be "sanguine about losing business" to competitors offering foolish prices or terms.
  2. Limit business so that no single event or related events can create an aggregation of losses that threatens solvency. Search "ceaselessly" for correlation among seemingly unrelated risks.
  3. Avoid moral risk: "trying to write good contracts with bad people doesn't work."
- General Re broke all three rules in 2001. It misjudged its costs by under-reserving, and it was "overly-competitive" in chasing and retaining business. If "winning" means market share rather than profit, "trouble awaits." "'No' must be an important part of any underwriter's vocabulary."
- Both Warren and the industry priced from experience rather than exposure. They ignored large-scale terrorism, so a huge risk was assumed without premium.
  - Experience is a useful starting point, for example how many 6.0+ California earthquakes occurred in a century. At times, though, it is dangerous.
  - Late in a bull market, directors and officers liability losses look rare, but exposure is exploding through "ridiculous public offerings, earnings manipulation, chain-letter-like stock promotions." Priced on exposure, the correct rate on excess D&O might be five or more times the rate that experience dictates.
- He admits he saw the terrorism risk before September 11th but didn't act. "I violated the Noah rule: Predicting rain doesn't count; building arks does."
- He still takes more risk than any other insurer knowingly takes. Berkshire is willing to lose $2 billion to $2½ billion in one event, as it did on September 11th, if it is paid properly for the risk. Its tolerance for huge losses is a competitive advantage, backed by liquid resources, non-insurance earnings, a favorable tax position and a shareholder base that accepts earnings volatility.
- He will cap total exposure "no matter what the competition does." For example, Berkshire won't cover many towers in one city without excluding losses from a nuclear blast and the fires that follow.
- Ajit Jain (18 associates) writes huge, fast, disciplined policies. Warren has known the details of nearly every policy since 1986 and never saw Ajit break a rule.
  - Examples written and retained: $578 million of property cover for a South American refinery above a $1 billion loss; $1 billion of third-party terrorism liability for large international airlines; £500 million on a North Sea oil platform above £600 million; and Sears Tower cover above $500 million.
  - His insight: "insurers produce outstanding long-term results primarily by avoiding dumb decisions, rather than by making brilliant ones."
- On reinsurance chains: a weak link in a "daisy chain" of retrocessionaires can sink everyone. Stress-test every participant under a mega-catastrophe in a bad economy. "You only find out who is swimming naked when the tide goes out." Berkshire retains its risks and depends on no one.

## Reserves, accounting language, and "EBITDA"

- "Bad terminology is the enemy of good thinking." Warren objects to "EBITDA," "pro forma," "loss development" and "reserve strengthening" as terms that make investors accept flawed concepts.
- Loss reserves are a liability for losses that have occurred but are unpaid, including IBNR (incurred but not reported) losses. Accurate estimates are vital, because otherwise managers don't know their true costs against the premiums they charge.
- GEICO nearly failed in the early 1970s by severely under-reserving. It believed its product cost less than it did, so it underpriced and sold more at larger losses.
- General Re recognized $800 million of costs from earlier years in 2001. The under-reserving hid its true costs, led to inadequate pricing, produced overstated profits, caused incentive pay it should not have paid, and brought income taxes earlier than needed.
- "The losses didn't 'develop' – they were there all along. What developed was management's understanding of the losses." A forthright label would be "loss costs we failed to recognize when they occurred" (or "oops").
- Insurance accounting is "a self-graded exam." Companies in trouble seldom grade tough, and under-reserving is common among companies struggling to survive. Surprises in insurance rarely help earnings, so conservatism is essential.
- He opposes discounting reserves to present value. Reserves can't be precisely established, since judicial broadening of policy language and medical inflation keep pushing them up, and discounting would give companies inclined to fudge a new tool.
- On EBITDA, FlightSafety spends $162 million on 27 simulators in 2002 against annual depreciation of $95 million. Those who think EBITDA equals true earnings "are welcome to pick up the tab."
- Goodwill amortization will end under new GAAP rules. That raises reported earnings "though not our true economic earnings."

## Operating businesses and mistakes

- Dexter shoes: three costly decisions, all his own. He bought it, paid for it with stock, and procrastinated when change was obviously needed. He wrongly concluded Dexter could cope with brutal low-cost foreign competition. The shoe operations lost $46.2 million pre-tax in 2001. Dexter went under the management of Frank Rooney and Jim Issler at H.H. Brown.
- R.C. Willey: the "closed on Sunday" policy defied conventional retailing wisdom, and Warren was skeptical it would work in Boise or Las Vegas. Chairman Bill Child put up $11 million of his own money for the Boise store, selling it to Berkshire at cost, without interest, only if it succeeded.
  - The store succeeded. The Henderson (Las Vegas) store now outsells every other store in the chain and runs at twice Warren's expectation.
  - Berkshire people now say "What does Bill think?" about retailing questions. Warren keeps the planned Kansas City Nebraska Furniture Mart (450,000 sq ft) open Sundays.
- Retailing in 2001: jewelry same-store sales fell 7.6%, pre-tax margin was 8.9% against 10.7%. Home-furnishings margin was 9.1% pre-tax.
- Shaw, Johns Manville and Benjamin Moore together earned about $659 million pre-tax in their first year. Berkshire swapped 4,740 A shares for Shaw's 12.7% minority interest.
- NetJets accounts for about half the industry by value of customers' planes. Scale (300+ planes) lowers positioning costs, but under current conditions the edge "will at best produce modest profits." It lost a small amount in 2001.
- Warren also notes Lou Simpson's independent $2 billion GEICO portfolio, whose results have recently beaten his own.

## Investing outlook, junk and distressed debt, and capital sourcing

- Warren's own equity results have been poor for several years. Berkshire's larger holdings have done poorly as a group (Coca-Cola, Gillette, American Express, Wells Fargo and others, with $28.7 billion of market value on $8.5 billion of cost).
- He likes the businesses, but doesn't think the equity holdings "as a group" are undervalued. He is "decidedly lukewarm" on stocks over the next decade, expecting only moderate returns because prices were still high after the bubble. The NASDAQ peaked at 5,132 on March 10, 2000 (recently 1,731), the day Berkshire hit $40,800, its lowest price since mid-1997.
- "The market outperformed business for a very long period, and that phenomenon had to end." A market that only keeps pace with business may disappoint investors who are new to the game.
- Junk bonds are unsuitable for the general public. Berkshire has never bought a newly issued junk bond, and losses in the field are often disastrous. Warren has made money in distressed debt for 50 years, in situations including Washington Public Power System, Chrysler Financial, Texaco and RJR Nabisco.
- FINOVA: Berkshire bought 13% of about $11 billion of debt at roughly two-thirds of face value. He expected bankruptcy but judged that liquidating the assets would pay creditors above his cost.
  - Berkshire and Leucadia proposed a prepackaged plan: creditors get 70% of face value plus full interest, and a new 7½% note for the other 30%.
  - To fund the 70% payout, a jointly owned entity (Berkadia) borrowed $5.6 billion through FleetBoston and re-lent it to FINOVA with a priority claim on its assets. Berkshire guaranteed 90% of the borrowing and earns 90% of the roughly two-point spread.
  - He pulled a follow-on offer after September 11th, because the aircraft assets and other receivables FINOVA held were impaired.
- "Debt is a four-letter word around Berkshire." Berkshire borrowed almost nothing from banks for three decades. When it needed $10 million in 1969 (Illinois National Bank), the Boston bank flew people to Omaha and said "we'll work out the details later." In 2001 FleetBoston did the same for a $6 billion loan, which 17 banks oversubscribed.

## Charity and culture

- Operating subsidiaries make their own charitable decisions, but former owner-managers must give to personal charities from their own funds, not company money.
- At the parent level, Berkshire gives only what shareholders designate. A fixed per-share amount goes to up to three charities of each registered A-share holder's choice. In 2001 that was $16.7 million, directed by 5,700 holders. Cumulative shareholder-directed gifts total $181 million.
- Warren compares it to paying a dividend that the shareholder then donates, but more tax-efficient. Berkshire follows owners' wishes, including for Planned Parenthood or Metro Right to Life, as long as the charity qualifies under 501(c)(3).
- Berkshire never considers religion, gender, race or sexual orientation in hiring or buying. Doing so would be wrong and "idiotic," because able and trustworthy people come from "a very wide spectrum of humanity."
- It is "not our style to tamper with successful business cultures," so acquired subsidiaries keep their existing employee-match programs.
- Headquarters has 14.8 employees, in 5,246.5 square feet, for a workforce of 110,000.
