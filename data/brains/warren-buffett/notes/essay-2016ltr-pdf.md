## How Warren reads Berkshire's book value versus intrinsic value

- Per-share book value rose 10.7% in 2016 (net worth up $27.5 billion). Over 52 years it grew from $19 to $172,108, a 19% compounded annual rate.
- Early on, book value roughly equaled intrinsic value because most assets were marketable securities marked to market.
- By the early 1990s the focus shifted to owning whole businesses. GAAP requires writing down "losers" but never writing up "winners", so intrinsic value now "far exceeds" book value, especially in property/casualty insurance.
- Warren has "no quarrel with the asymmetrical accounting", but it necessarily widens the gap over time.
- "Over time, stock prices gravitate toward intrinsic value." That is why Berkshire's 52-year market-price gain exceeds its book-value gain.

## Capital allocation, retained earnings and acquisition mistakes

- Warren and Charlie expect normalized earning power per share to rise every year, even if actual earnings dip with the economy or insurance catastrophes.
- Berkshire retains all earnings. It ranked first among US businesses in dollars retained in 2015 and 2016, and "those reinvested dollars must earn their keep."
- No magic plan: "dream big" and be ready to act fast. "Every decade or so, dark clouds will fill the economic skies, and they will briefly rain gold... rush outdoors carrying washtubs, not teaspoons."
- Mistakes he names:
  - Dexter Shoe (1993): $434 million, paid in 25,203 Berkshire shares worth over $6 billion at yearend 2016. Its value "promptly went to zero".
  - General Re (1998): issued 272,200 shares, a 21.8% increase in shares outstanding. Berkshire holders "give far more than they received".
- Course-correcting deals:
  - 1996: bought the other half of GEICO for cash.
  - 2000: bought 76% of MidAmerican Energy for cash (now 90%).
- Resulting strategy: build insurance, buy large diversified non-insurance businesses, fund deals mostly from internal cash. "Today, I would rather prep for a colonoscopy than issue Berkshire shares."
- Berkshire's shares outstanding grew only 8.3% from 1999 to 2016, mostly from the BNSF purchase, which he calls an issuance that "made good sense".
- After-tax operating earnings grew from $0.67 billion (1999) to $17.57 billion (2016). Investment gains are expected to stay substantial but random in timing, and they help finance business purchases.
- Blunders in the manufacturing, service and retailing group:
  - Some businesses produce "very poor returns".
  - He misjudged economics or industries, and sometimes the fidelity or ability of managers.
  - "I will commit more errors"; Charlie "is around to say 'no' to my worst ideas."

## Share repurchases: price is everything

- Exiting shareholders always benefit from repurchases. For continuing shareholders, repurchases make sense only below intrinsic value.
- His arithmetic: three equal partners in a $3,000 business. Buying one out for $900 gives each remaining partner a $50 gain. Paying $1,100 costs each $50.
- Whether a buyback creates or destroys value is "entirely purchase-price dependent."
- It is puzzling that announcements almost never name a price ceiling. Managers would never ignore price buying an outside business.
- Two exceptions even when shares are cheap:
  - The business needs all its cash to protect or expand operations and is uncomfortable adding debt (assuming it has a decent future).
  - A business acquisition or other investment offers far greater value than the undervalued shares.
- His line for CEOs and boards: "What is smart at one price is stupid at another."
- Berkshire policy:
  - He may buy large amounts at 120% of book value or less, a deliberate discount to intrinsic value because estimates can't be precise.
  - Berkshire will not "prop" the price at 120%.
  - Buybacks have been hard to do, likely because the clear policy signals intrinsic value is well above 120%.
  - Warren prefers the stock to trade in a narrow range around intrinsic value. Overpriced shares disappoint owners, and buying out "partners" at a discount isn't gratifying.
- Warren rejects calling buybacks "un-American". Capital is plentiful, and he knows of no enticing project that died for lack of capital.
- Berkshire likes investees' buybacks when shares are underpriced.

## Insurance float and underwriting discipline

- P/C insurance has propelled Berkshire since 1967 (National Indemnity and National Fire & Marine bought for $8.6 million). Premiums come in upfront and claims are paid later, creating "float".
- Float grew from $39 million (1970) to $91.6 billion (2016), and passed $100 billion after a huge policy. It may drift down, but no more than about 3% a year. Contracts are structured so no near-term demands threaten cash, "by design" and "never compromised".
- Underwriting profit makes float "free money", and Warren says you get "paid for holding it". Competition often pushes the industry into underwriting losses, which is what it pays to hold float. Falling bond yields will further hurt the industry's float income.
- Berkshire's edge:
  - Underwriting profit for 14 straight years, totaling $28 billion pre-tax.
  - Disciplined risk evaluation is "a religion, Old Testament style."
  - Unmatched financial strength gives investment flexibility: "When others are constrained, our choices expand."
- Float on the balance sheet is a full liability under GAAP. Warren says it is better seen as a "revolving fund": about $27 billion was paid to over six million claimants in 2016, while new business replaces it.
  - If float is costless and long-lasting, its true value as a liability is "dramatically less" than the accounting figure.
  - The $15.5 billion of insurance goodwill was almost all on the books in 2000, when float was $28 billion. Float has since grown $64 billion with no book-value credit, a "huge reason" intrinsic value exceeds book.
- The four disciplines of insurance:
  1. Understand all exposures.
  2. Conservatively assess likelihood and cost of loss.
  3. Set a premium that profits after losses and expenses.
  4. Be willing to walk away if the right premium isn't available.
  - Many insurers fail the fourth. "The other guy is doing it, so we must as well" spells trouble, especially in insurance.
- Catastrophe resilience: in a $250 billion industry mega-catastrophe (about triple any prior loss), Berkshire would likely still record a large profit, and would be ready to write business in a disrupted market.
- Units:
  - Ajit Jain (reinsurance) insures risks others won't or can't take. Warren says he has created tens of billions of value; he'd swap himself for another Ajit.
  - Tad Montross retired after 39 years at General Re, succeeded by Kara Raiguel.
  - Tony Nicely runs GEICO. Charlie's line: "it's great to have a manager with a 160 IQ – unless he thinks it's 180."
  - Berkshire Hathaway Specialty Insurance, led by Peter Eastwood, grew volume 40% to $1.3 billion in 2016 and was profitable during startup.
- 2016 pre-tax underwriting profit $2,131 million (2015: $1,837 million); yearend float $91,577 million.

## GEICO's low-cost moat

- Warren first visited GEICO in 1951 and was "blown away" by its cost advantage over industry giants. "GEICO would succeed because it deserved to succeed."
- Sales were $8 million in 1951. In 2016 GEICO did that much business every three hours.
- Auto insurance is a major family expense, so "only a low-cost operation" can deliver savings. Low costs create "a moat – an enduring one."
- Share rose from 2.5% (1995) to about 12% (2016). Employment grew from 8,575 to 36,085.
- In late 2016, auto loss costs rose and competitors pulled back from new customers. GEICO accelerated new-business efforts: "We like to make hay while the sun sets."
- "When insurance prices increase, people shop more. And when they shop, GEICO wins."

## Regulated capital-intensive businesses: BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy

- BNSF and BHE (90% owned) had huge long-lived regulated assets and long-term debt not guaranteed by Berkshire. They produced 33% of 2016 after-tax operating earnings and invested $8.9 billion in plant and equipment.
- Debt is safe because earning power far exceeds interest. BNSF's interest coverage was above 6:1 in a weak railroad year.
- He measures coverage as EBIT/interest, not EBITDA/interest, which he calls "seriously flawed."
- BHE's debt safety rests on recession-resistant utility demand and diverse earnings streams across regulators.
- Warren trusts regulation: governments have a self-interest in ensuring capital flows to essential projects, and Berkshire should earn regulators' approval with low prices.
  - Iowa: BHE averages 7.1¢/kWh versus a 10.3¢ national average. Base rates are frozen until 2029 at the earliest.
  - BNSF revenue is about 3¢ per ton-mile versus 4¢ to 5¢ at the other four major railroads (a crude comparison).
  - Iowa wind generation equaled 55% of retail sales in 2016, heading to 89% by 2020.
  - Railroads move a ton almost 500 miles on a gallon of diesel, about four times as fuel-efficient as trucks.
- 2016 net earnings: BNSF $3,569 million; BHE $2,542 million ($2,287 million to Berkshire).
- HomeServices (from the MidAmerican purchase) owns 38 realty companies with over 29,000 agents. Berkshire expects to buy more realtors.

## Operating businesses, accounting distortions and "adjusted earnings"

- The manufacturing, service and retail group (44 businesses reporting to headquarters) earned 24% after tax on about $24 billion of net tangible assets in 2016, with excess cash and little debt.
  - Returns vary: a couple exceed 100%, most are 12% to 20%, a few are poor.
  - Warren notes that "a business with terrific economics can be a bad investment if it is bought at too high a price." Berkshire has paid substantial premiums to net tangible assets, hence large goodwill.
  - "It's the growth of the Berkshire forest that counts." Don't over-focus on any single tree.
- He shows segment results off GAAP because amortization of purchase intangibles (about 19-year average life) is mostly not a true economic cost. He judges about 20% of the $1.5 billion 2016 charge to be "real". Once assets are fully written off, GAAP earnings rise with no real improvement ("my gift to my successor").
- GAAP depreciation can understate true economic cost. At BNSF, depreciation was $2.1 billion, but holding its own requires spending "far more." Reported railroad earnings exceed economic earnings. Even so: "Charlie and I love our railroad, which was one of our better purchases."
- On "adjusted earnings":
  - He dislikes managements that feature figures above GAAP by dropping "restructuring costs" and "stock-based compensation."
  - "Bad behavior is contagious": it can lead to practices like insurers underestimating loss reserves.
  - Berkshire restructures every year and has never told shareholders to ignore it. One-time costs should be explained when truly major, such as the Kraft Heinz merger.
  - Stock compensation is an expense. Berkshire pays similar executives in cash and counts it all as expense. Executives should have to explain why valuable pay items are not a cost, or why a payroll cost should be excluded.
  - He cringes at praise for managements who always "make the numbers". Business is too unpredictable, and a CEO focused on Wall Street is tempted to "make up the numbers."

## Finance businesses, investments, cash and taxes

- Clayton Homes:
  - Became America's largest home builder in 2016 (42,075 units, 5% of new US homes). It makes close to half of the manufactured homes in the US, which are about 70% of new homes under $150,000.
  - Its earnings benefited from very low rates, since it lends fixed-rate long and borrows short. Warren normally shuns that, but Berkshire as a whole is asset-sensitive.
  - It foreclosed on 8,304 loans (about 2.5% of the portfolio). It offers extensions (about 11,000 borrowers) and payment forgiveness (3,800 borrowers, $3.4 million). Of borrowers helped in the last two years, 93% remain in their homes. Foreclosure losses were $150 million.
  - Berkshire provided staying power in the Great Recession, when other lenders vanished. "We never count on synergies when we acquire companies," yet important ones appeared here.
- Marmon railcars: utilization fell to 91% from 97%. Warren expects earnings to drop in 2017 but likes the business.
- Pre-tax finance earnings: $2,130 million (2016).
- Top holdings at yearend 2016 included Wells Fargo (cost $12.7 billion, market $27.6 billion), Coca-Cola (cost $1.3 billion, market $16.6 billion), IBM and Apple. Kraft Heinz was carried at $15.3 billion against $28.4 billion market value (cost $9.8 billion).
- Todd Combs and Ted Weschler each independently manage over $10 billion. Warren usually learns of their decisions from monthly trade sheets.
- Bank of America preferred: $5 billion paying $300 million a year, with a warrant for 700 million shares at $5 billion before September 2, 2021. Yearend profit on the warrant would have been $10.5 billion.
- Cash:
  - Of $86 billion in cash and equivalents, 95% is held in the US, so repatriation taxes are not an issue. Offshore cash is "simply not worth as much."
  - Cash in insurers is slightly less valuable than parent-level cash.
- Stocks are not held "forever": no commitment that Berkshire holds marketable securities permanently. Economic Principle 11 covers controlled businesses only.
- Dividend taxes beat capital gains for corporations: gains carry about 35 cents per $1. Dividends cost about 10.5 cents per $1 for non-insurance parents, 7 cents above 20% ownership, and about 14% (11% above 20% ownership) for P/C insurers.

## The index-fund bet and why active management loses

- The 2005 argument: in aggregate, professional active managers underperform a low-cost index fund after fees. Warren offered $500,000 that no pro could pick at least five hedge funds to match an S&P 500 index fund over ten years. Only Ted Seides of Protégé Partners accepted.
- Results through 2016 (nine years):
  - Index fund: 85.4% total gain, 7.1% compounded annually.
  - Five funds-of-funds averaged 2.2% compounded. $1 million would have gained $220,000, versus $854,000 for the index fund.
  - Individual gains: 8.7%, 28.3%, 62.8%, 2.9%, 7.5%.
- The environment was "neutral", not stacked either way. Fees: roughly "2 and 20" at the hedge fund level plus about 1% on top at the fund-of-funds level. He estimates about 60% of gains went to the two layers of managers. "Fees never sleep."
- His reasoning:
  - Passive investors earn market average, so active investors in aggregate also earn average before costs. Whichever group has lower costs wins.
  - Smart people's efforts are "self-neutralizing."
  - In his lifetime he identified only about ten professionals early whom he expected to beat the S&P over long stretches.
  - Bill Ruane: "In investment management, the progression is from the innovators to the imitators to the swarming incompetents."
  - Luck: with 1,000 managers predicting, at least one will probably be right nine years running. The lucky monkey, however, "would not find people standing in line to invest with him."
  - Success breeds failure: good records attract money, size anchors returns ("What is easy with millions, struggles with billions"), and managers still seek assets since fees scale with AUM. His 1966 partnership letter, when managing $44 million, argued bigger size is more likely to hurt results than help.
- Advice: both large and small investors should "stick with low-cost index funds." Warren recommends a low-cost S&P 500 fund. Modest-means friends followed it. Mega-rich individuals, institutions and pension funds did not.
  - Consultants can't tell clients to keep adding to an index fund year after year; that would be "career suicide."
  - The wealthy want something superior, and he estimates the elite wasted more than $100 billion in a decade. Public pension funds have been hit especially hard.
  - "When a person with money meets a person with experience, the one with experience ends up with the money and the one with money leaves with experience."
- Jack Bogle deserves a statue; he is "a hero" to Warren.
- Warren does not oppose all fees: Berkshire cheerfully pays investment bankers who bring acquisitions and pays in-house investment managers for over-performance.

## Temperament, America's economic prospects and shareholder philosophy

- America's progress is "miraculous": wealth of about $90 trillion from a standing start 240 years ago. Credit goes to ingenuity, the market system, talented immigrants and rule of law. Debt defaults don't destroy wealth: "Money is always there, but the pockets change" (Gertrude Stein).
- "Babies born in America today are the luckiest crop in history."
- The Dow rose from 66 to 11,497 in the 20th century and a further 72% to 19,763 by yearend 2016. Naysayers prosper selling gloom, "But heaven help them if they act on the nonsense they peddle."
- On panics:
  - No one can predict them. Meg McConnell: systemic risk "tends to find us."
  - "Widespread fear is your friend as an investor, because it serves up bargain purchases. Personal fear is your enemy," and it is unwarranted.
  - Investors who avoid high, unnecessary costs and hold a collection of large, conservatively financed American businesses for a long time "will almost certainly do well."
- Berkshire's size precludes a brilliant result ("prospective returns fall as assets increase"), but a collection of good businesses, financial strength and owner-oriented culture should deliver "decent results."
- Disclosure and treatment of owners:
  - All shareholders should get new information simultaneously and have time to digest it. Berkshire releases data late Friday or early Saturday and holds its meeting on Saturday.
  - It does not talk one-on-one with large institutions or analysts. The shareholder "of limited means" who entrusts a substantial part of savings is the person Warren pictures when running the company and writing the letter.
- Managers are "All-Stars" who run businesses "as if they were the only asset owned by their families." Most have no financial need to work, and the joy of business "home runs" means as much as pay.
