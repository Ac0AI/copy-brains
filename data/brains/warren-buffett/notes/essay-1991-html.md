## Berkshire's 1991 results and the limits of size

- Net worth rose $2.1 billion, or 39.6%, in 1991. Per-share book value grew from $19 to $6,437 over 27 years, a 23.7% compound annual rate. Equity capital was $7.4 billion.
- Warren says Berkshire cannot keep that pace, because "the universe of opportunities that can significantly influence the company's performance constantly shrinks."
  - On $20 million of capital, a $1 million profit idea added five percentage points to the year's return.
  - Now it takes a $370 million idea (over $550 million of pre-tax profit) to add the same five points.
  - "There are many more ways to make $1 million than to make $370 million."
- He and Charlie Munger target a 15% average annual increase in intrinsic value. Keeping book value on that pace requires earning $22 billion over the next decade.
- Most of 1991's gain was a one-time re-rating. Coca-Cola and Gillette supplied nearly $1.6 billion of the $2.1 billion because their price-earnings ratios rose far faster than earnings.
  - He calls this a "double-dip" of earnings growth plus market reappraisal. The reappraisal was warranted but "can't recur annually: We'll have to settle for a single dip in the future."
  - Berkshire's Coke stake alone is now worth more than the $3.4 billion net worth it had when it loaded up on Coke three years earlier.

## Look-through earnings and thinking like a business owner

- Look-through earnings are Berkshire's reported operating earnings, plus the retained operating earnings of major investees that GAAP does not reflect, less an allowance for the tax Berkshire would owe had those earnings been distributed.
  - For these to support 15% intrinsic value growth, they must grow about 15% a year. Since 1965 they have grown about 23%, nearly matching book value.
  - In 1991 they fell 14% to $516 million (from $602 million). Causes: declining media earnings, the Gillette preferred's conversion to common on April 1, a break-even result at Wells Fargo, and lower insurance profits.
- Warren says individual investors should compute their own look-through earnings: total the underlying earnings attributable to the shares in the portfolio.
  - The goal is a portfolio (in effect a "company") that delivers the highest possible look-through earnings "a decade or so from now."
  - This forces long-term thinking about business prospects rather than short-term stock market prospects.
- His scoreboard analogy: "in the long run, the scoreboard for investment decisions is market price. But prices will be determined by future earnings. In investing, just as in baseball, to put runs on the scoreboard one must watch the playing field, not the scoreboard."

## Franchise versus business, and why media lost its franchise

- An economic franchise comes from a product or service that:
  1. is needed or desired,
  2. is thought by customers to have no close substitute, and
  3. is not subject to price regulation.
- The proof is the ability to regularly price aggressively and earn high returns on capital. Franchises can tolerate mismanagement: inept managers reduce profitability but cannot inflict mortal damage.
- "A business" earns exceptional profits only if it is the low-cost operator or supply is tight. Tight supply rarely lasts, even low-cost status faces constant attack, and a business can be killed by poor management.
- Newspapers, TV and magazines were franchises and could "both price aggressively and be managed loosely." Choices for information and entertainment multiplied while demand could not expand ("500 million American eyeballs and a 24-hour day are all that's available"), so they now behave more like ordinary businesses.
- Valuation math (deliberately oversimplified):
  - Old view: earnings grow 6% a year forever with no extra capital, discounted at 10%. That supports $25 million for $1 million of after-tax earnings (about 16x pre-tax).
  - New view: $1 million is "normal earning power" that "bobs around" cyclically. At the same 10% rate that is worth $10 million, or 10x after-tax (about 6.5x pre-tax).
  - "Dollars are dollars" whether from media or steel mills. Media earned a premium multiple only because they were expected to grow without much capital, and when that expectation changes, valuations "must change dramatically."
- Warren says the intrinsic value of Berkshire's media holdings (Buffalo News, Washington Post, Capital Cities/ABC) fell materially. Cyclical weakness hurts current look-through earnings but not intrinsic value; secular decline does.
- Berkshire does not sell businesses or "permanent" holdings merely because the money could be used better elsewhere. He is most comfortable with Cap Cities and Washington Post because both have strong managements, stayed out of the late-1980s irrational media auctions, and carry little debt. Most other media companies carry debt of five or more times current net income.

## See's Candy: twenty years of franchise economics

- Blue Chip Stamps bought See's on January 3, 1972. The asking price was $40 million, but $10 million was excess cash, so the true price was $30 million. Warren and Charlie, "not yet fully appreciative of the value of an economic franchise," looked at $7 million of tangible net worth and said $25 million was their limit. The sellers accepted.
- Results over the 20 years:
  - Candy sales rose from $29 million to $196 million; pre-tax profit from $4.2 million to $42.4 million.
  - See's now operates on only $25 million of net worth, so $18 million of reinvested earnings was added to the $7 million base.
  - Roughly $410 million of pre-tax profit was distributed to Blue Chip/Berkshire to deploy elsewhere.
- Profit growth must be judged against the incremental capital needed to produce it; See's needed very little.
- The one insight at purchase: See's had untapped pricing power. They were also "lucky twice over": the $25 million insistence did not derail the deal, and they found Chuck Huggins, whom they put in charge immediately.
  - His pay arrangement was a handshake deal conceived in about five minutes and never put in writing; it is unchanged to this day.
- 1991: dollar sales flat, pounds down 4% (all in the last two months, which produce over 80% of annual profit), yet profit rose 7% and pre-tax margin hit a record 21.6%.
- He says See's taught Berkshire to evaluate franchises, and "We've made significant money in certain common stocks because of the lessons we learned at See's."

## Buying whole businesses: H.H. Brown, management and compensation

- Berkshire bought H.H. Brown (work shoes and boots; about $25 million pre-tax earnings, close to 2,000 employees) after Frank Rooney, the founder's son-in-law and former CEO of Melville, mentioned it to a friend of Warren's. The investment bank hired to sell it never contacted Berkshire.
  - Warren notes that in four major purchases with banker-represented sellers, the bank contacted Berkshire only once.
  - A deal was done quickly; "With Brown, we didn't even need to take five" minutes.
- Why he wanted it: Frank's willingness to stay as CEO. Managers like him have no financial need to work but love the game. "Managers of this stripe cannot be 'hired' in the normal sense of the word. What we must do is provide a concert hall in which business artists of this class will wish to perform."
- Shoes are a tough business (about 85% of the billion pairs bought in the US are imported; heavy inventory and receivables), so only outstanding managers prosper.
- Brown's compensation: key managers get a $7,800 annual salary plus a percentage of profits after a charge for capital employed.
  - They "truly stand in the shoes of owners." Most companies "talk the talk but don't walk the walk," with systems "long on carrots but short on sticks" that treat equity capital as cost-free.
  - "Managers eager to bet heavily on their abilities usually have plenty of ability to bet on."
- Berkshire's acquisition criteria:
  1. Large purchases (at least $10 million of after-tax earnings).
  2. Demonstrated consistent earning power (no interest in projections or turnarounds).
  3. Good returns on equity with little or no debt.
  4. Management in place ("we can't supply it").
  5. Simple businesses ("if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it").
  6. An offering price.
- Terms: no unfriendly takeovers, complete confidentiality, an answer usually within five minutes, cash preferred. Stock is considered only if Berkshire receives as much intrinsic value as it gives.
- His favorite deal shape (Nebraska Furniture Mart, Fechheimer, Borsheim's): owner-managers want cash for family or inactive shareholders but want to stay significant owners and keep running the business.
- On unsolicited pitches: "if you advertise an interest in buying collies, a lot of people will call hoping to sell you their cocker spaniels." Of new ventures, turnarounds and auction-like sales: "When the phone don't ring, you'll know it's me."
- He also seeks large negotiated minority stakes like Capital Cities, Salomon, Gillette, USAir, Champion and American Express.
- Managing the managers: Berkshire's operating managers are so strong that Warren could take the Salomon interim chairmanship without Berkshire missing a beat. His job is "merely to treat them right and to allocate the capital they generate."

## Insurance: float and the cost of funds

- Berkshire's insurance business has changed so that combined ratios, its own or the industry's, are "largely irrelevant." What counts is the "cost of funds developed from insurance," i.e. the cost of float.
- Float is loss reserves plus loss adjustment expense reserves plus unearned premium reserves, minus agents' balances, prepaid acquisition costs and deferred charges on assumed reinsurance. Its cost is the underwriting loss.
- 1991: average float of $1,895 million, underwriting loss of $119.6 million, a cost of 6.31%. That was below the 7.40% year-end long-term government bond yield, and Berkshire has beaten the government rate in 20 of its 25 insurance years, often by a wide margin. Growth in float counts as favorable only because the cost has been satisfactory; the challenge is to garner float "at a reasonable cost."
- Industry view: Warren expects industry incurred losses to grow close to 10% a year (11% over the last 25 years). Premiums grew only 3.1% in 1991.
  - The industry's combined ratio failed to worsen as expected, but he is skeptical because insurers tend to under-reserve in bad times. Managements that paper over problems with accounting "achieve the same result as the seriously-ill patient who tells his doctor: 'I can't afford the operation, but would you accept a small payment to touch up the x-rays?'"
  - A combined ratio of roughly 107-111 typically produces break-even once float income is counted.
- Super-cat reinsurance: Berkshire is perhaps the world's largest writer.
  - About $100 million of annual premiums could produce anything from a $100 million profit to a $200 million loss. It is priced expecting to pay out about 90% of premiums over the long term.
  - A one-year accounting cycle is ill-suited because GAAP bars reserving in quiet years for certain losses in other years.
  - 1991's underwriting profit from this business was about $11 million. The biggest catastrophe was a September typhoon in Japan (insured loss of about $4-5 billion), triggering payments on only about 25% of Berkshire's policies.
  - In the 1980s "innocents" who did not understand the risks supplied much of this cover; "Berkshire itself was an innocent all too often when I was personally running the insurance operation." He expects innocents to return after a few quiet years and prices to "plunge to silly levels."
  - Berkshire's advantage is financial strength: when "the big one" comes, many reinsurers will find it hard to write checks.

## Concentrated, patient investing and admitted mistakes

- Berkshire's "Rip Van Winkle approach": of eight large holdings, one was new (Guinness) and six of the other seven were unchanged in share count. "The stock market serves as a relocation center at which money is moved from the active to the patient."
- What he looks for: "large businesses with understandable, enduring and mouth-watering economics that are run by able and shareholder-oriented managements," bought at a sensible price and then validated by business performance.
  - "Charlie and I are simply not smart enough, considering the large sums we work with, to get great results by adroitly buying and selling portions of far-from-great businesses."
  - Calling actively trading institutions "investors" is "like calling someone who repeatedly engages in one-night stands a romantic."
- His Omaha test: with only private Omaha companies to choose from, he would assess each business's long-term economics, assess the people running it, and buy into a few of the best at a sensible price, not an equal part of every business in town. Berkshire should do the same with public companies.
- He endorses Keynes's 1934 view: put "fairly large sums into enterprises which one thinks one knows something about and in the management of which one thoroughly believes"; spreading across businesses you know little about does not limit risk.
- Mistake of omission, Fannie Mae: in early 1988 Berkshire decided to buy 30 million shares ($350-400 million). After buying about 7 million shares the price rose, he stopped in frustration, then sold the 7 million because of his "distaste for holding small positions." By year-end 1991 the gain forgone was about $1.4 billion. He notes the error was not repeated with Coca-Cola, whose price also climbed during its buying program.
  - He says his worst mistakes are typically omissions on businesses he could understand, not missed technology or merchandising stories (Xerox, Apple, Wal-Mart) that Berkshire cannot spot early.
- USAir: the airline preferred is carried at a low value because the industry may stay unprofitable; the industry has lost money in aggregate since Kitty Hawk, and bankrupt carriers selling assets to fund losses push fares below solvent carriers' costs. This is a "burn-the-furniture-to-provide-firewood" dynamic, and he calls running an airline the toughest job in corporate America.
- Charity: managers should not spend company money on personal-attachment giving; "they should use their own money, not yours."
