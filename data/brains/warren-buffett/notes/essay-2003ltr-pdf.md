## How Berkshire thinks about intrinsic value, book value and benchmarks

- Warren says per-share intrinsic value is what counts, not book value. Book value is "a slightly understated gauge" of long-term intrinsic value growth.
- 1964-2003: per-share book value grew from $19 to $50,498, 22.2% compounded annually. Net worth rose $13.6 billion in 2003, up 21% per share.
- Book is less useful for judging a single year versus the S&P 500, because equities fell from an average of 114% of net worth in the 1980s to 50% in 2000-03.
- The benchmark that matters is the S&P 500, which shareholders can buy cheaply through an index fund. Unless per-share intrinsic value outdoes the S&P, "Charlie and I will be adding nothing to what you can accomplish on your own."
- He says Berkshire's freedom is a rare advantage: no historical industry commitments and no shareholders pressuring management "to dance to Wall Street's tune". Mistakes there are "unforced errors".
- He expects future performance to "fall far short" of the past, hoping only for results "modestly above average. That's what we're being paid for."
- He warns that growth-rate tables can be distorted by choosing tiny or buoyant base or terminal years. He shows operating earnings per share from several base years: $2.69 in 1968 growing to $3,531.32 in 2003, 22.8% compounded.

## Capital allocation: businesses versus stocks

- When valuations are similar, Warren strongly prefers owning businesses to owning stocks. For most of Berkshire's history stocks were much cheaper, so he tilted heavily toward equities.
- Today few stocks can be bought in large enough size to move Berkshire's needle, and significantly undervalued ones are hard to find.
- His acquisition criteria: businesses that (1) have favorable and enduring economic characteristics, (2) are run by talented and honest managers, and (3) are available at a sensible price. For businesses that qualify, "the bigger the better".
- Policy: buy stocks aggressively if they become significantly cheaper than whole businesses, and load up on bonds if they become attractive (as in 2002).
- Idle capital is "a painful condition to be in - but not as painful as doing something stupid."
- On asset managers: "when one tells you that increased funds won't hurt his investment performance, step back: His nose is about to grow." Managers often profit more from gathering assets than from managing them well.
- Default position when nothing is exciting: U.S. Treasury bills and repos. He never "reaches" for yield by lowering credit standards or extending maturities. "Charlie and I detest taking even small risks unless we feel we are being adequately compensated."
- He avoids macro forecasting: "the cemetery for seers has a huge section set aside for macro forecasters."
- In 2002 Berkshire bought about $8 billion of junk bonds when they were cheap. By 2003 he judged the sector unattractive: "Yesterday's weeds are today being priced as flowers." Berkshire also took a currency position (about $12 billion of foreign exchange contracts), as a partial offset to its dollar cash.

## Case studies in buying businesses: Clayton Homes and McLane

- **Clayton Homes.** The lead came from University of Tennessee finance students who gave Warren Jim Clayton's autobiography.
  - He already knew Clayton as the "class act" of manufactured housing, from a failed junk-debt purchase in Oakwood Homes, which went bankrupt.
  - He offered based on the book, his read of CEO Kevin Clayton as "able and a straight-shooter", public financials, and the Oakwood lesson.
  - The industry's flaw was a model built on retailers and manufacturers unloading terrible loans on naive lenders, worsened by securitization. The fix is significant down payments and shorter loans.
  - Clayton later agreed to buy Oakwood's assets, which probably returns a small profit on Berkshire's discounted Oakwood debt.
- **McLane.** Wal-Mart sold this distributor (about $23 billion of sales, roughly 1% pre-tax margin) because it was off its mainstream path.
  - One two-hour meeting with Wal-Mart's CFO, then a handshake. Money changed hands 29 days later with no due diligence: "We knew everything would be exactly as Wal-Mart said it would be - and it was."
  - Rival retailers had shunned McLane because Wal-Mart owned it, so independence can win accounts.
- He credits Goldman's Byron Trott, who has helped on three deals, for understanding Berkshire and for earning his fee.

## Insurance float and why management is the moat

- Float is money held that belongs to others: premiums are paid upfront, and claims can take years to be reported and settled. It is "wonderful - if it doesn't come at a high price."
- The cost of float is the underwriting result. The industry regularly loses money on underwriting. Berkshire had five terrible years when float cost over 10%, but operated at an underwriting profit in 18 of 37 years.
- Float grew from $20 million in 1967 to $171 million in 1977, $1,508 million in 1987, $7,386 million in 1997 and $44,220 million in 2003. In 2003 it came at no cost, with a $1.7 billion pre-tax underwriting profit.
- Insurance has no moat in product: "Insurers sell a non-proprietary piece of paper containing a non-proprietary promise." No patents, installed base, real estate or brand protect it. The critical variables are "managerial brains, discipline and integrity."
- General Re was fixed after discipline was restored in underwriting and reserving. Gen Re and National Indemnity were the only AAA-rated major reinsurers, and Berkshire retains virtually all risks it assumes.
- Ajit Jain (staff of 23) writes jumbo, catastrophe-type risks, so results are lumpy and an occasional horrible year is expected. Example: a $1 billion policy on PepsiCo's contest, with present-value exposure of $250 million, retained entirely.
- GEICO under Tony Nicely: premiums rose from $2.2 billion to $8.1 billion and personal-auto share from 2.1% to 5.0%, with strong underwriting.
- Long-tail insurers can report almost any earnings the CEO wants by misstating liabilities. Warren admits he missed Gen Re's under-reserving, which cost Berkshire inaccurate reporting and taxes paid early.

## Derivatives, accounting and an admitted mistake

- Gen Re Securities was being unwound at a cost: pre-tax losses of $173 million in 2002 and $99 million in 2003. It started with 23,218 contracts ("tickets") with 884 counterparties, and still had 7,580 with 453 counterparties at yearend 2003.
- He argues disclosures cannot reveal derivatives risk: "the more you know about derivatives, the less you will feel you can learn from the disclosures." He quotes Darwin: "Ignorance more frequently begets confidence than does knowledge."
- His confession: he and Charlie knew at the Gen Re merger that the derivatives business was unattractive, with illusory reported profits and risks that could not be measured or limited. Trouble there would correlate with trouble elsewhere, just when Berkshire's capital would be best used elsewhere. Charlie would have closed it quickly; "I, however, dithered", probably costing about $100 million pre-tax.
- 1974 precedent: after an insurance fraud of uncertain cost, Berkshire held extra cash and so could not buy as much of the then very cheap stocks.
- "Every tub on its own bottom": Berkshire borrows and re-lends to Clayton at its cost plus one percentage point, so a subsidiary pays an appropriate rate and is not subsidized. "Having a rich daddy can lead to sloppy decisions." Clayton also began retaining loans rather than securitizing, avoiding front-ended income under GAAP. Receivables are financed almost entirely with debt, as a bank would.
- He is wary of Berkshire's wider use of leverage and notes limited circumstances where it uses debt. AAA fixed-income arbitrage was financed with borrowed money only because there was no credit risk and exceptional liquidity.

## Boards, independence and CEO pay

- CEO pay is "the acid test" of reform. Negotiations over stock options turn "play-money" because the CEO cares far more than the committee. Consultants avoid offending "actual and potential" clients.
- Statutory "independent" directors have failed at mutual funds: they routinely rehire incumbent managers however poor, approve excessive fees, and sign on with a new manager when a management company is sold at a huge price.
- Two key board jobs: obtain or retain an able and honest manager, and compensate that manager fairly.
- Suggested annual-report declarations: (1) directors looked at other management companies and believe theirs is among the better ones; (2) they negotiated a fee comparable to what other clients with equivalent funds would negotiate. If directors won't say this, "If you don't know whose side someone is on, he's probably not on yours."
- Rules treating a director earning 100% of income from board fees as independent are "nonsense". Large dependence on fees can compromise independence.
- Low-cost index funds (such as Vanguard's) are the best choice for most who want to own equities.
- Berkshire's approach, "owner-capitalism": all eleven directors and their families each own over $4 million of stock bought in the market (no options or restricted shares). Director fees are nominal, there is no D&O insurance, so "You win, they win big; you lose, they lose big." "Who ever washes a rental car?"
- Directors need business savvy (the rarest quality), a shareholder orientation and genuine interest; without savvy, they could be "replaced, without loss, by a potted plant."
- The board's main job is choosing a successor; four internal candidates have been identified. Warren has over 99% of his net worth in Berkshire.

## Portfolio habits, Berkshire's businesses and other lessons

- Major holdings (market value, 12/31/03): Coca-Cola $10,150M (200M shares), American Express $7,312M, Gillette $3,526M, Wells Fargo $3,324M, Moody's $1,453M, Washington Post $1,367M. Last changes: Coca-Cola 1994, Amex 1998, Gillette 1989, Washington Post 1973, Moody's 2000. "Brokers don't love us."
- He is neither enthusiastic nor negative: these are excellent businesses whose prices now reflect that. He says his big mistake was not selling several larger holdings during "The Great Bubble".
- Realized gains are "meaningless for analytical purposes"; he decides on selling regardless of when earnings are reported. Berkshire should be seen "as an unfolding movie, not as a still photograph."
- Manufacturing, service and retailing earned 20.7% on average tangible net worth, but only 9.2% on carrying value after goodwill paid.
- Mrs. B's lesson at Nebraska Furniture Mart: "If you have the lowest price, customers will find you at the bottom of a river." Borsheim's runs a gross margin 20 points below rivals.
- NetJets: dominates fractional ownership (70% of net sales among four competitors). He accepts a European loss because forsaking Europe means second-tier status.
- Taxes: Berkshire will pay $3.3 billion for 2003, 2.5% of all U.S. corporate income tax. Corporate taxes fell to 7.4% of federal receipts, from 32% in 1952.
- Books recommended: *The Intelligent Investor* (revised by Jason Zweig), his favorite on investing; also *Bull!*, *The Smartest Guys in the Room* and *In an Uncertain World*.
- He ended the shareholder-designated gifts program ($197 million given since 1981) because boycotts hurt independent Pampered Chef associates: "nothing charitable in harming decent, hard-working people."
