## Book value, intrinsic value and buybacks

- Per-share book value grew from $19 to $155,501 over 51 years, "a rate of 19.2% compounded annually." Net worth rose $15.4 billion (6.4%) in 2015.
- Early on, net worth roughly equaled intrinsic value because most assets were marketable securities marked to market. By the early 1990s Warren had shifted to owning whole businesses, where accounting writes down "losers" but never writes up "winners." Book value therefore understates intrinsic value, and the gap keeps widening.
- He says Berkshire would be "delighted to repurchase our shares should they sell as low as 120% of book value," because that would instantly raise per-share intrinsic value for continuing shareholders.
- Market-value gains should over time exceed book-value gains, though the two diverge erratically in short periods.
- Intrinsic value can't be stated precisely, but it can be sensibly estimated from per-share cash and investments plus pre-tax per-share earnings from the businesses.
  - 2015 cash and investments: $159,794 per share (up 8.3%).
  - 2015 business earnings: $12,304 per share (up 2.1%), after deducting overhead, interest, depreciation, amortization and minority interests, but before income taxes.
  - Investment dividends and interest are excluded from earnings to avoid double-counting.
  - Insurance underwriting income ($1,118 per share in 2015; $1,434 average over a decade) is now included for the first time, because Berkshire has de-emphasized catastrophe coverage and grown bread-and-butter lines.
- Since 1970, per-share investments compounded at 18.9% and earnings at 23.7%, and the stock price tracked those measures. The main goal is to build operating earnings.
- Successors' blueprint:
  1. Improve subsidiaries' earning power.
  2. Add bolt-on acquisitions.
  3. Benefit from investees' growth.
  4. Repurchase shares at a meaningful discount to intrinsic value.
  5. Make an occasional large acquisition.
  - Berkshire rarely, if ever, issues shares.

## Buying and holding businesses: Precision Castparts, bolt-ons and partnerships

- Berkshire bought Precision Castparts (PCC) for more than $32 billion cash. It supplies aerospace components, has 30,466 employees in 162 plants across 13 countries, and was brought to Warren by Todd Combs. Warren says it "fits perfectly into the Berkshire model" and will substantially raise normalized per-share earning power.
- The "Powerhouse Five" (BNSF, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, Marmon, Lubrizol, IMC) earned $13.1 billion in 2015, up $650 million. Their earnings rose $12.7 billion since 2003 with minor dilution; BNSF was about 70% cash and the rest stock, adding 6.1% to shares outstanding. The goal is per-share growth, not just higher earnings.
- Smaller non-insurance businesses earned $5.7 billion, up from $5.1 billion.
- 29 bolt-on deals were contracted for $634 million total, ranging from $300,000 to $143 million. Warren encourages them "if they are sensibly-priced," since they fit existing businesses and are run by existing managers, meaning no extra work for him.
- Style: buy businesses already run by cost-conscious managers, avoid bloat, and create an environment where CEOs and their successors can thrive. Warren cites Munger: "If you want to guarantee yourself a lifetime of misery, be sure to marry someone with the intent of changing their behavior."
- Berkshire runs "extreme – indeed, almost unheard of – decentralization."
- The Kraft Heinz partnership with 3G (Jorge Paulo Lemann and partners) takes a different path: quickly cutting unnecessary costs after purchase. Berkshire owns about 27% (325.4 million shares, cost $9.8 billion), plus preferred paying $720 million a year that will likely be redeemed for $8.32 billion. Warren calls the redemption "good news for Kraft Heinz and bad news for Berkshire." GAAP forced a $6.8 billion write-up, leaving the carrying value far above cost and far below market.
- Berkshire joins only friendly deals: "At Berkshire, we go only where we are welcome." Hostile bids are left to others.

## Passive stakes in great companies

- Berkshire raised its ownership in all four big holdings: IBM to 8.4%, Wells Fargo to 9.8%, Coca-Cola to 9.3% and American Express to 15.6% (the last two via the companies' buybacks).
- Each extra percentage point across the four raises Berkshire's share of annual earnings by about $500 million.
- The four earned Berkshire's share of $4.7 billion in 2015, but only about $1.8 billion in dividends is reported. The roughly $3 billion retained is "every bit as valuable," funding buybacks and good opportunities.
- "It's better to have a partial interest in the Hope Diamond than to own all of a rhinestone." Warren prefers a non-controlling stake in a wonderful company over 100% of a so-so business.
- The willingness to invest passively or buy whole businesses doubles the chances of finding uses for cash. A large securities portfolio also provides funds for an elephant-sized acquisition.
- Berkshire holds an option to buy 700 million Bank of America shares for $5 billion before September 2021 (worth $11.8 billion at yearend) and is likely to exercise it just before expiry.

## Insurance float and underwriting discipline

- Property-casualty insurers collect premiums upfront and pay claims later, holding "float." Berkshire's float grew from $39 million (1970) to $87.7 billion (2015). Underwriting was profitable 13 straight years, totaling $26.2 billion pre-tax, with $1.8 billion in 2015.
- Float should be viewed as a "revolving fund," not strictly a liability. If it is costless and long-enduring, its true cost is far below the accounting liability. Berkshire paid $24.5 billion to over six million claimants in 2015, yet new business replaces the float.
- Contracts are structured so there are never immediate or near-term demands on cash significant to Berkshire. Float may decline at most 3% a year.
- The $15.5 billion of insurance goodwill represents the price paid for float-generating capability, and Warren thinks its true value far exceeds carrying value. It was largely on the books in 2000, and float has since tripled. Goodwill is worthless if an insurer suffers prolonged underwriting losses.
- The four disciplines of a sound insurer:
  1. Understand all exposures.
  2. Conservatively assess likelihood and cost of loss.
  3. Set a premium that yields a profit after loss costs and expenses.
  4. Be willing to walk away if the right premium can't be obtained.
  - Many insurers fail the fourth: "The other guy is doing it, so we must as well" spells trouble. Underwriting discipline is "a religion, Old Testament style."
- Industry competition and low interest rates mean the industry will keep earning subnormal returns on tangible net worth, and results over the next decade will likely trail the last.
- A $250 billion industry mega-catastrophe would likely still leave Berkshire with a significant profit for the year, with ample cash to write business in a disrupted market.
- Units:
  - Ajit Jain's reinsurance group insures risks others won't take, without overexposing Berkshire. Warren notes Ajit had no insurance experience when handed the reinsurance keys in 1986.
  - General Re (Tad Montross), once thought a huge mistake, is now "a gem."
  - GEICO (Tony Nicely) has a cost advantage that is "an enduring" moat: market share went from 2.5% in 1995 to 11.4% in 2015, and underwriting expenses were 14.7% of premiums.
  - Smaller commercial insurers earned $4 billion underwriting over 13 years (about 13% of premium volume), growing float from $943 million to $9.9 billion.
  - Berkshire Hathaway Specialty Insurance (Peter Eastwood) reached $1 billion of premiums in under three years.

## Accounting, earnings quality and mistakes

- Amortization is not uniformly a real cost. Software amortization is a real expense; amortization of customer relationships from purchase accounting is not. Warren calls about 20% of the $1.1 billion amortization "real" and says to add back the rest.
- Stock-based compensation is "the most egregious example" of managers asking owners to ignore real costs: "If compensation isn't an expense, what is it?" Analysts parroting such figures may not know better, fear losing access, or go along cynically.
- Depreciation is almost always a true cost. BNSF's depreciation charge falls short of the capital outlay needed to keep the railroad running, so GAAP earnings overstate economic earnings. EBITDA as a valuation guide makes noses lengthen.
- Interest coverage should be measured as EBIT to interest, not EBITDA to interest, which Warren calls "seriously flawed." BNSF's coverage was over 8:1 in a weak year.
- The manufacturing, service and retail group earned 18.4% after tax on $25.6 billion of average net tangible assets, with excess cash and almost no leverage. Some units earn 25% to over 100%, others 12% to 20%, and a few have very poor returns.
- Warren admits "serious mistakes I made in my job of capital allocation": misjudging a company's or industry's economics, or the fidelity or ability of managers. He expects more and hopes they occur in smaller operations.
- A great business can be a bad investment at too high a price, and Berkshire has paid substantial premiums to net tangible assets, shown as goodwill.
- SEC "risk factor" lists haven't helped him evaluate a business; they rarely give probability, cost range or timing. A threat 50 years out isn't a financial problem for today's investor.
- Warren expects a few risks to hit: BNSF losing coal volume, driverless cars shrinking GEICO's premiums, print newspapers declining, online retail, and cheaper electricity storage. None is crucial to Berkshire's long-term well-being, as it adapted when its original textile business was doomed. A diversified company can redeploy funds more freely than one-industry firms.
- He says the clear, present, enduring danger is a "successful" cyber, biological, nuclear or chemical attack on the U.S. Small annual probabilities compound: a 1-in-30 yearly chance gives a 96.6% chance over a century.

## Capital-intensive regulated businesses and Clayton Homes

- BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy together produced 37% of after-tax operating earnings. They hold long-lived regulated assets funded partly by long-term debt not guaranteed by Berkshire. They invested $11.6 billion in plant and equipment in 2015; BNSF alone spent about $5.8 billion, nearly three times its depreciation.
- Warren says low prices keep regulators and customers happy. BHE's Iowa rate was 6.8¢ per kWh versus 10.4¢ nationally. BNSF's revenue was just under 3¢ per ton-mile versus 4.2¢ to 5.3¢ for rivals.
- BNSF pre-tax income hit a record $6.8 billion; BHE earned $2.37 billion net.
- Clayton Homes sold 34,397 homes (about 45% of manufactured homes bought in the U.S.), up from number three with a 14% share when Berkshire bought it in 2003.
  - It keeps 100% of the mortgages it originates ($12.8 billion portfolio), unlike the 2008-era originate-and-sell chain. Warren notes "no skin in the game" drove reckless lending then, and says "When Wall Street gets 'innovative,' watch out!"
  - 2015 foreclosures: 8,444 mortgages, costing $157 million. Average loan: $59,942, with monthly payments averaging $522. 95.4% of borrowers were current at yearend.
  - Regulators reviewed Clayton 65 times in two years, with fines of $38,200 and customer refunds of $704,678.
  - Clayton funds long fixed-rate mortgages with short-term debt. The risk is offset because Berkshire always holds at least $20 billion of cash-equivalents (often $40-60 billion), which would earn more if rates rose, making Berkshire "heavily asset-sensitive."

## Productivity, America's prospects and temperament

- Warren says "babies being born in America today are the luckiest crop in history." U.S. GDP per capita is about $56,000, six times (in real terms) the 1930 level. At 2% real growth and 0.8% population growth, per capita output rises about 1.2% a year, or 34.4% over 25 years, roughly $19,000 more per person.
- "For 240 years it's been a terrible mistake to bet against America, and now is no time to start."
- Productivity is the engine of prosperity:
  - Farming employed 40% of the workforce in 1900 versus about 2% today. Corn yield rose from 30 to over 150 bushels per acre.
  - Class I railroads moved 182% more ton-miles from 1947 to 2014 with 86% fewer workers. Real freight prices fell 55%.
  - BNSF carried 71% more ton-miles from 1996 to 2015 with only 4% more staff.
  - GEICO has about 34,000 employees for 14 million policyholders, and Warren guesses the agency model would need at least 60,000. Direct distribution pioneers (State Farm, USAA, GEICO) cut the roughly 40¢ of premium dollar agency models consumed.
  - BHE's Iowa utility produced 29 million MWh with 3,500 staff versus 19 million with 3,700 in 1998, with no rate increase for 16 years while industry rates rose 44%.
- Warren acknowledges the costs: recent gains mostly helped the wealthy, and disruption can destroy workers' livelihoods, as at the Dexter shoe and New England textile operations. Investors who risked capital can fend for themselves, but he favors safety nets, including an expanded Earned Income Tax Credit, rather than blocking productivity gains.
- Diversified investors who sit tight are certain to prosper: the Dow rose from 66 to 11,497 in the 20th century.

## Insurance and climate change, and the Berkshire culture

- On the climate proposal, Warren says climate change is "highly likely" a major problem and applies a Pascal's Wager logic ("Noah's Law": build the ark if it may be essential). But policies reprice annually, so higher loss costs translate quickly into higher premiums. GEICO's loss cost per policy rose from about $30 (1951) to about $1,000, which made the business more valuable, not less. For a shareholder of an insurer, climate change "should not be on your list of worries."
- Home office: 25 people (24 plus Warren) oversee a company of 361,270 employees, with no hires or departures in 2015. Because "no one is hired unless he or she is truly needed," Berkshire never books restructuring charges.
- Managers run businesses "as if they were the only asset owned by their families." Many have no financial need to work, and the joy of hitting business "home runs" matters as much as pay.
- Disclosure: all shareholders get information at the same time (late Friday or Saturday releases), with no one-on-one talks with institutions or analysts. "There is no one more important to us than the shareholder of limited means who trusts us with a substantial portion of his savings."
- Warren advises: "Much of what you become in life depends on whom you choose to admire and copy," calling Tom Murphy of Capital Cities the best exemplar he and Charlie saw.
