## 1978 results and how Warren reads consolidated accounts

- Warren says full consolidation of Blue Chip Stamps (about 58% owned after the Diversified Retailing merger on Dec 30, 1978) mixes wholly and partly owned businesses and "tends to obscure economic reality more than illuminate it". It is a presentation never prepared for internal use and of no value in managing.
- He therefore reports segment by segment, and shows Berkshire's own share of each business's earnings. Berkshire owns about 58% of Blue Chip, which owns 80% of Wesco, so Berkshire's equity in Wesco's earnings is about 46%.
- Controlled businesses had about 7,000 employees and over $500 million in revenues.
- Operating earnings, excluding capital gains, were 19.4% of beginning shareholders' investment, near the 1972 record.
- Net worth per share roughly doubled over three years, about 25% compounded, helped by good operations and capital gains. Warren says neither the 25% nor the 19.4% is sustainable, as the insurance cycle turned down in 1979.
- He holds that capital gains or losses should not be used to judge a single year, but they matter in the long-term record.
- Table figures (pre-tax operating earnings, Berkshire share, after-tax) are "close approximations", not "holy writ". After-tax earnings from operations were $30.1 million in 1978 vs $23.1 million in 1977, plus $9.2 million of realized securities gains.

## Textiles: why undifferentiated, capital-intensive businesses earn poor returns

- Textiles earned $1.3 million on about $17 million of capital employed, a low return despite plant carried on the books at a small fraction of replacement cost.
- Capital turnover is low because receivables and inventory are high relative to sales. Low turnover plus thin margins "inevitably produces inadequate returns on capital".
- Warren's rule: producers of undifferentiated goods in capital-intensive industries earn inadequate returns except under tight supply or real shortage. While excess capacity exists, prices reflect direct operating costs, not capital employed.
- Management is diligent (product differentiation, lower costs, better fabric mix), "The problem, of course, is that our competitors are just as diligently doing the same thing."
- He hopes not to buy many more businesses with such economics, but keeps supporting the mills because they are major community employers, management reports problems candidly, labor is cooperative, and the business should average modest cash returns. This is despite "more attractive alternative uses for capital".

## Insurance underwriting: cycles, reserving and honest costs

- National Indemnity's segment run by Phil Liesche earned about $11 million underwriting profit on roughly $90 million of earned premiums. Warren credits the managers and founder Jack Ringwalt, whose philosophy "remains etched upon the company".
- Overall 1978 underwriting was good because the industry was "flying high". He expected the industry combined ratio to rise in 1979, possibly into an underwriting loss.
- Auto rates were only 3% higher in January 1979 than a year earlier, while repair and medical costs rose over 9%. In contrast, at yearend 1976 rates were up 22% against costs up 8%. Margins hold only if rates rise as fast as costs.
- Warren warns that self-delusion in reserving, especially in long-tail reinsurance and casualty lines, is easy and leads to inadequate industry rates. If major players don't know their true costs, the competitive "fall-out" hits even those who do.
- George Young's reinsurance unit generates large sums to invest relative to premiums (float), but underwriting is "not what it should be". Warren says Young would cut volume to get satisfactory underwriting.
- California workers' compensation volume was cut to about 25% of its level eighteen months earlier under Frank DeNardo.
- On expansion: "it is easier to buy one than create one", though some of his own insurance expansion efforts were lackluster or expensive failures. National Indemnity, bought in 1967, remains by far the best part.

## Four criteria for committing insurance capital to stocks

- Warren commits a big percentage of insurance net worth to equities only when he finds (1) businesses he can understand, (2) with favorable long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and (4) priced very attractively. Usually a few names pass the first three; price (4) often prevents action.
- In 1971 equity holdings were only $10.7 million at cost, since few excellent companies were available at interesting prices. Pension managers that year put a record 122% of net funds into stocks; in 1974 they put in a record-low 21%; in 1978 only 9%, a new low.
- From end 1975 to end 1978 the equity portfolio grew from $39.3 million (market equal to cost) to $129.1 million cost and $216.5 million market. Realized gains of about $24.7 million brought combined gains to about $112 million pre-tax, while the Dow fell from 852 to 805. He calls it "a marvelous period for the value-oriented equity buyer".
- Policy is concentration: avoid "buying a little of this or that" when lukewarm about business or price; when convinced, buy worthwhile amounts.
- He makes no attempt to predict markets: "successfully forecasting short term stock price movements is something we think neither we nor anyone else can do."
- He prefers prices stay low, since he expects to be a net buyer in most years. Consistent attractive purchasing is worth more than selling chances from a short-term run-up.

## Buying parts of businesses cheaper than whole businesses

- Warren finds small slices of "really outstanding businesses" in the stock market at prices "dramatically cheaper" than inferior businesses fetch in negotiated sales.
- Either corporations are making big mistakes buying whole businesses at negotiated and takeover prices, or Berkshire will profit by buying portions at the discounted market prices.
- SAFECO example: 953,750 shares, cost $23.9 million, market $26.5 million. He calls it probably the best-run large property and casualty insurer, with superb underwriting, conservative reserving and sensible investing, better than Berkshire's own operation or anything he could buy control of.
- Berkshire paid "substantially under book value", less than 100 cents on the dollar for the best company, when "far more than 100 cents on the dollar" is paid for mediocre companies. A new startup cannot be built at under 100 cents on the dollar either.
- Other large holdings (cost / market, $ thousands): Washington Post 10,628 / 43,445; GEICO preferred 19,417 / 28,314 plus common 4,116 / 9,060; Interpublic 4,531 / 19,039; Kaiser Aluminum 18,085 / 18,671; Knight-Ridder 7,534 / 10,267; ABC 6,082 / 8,626.

## Passive ownership and the value of retained earnings

- With a minor stake Berkshire can't direct SAFECO, but Warren asks why he would want to, as the record shows they manage better than he could. Even with control of such a company, "the proper policy also would be to sit back and let management do its job". Passivity costs only excitement and prestige.
- SAFECO earnings attributable to Berkshire were $6.1 million, but only dividends (about 18% of earnings) hit reported operating earnings. He views the retained balance as "just as real in terms of eventual benefit" and possibly worth "more than 100 cents on the dollar" if reinvested well.
- He is glad when wholly owned businesses retain all earnings if they can reinvest at attractive rates, and sees no reason to feel differently about well-run companies he partly owns.
- The reverse: in low-capital-need industries, or where management has a record of low-return projects, earnings should be paid out or used to repurchase shares, "often by far the most attractive option for capital utilization".
- He says look-through retained earnings may be as significant long term as reported earnings, and hopes markets keep letting insurance subsidiaries buy underlying earning power cheaply.

## Managers who think like owners: banking and retailing

- Illinois National Bank (Rockford, bought March 1969; Gene Abegg, Pete Jeffrey) earned about 2.1% of average assets, roughly three times major banks, with less asset risk. Since 1968: consumer time deposits quadrupled, net income tripled, trust income more than doubled, with costs tightly controlled. Berkshire must divest by Dec 31, 1980, most likely by spinning it off to shareholders.
- Cost insight: a manager of a high-cost operation is "uncommonly resourceful" at adding overhead; a manager of a tight operation keeps finding ways to cut costs even when already below competitors.
- Associated Retail Stores (about 75 popular-priced women's apparel stores; started 1931 with one store and $3,200; Ben Rosner and Leo Simon) has not grown and faces adverse trends, yet Rosner's merchandising, real estate and cost skills produced returns on necessary capital often near 20% after tax.
- Top managers Rosner (75), Abegg (81) and Louie Vincenti (73, Wesco) show an "almost passionately proprietary attitude". Warren says these unorthodox relationships have been rewarding financially and personally: managers who enjoy coming to work and "instinctively and unerringly think like owners".
