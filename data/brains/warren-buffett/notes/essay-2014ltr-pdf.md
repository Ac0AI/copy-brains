## Fifty-year record and what book value misses

- Berkshire's net worth rose $18.3 billion in 2014, lifting per-share book value of both share classes by 8.3%.
- Over 50 years under present management, per-share book value grew from $19 to $146,186, a 19.4% compounded annual rate.
- Warren calls book value "a crude, but useful, tracking device" for intrinsic business value, the number that really counts.
- The gap between the two has widened because Berkshire now mostly owns and operates large businesses. Those are carried at cost-based values and never revalued upward, however much they have grown.
- He added the stock price record to the performance table. Short-term price moves are erratic, but "over time, however, stock prices and intrinsic value almost invariably converge."
- He and Charlie think the 50-year rise in per-share intrinsic value roughly equals the 1,826,163% gain in the share price.
- His quantitative intrinsic value markers:
  - 2014 per-share investments rose 8.4% to $140,123.
  - Earnings from non-insurance, non-investment businesses rose 19% to $10,847 per share.
  - Since 1970, investments have compounded at 19% and that earnings figure at 20.6%.
- His main focus is building operating earnings, which is why he was pleased to swap Phillips 66 and Graham Holdings stock for operating businesses.

## How Berkshire builds per-share value: five levers and a capital-allocation edge

His five building blocks:
1. Improve the earning power of the subsidiaries.
2. Add earnings through bolt-on acquisitions.
3. Benefit from the growth of investees.
4. Repurchase shares when they trade at a meaningful discount to intrinsic value.
5. Make an occasional large acquisition.

He adds that Berkshire will rarely, if ever, issue shares.

- Per-share discipline: the "Powerhouse Five" lifted pre-tax earnings by about $12 billion in ten years. Three were bought for cash and BNSF about 70% cash, with a 6.1% share issuance. Warren says the goal is increasing per-share results, not just earnings.
- Bolt-ons: in 2014 subsidiaries contracted 31 bolt-ons costing $7.8 billion, ranging from $400,000 to $2.9 billion (Duracell, to sit under Marmon). He likes them because they deploy capital into fitting businesses run by existing managers, "no more work for us, yet more earnings." He wants them only if "sensibly-priced (Most deals offered us aren't.)"
- Capex: subsidiaries spent a record $15 billion on plant and equipment, well over twice depreciation, about 90% in the U.S.
- Flexibility: Berkshire will invest large sums passively in non-controlled companies or buy whole operating businesses. This "doubles our chances of finding sensible uses" for its cash. The stock market often offers pieces of wonderful businesses that are more attractive than whole businesses on offer.
- Experience: running businesses makes him a better investor, and investing makes him a better businessman. He had Todd Combs and Ted Weschler each chair a small subsidiary (together earning $100 million on about $125 million of net tangible assets) to deepen both skills.
- A bet on America: he calls a bet on ever-rising U.S. prosperity "very close to a sure thing." Real per-capita output has sextupled in his lifetime. "America's best days lie ahead."

## Partial ownership of wonderful companies, and the "Big Four"

- Principle: "It's better to have a partial interest in the Hope Diamond than to own all of a rhinestone." Berkshire prefers a non-controlling stake in a wonderful company to 100% of a so-so business.
- Berkshire raised its stake in American Express (14.8%), Coca-Cola (9.2%), IBM (7.8%) and Wells Fargo (9.4%).
- Each 0.1% of ownership across the four adds $50 million to Berkshire's share of annual earnings.
- Berkshire's share of their 2014 earnings was $4.7 billion (vs $3.3 billion three years earlier). Only about $1.6 billion in dividends is reported. The other $3.1 billion "are every bit as valuable to us."
- Retained earnings fund buybacks, which raise Berkshire's share of future earnings "without requiring us to lay out a dime," and fund opportunities that usually prove advantageous. Unrealized gains on the four were $42 billion.
- Bank of America: the option to buy 700 million shares for $5 billion before September 2021 (worth $12.5 billion at yearend) makes it effectively the fourth largest holding.
- Tesco mistake:
  - Berkshire owned 415 million shares (cost $2.3 billion).
  - Warren soured on management in 2013 but sold only 114 million shares.
  - Problems then worsened: market share fell, margins contracted, accounting issues surfaced.
  - "In the world of business, bad news often surfaces serially: You see a cockroach in your kitchen; as the days go by, you meet his relatives."
  - After-tax loss was $444 million, about 1/5 of 1% of net worth.
  - He admits "dawdling" and Charlie calls it "thumb-sucking."

## Insurance float: how it works and why it is worth more than book value says

- Mechanics: property-casualty insurers collect premiums upfront and pay claims later, holding "float" that will eventually go to others but can be invested meanwhile. Float stays fairly stable relative to premium volume, so it grows as the business grows.
- Float history ($ millions): 1970: 39; 1980: 237; 1990: 1,632; 2000: 27,871; 2010: 65,832; 2014: 83,921. It started with the 1967 purchase of National Indemnity for $8.6 million.
- Underwriting: Berkshire had an underwriting profit for 12 years in a row, $24 billion in total, $2.7 billion in 2014. If premiums exceed expenses and losses, "we enjoy the use of free money – and, better yet, get paid for holding it."
- Industry economics: intense competition often produces an industry-wide underwriting loss, which is what the industry pays to hold float. He expects subnormal returns on tangible net worth for the industry, worsened by low interest rates.
- Warren says avoiding poor underwriting is "lip service" elsewhere and "a religion" at Berkshire.
- Float as a revolving fund:
  - GAAP deducts all float as a liability, as if it were payable tomorrow and never replenished.
  - Berkshire paid $22.7 billion to more than six million claimants in 2014, yet new business adds float daily.
  - If float is costless and long-enduring, "the true value of this liability is dramatically less than the accounting liability."
  - Owing $1 that will never leave is "worlds different" from owing $1 that goes out tomorrow.
- Goodwill: the $15.5 billion insurance goodwill asset largely represents the price paid for float-generating capability. Its cost has no bearing on its value, and prolonged underwriting losses would make it worthless. He believes its true value is far above carrying value, "a huge reason" intrinsic value exceeds book value.
- Float safety: float can drift down slowly, at most about 3% a year. Berkshire's contracts cannot create sudden large demands relative to its cash. No policyholder can cash out at will, unlike many life insurance products that are susceptible to a "run."
- Four underwriting disciplines:
  1. Understand all exposures.
  2. Conservatively assess likelihood and probable cost.
  3. Set a premium that profits after loss costs and expenses.
  4. Be willing to walk away if the right premium isn't available.
  - "Many insurers pass the first three tests and flunk the fourth." "The other guy is doing it, so we must as well" spells trouble, "in none more so than insurance."
- Operations:
  - Ajit Jain's reinsurance group (float $42.5 billion) insures risks no one else wants or has the capital for, without overexposing Berkshire. A new commercial unit, BHSI, led by Peter Eastwood, is expected to reach billions in volume.
  - General Re (Tad Montross) has float "considerably better than cost-free." Soon after the 1998 acquisition it was beset by problems that made Warren believe he had made a huge mistake. It is "now a gem."
  - GEICO (Tony Nicely) gained share from 2.5% in 1995 to 10.8% at 2014. Warren says its low cost "creates a moat – an enduring one – that competitors are unable to cross." He saw the cost advantage when he first met GEICO in January 1951.
- Mega-cat resilience: a $250 billion industry loss (about triple anything ever experienced) would likely still leave Berkshire with a significant profit that year, awash in cash while other insurers face red ink or insolvency.
- Sale of promises: insurance is the sale of promises, so ability and willingness to pay matter most. Berkshire wrote a $3 billion single-premium policy in 2014 (the largest ever was the $7.1 billion Lloyd's deal in 2007). Only eight P/C policies in history have had premiums over $1 billion, all written by Berkshire.
- The 1967 National Indemnity deal was done in 15 minutes with no audit and a 1½-page homemade contract with no lawyers. NICO now has GAAP net worth of $111 billion.

## Regulated capital-intensive businesses, accounting reality, and the manufacturing group

- BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy:
  - Both hold large long-lived regulated assets, partly funded with long-term debt not guaranteed by Berkshire.
  - BNSF's interest coverage was over 8:1. Warren defines coverage as pre-tax earnings/interest and calls EBITDA/interest "seriously flawed."
  - BHE's utilities have recession-resistant earnings from exclusive essential services, and diversified earnings across regulators, which lowers its cost of debt.
  - BHE retains 100% of earnings, more than any other American electric utility.
  - BHE has 6% of U.S. wind and 7% of solar capacity. It is spending $15 billion on renewables and bought AltaLink for C$3 billion.
  - Warren trusts that governments will treat capital providers so funding for essential projects keeps flowing.
- BNSF problem:
  - It disappointed customers in 2014 and lost share to Union Pacific.
  - Warren says "we have a lot of work to do." Berkshire will spend $6 billion in 2015, about 26% of estimated revenue, vs BNSF's 18% average for 2009-2013 and UP's projected 16-17%.
  - BNSF carries about 15% of U.S. inter-city freight by ton-miles. Its fuel efficiency is about 500 miles per ton per gallon, with trucks using about four times as much fuel.
- EBITDA and amortization:
  - "Every dime of depreciation expense we report is a real cost." "When CEOs tout EBITDA as a valuation guide, wire them up for a polygraph test."
  - Amortization of intangibles like customer relationships arises from purchase accounting and does not reflect reality. Software amortization is real.
  - Of $1.15 billion of amortization, he calls about 20% "real." Investors should add back the rest.
- Manufacturing, service and retailing group:
  - Employed $24 billion of net tangible assets and earned 18.7% after tax, despite excess cash and little leverage.
  - Individual businesses range from 25% to well over 100% after tax on unleveraged net tangible assets, to 12-20%, to a few very poor ones. Warren says the poor ones came from his own capital-allocation mistakes: "I simply was wrong in my evaluation of the economic dynamics."
  - Even terrific businesses "can be a bad investment if it is bought for too high a price."
  - The biggest winners' value over carrying value is in insurance and regulated businesses.
- Other businesses:
  - Clayton Homes sold 30,871 homes, about 45% of U.S. manufactured homes (vs 14% when bought in 2003 for $1.7 billion).
  - Clayton kept lending in 2008-2009 with Berkshire's backing, and its blue-collar borrowers proved better credit risks than many higher-income ones.
  - Marmon builds its own tank cars and books no profit on transfer to leasing, so the fleet is carried below retail value.
- Van Tuyl Automotive: 78 dealerships with local managers as owner-partners, and $9 billion of sales. Warren sees room to build multiples of that, if dealerships can be bought at sensible prices and manufacturers approve.
- 3G Partnership: Warren says Heinz "is run far better" by 3G's Alex Behring and Bernardo Hees "than would be the case if I were in charge." Berkshire expects more partnerships with 3G, Mars and Leucadia, only on friendly transactions.

## Risk, volatility and why stocks beat dollars over decades

- Volatility is not risk. Business schools treat the two as equal, which Warren calls "dead wrong."
- Over 1964-2014 the S&P 500 went from 84 to 2,059 (11,196% with dividends) while the dollar lost 87% of its purchasing power. It now takes $1 to buy what 13¢ bought in 1965.
- His definition of investing: "the transfer to others of purchasing power now with the reasoned expectation of receiving more purchasing power – after taxes have been paid on nominal gains – in the future."
- Conclusion: for multi-decade investors, a diversified portfolio of American businesses bought over time is far safer than Treasuries or other dollar-based securities, which are "far riskier." Holding stocks for a day, week or year is riskier than cash, and anyone with near-term needs should hold Treasuries or insured deposits.
- Fear of volatility can lead to risky behavior. In 2009 pundits pushed T-bills and CDs when the S&P 500 was below 700. By 2015 it was about 2,100, and savers earned "a pittance."
- Ways investors make stocks risky:
  - active trading and market timing
  - inadequate diversification
  - high fees to managers and advisors
  - borrowed money
- "Borrowed money has no place in the investor's tool kit." "Market forecasters will fill your ear but will never fill your wallet."
- Institutions as a group have underperformed the patient index-fund investor, partly because consultants steer them to high-fee managers: "a fool's game."
- Most advisors are better at generating fees than returns, and their core competence is salesmanship. He recommends Jack Bogle's *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing*.
- Ben Graham quote on the cause of investment failure: "The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, but in ourselves."

## Mistakes and lessons: cigar butts, Charlie's blueprint, and See's

- Textile origin:
  - BPL bought Berkshire in December 1962 at $7.50 (working capital $10.25, book $20.20). Warren compares it to a "discarded cigar butt that had one puff remaining."
  - Seabury Stanton verbally agreed to $11.50, then tendered at $11.375. Irritated by the eighth of a point, Warren didn't tender and bought more.
  - He took control in 1965 and later called not tendering "a monumentally stupid decision."
  - He struggled with textiles for 18 years before closing the operation in 1985.
  - Had BPL tendered, its weighted annual return on Berkshire would have been about 40%.
- Second mistake: he bought National Indemnity (in 1967) through Berkshire instead of BPL. Its partners would have owned 100% of the insurer and avoided the unproductive textile funds. Instead of marrying a 61%-owned terrible business to an excellent one, this diverted about $100 billion from BPL partners to strangers.
- Another one: the 1975 Waumbec Mills purchase looked like a "bargain" on assets and "projected synergies" but was a disaster.
- Cigar-butt limits: the approach worked while he managed small sums and was a great success in the 1950s. It is "scalable only to a point" and marginal businesses are the wrong foundation for an enduring enterprise. "Selecting a marriage partner clearly requires more demanding criteria than does dating."
- Charlie's blueprint: "Forget what you know about buying fair businesses at wonderful prices; instead, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices." Warren says Berkshire was built to it, with him as general contractor and subsidiary CEOs as sub-contractors.
- See's Candy (1972):
  - Earned about $4 million pre-tax on $8 million of net tangible assets, with a durable competitive advantage and pricing power.
  - The family wanted $30 million. Warren wouldn't pay more than $25 million (three times net tangible assets made him "gulp"), and the sellers accepted.
  - See's has earned $1.9 billion pre-tax on only $40 million of added investment, cash that bought other businesses ("Envision rabbits breeding").
  - Lesson: it also taught him the value of powerful brands.
- Dexter Shoe (1993):
  - Berkshire paid $433 million, in Berkshire stock, and the business went to zero because of foreign competition he didn't foresee.
  - The shares are now worth about $5.7 billion.
- Paying with stock: "Trading shares of a wonderful business ... for ownership of a so-so business irreparably destroys value." The intrinsic value of shares given must not exceed the intrinsic value of the business received. "You can't get rich trading a hundred-dollar bill for eight tens."
- On acquisitions at other companies:
  - Bankers focus on "customary" premiums and EPS accretion, not intrinsic value math, and CEOs conjure "synergies" (he says he never heard "dis-synergies" mentioned in 19 boardrooms).
  - Post mortems comparing reality to the original projections should be standard practice.
- Losses: only three investment losses cost 1% or more of net worth at sale, all in 1974-1975 (one 2%).

## Conglomerate advantages, capital allocation, and why Berkshire is the "home of choice"

- Why conglomerates have a terrible reputation:
  - In the 1960s, ITT, Litton, Gulf & Western and LTV drove their stock to about 20x earnings and then issued shares to buy businesses at about 10x.
  - Pooling accounting automatically raised per-share earnings, presented as managerial genius.
  - This forced them to bottom-fish for mediocre businesses.
  - Such serial issuance of overpriced shares, like a chain letter, "redistribute[s] wealth, but in no way create[s] it."
- Warren says neither BPL nor Berkshire has invested in companies "hell-bent on issuing shares," which he calls a sign of promotion-minded management, weak accounting, an overpriced stock and often dishonesty.
- Jimmy Ling's LTV: its split-up of Wilson & Co. made "Meatball, Golf Ball and Goof Ball." Charlie's maxim: "never underestimate the man who overestimates himself." Warren's rule: "2+2 will always equal 4." When someone says that math is old-fashioned, "zip up your wallet, take a vacation and come back in a few years to buy stocks at cheap prices."
- Why the form works for Berkshire:
  - It can move huge sums between businesses "without incurring taxes or much in the way of other costs."
  - It has no industry bias or colleagues defending the status quo: "If horses had controlled investment decisions, there would have been no auto industry."
  - It can buy pieces of wonderful businesses (common stocks).
  - It avoids the taxes, intermediaries and agency costs that stand-alone companies face when redeploying capital.
  - See's excess cash funded other purchases rather than being distributed.
  - Limit: Warren and Charlie can only evaluate businesses whose economics they understand.
- Home of choice for owners:
  - Sellers who sell to competitors expect cuts to staff, and private-equity buyers pile debt onto businesses ("'equity' is a dirty word for many private-equity buyers; what they love is debt"), then re-leverage and flip them.
  - Berkshire offers a permanent home that keeps people and culture, adds financial strength and ends dealings with banks and analysts.
  - On spin-offs: they would lose control value, capital-allocation flexibility and tax advantages (utility tax credits depend on Berkshire's taxable income elsewhere) and add duplicate costs. He mocks bankers urging 20-50% premiums to buy and later spin-offs to "unlock shareholder value."

## Financial strength, CEO selection, culture, and the next 50 years

- Permanent loss risk: the chance of permanent capital loss for patient Berkshire shareholders is "as low as can be found among single-company investments." Warning: buying at about double book value can turn a sound investment into a rash speculation. Repurchases will only occur well below intrinsic value.
- Holding period: buy Berkshire only with a horizon of at least five years, never with borrowed money. The stock has fallen about 50% from highs three times since 1965. Ben Graham: "In the short-term the market is a voting machine; in the long-run it acts as a weighing machine."
- Fortress balance sheet:
  - Requires a large reliable earnings stream, massive liquid assets, and no significant near-term cash requirements.
  - "Cash... is to a business as oxygen is to an individual: never thought about when it is present, the only thing in mind when it is absent."
  - Berkshire keeps at least $20 billion in Treasury bills, and supplied $15.6 billion to American businesses in three weeks of late 2008.
  - No short-term debt maturities of size and no derivatives or arrangements with large collateral calls (it stopped writing derivatives once they required full collateral).
  - "We will never play financial Russian roulette," even with 100 chambers and one bullet. "It is madness to risk losing what you need in pursuing what you simply desire."
  - They prepare for the "thousand-year flood": "tomorrow is always uncertain."
- Return outlook: percentage gains will not come close to the past 50 years because "the numbers have become too big." In 10-20 years Berkshire may be unable to reinvest all earnings, and directors will choose dividends and/or buybacks. If shares are below intrinsic value, "massive repurchases will almost certainly be the best choice."
- Delegation: no committees, budgets required of subsidiaries, legal office, or HR/PR/IR/strategy/acquisition departments at headquarters. About 25 people work at HQ. Managers get trust, with an active audit function ("no sense being a damned fool").
- Fighting the "ABCs of business decay" (arrogance, bureaucracy, complacency): General Motors, IBM, Sears and U.S. Steel fell despite once-unassailable strengths. Charlie: "Tell me where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there."
- Choosing a CEO:
  - Capital allocation plus selecting managers.
  - Rational, calm, decisive with insight into human behavior, and aware of his limits ("I'm no genius, but I'm smart in spots and I stay around those spots").
  - Character is crucial: "all in" for the company, with neither ego nor avarice driving pay to match the most lavish peers. His behavior sets the tone for managers.
  - Candidates are young internal people the board knows, so the CEO can serve well over ten years. The board and Warren believe they have the right successor.
  - Howard Buffett would be a non-executive Chairman, a "safety valve" for directors if a CEO needs replacing, since a mediocre CEO who is also Chairman is hard to replace.
- Governance and alignment:
  - "Although our form is corporate, our attitude is partnership."
  - Directors are paid token fees, buy shares in the market, and the company carries no directors and officers liability insurance ("directors walk in your shoes").
- Managers: operating managers run businesses "as if they were the only asset owned by their families." Most don't need the money. "The joy of hitting business 'home runs' means as much to them as their paycheck."
- Shareholders: a proxy resolution for a dividend lost by 89 to 1 among A shares and about 47 to 1 among B shares, with 98% of voting shares saying reinvest earnings.
- Fair disclosure: new information goes to all shareholders simultaneously, usually late Friday or Saturday. They don't talk one-on-one with large institutions or analysts.

## Berkshire's acquisition criteria

- Size: at least $75 million of pre-tax earnings (unless the business fits an existing unit); the preference is a $5-20 billion deal.
- Track record: consistent earning power, not future projections, and no turnarounds.
- Good returns on equity with little or no debt.
- Management in place ("we can't supply it").
- A simple business ("if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it").
- An offering price known upfront.
- No unfriendly takeovers and no auctions. Cash preferred; stock only if Berkshire receives as much intrinsic value as it gives.
- Complete confidentiality and an answer within about five minutes.
- Tone: "if you advertise an interest in buying collies, a lot of people will call hoping to sell you their cocker spaniels." On turnarounds and auctions: "When the phone don't ring, you'll know it's me."
