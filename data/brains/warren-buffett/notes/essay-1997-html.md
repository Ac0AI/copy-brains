## Performance is measured against the S&P, not in absolute terms

- Berkshire's 1997 net worth gain was $8.0 billion, a 34.1% rise in per-share book value. Over 33 years, book value per share grew from $19 to $25,488, a 24.1% compounded annual rate.
- Warren refuses to call 34.1% a triumph in a bull market. His analogy is the "preening duck that quacks boastfully after a torrential rainstorm"; a right-thinking duck compares itself to the other ducks on the pond.
- The passive S&P Index nearly matched Berkshire in 1997. His verdict: "Quack."
- Berkshire is at a structural disadvantage in booms. The Index and mutual funds bear no tax cost, while Berkshire paid or accrued $4.2 billion of federal income tax in 1997, about 18% of beginning net worth.
- The yardstick is a modest advantage over the Index over time, not every year. He rejects the Cubs-fan attitude: "Everyone has a bad century now and then."
- What counts is per-share intrinsic business value, not book value. The two usually move roughly in tandem.

## Size erodes returns: "We weren't smarter then, just smaller"

- In 1951, in Ben Graham's class at Columbia, an idea giving a $10,000 gain improved his yearly performance by 100 percentage points. In 1997, an idea producing $500 million of pre-tax profit adds one percentage point to Berkshire's performance.
- His 1950s annual results beat any later decade by nearly thirty percentage points. "We weren't smarter then, just smaller." Charlie Munger's experience was similar.
- "For anyone deploying capital, nothing recedes like success." Any performance superiority at Berkshire's size will be minor.
- Berkshire's offsets are its businesses' long-term prospects and managers who are wealthy and "motivated by the joy of accomplishment, not by fame or fortune."

## Waiting for the fat pitch (Ted Williams discipline)

- Prices were high for both businesses and stocks, so fresh money earns little in prospective earnings. He has no view on whether prices will fall.
- Ted Williams, in The Science of Hitting, split the strike zone into 77 cells. Swinging only at his best cell meant batting .400; reaching for the worst cell (low outside corner) meant .230.
- The business pitches Warren sees in 1997 are "just catching the lower outside corner." Swinging locks Berkshire into low returns.
- The catch: letting them all go gives no assurance that better ones come. Perhaps past low prices were the aberration. He adds that standing with the bat on his shoulder "is not my idea of fun."
- Default use of new money when the favorite commitment is unavailable ("a well-run and sensibly-priced business with fine economics"): very short-term, highest-quality instruments.

## Unconventional, non-business commitments and their risk

At yearend 1997 Berkshire held three non-traditional positions. Warren says these carry no certainty of profit, unlike a wonderful business bought at a good price, where "we are going to make money -- the only question being when." With alternatives, "we think that we are going to make money" and will sometimes realize substantial losses.

- **Oil derivatives:** contracts for 14.0 million barrels remained of a 45.7 million barrel position set up in 1994-95. Contracts for 31.7 million barrels settled in 1995-97 for a pre-tax gain of about $61.9 million. Unrealized gain was $11.6 million. Oil had seemed "modestly underpriced" at purchase; he now has no opinion.
- **Silver:** 111.2 million ounces bought, with a 1997 pre-tax gain of $97.4 million. Bullion inventories had fallen and he and Charlie concluded a higher price was needed to balance supply and demand. Inflation expectations play no part in the valuation. He had bought silver 30 years earlier, expecting US demonetization.
- **Zero-coupon Treasuries:** $4.6 billion at amortized cost, with a $598.8 million unrealized pre-tax gain. Zeros move sharply with rates: losses if rates rise, outsized gains if they fall.
- On the macro bet: "you pay Charlie and me to use our best judgment -- not to avoid embarrassment." Skin in the game: the Munger family has over 90% of its net worth in Berkshire and the Buffetts over 99%.

## Savers should want lower prices (market fluctuations)

- The hamburger test: if you will buy beef or cars for life and don't produce them, you want lower prices. Likewise, a net saver in stocks should want lower stock prices. "Only those who will be sellers of equities in the near future should be happy at seeing stocks rise."
- Berkshire shareholders are automatic savers because Berkshire retains all earnings and uses them to buy businesses and securities. The cheaper the buys, the better.
- Investees that repurchase shares benefit more when prices are low. Repurchases by Coca-Cola, The Washington Post and Wells Fargo at very low past prices helped Berkshire far more than today's repurchases at loftier prices.
- About 97% of Berkshire shares are held at yearend by the same investors who held them at the start, so they are savers.
- Reframe the headline "Investors lose as market falls" as "Disinvestors lose... but investors gain." There is a buyer for every seller. Low prices in the 1970s and 1980s rewarded permanent holders; few such opportunities appeared in recent years.

## Valuing the stock market and Graham's margin of safety

- He does not predict the market but values it roughly. At the 1996 annual meeting (Dow 7,071, long-term Treasuries at 6.89%), he and Charlie said the market was not overvalued if (1) interest rates stayed put or fell and (2) American business kept earning its high returns on equity. Rates fell and ROE stayed high.
- Returns on equity are "not a sure thing to remain at, or even near, their present levels."
- His 1979 Forbes article: "You pay a very high price in the stock market for a cheery consensus." In 1979 skepticism prevailed and pessimism drove prices to attractive levels. In 1997 there is a "very cheery consensus."
- Lower rates make each dollar of earnings more valuable, but price levels "have materially eroded the 'margin of safety' that Ben Graham identified as the cornerstone of intelligent investing."
- Look-through earnings are his better measure of economic progress. They equal reported operating earnings, plus Berkshire's share of investees' retained operating earnings, less a hypothetical tax (14%, the rate Berkshire pays on dividends). 1997: $1,292 million reported plus $743 million undistributed investee earnings, less $105 million tax, totals $1,930 million.
- He prefers investees retaining earnings because they can often reinvest at high returns. Coca-Cola ($216M), American Express ($161M) and Wells Fargo ($103M) were the largest contributors to undistributed earnings.

## Insurance float and its cost

- Float is "money we hold but don't own." Premiums arrive before losses are paid, sometimes years later, and the insurer invests the money.
- The cost of float is the underwriting loss. An insurance business is valuable if its cost of float is below what the company would otherwise pay for funds, and "a lemon" if above market rates.
- Berkshire's float formula: net loss reserves, loss adjustment reserves, funds held under reinsurance assumed and unearned premium reserves, minus agents' balances, prepaid acquisition costs, prepaid taxes and deferred charges on assumed reinsurance.
- Float grew from $17.3 million (1967) to $7,093.1 million (1997), a 21.7% compound rate. Underwriting profits in each of the last five years (1993-97) made the cost of float negative: "we have been paid for holding money."
- Accounting irony: float is a balance sheet liability, yet worth more to Berkshire than an equal amount of net worth.
- Reserve estimation gives insurers "enormous latitude," so outsiders struggle to know true float cost. Errors can be huge, and "virtually all surprises in insurance are unpleasant ones." Berkshire reports conservatively.
- Warren says understanding float is necessary to judge Berkshire's intrinsic value. Float was expected to dip in Q1 1998 as large contracts expired.

## Super-cat insurance and catastrophe-bond mispricing

- Super-cat policies are bought by insurers and reinsurers to limit mega-catastrophe losses. It is the most volatile insurance line. Most years show large profits with an occasional huge loss: "a truly terrible year in the super-cat business is not a possibility -- it's a certainty. The only question is when."
- 1997 was lucky (no huge insured catastrophes), so premiums dropped to the bottom line. Super-cat underwriting earned $283.0 million pre-tax.
- Warren criticizes "catastrophe bonds" sold to "innocents" who rely on salespeople. A true bond obliges the issuer to pay; these lay a provisional promise to pay on the purchaser. The label is meant to dodge insurance licensing laws and to make buyers assume less risk.
- Dice example: a 1-in-36 chance of a 12, with a $50 million payout against a $1 million annual premium, is underpriced. There is a 75.4% chance of going a decade without paying, so it looks like easy money until you go broke.
- Catastrophe odds are fuzzy and hired "experts" are paid up front regardless of accuracy. "an expert can invariably be found" to say 1 in 100 instead of 1 in 36, and he probably believes it, which makes him "less reprehensible -- but more dangerous."
- Inflows from such investors hurt pricing, so Berkshire wrote less in 1998. Its worst-case after-tax loss was about $600 million (the California Earthquake Authority policy maximum), around 1% of market value. Warren would take more exposure at appropriate prices.
- Ajit Jain built the business from scratch and has "the discipline to walk away from business that is inadequately priced."

## GEICO: low cost, shared gains and incentives that fit

- CEO Tony Nicely lifted voluntary auto policy growth from 10% in 1996 to 16% in 1997. New voluntary auto policies rose from 617,669 (1996) to 913,176 (1997); policies in force reached 2,949,439.
- Underwriting profit was 8.1% of premiums, above the 4% target. GEICO deliberately cut average rates to pass low-cost benefits to customers.
- Warren welcomes tougher industry competition, since "a tough market helps the low-cost operator." Marketing spend for 1998 was set above $100 million, up 50%, against a 3% market share in a roughly $115 billion industry.
- Compensation: GEICO's 10,500 associates got a 26.9% profit-sharing contribution ($71 million), driven by two variables, policy growth and profitability of seasoned business (policies over a year old, past acquisition-cost losses). Executive cash bonuses used the same variables.
- "We distribute merit badges, not lottery tickets": no Berkshire subsidiary ties pay to Berkshire's stock price, which employees cannot meaningfully affect. Bonuses track each unit's own business performance.
- Lorimer Davidson, then at GEICO, spent a Saturday in early 1951 teaching a 20-year-old stranger about GEICO and insurance, shaping Warren's whole understanding of the field.
- Other primary units posted a combined 15.0% underwriting profit (National Indemnity traditional business 32.9%).

## Acquisitions: criteria, referrals and why not to issue stock

- Criteria for Star Furniture and International Dairy Queen: "understandable; possess excellent economics; and are run by outstanding people."
- Sourcing trick: after buying into an industry, he asks new partners, "Are there any more at home like you?" The Blumkins (Nebraska Furniture Mart) named three furniture retailers, leading to R.C. Willey and Star Furniture years later.
- Star was agreed in a single two-hour meeting with Melvyn Wolff, with no need to check leases or employment contracts: "I knew I was dealing with a man of integrity and that's what counted." Wolff and his sister funded special payments to all employees from their own proceeds.
- International Dairy Queen (5,792 stores in 23 countries) deal: sellers could pick cash or Berkshire stock of slightly lower value, tilting them toward cash. Only 45% of shares chose cash.
- The confession: in stock-only mergers, Berkshire shareholders are "slightly worse off" than if he hadn't done them: "when I've issued stock, I've cost you money." Issuing shares dilutes ownership of every wonderful business Berkshire already owns.
- Baseball analogy: it's a bad trade to give up a .380 hitter to get a .350 hitter. Berkshire's roster is full of .380 hitters, so it is "almost impossible for us to 'trade up.'" Cash deals (National Indemnity, See's, Buffalo News, Scott Fetzer, GEICO) did far better.
- A takeover premium only makes sense if (a) the acquirer's stock is overvalued relative to the target's or (b) the combination earns more than the two apart. Synergies are usually unrealistic and costs are often "illusory"; serial share-issuers tacitly concede overvaluation. When a CEO wants a foolish deal, staff and advisors will produce supporting projections: "Only in fairy tales are emperors told that they are naked."
- On options: when buying an option-issuing company, Berkshire replaces options with equivalent cash compensation so the true cost hits earnings. He calls the standard accounting that ignores option cost "dead wrong."

## Operating subsidiaries and capital allocation within Berkshire

- Operating pre-tax earnings per share grew from $1.09 (1967) to $717.82 (1997), 24.2% a year. Pre-tax operating earnings rose from $1 million to $888 million.
- Subsidiaries like Buffalo News, See's and Scott Fetzer pay 100% of earnings to Berkshire, while public peers retain two-thirds or more. Berkshire redeploys the cash elsewhere. These three alone paid $1.8 billion, so their records understate their contribution.
- Warren wants executives he likes, trusts and admires. The US Airways and Salomon episodes show it: he calls his US Airways record "unblemished by success", saying he was wrong to buy and wrong to try repeatedly to sell at 50 cents on the dollar. The recovery coincided with Stephen Wolf becoming CEO and with Warren and Charlie leaving the board. US Airways stock rose from $4 to $73.
- Convertible preferred outcomes: Gillette $600 million became $4.8 billion; First Empire $40 million became $236 million.
- Roberto Goizueta at Coca-Cola: Warren calls the 100+ letters from him "a guidebook for success in both business and life." Roberto relabeled Warren's "focus" for his own "obsession." Goizueta prepared Doug Ivester's succession long before it seemed necessary.
- Amex: on the verge of selling at the Perc conversion in 1994, he was persuaded by Hertz CEO Frank Olson on a golf course that the corporate card was a terrific franchise. Berkshire turned buyer, ending with 10% and a $3 billion gain. He credits "110% luck, the balance skill."
