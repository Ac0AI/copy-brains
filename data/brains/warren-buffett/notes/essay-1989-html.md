## Intrinsic value, book value, and the limits of compounding

- Warren reports a 44.4% gain in net worth for 1989 ($1.515 billion). Book value per share grew from $19.46 to $4,296.01 over 25 years, a 23.8% compound annual rate.
- He says book value is not what counts. Intrinsic value does: "all future cash flows of a business - in and out - and discounted at prevailing interest rates." Valued this way, businesses "from manufacturers of buggy whips to operators of cellular phones, become economic equals."
- Berkshire's intrinsic value compounded faster than book value. At the start, book value was tied up in a textile business worth less than its carrying value, whereas most businesses are now worth more than carrying value.
- Much of the past gain was a "double-dip": gains in the portfolio companies' intrinsic value, plus the market re-rating their prices up toward that value. That catch-up is over, so he expects a "single-dip" going forward.
- "In a finite world, high growth rates must self-destruct." He cites Carl Sagan's bacteria that double every 15 minutes. From a $4.9 billion base, averaging 15% will be much harder than averaging 23.8% from the original $22 million.
- Many holdings were priced high relative to intrinsic value at year-end. Warren says he will keep most of them anyway ("'til-death-do-us-part"). He says a roughly 10% yearly drop in the portfolio's aggregate value would reduce net worth, and he expects a decline in at least one of the next three years.

## Taxes, deferred liabilities, and why Warren holds long

- The 1989 tax charge was about $712 million: about $172 million paid currently and $540 million deferred, mostly on unrealized stock gains. He reserved 34%. Pre-1987 gains of about $1.2 billion carry reserves booked at the old 28% rate.
- A deferred tax liability of over $1.1 billion on unrealized gains is not like a trade payable, nor is it a meaningless fiction. He calls it "an interest-free loan from the U.S. Treasury that comes due only at our election." It can only finance the particular appreciated stocks. It works like a transfer tax paid only if Berkshire moves from one asset to another. In 1989 Berkshire sold small holdings, incurring about $76 million of tax on $224 million of gains.
- Illustration: $1 doubled each year for 20 years, taxed at 34% on each sale, ends at about $25,250. One investment that doubles 20 times, taxed once at the end, ends at about $692,000 (from $1,048,576 pre-tax). The difference is only the timing of tax payments, roughly 27:1 in tax collected.
- He says Berkshire does not hold long because of this arithmetic. It might earn more after tax by moving often, and he and Charlie did that years ago. He now prefers to stay put because splendid business relationships are rare and enjoyable. Giving them up for unknown, likely average people would be "akin to marrying for money - a mistake under most circumstances, insanity if one is already rich."

## Look-through earnings and retained earnings of investees

- GAAP does not let Berkshire report its share of retained earnings at its major investees. In 1989 it received about $45 million after tax in dividends from five investees, but its share of their retained earnings was about $212 million. This excludes large capital gains at GEICO and Coca-Cola. With that $212 million distributed and taxed, operating earnings would have been close to $500 million rather than about $300 million.
- Warren thinks retained earnings may be worth more than distributed ones. They are deployed by "talented, owner-oriented managers who sometimes have better uses for these funds in their own businesses than we would have in ours." He says he would not make this generous judgment about most managements.
- Berkshire's earning power is best measured by "look-through" earnings: reported operating earnings plus its share of investees' retained operating earnings, excluding capital gains. For intrinsic value to compound at about 15% a year, look-through earnings must grow at about that pace.
- Berkshire's results are presented by business rather than under GAAP purchase-price accounting, which he says is more useful to investors and managers. Goodwill amortization and other purchase-price adjustments are shown separately instead of being charged to the businesses.

## The operating businesses and the managers behind them

- The "Sainted Seven Plus One" (Borsheim's, Buffalo News, Fechheimer, Kirby, Nebraska Furniture Mart, Scott Fetzer, See's, World Book) earned 57% after tax on average equity, with no net leverage (cash equivalents matched funded debt). Warren says most managers "have no need to work for a living; they show up at the ballpark because they like to hit home runs." He compares his lineup to the 1927 Yankees.
- **Borsheim's and Nebraska Furniture Mart share one formula:**
  1. Unparalleled depth and breadth of merchandise at one location.
  2. The lowest operating costs in the business.
  3. The shrewdest buying, helped by huge volume.
  4. Gross margins, and so prices, far below competitors'.
  5. Friendly personalized service with family on hand.
- Borsheim's expense ratio is about one-third that of comparable jewelers. Sales are more than twice what they were four years ago. It was bought without an audit, and every surprise was positive: "If you don't know jewelry, know your jeweler." NFM's carpet department held a 75.3% consumer share in Omaha in Q4 1989, over six times its nearest competitor.
- **See's Candies:** pounds sold rose 8% on a record 1988, with excellent same-store gains. Advertising rose from $4 million to $5 million, and newspaper advertising more than tripled. Chuck Huggins runs over 27 million pounds of candy a year through 225 shops plus mail and phone orders.
- **Buffalo News:** it ranks first among major metro papers in household penetration and has a news hole of 50.1%. It set its seventh straight profit record. Erie County population fell from 1,113,000 (1970) to 966,000 (1988), yet Sunday circulation rose from the 207,500 the Courier-Express sold in 1975 to 292,700. Warren says high penetration lets retailers "talk to the entire community through a single megaphone." He believes publisher Stan Lipsey adds at least five points of margin versus an average manager.
- **Mrs. B (Rose Blumkin)** left NFM at age 96 after disagreeing over the carpet department, where sales fell 17% in three years while other departments rose 24%. Warren says the family was right. At 96 she started a new carpet and furniture business.
- Fechheimer and Ralph Schey's group (World Book, Kirby, Scott Fetzer) run so well that Warren and Charlie have never visited Fechheimer's plants. Kirby's international share of unit sales rose from 5% to 20%. Warren says buying Ralph was as important as buying Scott Fetzer's businesses, since his capital management funds other Berkshire commitments.

## Insurance: commodity pricing, capacity, and writing CAT covers

- Property-casualty combined ratio: below 100 means an underwriting profit. With float included, a ratio of 107-111 is about breakeven. Industry incurred losses grow about 10% a year (11% over 25 years), and premium growth must match to hold the ratio steady. 1989 premiums grew an estimated 2.1% with a 110.4 combined ratio.
- The "underwriting cycle" was a lag effect from the cartel era. Today hundreds of sellers offer a commodity-like product at independent prices, which "is certain to cause subnormal profitability in all circumstances but one: a shortage of usable capacity." Capacity "primarily depends on the mental state of insurance managers," and shortages come only when insurers are frightened. He compares the industry to steel: needing better profits has nothing to do with getting them.
- Warren will write no insurance carrying "a mathematical expectation of loss." Premium volume may fall to $150 million in 1990, from a high of $1 billion in 1986.
- After Hurricane Hugo (over $4 billion insured loss) and the California earthquake, CAT cover capacity ran short. Berkshire advertised up to $250 million of coverage in trade publications and booked a substantial amount in ten days. CAT premiums usually run 3% to 15% of coverage, a level Berkshire had thought inadequate.
- Berkshire keeps the risk rather than laying it off. Its edge is about $6 billion of regulatory net worth, plus "we simply don't care what earnings we report quarterly, or even annually, just as long as the decisions leading to those earnings (or losses) were reached intelligently." A full $250 million loss would cost about $165 million after tax: "a blow only to our pride, not to our well-being." Most managers avoid this posture, but Warren says "We are willing to look foolish as long as we don't feel we have acted foolishly."
- Berkshire adds capacity when it is short and backs off when it is abundant. It holds unusually large float relative to premiums, which should keep underwriting results tolerable for a few years.

## Marketable securities: Coca-Cola, convertible preferreds, and arbitrage

- Selection criteria: the highest after-tax return by "mathematical expectation," limited to investments Berkshire understands, aiming to maximize eventual net worth and not reportable earnings. There is no bias among five categories: long-term stocks, medium-term bonds, long-term bonds, cash equivalents, and arbitrage.
- Warren raised Coca-Cola from 14,172,500 to 23,350,000 shares (cost $1,023,920,000, market $1,803,787,000 at year-end). He sold Cokes in 1936 and noticed the product for 52 years but bought no shares until summer 1988, having instead put money into "street railway companies, windmill manufacturers, anthracite producers, textile businesses, trading-stamp issuers." His praise for Roberto Goizueta, CEO since 1981, is that he has a "perfect" mesh of marketing and finance. Warren says he should have bought earlier and resolves to react faster to the next glaringly attractive idea.
- Other year-end holdings: Capital Cities/ABC (cost $517.5 million, market $1.69 billion), GEICO (cost $45.7 million, market $1.04 billion), Washington Post (cost $9.7 million, market $486 million), Freddie Mac.
- Arbitrage: Warren did little in 1989 because deals made no economic sense and arbitraging them "comes too close to playing the greater-fool game." He will do it only "when we like the odds."
- Bonds: Berkshire sold low-coupon WPPSS issues bought at big discounts, some of which had roughly doubled and paid 15%-17% tax-free. It also sold medium-term tax-exempts for about $51 million of pre-tax profit.
- Berkshire bought three convertible preferreds in 1989:
  - **Gillette:** $600 million, 8 3/4% dividend, convertible at $50.
  - **USAir:** $358 million, 9 1/4%, convertible at $60.
  - **Champion:** $300 million, 9 1/4%, convertible at $38.
  - All have mandatory redemption in ten years, and Berkshire's voting rights are unrestricted on an as-converted basis.
- Berkshire understands Gillette's economics ("go right out and get" the new Sensor razor), and has no ability to forecast airlines, paper, or investment banking. In those it structures investments differently, as "agnostics, not atheists." He wants to link only with people he likes, admires and trusts. Returns will be mediocre if industry economics are bad, and these are not a substitute for buying wonderful businesses or 80%+ of a fine company, "our favorite form of capital deployment."

## Zero-coupon bonds, EBDIT, and Wall Street excess

- Berkshire issued $902.6 million principal of zero-coupon convertible debentures at 44.314% of face (about $400 million raised), due in 15 years. This is equivalent to a 5.5% semiannual-compounded rate, and each bond converts at $9,815 per share, a 15% premium. Berkshire deducts the accrued 5.5% for tax, creating positive cash flow, so its effective cost is well below 5.5%.
- Warren calls Series E savings bonds and stripped Treasury coupons benign zeros, since they remove reinvestment risk for long-term holders. But "what the wise do in the beginning, fools do in the end": junk issuers use zero and PIK bonds because "it is impossible to default on a promise to pay nothing."
- Debt standards slid: first free cash flow had to cover interest and some principal, then deals were priced so all cash went to interest, then "EBDIT" replaced real cash flow, and finally EBDIT was measured against cash interest only. He calls ignoring depreciation "delusional": at 95% of businesses, capital spending roughly equal to depreciation is as real an expense as labor.
- His advice: "Whenever an investment banker starts talking about EBDIT... zip up your wallet." Test a deal by asking the promoter to take zero-coupon fees. Bankers should act as gatekeepers, like a responsible bartender. He notes that savings and loans that booked unreceived interest on zeros pushed the cost onto taxpayers. He says Berkshire has owned some zeros and might buy more if the market becomes sufficiently distressed, but never new issues from weak credits.

## Mistakes of the first 25 years

- **Cigar butts:** Warren bought Berkshire because the price looked cheap, though the textile business was unpromising. A "cigar butt" with one puff left gives you a profit only if you are a liquidator. The low business return erodes the bargain, and "never is there just one cockroach in the kitchen." "Time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre." Hochschild Kohn, bought at a discount to book value, was sold three years later for about what he paid. His conclusion: "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price."
- **Jockeys and horses:** good managers do well in good businesses but "not on broken-down nags." A management with a reputation for brilliance meeting a business with a reputation for bad economics leaves the business's reputation intact.
- **Avoid dragons:** after 25 years he and Charlie have not learned to solve difficult business problems; they avoid them. They look for "one-foot hurdles" and not seven-footers. Exceptions are a marvelous business with a one-time, solvable problem (American Express, GEICO).
- **Institutional imperative:** rationality wilts under four forces: (1) institutions resist change, (2) projects expand to soak up available funds, (3) the leader's craving gets quick support from studies prepared by subordinates, and (4) peer behavior is mindlessly imitated. Berkshire is organized to minimize this, and Warren favors investees that are alert to it.
- **People:** "We've never succeeded in making a good deal with a bad person." Going into business only with people he likes, trusts and admires will not make a poor business prosper, but it lets an owner accomplish wonders in businesses with decent economics.
- **Unseen mistakes:** the worst were purchases he understood but did not make, on which Berkshire paid a huge cost for "thumb-sucking." Missing opportunities outside one's circle of competence is no sin.
- **Conservatism on leverage:** higher leverage would likely have beaten 23.8%. But he rejects 99:1 odds: "A small chance of distress or disgrace cannot... be offset by a large chance of extra returns." He and Charlie "enjoy the process far more than the proceeds."

## What Warren looks for in acquisitions

- **Criteria:**
  1. Large purchases (at least $10 million of after-tax earnings).
  2. Demonstrated consistent earning power (no projections, no turnarounds).
  3. Good returns on equity with little or no debt.
  4. Management in place.
  5. Simple businesses ("if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it").
  6. An offering price.
- No unfriendly takeovers. He promises confidentiality and an answer within five minutes, prefers cash, and would issue stock only when Berkshire gets as much intrinsic value as it gives. Auction-like sales, new ventures and turnarounds are out: "Please include me out."
- His favorite form is the Blumkin-Friedman-Heldman mold: owner-managers who want cash but wish to remain significant owners and keep running the company. He also wants negotiated large, non-controlling blocks like those in Capital Cities, Salomon, Gillette, USAir and Champion.
- Harry Bottle turned around K&W Products (quadrupling profits), then bought a product line at a bankruptcy auction for a pittance. Berkshire's NYSE listing narrowed dealer spreads from 3% or more to under 1%.
