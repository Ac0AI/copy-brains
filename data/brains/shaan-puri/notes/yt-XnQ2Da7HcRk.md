## The economy runs on fear: sell to the root insecurity, but aim at option three

- Shaan's observation: "The economy is just smart people paying beautiful people to promote stuff to insecure people."
- Selling by audience:
  - Mothers: sell safety for their children.
  - Women: sell beauty or youth.
  - Men: sell status, power and importance.
- From his club-promoter days: people pay $500 for a bottle that costs $50 across the street, because the club is selling status.
- Know the root insecurity of the buyer and pull the strongest lever.
- The world is predatory by default. He argues you should see it "as it is" before using agency to shift it toward how it should be.
- He cites direct-mail copywriter Gary Halbert: "Never has more money been lost than selling somebody what they should want versus what they do want."
- Meet people where they are. Frame the offer and benefits so they resonate with what people actually want.
- Option one is manipulation that succeeds. Option two is the noble nonprofit that never learned what people want and gets nowhere. Shaan says don't debate them. Two bad options means "go create another one".
- Option three:
  - Combine an understanding of human psychology with a noble cause.
  - Tell stories, and show the happy ending instead of the work required.
  - Sell benefits, not features.
- If you are noble but can't reach people, or can reach people but do nothing noble, you are "pissing your talents away".

## The ABC method: only know A, B and Z

- Overthinking is highly transmissible. The cure is to simplify.
- The trap is mapping A through Z. Doubt about each step multiplies, and by halfway you are exhausted and paralyzed.
- Only three letters matter:
  - **A:** an honest view of where you are now. Self-delusion stalls you.
  - **B:** the next incremental action toward Z.
  - **Z:** the happy ending you want. It stays fixed.
- Once B is done you have a new A and a new B, and Z stays the same.
- His trainer's corner-store analogy:
  - Walking to the corner store for ice cream, you don't doubt you'll arrive, so you only think about the next turn.
  - He uses this for goals like podcasting, getting in shape or running the race with friends.
  - The trainer notices that high achievers are "stressor achievers": long stress, brief relief, new goalpost, new stress.
- Podcast example:
  - Define Z (his dream guests, his own studio), then describe A honestly: current guests, skill level, outreach volume, prep time.
  - B examples: send ten outreach messages this week, or study how Chris Williamson writes intros (he reads a tweet instead of a long-winded opener).
- Progress equals happiness for Shaan. Humans are wired to feel happy during progress, relative to where they are, so this formula helps you feel good while hitting goals.
- Early-stage projects feel great because of nonlinear momentum: big acceleration from a cold start.

## Advice-seeking is permission-seeking; the world is gated by courage, not intelligence

- "All requests for advice are actually requests for permission." People "don't want information. They want a short-term loan on courage."
- Asking five people for advice is usually looking for someone to validate the decision you already want to make, like wanting someone to approve your parking.
- His tweet: the world is not intelligence-gated. The real gates are courage, action, energy and wisdom. Plenty of people no smarter than us have better marriages and lives.
- Ask yourself "what permission am I seeking?" Often you already know which direction you're called to go, and fear is why you aren't going.
- Giving it to yourself:
  - Support from others is nice. He asks successful people "who was your first believer?" and they can answer immediately.
  - You can also grant the permission yourself once you see that's what you want.
- Example: his piano and singing teacher got him to try singing, and said he had worked with people who started worse. The teacher had not sung until high school, was mocked for it and shut it down, until a choir teacher heard him and said "who told you you can't sing?" He said not singing "was killing me."
- Fear underlies every label:
  - Procrastination, stress and anxiety are "fancy words" for fear.
  - Ask "why" about fear of failure and it ends at "I'm not enough, I won't be loved."
- Courage is feeling fear and acting anyway. Nobody calls a sip of water brave, unless the person drinking has a private terror of it.
- Parenting example:
  - His son wouldn't go into classes (jiu-jitsu, tennis, basketball). Shaan stopped getting frustrated and told him he used to cry at gymnastics too.
  - He framed it as courage and fear fighting, "Pikachu vs. Charizard", where sometimes fear wins. A month later his son said, "I didn't want to let the fear win today."

## Move to the white-hot center of your interest; soil matters more than the flower

- His claim: moving to San Francisco was the most impactful thing he did.
- The founder-market line: "when a great founder meets a bad market, only one of them retains their reputation. It's the bad market." The same applies to life: "It is far easier to succeed in great soil."
- Inertia and attachment to the familiar (hometown, friends, job) keep people in soil they wouldn't choose if they started from scratch.
- His advice to anyone in their 20s: move to the white-hot center of your interest.
  - Proximity is power. Ideally live in the same house as people with similar dreams.
  - He calls it "the single greatest variable you can change in your life", more than any book or podcast.
- What San Francisco taught him on day one:
  - At a coffee shop he overheard a founder pitching "peptides for dogs" to an investor.
  - That night a JP Morgan employee at a startup event apologized for his job and said he'd leave soon. Status there goes to chasing a startup idea, not to working at big corp.
  - Before that, he had been the weird one for being unemployed and trying to build something.
- Seasons: what is right in one season of life may not be in another. Answer the call you feel, and moving can accelerate it.

## Follow obsession, use fuel sources deliberately, and connect the dots by making them bold

- Discipline, motivation and obsession:
  - Shaan treats them as different fuel sources for the same engine: discipline (Goggins), inspiration or motivation, and obsession.
  - He compares them to calories. Some are clean and sustainable, some are dirty and unsustainable, and you choose which fuel you use.
- "Inspiration is like an avocado": very perishable, so act on it quickly.
- Trusting yourself to follow inspiration even when you don't know where it leads is a muscle and a way of living, "like some people sit upright, some people slouch."
- Steve Jobs example:
  - Jobs took a calligraphy class in college out of pure interest.
  - That led the Mac to launch with multiple fonts, which helped it succeed.
  - You can't connect dots looking forward, so "make every dot as bold as you can": do genuine-interest things fully, without knowing where they lead.
- Windows vs. doors:
  - A friend (his mentor) told the youngest VC in San Francisco, "you only want to open windows, never doors." Windows let you see the other side first. With a door you knock without knowing.
  - Sometimes nobody answers or the other side isn't what you wanted, but knocking enough times creates surface area for luck.
  - Shaan now asks where he is gravitating toward windows and which door he could knock on.

## Winning is balanced success; the final 10% costs more than the first 90%

- After selling a company he had "all the money I'll ever need", then hit the "dog who caught the car" problem: who am I if not the person chasing this?
- He hired a coach, Dr. Jack Kane (met through Andrew Wilkinson), who told him the fable of Iron John.
  - The three types of men in the story: good boys, wild men and great kings.
  - A good boy pleases others and does what is expected: school, job, house, two kids, country club. He has dissatisfaction because he never asked what his soul wanted.
  - A wild man is a free man, not a bad man. He is courageous, disciplined, has principles and serves his own needs.
  - A great king is a wild man who starts caring about things beyond himself, and "a good boy never becomes a great king."
  - Shaan says he's the wild man today and wants to become the great king.
- His circle-of-care model:
  - A baby cares only about itself. Each new thing you care about forces you to grow.
  - When you care about something more than your comfort, you have to change.
  - A king serves a kingdom (family, friends, community, strangers online, people with a rare disease).
- The "what serves you most will also screw you" idea:
  - His own superpower, storytelling and persuasion, means he is "patient zero" for his own persuasion.
  - He uses a sword/power-tool analogy: know the downside and wield it with care.
- "Arrival fallacy":
  - Many people who hit their goals become aimless, or run it back to prove it wasn't luck (he cites an Airbnb founder).
  - He is less impressed by success than by success achieved with balance, fun and being a good person.
  - His view: pure success has a formula, while success plus a good marriage, family and integrated self is "much closer to art than science."
- "Greatness bubble":
  - Nonlinear progress means beginner's gains come fast, then a plateau, then the last 10% costs enormously.
  - Extremes (bodybuilders, billionaires, Olympians) are highly visible but not desirable.
  - Shaan: "We confuse an extraordinary result with an extraordinary life." He knows "bankrupt billionaires" and "fat doctors."
  - He admires Goggins for one hour a day (workouts) and doesn't want to be him.
- Kevin Kelly story: asked what being Kevin Kelly cost, he said the cost was "greatness". He surrendered the biggest-empire version for a freer life.
- "Enough" is the revolutionary stance: it's okay to be satisfied.
- Be great, don't just accomplish great things:
  - Be great in moment-to-moment interactions, like how you greet someone or compliment a shirt.
  - Example: a friend, Ramon, a single dad, started a football team for his son and later coached other boys with no football background. He was asked to give the commencement speech, and instead brought a formerly shy kid on stage and played "Gangnam Style".

## Relationships: heal to 101%, ask "what are we trying to do here?", and reset with play

- Gottman insight: he claims Gottman could predict with about 90% accuracy whether couples stay together from watching them briefly. It's about how well you fight, not compatibility.
- After every cut, relationships heal to either 99% or 101%. Heal to 99% and the relationship degrades over time. A small 2% swing matters.
- In the moment you're focused on the cut ("how dare you"), not the healing.
- Ask "what are we trying to do here?" Not about the situation: "I love you. You love me. We're on the same team."
- Reaction vs. response:
  - Great people respond instead of reacting. We give better advice to others than to ourselves because in conflict we go into fight-or-flight.
  - Rewind phrase: "I didn't like my reaction back there, let me try again." He literally makes a rewind sound effect at home.
- Improv lesson:
  - The first class taught nothing about being funny. It was about failing: when you say something stupid, say "whoop," and everyone responds "whoop," like shaking an Etch A Sketch.
  - Lesson: have a way to clear the slate.
- Play can break tension when logic can't.
  - He tells a story (admitting he doesn't know how true it is) of a US president who walked out of a tense Russia meeting, turned at the door and playfully reintroduced himself.
  - Humor, silliness and play are legitimate tools alongside "heavy" emotional work.
  - "We're not as sophisticated as we hope to believe" (a laugh track makes things funnier).
- His current focus: making a movie, building a "crazy magic thing" in New York, and "projects with the boys", picking creative projects with inspiring people.
