## Underdoing the competition as positioning

Shaan calls "underdoing the competition" one of his favorite business tactics. Instead of piling on features like everyone else, a company goes the opposite direction and does less.

- His example is the Slate truck, a $25k electric truck with an old-school vibe, which he calls "genius".
- Cars keep getting more complicated: touch screens, butt warmers, and a "Maybe-Gonna-Kill-You" driving mode.
- Slate has no screens and no seat heaters, and it has roll-up windows.
- It is good only for local driving, with a 150 mile battery range.
- At the launch event, Slate said the truck has old-school knobs instead of digital buttons, and "the crowd literally roared."

## All positioning is counter-positioning

Shaan's rule is: "In marketing: all positioning is counter-positioning." You define yourself by what the rest of the market is not.

- Every other electric car is fancy, expensive and complex.
- Slate is old school, affordable and simple.
- He argues the result is a change of category. Slate would have been last place in the fancy electric car market. Now it is first place in the simple, fun electric truck market.
- The takeaway is to stop competing for rank in a crowded category and pick the opposite attribute of the leaders.

## Be the only, not the best

Shaan backs the idea with Kevin Kelly's line: "Don't aim to be the best, be the only." Underdoing is a way to become the only option in a niche instead of one of many in a crowded field.

## Nintendo Wii as the proof case

Shaan calls Nintendo the best at this tactic.

- Xbox and Playstation were racing to build the most cutting-edge, high-graphics systems.
- Nintendo shipped a goofy, low-fi Wii at half the price.
- The games were simple enough that "even your mom could play" (Wii Tennis, Bowling).
- The graphics were simple too: cartoon characters rather than ultra-realistic ones.
- It sold over 100M units, outselling both Xbox and Playstation at the time.

## How to apply the underdo tactic

The steps below are Shaan's pattern as shown in his two examples.

1. Look at the direction your whole market is moving, such as more features, more complexity, higher price or higher fidelity.
2. Go the opposite way: fewer features, a lower price, a simpler product.
3. Accept the limits on purpose, as Slate did with its short range and its local-driving-only focus.
4. Market the simplicity as the headline. The knobs and roll-up windows got a roar from the crowd.
5. Aim to be first place in a new, simpler category rather than last place in the existing one.

He closes with a quote from Ralph Waldo Emerson: "Indeed, to be simple, is to be great."
