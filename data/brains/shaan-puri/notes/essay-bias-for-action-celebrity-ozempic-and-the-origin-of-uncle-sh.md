## Bias for action is a trainable skill built from reps, noticing, and better prompts

Shaan argues that a high action bias can be taught, but only if you first believe it is a skill rather than an inborn trait. He compares it to a player having a "motor" in sports: someone who just keeps moving and taking action, and he thinks the idea applies to every area of life.

His four-step method:
- **Believe it's a skill.** If you think it's a trait you're born with, you can't train it.
- **Get reps in small, safe places first.** He says "quit my job to start the company" shouldn't be the first bold action you ever take. Example: when you have an idea to improve your home office, grab the keys, drive to Home Depot, buy the parts, watch YouTube, and hammer it out in one night.
- **Pause to notice each time you act.** Awareness is a "superpower." When you pick up the phone and call instead of procrastinating, or lace up shoes and walk instead of being tired, take note, so your brain reinforces "there's more evidence of me having an insanely high action bias."
- **Change your factory settings.** See the next section.

## Rewrite the questions you ask your brain to get faster, bigger answers

Shaan treats his brain like ChatGPT: you put in a prompt and it spits out an answer. Rather than trying to make the AI smarter, "it's much easier to try just changing the prompt first."

- His default questions in a situation were "what should I do?" and "will this work?" He calls these weak questions.
- Better questions: "how can I take massive action on this idea?" and "how can I move with speed on this?"
- Adding words like "massive action" and "move with speed" makes the brain "come up with a completely different answer."

## Flipping as an untried early-stage investing strategy

Asked for the investing equivalent of basketball's three-pointer discovery, Shaan says edges like this are hard to find and offers a "half baked" answer: **flipping**. Real estate has both buy-and-hold investors and flippers, but tech investing only has buy-and-hold.

- As an angel investor he makes many bets and waits about 10 years hoping 1-2 become multi-billion dollar winners.
- His estimate is that more than 50% of his seed bets raise another round, at an average valuation increase of roughly 2.5-3X (he says he'd have to run the numbers).
- Illustrative math: 10 investments at $100k each; 60% raise a new round at a 3X increase and are sold. That turns $1M into $1.8M (80%), usually in under 2 years, about a 34% IRR, which he says would outperform 95% of venture funds with far more liquidity.
- Bonus for founders: if the seed investor sells to the new investor, the founder takes less net dilution.
- Why nobody does it: **perception**. Angels are supposed to be true believers who hold to exit or IPO, but "those are just cultural norms, not rules." It's the one tech-investing strategy he'd love to see someone try.

## What he took from the inside of Amazon

Shaan clarifies that he sold his first company to Twitch (owned by Amazon), not Amazon directly, and was there about 2 years. He says it was like spending a summer in Bezos' pool house, not the main house. Two takeaways:

- **Big companies aren't as sophisticated as they look.** He had assumed his scrappy startups were doing things the dumb way and big tech must be sophisticated inside. He found some impressive things but "mostly a zoo of mediocrity."
- **Writing culture is the best habit he kept.** Writing memos forces clarity of thought and is the best way to communicate inside companies. He still uses the Amazon 6-page memo.

## Why names matter and the Uncle Shaan nickname

Shaan believes "names matter," pointing to Whoopi Goldberg, who he says wouldn't have been as successful as Caryn Johnson.

- He tried to self-assign a nickname ("Shaan The Don," "Shanny J," "LL Cool Shaan," then "Shaan P," like Gary V). It flopped; nobody used it.
- "Uncle Shaan" stuck only when a friend, George, suggested a YouTube channel where people call in with questions for Uncle Shaan, and other friends started saying it.
- His aim is to be "the internet's fun rich uncle."
- Lesson: "don't try to give yourself a nickname, that's not how it works."

## AGI definitions and his own test

Shaan says the common expert definition is "when computers are as smart as the smartest humans at a wide range of cognitive tasks," meaning expert level at pretty much everything humans can do. But the definition keeps changing and nobody agrees.

- The Turing test was once the milestone, and AI has now passed it with "nobody freaking out."
- **His definition:** AGI is the point when AI is better at developing AI than humans. At that point AI improves exponentially without human involvement.
- He sees open questions after that: whether it stays a tool or develops its own agenda, and what stops a human from using it for evil.
- He relays an investor friend (ex-Peter Thiel) saying "GLP > GPT," meaning Ozempic is a bigger deal than ChatGPT, and says you should be more scared of gaining 25 lbs than of AGI.

## Ozempic as "cheating" and the status-symbol question

Shaan is "100% Team Ozempic-Is-Cheating." People are free to take it, but he can't give the same respect he gives someone who works hard and disciplines mind and body to lose weight, versus someone taking a shot every 2 weeks.

- He asks what becomes the new status symbol if losing weight gets easy. In the Victorian era, with scarce food, everyone was skinny and being fat was the status symbol.
- He jokes he hopes society returns to the Roman era, when a huge nose was considered masculine and attractive.
