## Frame the idea, not just the hook

- Shaan says hooks are "somewhat overrated" and frames are underrated. A hook is the attention-grabbing first line. A frame is how you present the idea itself, and how you make it relevant by connecting two ideas that seem unrelated.
- Example: instead of describing what Hasan Minhaj does to open a set, frame it as "Here's what you can learn about public speaking from one of the greatest public speakers in the world." Same content, new frame.
- He argues the great writers are great at framing ideas, more than at writing the perfect one-line hook.
- His Clubhouse thread used a frame: "everyone seems to think Clubhouse is the next big thing, but I think it's going to fail. Here's how I think it all goes down." The thread (about 20 million readers) was written like a screenplay: "you're the founder of Clubhouse," with a setup of Kanye in your DMs and VCs throwing money at you.
- The contrast was a dry thread by the founder of Secret that opened with jargon ("existential threat number one, time to value ratio"). Shaan says it felt like homework.
- He says "here's how it goes down" is gossip and story, while "here's why I'm right" is logic.
- His Dave Chappelle example: Chappelle wanted people to boycott Netflix over his show. He told two stories (a veteran comedian taking his joke when he was 15, and losing $100 to a three-card monte crew) and framed Hollywood contracts as the same con. Shaan says it worked; Netflix pulled the show.
- Shaan also credits his friend Trevor's "jungle tiger vs zoo tiger" frame for making growth-mindset research memorable. He says memorability is massively underrated: "What's the point of having the good ideas if nobody remembers them?"

## Story is intention, obstacle and a five-second moment of change

- Shaan learned the core of storytelling from Aaron Sorkin, who said he could teach a "30-second masterclass": intention and obstacle. At any moment you should be able to pause and ask what the character wants and what is in their way. If that is unclear for 5 minutes, attention drifts.
- Intention includes why they want it, how badly, and what happens if they don't get it.
- Stakes don't have to be life or death. He gets "bonus points" for low-stakes moments, and says stories like "I was skiing and there was an avalanche" are unrelatable and even unlikable.
- His example: at an executive offsite after his company was acquired, he told a story about cooking Brussels sprouts for his mom, whom he had never invited over because he didn't know how to host. People came up at the break to say it was hilarious.
- From the book Storyworthy: stakes come from emotion (embarrassment, ego). Don't tell "vacation stories" or "cool party" stories.
- "A story is a five-second moment of change." The ending is the opposite of the opening (rom-coms, Die Hard, Scrooge). The heart of the story is the moment of transformation, usually when the character is cornered or hits rock bottom.
- His advice: if you can't name the five-second moment of change, you don't have a great story yet.
- A story is not a record of what happened. Include events only so far as they explain how the change came about.

## Workshopping an origin story: signature stories and zooming in

- Shaan recommends having 4-5 "signature stories" (origin story and others) that would tell someone everything about you.
- Workshopping David Perell's origin story, he gave this feedback:
  - Cut details that muddy the message (the raise and promotion didn't fit a story about not doing well).
  - Add concrete low moments that make the "before" state real, such as a performance review where "needs improvement" was far longer than the pros.
  - Don't skip the middle. Explain what triggered the turnaround, the first thing you did, and the resistance you overcame ("you fast-forwarded the montage too fast").
- He cites the Pixar rule that the audience loves the hero for how hard they try, not for winning. An instant success isn't a good story.
- Zoom into moments instead of summarizing, with a visual or audio detail and a feeling. His example is Tony Robbins: washing dishes in the bathtub, one chair, a song playing, then running even though his lungs burned. Robbins has to convince you he was a loser for the "after" to land.
- Self-deprecation and low status build likeability. Hasan Minhaj told him "comedy is a low-status game," and opens a special with his own fertility problem. After a stiff dinner intro, a friend told Shaan to tell a funny story instead of trying to sound impressive.

## Writing mechanics: write like you talk, one reader, emotion first

- "Write like you talk." He often says the story out loud first, then writes down what he said. Outline by answering: what's the story, what makes it interesting, where does it start, where does it end.
- He uses voice transcription and walks while he talks to get first drafts down.
- School teaches you to pretend (big vocabulary, page minimums). In the real world, good writing is simple, easy to read, entertaining and has a voice.
- Length is only as long as it is interesting. Match it to your buy-in with the audience: on a podcast you can riff, but cold traffic needs a strong headline and first line. TikTok gives under a second.
- Write to one person at one moment. He cites Steve Bartlett's "Jenny in her bedroom" and BuzzFeed's "Debbie at her desk" (the bored-at-work network).
- Work backwards from emotion. Chris Quigley's agency had about an 8-in-10 viral hit rate with this method; Shaan says people only share things that are "LOL, WTF, OMG, AW." Pick the reaction, write to it, then check if it will create it, and juice it up.
- From Gary Halbert's Boron Letters, he took the habit of writing to one specific person you have a warm relationship with (a student, a little cousin, a younger self). Sometimes he plays the sensei; sometimes the beginner who admits "you probably know four better ways."
- He addresses the questions he thinks the reader has at that moment, like a good podcast host would.
- "Imitate, then innovate": deconstruct a favorite writer, find the general principle, then apply it.
- Not everything needs a story. "Comedy is great but not every line should be a joke."

## State, curiosity and getting unstuck

- He cites Miss Excel, who says she changes her energy before making content, because "all content is energy transferred through the phone." Shaan says no athlete or performer goes on cold, yet most writers start cold.
- Changing your state has three levers:
  - Physiology: sprints, push-ups, air squats, cold water. His poker-pro friend does wind sprints in the parking lot during tournament breaks.
  - Focus: put your attention on what you want, not on the deadline or the difficulty.
  - Story: what you tell yourself. Moving from "I'm doing David a favor" to "one person will hear one thing that changes their life" changes the words that come out.
- He once got out of a writing slump after a coach pointed out that his story had become "I want to be a creative force." What had worked before was finding an interesting idea, figuring it out, and sharing it.
- He quotes Paul Graham: excitement is the engine and the rudder. When he drifted to ambition or money as the driver, things went worse.
- He holds opposing traits together (creative and analytical, visionary and detail-obsessed like Steve Jobs). "If you're in your head, you're dead" is a life philosophy about being present, not a ban on analysis. Use logic for logic problems and emotion for emotion problems.
- To get unstuck: if you're stuck in specifics, go general ("what am I really trying to do?"). If you're stuck in the general, go specific ("what was the best idea I heard in the last 2 days?"). He cites Ray Bradbury's "Don't think" sign.

## Practice, learning from other fields, and building a binge bank

- He looks for valuable skills that people don't practice or are embarrassed to say they work on: storytelling, enthusiasm, humor, energy. He paid $5,000 to a personal-branding consultant and calls it "the lamest thing ever," but took a useful lesson from it.
- Learn from the masters of the craft (Sorkin, Chappelle, Minhaj, Mr. Beast, Steve Bartlett), not from business people. Mr. Beast studies TV and movie character development because looking only at other YouTubers leads to "a sea of sameness."
- Expect about 100 reps. Do 100 and improve one thing each time. Mr. Beast gives the same advice: make 100 videos, improving one thing each time. Shaan says most people never do it, and those who do never need to come back.
- Create a "binge bank." Two young creators told him each video gets few views, but they are stacking material so anyone curious about them can spend an hour and leave respecting them. He says this beats a resume or portfolio, and it is a way to stay motivated when feedback is small.
- Distribution is earned over time, via the "1-2-3 rule of interestingness" from his friend Jason. One interesting thing is "interesting," two is "those were interesting," three is "you're interesting." On the internet it may take about 20. Seed the first readers with friends, group chats, and personalized messages.
- On Twitter he reached about 400,000 followers. He says he ignored the usual advice (consistency, a niche) and would likely have a million if he had followed it. What worked was having an interesting take when attention was on a subject (his metaverse thread, written when Facebook rebranded to Meta). He says you need two skills: interesting things to say and packaging.

## Brand as a feeling, editing, humor and training a writer

- Personal brand lesson: "People will follow you to the ends of the earth if you are giving them a feeling that they can't get anywhere else more consistently than anybody else." Examples: TED sells inspiration, comedy podcasts sell camaraderie, Twitch sells the feeling of playing without playing. Pick the feeling, then deliver it consistently.
- Editing process, learned from Sam Parr: write a rough draft, then walk away for roughly 4-24 hours (workout, kids, walk) before editing. When you return, ask whether it does what you wanted and what's blocking it.
- His mindset: it's a river and your ideas are good; remove the rocks. He says Pixar frames each draft as removing the suck.
- Humor:
  - It is the sauce, not the entree.
  - All humor is surprise: set an expectation, then subvert it. He cites Seinfeld's New York Times piece (pooper-scooper streets, towed car, "the greatest place I've ever been").
  - Funny words matter ("pooper scooper").
  - A Theo Von exercise is to rename one thing six different ways ("Jesus' binder").
  - Add a little of the opposite emotion, like Tabasco.
  - His strategy: "pretty smart, kind of funny" beats being the funniest or smartest.
- At Milk Road, a crypto newsletter, he trained a writer who had never written professionally by:
  - Using a consistent format and breaking down the opening line by line, with 10 examples of good.
  - Starting every issue with the same opening pattern (a joke tag like a toaster strudel comparison, "second best feeling in the world... the dolphin hand out the car window").
  - Having him send a voice memo about the news before writing. If it isn't interesting in a voice note, it won't be interesting in text, which filters out topics you're not truly interested in.
  - Making the goal "give you a reason to open this that's not based on the Bitcoin price."
