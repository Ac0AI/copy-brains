## Excuses are neutral: pick ones that move you forward

Shaan's core claim: "Life changes once you realize everything is an excuse." An excuse is "simply a reason that you make up and choose to believe in," and it can be positive or negative. The things we call reasons are all just excuses, so you get to decide which ones to hold.

- He asks: "Are you going to find excuses to feel shitty, and be shitty? Or find excuses to feel good, and do good?"
- The shift is from treating circumstances as causes to treating them as stories you pick.
- Applied to work and discipline, it means choosing the interpretation that gets you to act.

## Same circumstance, opposite excuse: the tired-workout example

Shaan gives a simple example of one fact producing two opposite decisions.

- Shaan wakes up tired and takes it as a great excuse to skip his workout: "I'm tired! Surely I need rest."
- His trainer wakes up tired and reasons the opposite: he needs a quick workout to get his body moving so he doesn't feel sluggish.
- The trainer "found an excuse _to do it_." Shaan "found an excuse to _not do it._"
- Lesson: the condition (tiredness) doesn't decide the behavior. The story you attach to it does.

## Pain as fuel: heroes and villains share the same back story

Shaan cites Alex Hormozi's line on pain as something you can use or be used by:

"Heroes and villains always have the same back story, pain. The difference is what they choose to do about it. Villain says 'the world hurt me, I'll hurt it back.' Hero says 'the world hurt me, I'm not gonna let it hurt anyone else. Heroes use pain. Villains are used by it.'"

- He endorses this as the frame for choosing a positive excuse.
- The same setback can justify quitting, bitterness or harm, or justify building something and helping others.

## Mike Posner's walk across America: an excuse to restart

Shaan tells the story from a podcast he did with Mike Posner (writer of "I Took A Pill In Ibiza").

- After his friend Avicii died, Mike used the sadness as an excuse to do something on his bucket list, because life is short: walk across America.
- He covered about two-thirds of the way, was bitten by a rattlesnake and nearly died. He was hospitalized, doctors said he might lose the leg, and he spent a few weeks re-learning how to walk.
- Once he could walk again, he went straight back to the spot where the snake bit him and resumed.
- Nobody would have faulted him for stopping. Mike's framing: "everyone expected me to quit. Either this is an excuse to stop. Or it's an excuse to restart. So I went back and restarted. And when I did that, I became someone new."
- Takeaway Shaan draws: the same near-death event can be a reason to stop or a reason to restart, and the person chooses which.

## How to use the idea

- When you catch yourself with a reason not to do something (tired, hurt, setback), recognize it as an excuse you are choosing, not a fact that forces your hand.
- Look for the opposite excuse that pushes you toward action, as the trainer did with tiredness and Mike did with the snakebite.
- Treat painful events as material to use rather than something that uses you.
