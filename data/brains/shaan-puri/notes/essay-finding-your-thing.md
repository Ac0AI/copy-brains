## Finding your thing is the only true shortcut

Shaan argues that everyone he admires found their "thing," and that finding it is "the only true shortcut to life." He frames it with an analogy: you realize you have a dirt bike, so a little dirt path is perfect for you, even if it won't suit people in cars.

- Michael Phelps: double-jointed elbows, a torso 8 inches too long, and half the lactic acid production of a normal human. "Built like a flying fish," so he found swimming.
- Joe Rogan: liked getting high and having funny conversations with friends about everything from fighting to aliens. Shaan couldn't hold a 3-hour conversation with a stranger, but Joe can, so he found podcasting.
- The point: your thing is what fits your unusual wiring, not what is generally prestigious.

## The question that surfaces it: what is everyone else mysteriously bad at?

Shaan cites a quote from Ben Kuhn (CTO of Anthropic): "what does it seem like everyone else is mysteriously bad at?"

- He recommends replacing "what am I great at?" with "what does everyone else mysteriously suck at?"
- Reason: most people are too humble, or too self-critical, to admit what they are great at. Noticing what frustrates you about others' incompetence is an easier signal to read.
- Application: pay attention to what seems obvious or easy to you but that others keep getting wrong.

## Shaan's own path: 10 years of "founder," then explaining things

- He always wondered why other people are so bad at explanations. In school he hated how teachers explained things ("too complicated, too boring"). As an investor he was surprised how bad founders are at telling their own story.
- From age 20 to 30 he tried to make being a great CEO his thing. He "wanted" it to be his thing, but wanting doesn't make it so. He was "a B+ at it. But in the real world, B+ doesn't get you jack sh\*t."
- It took 10 years to find his actual thing. He then started podcasting for fun and writing, which was the thing he thought others were mysteriously bad at: explaining things.
- Results: the podcast hit 100m+ streams, his Twitter grew to 450k followers, and his newsletter grew fast and was sold within 1 year for millions of dollars.
- Lesson: don't force a thing you want to be good at; follow the thing you do naturally and enjoyably, and where you have an edge over others.

## Ben Levy's thing: keeping in touch

Shaan's cofounder Ben Levy finds it strange that everyone is terrible at keeping in touch. Shaan admits he is guilty of it: he forgets to reply, doesn't check in, and will "have a great lunch meeting, then forget to keep in touch for 2 years."

Ben does the opposite:
- Always replies and loves to check in.
- Texts you if you write a blog post, to say it's great.
- Sends a gift if you have a baby.
- Sends useful links relevant to your project, knows your favorite sports team, and texts after every game.
- Texts about 100 unique people every day, with no agenda, just because he likes it.

Payoff: "We've directly made millions of dollars just because Ben happened to be staying in touch with someone and they thought of us when an opportunity sprang up."

## Takeaways

- Everyone has a thing; you just have to find it, and you may not know it today.
- Your thing is often something you do for fun or without effort that others find hard.
- A "B+" at the thing you wish you were good at loses to being exceptional at the thing you're naturally wired for.
- Habits others see as chores (staying in touch, explaining clearly) can be compounding advantages and a source of deal flow.
