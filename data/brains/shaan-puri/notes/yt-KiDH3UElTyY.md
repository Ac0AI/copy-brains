## Pick projects and partners before effort: hard work is overrated

- Shaan says "hard work is overrated." He ranks it maybe the fourth or fifth most important variable, behind project selection, who you work with, timing and luck.
- Project selection comes first. "Choosing what you work on is far more important than how hard you work." He notes that in the restaurant industry, no amount of effort changes your outcomes much.
- He thinks successful people credit hard work because it "sounds good" and gives them air cover. It implies everyone had an equal shot.
- Hard work is still good for building skill and showing you are serious. In your 20s you can throw hours at problems. As you get older, have kids and have less energy, your judgment has to make up for the lost time.
- Most projects fail but the skills stick. His sushi restaurant failed, but along the way he learned After Effects, iMovie and Photoshop "just enough to be dangerous." That helped him mock things up himself in Silicon Valley.
- Skills can't be inherited, bought or taken away. He paraphrases Derek Sivers on this. Skill is "the key that unlocks an infinite number of doors."
- Skill transfer example: a founder who learned paid ads in small DTC e-commerce moved to a venture-backed B2B software company. Most people there were 0 to 6 out of 10 at paid ads, so it was "shooting fish in a barrel." He was selling a $20,000 recurring contract instead of a $20 widget, and the company hit a roughly $300-400M valuation within two years.
- Picking projects and picking partners are both skills. Few people even use the phrase "project selection."

## Choose a project by whether the work itself is the reward

- His rule now: "Am I doing this for a result or because I like doing it?" He no longer works on something for a future payoff.
- "The work has to be the win." The result becomes a bonus. If you do it for the payoff and it fails, which is most of the time, you have wasted the time.
- He spent 10 years doing things only because of "ooh, if this worked it'd be amazing." He believes he'd be further ahead had he followed enjoyment earlier, though you can't A/B test a life.
- The flywheel: you enjoy it, so you do it all the time. Doing it all the time makes you really good. Being really good gets the results. Without enjoyment you only work as hard as your willpower allows, you get so-so good, and you get a so-so result.
- He cites Paul Graham's idea to let "interestingness" be the filter. Your taste profile can't be copied, so it filters out other people, and it keeps you doing the work.
- The cost to avoid is opportunity cost, which is bigger than taxes or expenses for anyone smart enough.

## Find your thing: ask other people and audit your five-to-nine

- Other people spot your strengths before you do. His example is Naval, who wanted to be a physicist. His mom said he was a business guy because he kept pointing out how restaurants could raise revenue or change their menus.
- Ask friends: "What's my superpower? What comes easy to me that's harder for other people?"
- Ask yourself where you do things that feel like play to you but look like work or a grind to others. Look at your five-to-nine, not your nine-to-five, and at what you do beyond what is rational.
- His own example: before a recording he was reading the Nevada annual report on Las Vegas Strip casino revenues for 2024 vs 2023, as a possible recession indicator. Nobody assigned it. Years of those rabbit holes became the library of facts and stories he uses on the podcast.
- As a kid he never played NBA 2K games. He ran franchise mode, scouting players, making trades and raising concession prices. That was practice at being a manager or CEO.
- Don't hide or fix your oddities. Ask what superpower they give you and where they would be valuable.
- His wife is OCD and loved arts and crafts. She started bedazzling phones with Swarovski crystals, and as a freshman celebrities were asking for custom pieces and she was making thousands of dollars a month.

## Be serious: the rule of 100 and the competition thinning out

- At a Tony Robbins event, a Simon Says game among 10,000 people ended with five on stage. When asked, only about 50 people had believed they would win. Shaan's takeaway is that you were competing with 50, "not 10,000." Most people are not serious, so if you are, your odds are much better than they look.
- Jimmy Donaldson (MrBeast) tells would-be YouTubers to make 100 videos, improving one thing each time (intro, caption, outro, music, pacing, editing). Then come back and he'll say what's next.
- Nobody comes back. Jimmy says that by 100 videos they no longer need him because they have momentum.
- Jimmy himself posted for roughly seven years with almost nobody watching before it took off. Shaan says the rule of 100 applies to any pursuit.
- Everything Jimmy does is public and has been for a decade, yet people still ask for the secret.

## Proximity is power and network selection

- From Tony Robbins: proximity is power. "Pull, not push." If everyone you like and respect works out, it is almost easier to do it than to resist.
- Poker example: the people who got good lived in a house with five other obsessed players, ate and slept poker and reviewed hands at dinner. They improved far faster than others. Same logic as learning Spanish by living in Mexico instead of using Duolingo.
- He moved to San Francisco for startups. Three times he asked who the most impressive founder was, and each time they had already moved there. He changed his phone number to a San Francisco area code, told people he was moving, only job hunted there, signed a lease and flew in on a one-way ticket.
- James Currier (NFX, roughly a $1.2B fund) pushes the network view. Where you go to college is a network you join. Moving to cheaper taxes can be a dumb trade if you leave the "white hot center" of tech or AI. For good people, opting into a better network is almost always worth more than the incremental dollar, especially early, because it compounds.
- He told his former intern that a sales job at about $12k a month would put him "another sales guy," and that working with him would be the better network. He admits this was a mistake of the same kind he made early on.
- Your info diet is also a network. If you consume what everyone else consumes you'll think what they think. To differentiate, change your info diet, the people you spend time with and what you do in your free time. Jiu-jitsu is one example of a different hobby that opts you into a different network.
- Better does not mean you can't win elsewhere. It means higher odds and an easier path. "There's no bonus points for doing everything the hard way."

## Partner selection: Buffett's three plus "down"

- Buffett's framework is energy, intelligence and integrity. Without integrity you get a smart crook. Without intelligence you don't get anywhere.
- Shaan adds a fourth: being "down." It is his highest priority. Down means:
  - willing to try a half-baked idea
  - up for adventure over the safe path
  - willing to buckle down and grind without running from difficulty
- Example: Ben (now his business partner) had a SaaS business making about $1M a year in profit. Shaan asked him to spend time on a random project with only a rough idea. Ben said he was down. They have done five to seven things together since, and every one has been fun and has worked.

## Strategically broke year and the sushi restaurant

- After college he took a $120,000 a year job in a boring industry. A month and a half in he quit, because his group had won a business plan competition for a sushi chain idea, Sabi Sushi ("the Chipotle of sushi"), with $25,000 in prize money for the three of them. That was about $8,000 each for a year.
- He chose to be "strategically broke." His banker friends were money rich and time poor. He chose to be time rich, adventure rich and learning rich instead, learning 12 times more to match the 12x income gap.
- What he learned: P&Ls, margins, pitching investors, lease negotiation, real estate, sales, marketing, design, video editing, blogging, hiring and firing.
- Negotiation example: he found that a liquor license puts a lease on file with the city. He looked up what a restaurant with a license paid, then negotiated his own rent down using that figure.
- Lifestyle was minimal: an air mattress and a garbage bag nailed to the wall as a bin.
- Side income: coaching basketball at a school for autistic children and tutoring stats at $25-30 an hour. He calculated the minimum he needed to earn for maximum freedom.
- He applies the same principle now. He works out the minimum funding a company needs to reach its next milestone, such as 1,000 customers who love it. He asks what amount of money feels good while leaving the most time for family and projects.
- He quotes Dharmesh Shah (HubSpot) that his first business, run by ignorance, did better than his second, and that "just because I was ignorant doesn't mean I was wrong."
- He quit the sushi restaurant too, as it did not work out.

## Reversing decisions, motion over direction, and quitting the wrong thing

- Shaan says he is not good at making decisions but is "great at reversals." Once he sees a decision is wrong he cuts it off fast, he says partly because he can't tolerate it.
- Underneath most reasons for staying is fear: of rejection, embarrassment, failure. People use code words like "stress." Many would "rather live in pain than go toward an unknown."
- His dad backed the sushi plan, even calling it a terrible idea, because Shaan was newly switched on and building skills. The analogy: you're on a foggy beach and want paradise but can't see it. You can wait for the fog to clear or paddle out in your small boat. Once you're moving it is easy to change direction, so "sometimes life is about motion, not direction."
- Selling Bebo (his company, run in a lab funded by a billionaire): the office was the nicest in San Francisco, with a private chef, a Friday masseuse and a built-in bar. A friend, Sully, told him over a casino buffet at 1 a.m., "I don't get what you're doing." Six years in, the project was neither a success nor an obvious failure.
- Sully's inertia test, the thought experiment: if the lab closed tomorrow, would you call the same people and start this same thing? Shaan realised the answer was no.
- The next day he told his co-founder to sell the company or end it. He asked the investor for 30 days to sell it or he'd walk away and hand over his equity. They signed a definitive agreement about 45 days later, with diligence still to come.
- The ideal answer to "would I be doing this if I weren't already?" is a "hell yes." Tweaks, like the commute, are fine to fix. He borrows from Elon: "The biggest waste of time is doing something well that needn't have been done at all."
- Mediocrity is the real risk for high-potential people: "it'll sap your will, your time, your resources, your energy, your belief in yourself." Failure is quick and keeps your time at your disposal.

## Make life intentional: Misogi

- A Misogi (from Jesse Itzler, who was a podcast guest) is one grand challenge each year that is hard, memorable and meaningful. Shaan wants one so years don't blend together. Time otherwise fills with Zoom calls, errands and weekly stand-ups.
- His 2025 Misogi was learning to play the piano well enough to jam, and to play in a "dad band."
- He wants to be able to play for the rest of his life, including at 80.
