## Project selection beats hard work

- Shaan calls hard work "massively overrated" and ranks it fourth or fifth in importance. Number one is what you work on; number two is who you work with.
- Hard work is a threshold, not a differentiator. Going from 40 to 70 to 90 hours a week matters far less than people think.
- His example: restaurant cooks and janitors work the hardest of anyone, yet "hard work will let you win the game that you're playing but it doesn't help you if you chose the wrong game."
- He criticizes school for forcing a major at 17 or 18 with no preparation. Picking the project is the highest-leverage decision, and it is treated as a throwaway.
- Simple decisions that take little time can separate success from failure. He calls himself "somewhat lazy but very happy and successful."
- He says hard work is overrated partly because it is virtue signaling. Genetics, birthplace and resources are out of your control, so people credit the one visible choice they made.
- People credit themselves for wins and blame luck or the economy for losses.
- He rejects the "no self-made man" idea as true but not useful. His rule: "pick the ones that serve you."

## Enthusiasm as an underrated skill

- At 24 he joined a Silicon Valley company of about 20 people as the youngest, with no coding or design skill. His edge was enthusiasm: painting a picture that excites him, then sprinting at it.
- He defines enthusiasm as borrowing happiness and excitement from the future and deploying it today. It matters most when things are neutral or bad, because most hard things have deep dips.
- Silicon Valley rewards optimism: "the cynics get to be right and the optimists get to be rich." Cynics are right on the eight of ten startups that fail and miss the two that work.
- Set your emotional home near ten, not zero. Many smart people deliberately start at neutral. He cites a Conor McGregor line: "you got to feel some type of way so why not feel unbeatable."
- Vicious cycle: low belief leads to minimal action, then a minimal result, which reinforces the belief. The virtuous cycle is the reverse. Enthusiasm lifts default belief, then action, then results.
- Tactic: "honey I'm home" energy. Before walking into a meeting or your home, decide to enter with that high energy. Hold it for about 45 seconds and people respond differently. Relationships fade when the early-days enthusiasm dips.

## Storytelling: intention, obstacle, stakes

- Stories are the natural transfer mechanism between people. Religions and The Lion King stick because they are encoded as stories, while seventh-grade lessons do not.
- Almost nobody is taught storytelling, so even a little effort puts you in the top few percent.
- Core principle from Aaron Sorkin's masterclass: "intention and obstacle." A hero wants something and something is in the way. Examples: Die Hard, the show Hijack, a romantic comedy.
- Add stakes to raise the story. Stakes need not be life or death; you only need to convince the audience this matters hugely to the character.
- His example is a low-stakes story told at an executive retreat. He was cooking brussels sprouts for his mom to look like an adult in his first apartment. Everyone else was one-upping each other with fires, and the small story won.

## If you're in your head, you're dead

- Smart people try to think their way to happiness, which he compares to talking your way through cooking. Thinking won't solve a feeling problem.
- Most people assume think leads to feel leads to do. He argues the loop runs both ways, and acting is the easier lever: do something physical and you feel differently, then think differently.
- Examples: 50 push-ups, a cold plunge, or submerging your face in ice water for a panic attack (a tip from Emmett Shear, citing the mammalian dive reflex).
- Make big decisions while moving, not slumped at a desk eating muffins.

## Mental minimalism and orienting principles

- He proposes "mental minimalism." Collecting mental models and frameworks like stamps is wasted energy. He prefers a few principles, in the spirit of Bruce Lee's one kick practiced a thousand times.
- The midwit meme (Neanderthal, overcomplicator, Jedi): the simple and the sage answers match. Example: lift weights and eat protein.
- Knowledge is knowing what to do. Wisdom is doing it. People over-index on knowledge and under-index on doing.
- Test he applied to a founder: asked what went wrong at his last company, the founder talked 20 minutes. The answer should be short.
- Pros-and-cons lists tie people in knots. Reduce a decision to one reason.

## Time horizons and why failure teaches less than people claim

- Change a decision by shrinking or stretching its time frame. Shrink it ("what's the best I can do in 45 minutes?") or stretch it ("I want to do awesome things with awesome people long-term").
- Story: he missed three deals that became billion-dollar companies because he had no money. He then asked how to invest without money, did a deal with an older friend and took less economics. The friend texted that he didn't care about the deal and wanted to do deals for 20 years. Long-term orientation changes behavior.
- A friend's "worry time": worry about money only Sunday 11am to noon, which frees you the rest of the week. His trainer's version: "you don't plant the seed and then dig it up the next day."
- He doubts "I failed but I learned." Ask what they learned and most can't say, or learn the wrong lesson.
- Example: a young employee bought a delisted stock, doubled down and got lucky. The takeaway for the kid was "I'm a genius" rather than "I got lucky."
- Memory of your own thoughts is poor, so keep a system. Write down your reasoning at decision time, then review it later. Judgment is the most important skill and can be trained this way.

## Being a billionaire is a stupid goal

- Billionaires give most of it away, which signals that abundance beyond a point doesn't matter.
- His own money checkpoints: not having to work because investments cover you, and getting out of debt.
- Past about $50M versus $500M, the difference is minor ("one rents the jet, the other owns the jet").
- Prefer abundance in time, youth, energy, freedom, generosity and fun. At 21 he wanted what 40-year-olds had; at 35 he wants what 21-year-olds have.
- He picked his path by asking who is having the most fun in their career. He found it hard to name people at a "10." Many successful people are stuck at "seven" and don't know there is a higher setting.
- Joe Rogan example: turned his hobbies into work (UFC commentary, comedy, podcasting) while avoiding the traps, such as constant touring, a content treadmill and censoring himself. Pick goals and also "anti-goals."
- Cautionary tale: a college friend's father made $1M a year as a consulting partner but traveled Monday to Thursday for her whole life, and she resented it.

## Spend on services, not things

- Season of life: from Bill Perkins's *Die With Zero*, a friend quit to backpack Europe at about 22-25, while Perkins stayed and went at 33, when it was less fun. Do things when they have maximum impact.
- Most people buy luxury goods. Shaan says services beat experiences and experiences beat things.
- He hired a private chef for less than a luxury car costs, and an assistant (via Shepherd, based in the Philippines) for about the cost of a couple of purses. The assistant screens email, handles his calendar, and helps him be a better friend by sending gifts and tracking birthdays.
- His rule on others' wealth: treat it like Costco samples. After he got defensive about a Las Vegas mansion, a friend called out his bitterness. He now treats exposure to other lifestyles as a cheap way to learn what he wants and doesn't want.
- He and a friend agreed to give each other blunt feedback and receive it "as a gift."
- Heuristic: don't follow what most people do, because the average outcome (obesity, divorce, no savings) isn't one you want.
