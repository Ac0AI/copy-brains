## Why podcasts are the hardest audience to grow

Shaan names this as the hardest thing he has promoted. He uses Eric Ries's three engines of growth (from The Lean Startup) to explain why.

- **Viral engine:** "Using your product naturally spreads like a contagion." Podcasts are "the ultimate single player experience". They are a one-hour solo listen in headphones, so they don't spread the way music does at gyms or parties.
- **Paid engine:** it works when you put money in and get more money out, as e-commerce brands do with Facebook ads. Podcasts fail here because there is nowhere obvious to advertise, conversion is low, and the ad revenue arrives later and is small compared to acquisition cost.
- **Sticky engine:** the only one left. People who really use you stay, engagement deepens, and they eventually tell others voluntarily. He calls it "annual compound interest of like 5%", so "you gotta get rich slow".
- Early on, low feedback is a strength. Nobody can see your view count, so you aren't discouraged. Guests will also come on with 100 listeners because they can't see the numbers.
- Later it becomes a weakness, because you want the feedback loop. MFM pushes people to YouTube even though it earns less there than from audio downloads. He wants the best product, and comments let him learn and re-engage.

## The trust equation: audience quality × size × trust

Shaan rejects size as the main audience metric. He maximizes a "trust equation":

- Trust equation = quality of the audience × number of those people × how much they trust you.
- One view or listen is not equal to another. A teenager on TikTok is not the same viewer as a company CEO.
- TikTok can give huge numbers but only seven seconds of attention before a swipe. Hours of long-form content builds far more trust.
- For creators, he says to choose "the right 10,000" who trust you a lot and stack the number slowly. That beats a random 100,000.
- For marketers, the arbitrage is finding assets that are underpriced because their trust is higher than their headline number.

## Twitch streamers and G Fuel: buying trust cheaply

Shaan saw this while at Twitch, which acquired his first company.

- Traditional brand marketers judged creators by Instagram followers or YouTube subscribers.
- A Twitch streamer is live in their bedroom, unedited and unscripted, six to eight hours a day. Twitch is 10-20x smaller than YouTube or Instagram as a network, but watch time is comparable, and that builds enormous trust.
- Big brands held back because execs didn't understand Twitch, couldn't track clicks, or feared a streamer saying the wrong thing.
- G Fuel (a Red Bull / Monster-style energy drink) went to about five streamers, then:
  - Gave each their own branded shaker, with the streamer's face on it, in a different colour for each.
  - Spent nothing beyond that.
  - Went from zero to $40 million in sales in about 12 months.
- Shaan says the same underpricing was true of podcasters a few years earlier, and he calls it "less so" now for both. He uses the same approach to grow Milk Road and his e-commerce business today.

## The "no link, complain about it" Thunder Clap launch

This is the launch tactic Steven Bartlett used at Shaan's company, which Shaan describes in detail.

- Bartlett had built a network of themed meme pages (Freshman Problems, Harry Potter memes, overheard-on-campus accounts). He bought pages for around $1,000 or partnered with their teenage owners.
- For the app launch, every page posted on the same day, which they called a Thunder Clap.
- The rules were:
  - No links. A kid once told Shaan "links are gay", and Bartlett had the same instinct that a link instantly makes a post look like an ad.
  - Don't praise the product, because that also sounds like selling.
  - Complain about it instead, such as "I haven't gotten anything done today because I'm so addicted to this stupid app" or "if one more friend tells me to download X, I'm gonna lose it".
- Followers saw the same unknown app three times on one scroll and went to the App Store to search the name.
- The result was about 500,000 downloads in roughly five days with no marketing spend, and #1 in the entire free App Store.
- Shaan's takeaways:
  - How you talk about a product matters. Fake-authentic, curiosity-building talk beats pushing a product at people.
  - The app churned users after about 10 days, so the distribution method was worth more than the app. Bartlett went on to build the agency Social Chain from it.
  - Bartlett's personal vlog content was aimed at the right audience, not a big one. Each client was worth hundreds of thousands of dollars, so paying a camera person to follow him was a good investment.

## Two failed audience hacks first

Shaan says people know My First Million but not his earlier false starts.

- Attempt one: he bought the failed social network Bebo back out of bankruptcy for about $1 million, planning to relaunch a different product under the name and its press story. It failed.
- Attempt two: he and Bartlett cold-called Logan Paul's manager, but Logan's team wanted a lot of money and they passed.
- The Thunder Clap only came after those. The actual value from Bebo was that it attracted Bartlett, who cold-emailed to work for him.
- He chose podcast, Twitter and newsletter because he is not a charismatic on-camera personality like Bartlett and wanted a medium that suits him ("a face for podcasting").
- Results he cites:
  - Podcast: 20 million+ downloads a year.
  - Newsletters: hundreds of thousands of subscribers.
  - Twitter: about 400,000 followers.
  - Those audiences let him spin off multiple businesses earning millions in profit.

## Project selection and starting with a kickoff doc

Shaan says what you choose to work on matters more than how hard you work.

- He failed for his first eight years of entrepreneurship. His learning came from "trial and error" done intelligently. Years of doing the same thing do not count as improvement. "It's the rate of learning and the rate of improvement that matters."
- He writes a one-page "kickoff doc" before any project to get clarity on what he is trying to do.
- He started the podcast the day after selling his company, after a 4-hour walk through the Mission in San Francisco. He wanted to be "in a million people's earballs in the morning", like Tim Ferriss.
- His stated downside was to lose $10,000 and have 100 great conversations, with the upside of accidentally building an audience. He also disliked asking people for coffee meetings, so a podcast invitation replaced them.
- Questions he asks to judge a project:
  - If you knew you'd be paid $5 million a year regardless, what would you do? This strips away the variable rewards of success odds and money.
  - Would you keep doing it if it never grew? He says yes for the podcast, which is why he knew it was right.
  - If the logical reasons say don't but you still want to, lean in. An immediate payoff beats a future one.
- Audience-first only makes sense if you actually like the content creation. He says it is no easier than building a product directly, only different, and many people give up because they expect it to be faster.
- Mindset quote from Naval: "Impatience with action, patience with results." Beginners expect things to happen faster than they do.

## When to quit a project

Shaan's own bias is to quit sooner, but from investing in about 100 startups and talking to thousands of founders, he thinks most people stick with things too long.

- "Quitting is underrated." Time is finite and projects are infinite.
- Survivorship bias distorts perseverance stories. He calls Pinterest's founder changing Apple Store laptop homepages "great story, terrible advice".
- His process:
  1. Before starting, talk to people who have done it. Look at the average successful journey, not the average including failures, so you know how long it takes. Check whether you even want their day-to-day.
  2. Set a worry time (a concept from Justin Mares). Plant the seed, water it for about 90 days, then assess, rather than digging it up daily.
  3. If you keep going against the evidence, say so out loud and don't delude yourself.
  4. After roughly six months to a year, ask: knowing what I know now, do I believe more, less or the same that this is valuable, that it is possible, and that my method gets me there? The answer should rarely be "the same".
- Don't be held hostage by your initial conviction. He expects to be wrong more than half the time.
- He warns that "marketers are great at bullshitting" and must be careful not to market to themselves.
