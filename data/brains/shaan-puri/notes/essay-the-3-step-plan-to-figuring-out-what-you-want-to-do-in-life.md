## Test-drive the dream before committing to it

- Shaan argues that whatever job you think you want is "probably wrong," because most people name a career because it sounded good to friends, parents and college counselors.
- His rule: "Try before you buy." Take a 2-week test drive of the job instead of letting the dream "float in a magical theoretical bubble."
- His own case: he spent 10 years saying he wanted to be a sports medicine doctor. He met an orthopedic surgeon for a sports team at a wedding and asked to shadow him for 2 weeks. He learned right away that he did not want the job because it has very little creativity.
- The lesson he drew: creativity and variety matter a lot to him. Those 2 weeks saved him from 8 years of med school and another 20-30 years in a job that "didn't light me up."
- Possible outcomes of a test drive:
  - **Yes**: invest a decade becoming great at it.
  - **No**: glad to find out now.
  - **Maybe**: treat it as a no, citing Derek Sivers: "if it's not a Hell Yes, it's a No".

## Why the "decade of suck" is survivable only if you like the game

- Shaan describes his early years as roughly 10-12 years of flat-line progress before the big upswing that everyone focuses on.
- His claim: people don't get through that stretch on willpower alone (unless they are David Goggins). The ones who make it enjoy the game and "play to play" rather than "play to win."
- They keep going while friends get promotions and buy houses, even when "the scoreboard says: Life 12, You 0."
- They keep playing until they get so good "the game has no choice but to give them the win."
- This is why he says to find a game you love. Loving it is the only thing that sustains the repetitions.

## Give yourself a "season of wandering"

- High achievers get anxious without an answer for their life, so they prematurely commit to something, anything. He cites Tim Ferriss: most people would rather be unhappy than uncertain.
- The fix is to name the era and time-box it. His reasoning: people behave differently when something has a name and a date (he points to Mardi Gras and Burning Man).
- He calls it a "season of wandering": 3 to 12 months where you experiment, dabble, try things and follow your curiosity and excitement.
- He calls it "wandering" because it lets you think creatively. There is no wrong path as long as you follow curiosity.
- What to look for while wandering: "clues, not answers." Like a detective, you don't seek the answer directly; answers come from clues.
- His closing argument for permission: most people say they can't afford the time, money or courage, but "you only get one ride on this rollercoaster," so the better question is whether you can afford not to.

## Clue 1: energy, and "play to you, work to others"

- Track what gives you energy versus what drains you. His examples: he gets drained at social events and gets tons of energy from reading books.
- The sharper test: "what feels like play to you, but looks like work to others?" He calls that the moneymaker.
- His example is writing. Most people hate it and dread the blank page, but he loves it.
- He followed that clue to Milk Road, a newsletter company. A year later it had 250,000 subscribers and sold for millions of dollars.
- His thesis: "Energy is the key to success," not hard work. His energy comes from loving what he does rather than grinding harder.
- Why love is a competitive advantage: people who love it do it more, the more you practice the better you get, and the better you get the more money you make.
- His definition of a great career: doing things you love, with people you love.

## Clue 2: find blueprints

- He quotes Tony Robbins: "Success leaves clues." The fastest shortcut in self-improvement is to find someone who already has what you want and download their habits and mindset.
- The question to ask: "Who's living the life I want?" Be specific about what "the life" means.
- His own example of refining the blueprint: he wanted success, then met rich men who were busy, stressed and divorced, with kids who resented them. He updated the target to money and fun and a house full of love, then sought out people who had all three.
- When he found someone matching that profile he drilled in with two questions: what do they do, and how did they get started?

## Get outside your bubble: there are 10,000x more ways to win

- Examples of unexpected paths he ran into:
  - A man who got very rich selling the conveyor belts that go on treadmills.
  - An older ping-pong friend who imports fancy soaps from Italy and resells them to 5-star hotels at a big markup, making $3M a year. He doesn't make the soap; "he just saw a gap and filled it."
- His takeaway: there are 10,000x more ways to win than you realize. You may not do any of them, but they open your eyes to possibilities.
- His parents had normal 9-to-5 jobs, so they could only teach him that flavor. His line: "you can't ask someone for directions to a place they've never been."
- Instead of complaining about his upbringing, he moved to SF because he wanted to be a founder and that's where founders live: "Proximity is power."
- His result: he gets paid to talk (his podcast), has built and sold multiple companies, and works on whatever he wants, whenever and from wherever he wants. He says he'd do podcasting, writing and investing for free.

## The 3-step plan, and his stance on luck

- The plan in short:
  - **Pop the bubble**: test drive the job before committing.
  - **Season of wandering**: take 3 to 12 months to experiment and follow curiosity.
  - **Look for clues**: ask what feels like play to you but looks like work to others, and find blueprints in people who already have what you want, then study and hang around them.
- Shaan admits luck matters (he says he "rolled the dice and hit a hard six"), but argues that if you think it's all luck you're a victim of circumstances: "Luck matters, but action matters more."
