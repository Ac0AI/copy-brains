## Cheap intelligence turns average into a commodity

- Ruben's core claim: "Your salary is being compared to a subscription now." AI cost per unit of intelligence fell about 6,000x in 4 years, and the 2026 DeepSeek model is smarter than the original GPT-4.
- Tasks that once needed an expensive human (memo, market scan, spreadsheet, landing page, sales email, first draft, contract review, 20 campaign ideas) now take a prompt, about 30 seconds and about $20/month. He says the output is "fine. Sometimes good. Sometimes better than the junior person they hired last year."
- He cites crashing junior jobs as proof the market has decided AI beats juniors.
- The threat is not that AI becomes perfect. It is that AI "becomes cheap enough to replace good enough."
- The better question is not "Will AI replace me?" but "What happens when the thing you used to sell becomes almost free?"
- Knowledge work was priced around 3 hidden costs: knowing enough, thinking the work through, and producing the final thing. AI attacks all three.
- Cheap tools don't need to be perfect, just good enough that people use them 100 times more.

## Jevons Paradox: AI means more work attempted, not less

- Ruben invokes Jevons Paradox (which he spells "Jevon's"): when a resource gets more efficient, total consumption rises because lower costs unlock new demand.
- His prediction: AI doesn't mean less work, it means more work gets attempted (more drafts, tests, research, prototypes, analysis, cold emails, content, dashboards, legal first passes, financial models).
- The lazy prediction is that AI replaces people. The better one is that AI raises the amount of work humans are expected to manage.
- Intelligence is becoming a commodity. You lose not because AI can think, but because everyone around you can think 100x more often while you work as if thinking were still expensive.
- Intelligence used to be the bottleneck: ideas waited on analysts, designers, writers, interns, consultants, developers and manager reviews.
- Now the first serious attempt is almost free (the final work is not). Before, you tested 1 headline; now 50. Before, you read 3 competitor pages; now you scan 300. Before, 1 client deck; now 4 versions for 4 buyers. Before, one question to one analyst; now 10 questions you never would have asked.
- "Impossible volume is now possible," and when volume changes, the job changes.

## Average dies first; effort is no longer a moat

- AI is very good at average: average memo, sales email, research summary, content calendar, Python script, slide outline, support answer, job description, legal summary, market analysis, "strategy."
- Average used to look professional because it took time. Now it looks suspicious because it took 12 seconds. The worst sentence a client can say: "Did you just use ChatGPT to do this?"
- Knowledge workers used to have a moat made of effort (sitting there, typing, knowing the format, searching, assembling). AI removed most of it, and the market now asks "Why am I paying a human for this?"
- His instruction: "Stop trying to make average work. Move above it."

## Judgment and taste are the new scarce work

- When intelligence gets cheap, judgment gets expensive. Everyone can generate options but few can pick; everyone can draft but few can decide what should exist; everyone can summarize but few know what matters; everyone can make a slide but few make the one that closes.
- The valuable person looks at 20 versions and says "This one," "This angle," "This risk," "This is wrong," "This will fail with our buyer," "This sounds good, but solves nothing," "This should never be sent."
- Taste is trained by doing the work: thousands of reps, bad calls, awkward meetings, failed launches, clients who didn't buy, bosses who killed the project, users who clicked the wrong button. "AI has read about those things. You lived them."
- He expects this gap to get more expensive over time.
- Directive: "You must find a way to extract your taste and give it to AI," because taste trapped in your head can't be used by AI.

## Turn expertise into markdown context files

- Ruben's method: put your standards, taste, mistakes, audience, rules, constraints, likes and hates, and "the weird little things only you know" into markdown files, and give them to AI before important tasks. This feeds AI what it can't scrape from the internet and stops the average answer.
- "A prompt is a one-time request. But a file (inside a folder) is reusable memory."
- Folder structure he uses (built for Claude Cowork; works in other AI by uploading the files into a "Project", including ChatGPT):
  - ABOUT-ME/: about-me.md, anti-ai-writing-style.md, my-company.md
  - TEMPLATES/: examples-i-love.md, examples-i-hate.md, plus examples of work you love
  - OUTPUTS/: whatever AI produces
- Keep it small: "Please don't dump 400 PDFs into a folder and call it context."
- Starter prompt: "I want to [task] for [success criteria]. Start by reading my about-me folder and its files. Then, ask me questions to complete your task with my answers."
- All his AI sessions start with a Q&A so the AI is prompted better. It feels slow, and he says that's good because transferring taste takes time.
- "Most people want AI to skip thinking. You want AI to extract your thinking." Your value is your unique way of thinking, not the manual work. "Stop selling hours. Start selling taste."

## Stop selling hours; become a system person, not an answer person

- "I spent 6 hours on this" no longer signals quality, because time spent used to signal quality only when production was expensive. What stays expensive: outcomes, taste, trust, responsibility.
- Better arguments than hours: understood the real problem, knew what to ignore, found the missing risk, made the decision easier, shipped the version that worked, saved the team from "doing the wrong thing beautifully."
- The answer person (answers questions, drafts, researches on request) gets replaced first. The system person asks "Why do we keep needing this?" and builds the repeatable way to handle it: template, checklist, context file, project folder, recurring review, workflow, trained AI setup for the team, a way for others to get 80% of the answer without asking.
- "You stop being the bottleneck. You remove bottlenecks."
- Prompt he gives: list 5 tasks people keep asking you to do, paste your exact step-by-step process with tools, then ask AI for each task to (1) identify the repeated pattern, (2) say what context files are needed, (3) turn it into a reusable checklist, (4) draft the first workflow version, (5) say what should stay human.
- He means simple recurring systems, not "monster-automation sending emails on your behalf to your loved ones."

## Use AI where you already have taste; take responsibility

- People use AI backward ("I'm bad at this, AI will do it"). If you're bad at the thing you can't judge it and will "approve clean garbage."
- Examples: great at sales, have AI draft 20 angles and you'll spot the 19 weak ones; great at finance, have AI test your model and you'll catch the lazy assumption; great at writing, use AI for raw material and cut the fake voice; great at operations, have AI map workflows and you'll see the missing handoff. "Your skill becomes the steering wheel."
- Where humans still win: knowing what should exist, what to ignore, who needs to trust it, what will fail in the real world, when an answer sounds right but is wrong, how to fit work to your exact context, and "taking responsibility when the output matters."
- Responsibility is the big one: "I pay my lawyers to take responsibility. Be like my lawyer."
