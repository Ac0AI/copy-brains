## AI removes the friction of stopping, so people work more, not less

Ruben argues that AI did not reduce workload; it intensified it. He says he met "dozens of people this year" who work past midnight, ship more than ever, feel productive, and are also more stressed, lost and exhausted. His diagnosis: most AI users sprint fast in the wrong direction.

- Every earlier tool (Excel, email, Google search) felt like work. Prompting feels like chatting with a friend, so it doesn't register as work.
- People prompt before bed, at lunch, in meetings and while files load.
- "AI removes the friction of *starting*. That's great. But it also removes the friction of *stopping*. And if stopping feels harder than continuing, you have an addiction."
- Doing more is not the same as doing better.

## Harvard 8-month study: three ways AI intensifies work

Ruben cites a Harvard (HBR, Feb 2026) 8-month study at a 200-person tech company where nobody was forced to use AI, yet everyone ended up working more. Three patterns appeared across the board:

- **Role creep:** people did other people's jobs because AI made it feel easy. Product managers wrote code, researchers did engineering tasks, and everyone absorbed work that used to require extra hires.
- **Work spilling into breaks:** evenings, lunches, commutes and "quick 5 minutes." The casual, conversational style of prompting let work into the time the brain needs to recover.
- **More multitasking:** multiple AI threads at once, constant output-checking, a permanent state of juggling.

Every person in the study felt more productive, and most felt busier. Ruben paraphrases one engineer: you thought you'd work less because AI makes you faster, but you work the same or more.

## The real enemy: performative productivity

Ruben calls the hidden problem "performative productivity." His scenario: it's 11 pm, you shipped a landing page, rewrote the sales deck, drafted a content calendar, fixed an email sequence, and you can't name one thing that moved you forward. The common complaint he hears is "I did so much today, and I have nothing to show for it."

- AI lets you skip the hardest part of work: deciding what's worth doing.
- Before AI, starting something was hard, which forced you to ask whether it was worth starting. Now you type a sentence and something exists, so nobody pauses to ask "does this actually matter?"
- The result is momentum that is circular: fast movement, no progress.
- He is still pro "fast shipping + 'show don't tell' using AI" ("It's magic"), but says you must fight directionlessness so you pursue something worth pursuing.

## Ruben's 5 rules against performative productivity (his own company gameplan)

1. **Write one sentence about what you're solving before you prompt.** If you can't state the problem in one sentence, AI generates noise you then spend the night editing. Example: "Grow my newsletter 10% faster."
2. **Kill 3 tasks for every 1 task AI helps you add.** AI makes adding easy and nobody teaches subtraction. For each new thing shipped, ask which 3 things to stop. He calls this taste: saying no to more things so you can say yes to the right ones.
3. **Set a "last prompt" time.** Like a cutoff for email, set a hard stop for AI. His example: after 8 pm, no prompts. He has talked to people prompting at midnight or 2 am ("just one more"). "The most productive behavior you can have is to rest. The work will be there tomorrow. Your brain won't be if you don't let it rest."
4. **Ask whether you're doing more things or the right things.** If you can't honestly say you're closer to your actual goal, "you're busy. Not productive." He uses a matrix (from his "Happy New AI Year" post) and shares a Google Sheets template to build your own.
5. **AI writes v1, but you decide whether v1 should exist at all.** He calls this the most important rule. That AI can draft something in 30 seconds doesn't mean it should be drafted. Illustration: anyone can make a LinkedIn post in 30 seconds, but very few can grow an account to 10,000 followers in 17 days (he says his team did, going from 49 to 10,000 followers in 17 days). If the answer to "does this need to exist?" is "I don't know, but it's easy, so why not," that's a problem. "Don't sprint before knowing where you're going."

## Two-prompt workflow to find your real goal and audit your week

Ruben's concrete starting method: open Claude (or whatever tool you use) and run two prompts, in order, without skipping the first. The idea is to use AI to "see clearly" rather than to produce more.

- **Prompt 1, find your real goal:** list everything you're working on, and ask the AI to ask tough questions to determine which ONE item matters most to your growth in the next 90 days. Instruct it to challenge you, not let you pick 3, and force you to pick 1.
- **Prompt 2, audit your week:** give it your one goal plus everything you did that week. Ask it to score each task on whether it directly moves you toward the goal or was performative productivity, "be brutal," then produce a plan for next week containing only what matters.
- He says you'll want to argue with the answer, and that is how you know it's working.
- His framing: "Most people use AI to *produce more*. Start using it to **see clearly**." That is the difference between an AI-holic and someone who knows what they're doing.
