## Do the thinking first, invite AI in second

- Ruben opens with a warning: he uses ChatGPT every day, and passive use can make you "dumb."
- His takeaway from the MIT study is that the damage comes from passive use, not from AI itself.
- He argues that "do the hard thinking first, invite AI in second" keeps your memory circuits active while the AI polishes, fact-checks or expands ideas you already own.
- Practical rule: write a quick outline or rough first draft on your own. Only then open ChatGPT to challenge the structure, add counter-arguments or surface examples you missed.
- In his words, you stay in "the cognitive driver's seat; the model becomes a booster, not the steering wheel."
- His closing recipe: draft with your own neurons first, interrogate the AI like a sparring partner rather than a fortune-teller, and own the final cut (tone, ethics, responsibility).

## What the MIT "cognitive debt" study found

- The study had 54 students writing SAT-style essays: 18 with ChatGPT only, 18 with Google search, 18 with their own brains only.
- Four minutes after writing, 15 of 18 ChatGPT-only participants (83%) could not recall a single sentence. The comparison group not using ChatGPT was at 11%.
- Brain-only writers felt "total ownership" of their essays. ChatGPT users said theirs "felt half-mine at best."
- EEG showed brain-to-brain connectivity roughly halved with ChatGPT: about 80 active "lines" for brain-only writers versus about 40 for ChatGPT writers.
- Ruben's reading: if the model does most of the thinking, you practice less thinking yourself. Researchers call this gap "cognitive debt," because users skim, accept and move on.
- He lists four blind spots: only essay tasks, a short time frame (4 months), an unrealistic lab setting (32-channel caps, one tab, no Slack or WhatsApp), and no control for prior writing skill or knowing how to use AI. He says that last one "makes all of the difference."

## Evidence that active AI use pays off

- In the study's 4th session, brain-only writers were finally given ChatGPT. Their brain-network activity surged above every earlier session and graders gave their essays the highest scores.
- A field experiment with 758 Boston Consulting Group employees found GPT-4 users finished tasks 25% faster, with work judges rated 40% higher in quality.
- Ruben notes that half of a consultant's work is research plus making slides, so pairing deep research tools (ChatGPT, Perplexity) with slide tool gamma.app makes someone better at the craft.
- GitHub data he cites: Copilot users cut coding time by 55% and time-to-merge halved.
- Creative work: studies in Science Advances found AI-assisted stories judged more creative and engaging, especially for less-experienced writers, though a human still has to curate and vary ideas to avoid sameness.
- His conclusion: top performers never copy-paste and call it a day. They "iterate, reframe, and blend" AI output with their own expertise, ending up both faster and better.

## Workflow: "Draft, Then Ask"

Goal: understand the problem before the model suggests phrasing or arguments.

1. Do a 10-minute solo draft. Ignore spelling and time yourself, which overcomes writer's block.
2. Gap scan: mark areas that feel thin, repetitive or weakly evidenced.
3. Open ChatGPT and give it full context (share a paper as a PDF). Then ask specific questions, such as "Give me three counter-arguments to Point B" or "Suggest two historical examples for my thesis."
4. Curate, don't copy: paste only what strengthens your narrative, rewrite in your tone, or train ChatGPT on your tone by giving it a snippet.

## Workflow: "Socratic Chat" and "Reverse-Prompt"

**Socratic Chat** (for people afraid of confirmation bias). It turns ChatGPT into a relentless critic that keeps your executive-control loops active.

1. State your thesis (example: "I argue that universal basic income boosts entrepreneurship").
2. Ask GPT to destroy it: "List the three strongest objections, with evidence."
3. Rebut each objection or concede and adjust your claim.
4. Ask GPT to rate the new draft 1-10 for internal consistency and name the weakest point.
5. Repeat until GPT's score is 8 or higher and you can verbalize every point without the prompt visible.

Ruben has a separate guide on this, "How Socrates prompts ChatGPT."

**Reverse-Prompt** (for tightening ambiguous drafts, specs or prompts).

1. Feed GPT your text.
2. Say: "Guess the exact prompt that generated this. Show it."
3. Any section that comes back generic signals vagueness, so tighten the objective, constraints or style.
4. Rewrite the original with precise constraints and cut fluff.

## Workflow: "AI Pop-Quiz Loop" for retention

Goal: turn ChatGPT into a just-in-time memory trainer, directly countering the 83% recall failure.

1. Finish your AI-assisted document and close extra tabs so only ChatGPT is open.
2. Prompt: "Generate five short-answer questions that test the key facts, stats, and arguments in my essay. Hide all answers."
3. Answer from memory in the chat, with no scrolling back.
4. Type "show answers" and score yourself, or ask GPT to grade you while acting like an expert on the matter. Follow with "Explain differently the questions I missed."
5. Redo missed questions until you hit 5/5.

He frames this as ideal for students or anyone who needs to learn something.

## Workflows: "Chain-of-Thought Reveal" and "Silent Dialogue"

**Chain-of-Thought Reveal** (for people who can spot a mistake fast). Goal: see every hop in GPT's reasoning so you catch leaps, assumptions or hallucinations before they infect your work. He recommends o3 because you can see its reasoning.

1. Prompt: "Solve <problem>. Show every reasoning step, no matter how small."
2. Highlight any step that feels like a jump.
3. Cross-examine: "How did you get from Step 4 to Step 5? Cite data."
4. Keep only the steps you can restate in your own words.

**Silent Dialogue** (for people who love to speak). Articulating a problem out loud surfaces hidden assumptions, so GPT acts as a silent listener.

1. Open the ChatGPT phone app with Advanced voice mode on.
2. Set the role: "Act as a silent rubber duck. Reply only 'go on' every 60 sec unless I ask a direct question."
3. Talk the problem through from the top.
4. Note the line where you stumble; blockers usually appear where you can't explain a rationale.
5. Ask a pinpoint question about that gap (example: "Why would variable X be undefined here?"), integrate the fix and repeat.
