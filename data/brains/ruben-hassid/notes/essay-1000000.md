## Core principle: nobody cares about you, they care about what's useful to them

- Ruben grew his own LinkedIn account from 8,000 followers (pre-ChatGPT) to 770,000 in 3 years, using ChatGPT first, then Claude, Grok and Gemini. He did the same on 5 different accounts organically, for a combined 1.56 million followers.
- He gained 344,258 followers in 2025 alone, on one account, with no ads (roughly 1,000 a day).
- He says he never writes a personal post: "No one cares about me. Strangers can't relate to my achievements."
- A prompt like "this is my achievement, make a viral LinkedIn post" yielded 8,000 impressions and +46 followers. At that rate, 1 million followers would take 21,739 days (about 60 years).
- Personal posts make people around you proud (mom, kids, boss). They don't make new people want to follow.
- Readers read alone, facing a screen, with their own life problems. "Either I help YOU now, or you won't care enough to follow me."
- Bragging example: "my Substack made me hundreds of thousands of dollars" gets "why is he bragging?". "Here's how YOU can generate $1,000 with Substack" gets attention.
- People follow people who are useful to them. They praise the personal posts of people they know. His question: "Do you want to be praised or get new followers?"

## Why AI alone doesn't win: only taste and consistency are left

- AI made the effort to start and a reasonable strategy easy, so both are now worthless as differentiators.
- What remains scarce is taste and consistency. His formula for 1M followers: AI + taste + consistency.
- AI can produce 1,000 post variations an hour and apply any public "LinkedIn growth strategy", yet that doesn't produce stars.
- If a shortcut feels tiring, that's the point: "If you can't pursue the effort, the goal isn't worth pursuing." The effort is his competitive advantage.

## Finding the post angle: a clarifying-questions prompt

Ruben's workflow for turning expertise into a reader-centered post:
1. Open Claude (Cowork) and upload your "about me" text file.
2. Paste a prompt asking Claude to find the best angle and topic for one LinkedIn post that is useful, relatable and 100% about the reader, not bragging.
3. The prompt tells Claude to read the uploaded files first, not execute yet, and ask clarifying questions (via AskUserQuestion) about: the real problem the audience struggles with, which piece of expertise is most useful to a stranger, and how to frame it so the reader sees themselves in it and has a clear reason to follow.
4. Work only starts once aligned.

The aim is to force "useful" over "personal".

## Hooks: break the reader's pattern

- A hook is the first two lines on LinkedIn, before "… more". Ruben defines it as exactly 2 sentences of about 55 characters each. If people click "… more", you win.
- The job of a hook: stop the scroll. Readers are bored, understimulated and doomscrolling. Example he gives: writing "Abuse me" mid-newsletter made the reader's thumb stop.
- He argues every successful thing broke a pattern: Nirvana (vs hair metal, a $20 guitar), Picasso (painted what people felt), iPhone (removed the keyboard), Google (white page and a box), Galileo.
- Hook prompt (a follow-up to the draft post): write hooks that are about the reader or a universal tension, never about Ruben; create an open loop; no achievements, no emoji openers, no hashtags; should feel like a friend's text that makes you reply "wait, explain."
- The five techniques in the prompt, with his examples:
  - Contradiction: "The worst LinkedIn posts get the most followers."
  - Specific number + unexpected context: "I mass-unfollowed 2,000 people. My engagement tripled."
  - Direct accusation: "You're writing LinkedIn posts for your mom, not your audience."
  - Stolen thought: "You know your LinkedIn posts are boring. So does everyone scrolling past them."
  - Absurd reframe: "Your LinkedIn hook has 1.2 seconds to live. Most die instantly."
- Claude generates 10 options by technique via an AskUserQuestion form. The key is the conversation with the AI.
- Claude won't write the perfect one-shot hook, but it will create 100 variations. Your taste picks one; posting 100 times sharpens your taste.

## Media: the image matters more than the text

- Ruben claims the image takes about 80% of the screen, the hook 20%, and the rest of the text 0%. He compares media to a book cover.
- Three misconceptions he corrects:
  - "The text is what matters": only the hook and media matter.
  - "Videos are the best format": people on LinkedIn don't binge video.
  - "I need a strong recognizable brand": the more it looks like an ad, the worse it performs. Be scrappy.
- Best media: a single 1080 x 1350 image, scrappy and unbranded, useful right now, something you want to send to a colleague or bookmark. "This is the opposite of an ad."
- Reverse-prompting workflow with Gemini:
  1. Search Pinterest for your niche + "graph", "cheat sheet" or "infographic" (his example: "rare earth infographic") and download a good one.
  2. Upload it to Gemini (Thinking model) with: "Extract all of the information from this infographic so another designer could remake it. Your goal is to brief it, as if the designer was Gemini nano-banana (reverse prompting)."
  3. Find a second Pinterest image in an organic style (he searched "handwritten style infographic").
  4. Follow up: "Now remake this infographic with the information, but with this style I just uploaded (copy only the style, your content comes from what you just shared)."
- He says the result is something a designer could charge hundreds of dollars for ("I could have paid $500").

## Consistency: post daily, action produces information

- Ruben says he never missed a day in 3 years and tracks posts in a Notion calendar.
- "Action produces information." He wrote thousands of posts and learned from each; that is the difference between him and most people.
- No AI prompt makes you consistent. The requirement: one post a day, about 30 minutes. "Someone infinitely dumber than you is getting what you want by simply being consistent and using AI smartly."
- It means sacrificing other things that don't serve the goal. His advice: go all in.
- Comparison: you can't expect a bodybuilder's body working out twice a month.

## Pick a target and go all in with AI

- Ruben says most people don't truly want 1,000,000 followers, so they don't follow the playbook. Followers may not be what you really want, but pursuing a target generates information.
- AI made action cheap, so some people get "a godlike amount of information from a godlike amount of action."
- Advice: pick a target, pursue it with everything, and use AI to build an unfair advantage "so unfair it feels insurmountable to catch up with you."
- From planning to mastering:
  - Join a community of obsessed AI users (his Slack has 1,108 professionals).
  - Pick one tool (likely Claude for most) and master it.
  - Use AI to produce action, to get information.
- Reader behavior note: he expects about 300 of his 300,000 weekly email readers to actually apply the guide, and urges applying it immediately and sharing it with a team chat for accountability.
