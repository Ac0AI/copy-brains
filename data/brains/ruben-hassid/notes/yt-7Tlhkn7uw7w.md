## How Ruben built EasyGen's affiliate program to ~18% of revenue

- Ruben said EasyGen's affiliate revenue started at roughly 10% of revenue and had grown to about 18% when he recorded this, and "could go as near as 20%".
- He puts it at about $10,000 per month from affiliates, on roughly $50K MRR overall, with about 40,000 weekly users.
- Concentration is extreme: about 80% of affiliate revenue comes from just four people, and two of those four are his own employees (Anisha and Axel), paid through Rewardful on top of their salaries.
- He calls this a form of equity for staff: "imagine you have a $12 a month SaaS where you don't have to do anything, no customer support", and you're actively growing it by supporting his journey.
- He built the program reactively. Members of the EasyGen paid-users WhatsApp group kept asking for an affiliate program. Early on people DM'd him asking where to sign up because he had forgotten to put a link in the app.
- The fix was a button labelled "affiliate" in the web app's left sidebar that opens the Rewardful signup page.
- Affiliates get 20% recurring, which is $12 per month per referred customer. His biggest affiliate has around 50-55 referred customers and earns about $600-700 a month.
- He has never done active affiliate recruitment, and says maybe he should.

## Turning employees into the top affiliates by growing their LinkedIn accounts

- Ruben used his own team as case studies. He had planned to prove EasyGen by creating a fake LinkedIn ghostwriter account, then dropped the idea. His warning: never create a fake LinkedIn profile, because it breaks the terms and puts the real account at risk.
- Instead, around April 2024, he offered Anisha and Axel (both on his media company team) a deal. They picked their own topics and personal brand, and he handled the content.
- Process:
  - He recorded a weekly YouTube playlist (a paid newsletter product) showing how he wrote a week of LinkedIn posts for each of them.
  - It took roughly one hour a week.
  - He ran this for 23 consecutive weeks, using EasyGen plus Canva and Perplexity to refine topics.
- Anisha's brand: empowering people to write their first LinkedIn post. She was at 6,000 followers by week 5 and later passed 30,000.
- Axel's brand: "my eyes of AI". She tracks AI news and demos across LinkedIn, Twitter, Reddit and Discord, and turns that into a daily post. She went from 0 to about 25,000 followers.
- Each post ends with a line like "by the way, this post was made by EasyGen, and if you want $10 off your first month use my code".
- Result: both became two of his biggest affiliates.

## Coupon-code tracking and who makes a good affiliate

- Ruben tracks affiliates with Rewardful's coupon-based tracking rather than links.
- His reasoning: people tend to go straight to the Stripe payment page, and when someone clicks a link they worry it isn't working. With a coupon they can see the discount applied in Stripe, so it feels trustworthy. He says it is "much sturdier on my end".
- The affiliate pitch for a SaaS is recurring income with no investment, no customer support and no payroll or cash-flow costs. He notes some people refuse because they know he keeps 80%, but says it is still a good deal if you believe in the product.
- What makes a good affiliate, in his view:
  - They believe in the channel. For EasyGen that means believing organic LinkedIn is a huge opportunity. His argument: LinkedIn has about 1.15 billion users and only around 0.6% post weekly, a gap he says doesn't exist on Instagram, TikTok, Twitter or YouTube.
  - They genuinely use and love the product. Zach, a top affiliate, is often the first to report bugs.
  - They benefit from inbound leads. His example is Emanuel Oren, a broker who matches clients with 3D design agencies for a 20% cut. He stopped cold calling and now posts 3D case studies on LinkedIn, so leads come to him. He became a super affiliate.
  - Good fits are sales, HR and VC people who need inbound.
- Ruben says the best affiliates are customers who use the product daily, and that he makes sure he is the most prominent user of EasyGen himself. His employees are the second best users and also his biggest affiliates.
- He suggests affiliates publish a "tech stack" list the way beauty creators publish Amazon lists, and share it whenever someone asks what tools they use.

## How EasyGen got its first users: friction and a WhatsApp waitlist

- At launch EasyGen was a Chrome extension that was not on the Chrome Store. Users had to join a WhatsApp waitlist, which he accepted manually, then watch a Loom video and install the package by hand.
- He did this deliberately to filter for people who really wanted it and to have conversations with them. He had hundreds of signups per day and about 4,000 people across four WhatsApp groups of roughly 1,000 each.
- Once the product was paid, he moved paying users into a VIP group, which is still active.
- The most common request was an automation that schedules a whole week of posts. He refuses to build it: "if you behave like a bot", platforms with large teams hunting bots will target your account. He keeps a blog post titled "never automate".
- He also refuses to connect to the LinkedIn API for analytics, because accounts get blocked.
- He stopped the waitlist later, and says the WhatsApp community let him educate users, since good LinkedIn posting was not even a topic ten years ago.

## Distribution channels: content-led, no SEO and no paid

- Ruben calls himself a non-traditional founder: no SEO, no paid ads, barely any influencer marketing.
- He says 99% of revenue outside Rewardful comes from his own LinkedIn plus a little from other channels and his newsletter.
- His newsletter has about 46,000 subscribers and goes out free every Sunday. It covers how AI grows LinkedIn, across all his systems, not just EasyGen.
- His method is to share his real process as content. Example: he clipped a Donald Trump Stargate clip with Opus Clip, researched it with DeepSeek, generated about 10 post versions in EasyGen and patchworked a final one. The post got 1.1K likes, 400 comments and 100+ reposts, and he plans to explain the process in the next newsletter.
- On LinkedIn he went from 8,000 to 400,000 followers over about two years. He argues AI is "not to be lazier but to be greater": he generated around 100 posts and hand-picked the most impactful one.
- His reach last year was 125 million people on LinkedIn.

## Community philosophy: aim for 1,000 true fans, not millions of views

- Ruben says going viral is useful for attention and data, but going viral for any reason isn't a goal. The strongest community he has is only about 1,500-2,000 people.
- His rule of thumb: aim for 1,000 community members over three years, which is about 300 per year or roughly one new person per day.
- A community member is someone who actively follows your journey, not someone who reads one post.
- Pick one mission and solve one problem. Example he gives for the host: affiliate programs for SaaS founders. Then you become the category: "you have a SaaS and you need affiliate, read this content and check Rewardful".
- He compares being a "stepping stone" in someone's journey to niche YouTubers (a grilled-cheese channel, a Japan restaurant POV channel) he saves for later use.
- Most people quit because they lack patience for one person a day over three years. His analogy: nobody expects muscle on day one at the gym.
- He believes everyone will eventually need to market themselves (for promotions, freelance leads, or portfolio), even with a full-time job.

## Bootstrapping, team and EasyGen roadmap

- He is bootstrapped and declined VC offers. His first month took his bank account from zero to about -$50K of his own money. He had budgeted that he could sustain it for about a year, and treated it as investing in himself rather than the S&P 500.
- His preference: "a business making 100K a month where I own 80% of the company and have a lot of fun" over one making $10M a month while being crunched by VCs.
- Team: a CTO who owns 20%, two developers (one team in India), a project manager, and a newly hired customer support person. Marketing is just him plus Rewardful.
- He says he builds the prompt engineering himself, and EasyGen is an agentic flow of multiple LLMs rather than a single-model wrapper. He satisfies his own taste first and sometimes ignores users with small followings.
- He explicitly positions EasyGen as a replacement for a ghostwriter you'd pay about $2,000 a month, not a ChatGPT alternative.
- Roadmap for 2025:
  - Mission and ideation (what to post based on trends in your niche).
  - "Sessions": one topic generates 10-20 post versions fast, with the ability to patchwork between them and edit sections by prompting, similar to ChatGPT Canvas.
- Focus is his stated edge: he is bad at doing many things at once but very good at being hyper-focused.
