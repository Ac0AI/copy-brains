## Why Ruben says founders need a brand and distribution

- Ruben says first-time founders tend to be technical and want to build the best product. Second-time founders focus on marketing, because a great tool can still go unnoticed if you can't share it.
- He learned this at 17, running a techno music label. Bedroom producers spent 10-15 hours a day perfecting tracks, assuming quality alone would make them known. Ruben's view: "Without a platform, without a community, you can be the very best DJ in the world... you're never going to have your first success."
- He treats the audience as the asset. A person's content stays with them across companies. A company can change in 10 years, but "your content is your content."
- He believes content is like fitness: you don't have to be a content creator full-time, but a healthy amount of it helps everyone.
- His advice to founders: before building, talk about the solution to other people as early as possible. Be "obsessed first driven by the solution and the people you want to help rather than the product itself." He cites the Reddit founder printing flyers and posting them around his campus.

## What "personal branding" really means

- Ruben says personal branding does not mean being personal. It means you are not a company. People relate to a human more than to a corporate page.
- Content doesn't have to share your life story. He rarely tells his own story (some podcasts, his WhatsApp community, a touch on stage). People follow him because he learns something and shares it.
- The reader's frame: "I am at point A, I need to be at point B now." The creator's job is to transform the reader, not to show their breakfast.
- Two extremes to avoid:
  - Too personal: the audience thinks "help me first".
  - Too company-centric ("my company, my company, my company"): readers feel you are pushing your product down their throat and only want their money.
- The middle path for a founder: embody the person who needs the solution. Example: for a habit tracker, put about 80% of content on how hard it is to abandon a bad routine and how good it is to embrace one good routine, not on the tool itself.

## Content as an umbrella around the product

- Ruben says his content is not just about EasyGen. It covers Pinterest, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude, design, scheduling, and mental frameworks. EasyGen is one tool inside a transformational journey ("go from A to B").
- Design advice: good social design should look like "everything but an ad".
- He runs a paid newsletter started two months before this talk (when EasyGen launched).
- The product is a solution inside a bigger process. You can recommend that people use it, but you teach the whole process.

## The two-employee experiment on LinkedIn

- Context: Ruben has 400,000 followers. He could not just tell people to use EasyGen, so he tested it on two employees, Axel and Anisha, who had barely posted on LinkedIn.
- Setup:
  - Each week he recorded a one-hour video making a week of content for each of them.
  - Axel's niche: the latest AI demos (videos). Anisha's niche: overcoming mental blocks (she is introverted).
  - The employees spend at least 45 minutes a day finding topics and images or videos, send them to Ruben (30-45 minutes), he writes the post with EasyGen, then they post and engage for 15-30 minutes. Roughly one hour a day each on their own brand.
- Results: no ads, purely organic. 1 million views in 25 days. A later update: 3.2 million views in 17 days (as he stated it). He ran 11 videos over 11 weeks.
- He rejects the "secret" explanation (pods, ads, his likes on their posts): "If any time I would like a post it would make people go from zero to three million views in two months I'll be very rich."
- Employer angle: even 30 minutes to an hour a day on their brand is positive for him and for them. He planned to do the same with two new employees.
- His success criterion for the test: if it went badly, he would improve EasyGen. It went well, so he keeps going.

## EasyGen: how it works and why it differs from ChatGPT

- Origin: Ruben built it for himself, then for his team, in February 2023. He spent about 6 months improving it. Staff found early versions too hard to use, so he made it easy (hence "EasyGen"). He says he and his team are the primary customers, so he won't change things just on request.
- Positioning: it is a ghostwriter, not a generic writer. A human LinkedIn ghostwriter costs about $2,000-$5,000 per month and involves lots of back and forth. EasyGen costs roughly $2 a day, he says.
- How to use it:
  - Type your topic as a rough first draft or brain dump. It is not a ChatGPT-style prompt. Example topic he typed: "personal branding is not about being personal but being relatable... explain why it matters to my audience, not me".
  - Press generate, regenerate 2-3 times, combine parts, edit. He copies the topic before generating because LLMs can hallucinate and a regeneration is sometimes needed.
  - Output is about 80-90% good by design. A post that used to take 1-3 hours now takes 15-20 minutes.
  - Optional topic suggestions exist but he says people use them badly and he may remove them.
- Why LLM output sounds generic: an LLM picks the highest-probability next word, like phone autocomplete, so it trends generic. ChatGPT posts often start with "Are you tired of...?" Good content isn't generic, so the human supplies the first draft and the angle ("you are the captain, AI is the pen and paper").
- Planned: voice or video input, a web app, and possibly design and ideation help. It is LinkedIn-only for now because posts of about 3,000 characters fit it.
- Stack and stage: Chrome extension (beta), solo-founder bootstrapped, with a web app planned. He said the web app would come mid-August for paid users and early September for others, as stated in the talk.

## LinkedIn mechanics and platform opinions

- Microtesting: the platform shows a post to a small sample (for example 20 of 1,000 followers). If the sample reacts (likes, reposts, comments, shares), it widens the audience step by step. If people scroll past, it stops.
- Language rule: pick one language. Posts in a language part of your audience doesn't read get scrolled away, which hurts microtesting. If your audience and clients are in France, write in French. "You should never do both."
- Bots: platforms employ large engineering teams to find bots, so automation and AI agents for social media get punished. He doesn't believe in AI agents for social media. Being social takes time but is worth it.
- LinkedIn's AI comment feature: he thinks it is dangerous. He says about 20-30% of his comments already look AI generated (from real people, not bots). If it reached 50-70% he might stop posting there, because he posts to inspire people and connect one-on-one.
- On AI in general: AI is the pen and paper, not the end product. The skeptic argument ("AI content isn't real") echoes the old complaint that computer-written books weren't real books. Pen and paper has produced many bad books too. He says the best startup combination is a content creator obsessed with a problem plus a technical co-founder obsessed with the same problem. He cites his own pairing with his CTO, Pete, and mentions Sam Altman as an example of strong communication paired with deep technical teams.
