## Why most people don't "get" AI: they never try it

- Ruben says everyone in San Francisco talks about AI, but talking is not understanding.
- His blunt answer to who understands it: "the only people who've tried it, and tried it for long enough."
- Most people love discussing it, few actually test it or read the documentation.
- His edge as a writer: "I test what I write about." It is like explaining how to ride a bike after actually falling off one.
- Example: a law firm kept saying it "couldn't use Claude" for ethics reasons. He asked if they had read Anthropic's SOC 2 compliance or privacy documentation. They hadn't.
- He expects the next 5-10 years to be decided by people who live and breathe AI in their work, and the gap between them and everyone else to keep widening.
- He compares not using AI to not using the internet or phones: a basic part of the economy now.
- He frames it as a new way of working, like Excel's first ad where two executives in an elevator are amazed that you can "edit the numbers in a spreadsheet."

## "Clickers" and getting scared people started

- He divides people into "clickers" and non-clickers. His mother is a pharmacist and asks permission before tapping anything on her phone, fearing it will blow up.
- Clickers open the tool, click everywhere, press the new tab, and aren't afraid to experiment. Like a toddler who keeps climbing and running.
- His mission is to turn more people into clickers; they may surpass him later, and that is fine.
- Common mistake: people equate AI with "agents" and email automation. He never writes about email automation because he thinks people don't want it.
- His entry-point tactic for beginners is Whisper Flow (voice-to-text):
  - Press a key, speak, and it types.
  - It learns names you correct ("Margo", "Scott" with double t).
  - Dictating a to-do list for a colleague shows them it is faster than typing.
- Why it works: beginners write lazy prompts because adding context is tedious. Speaking makes giving rich context easy, and the quality of ChatGPT/Claude answers improves.
- Result: the user realizes AI is not a monster that eats their computer, just a normal tool that makes their day better.
- His closing advice: book a 15-minute meeting with his newsletter twice a week and actually try the tutorial. After a year you "master AI before it masters you."

## Taste, and why "Don't Pick 27"

- Core idea: AI does not make everyone better. It makes the average much better, and the average becomes "insanely good," but still average.
- Canva analogy: if only one designer had Canva templates 20 years ago, they would be the richest designer. Once everyone has them, a template is just a tool. A founder raising a billion-dollar round doesn't want a Canva-template deck.
- Law example: "I don't pay my lawyer to draft contracts. I pay my lawyer to find loopholes and pitfalls." He needs what is not inside the AI.
- If your work is just templates (Canva decks, simple contracts, filling PDFs), you have no advantage, and templates get cheaper and better daily.
- His book, "Don't Pick 27", has no deadline and may take 5 years:
  - Ask an LLM for a number between 1 and 100 and it often picks 27, because humans disproportionately pick 27 (and 47, 37).
  - If AI is the taste-maker and decision-maker, you always get the average.
  - Taste remains the one thing left: you ask the AI, and you decide what is good after it answers.
- His stated key message: "The average will get better and better, but the average remains average. Our role as humans is to understand what the average is, how good it is, and how to be better than the average."

## How he prompts: avoid delegating the thinking

- He pushes back on the "ChatGPT makes you dumb" study: only 50 people, and it really showed that using it without thinking first activates less of your brain.
- Rule: know where you're going first, give enough context, then evaluate the result. Treat the output as not final; expect to tweak it.
- His standard prompt opener: "I want a task with success criteria," so he defines the goal up front instead of asking "tell me what to do."
- Don't ask for one answer. Better: ask for 20 totally different angles (his interview example: 20 ways to interview Ruben), give it all the info you have first and have it verify, then you pick the best 4.
- He calls this the "pick your own adventure" pattern, with AI and human in the loop. He often skips his own notes in a live interview because reading the room cannot be done by an LLM.
- He criticizes the "Jarvis people" (almost all men on Twitter): they build agent orchestrators and OpenClaw setups to look productive, but cannot show what they actually produce.
- Referencing a Steve Jobs exchange with a heckler: what matters is what people actually need and use, not what tech enthusiasts like. He asks to see real results for knowledge workers who don't code.
- He argues agents are hard to set up and maintain for non-coders. His newsletter "How to AI" is about what is practical and impactful today in writing, research and running a business.

## Tool stack for knowledge workers (spring 2026)

- His view: most knowledge work is two things, researching information and writing information (memos, briefs, outreach, follow-ups).
- General-purpose pick: Claude, and specifically Claude Cowork. He sees no competitor today but expects ChatGPT, Codex, Gemini and Microsoft Copilot Cowork to catch up.
- For research: ChatGPT's extended thinking and deep research. He once ran a deep research on peptide markets that ran about an hour and a half.
- Specialized tools: Gamma for slide decks, Granola as a meeting note-taker (people forget around 70% of meetings), Whisper Flow for dictation.
- Context-connector workflow:
  - Connect Gmail, Slack and Granola to Claude.
  - For a contract negotiation, Claude pulls the email threads with his partner and lawyer, Granola meeting notes and Slack threads.
  - He uses it as a thinking partner, not an answer machine. He asks Claude to question him with the "ask user questions" tool (pop-up form: aggressive, calm, or another approach?).
  - He calls this the "IKEA effect": you build it with AI and human in the loop, which is where the flavor comes from.
- Cost note: a Claude Cowork plus Lovable, Supabase, Railway and GitHub setup lets non-coders build apps, but costs a lot; typical users probably don't want to spend more than $1,000 a month.
- His consulting client example: the largest salmon exporter (about half a billion dollars in revenue, small team). They don't need Lovable or Claude Code; a 10-20% productivity gain from sensible tooling is enough. He thinks traditional real businesses will benefit most, using the Pareto rule (the 20% that yields 80%).

## Content philosophy: the IKEA instruction manual

- His analogy: he writes "IKEA instructions." The reader is a person at 2 AM with a box in the living room who has never held a hammer.
- "I'm just the instructions to get there." He wants readers to use his content, not be fans of it.
- Inspiration: French Touch artists (Daft Punk, Justice) who refused interviews and hid their faces so people focus on the music.
- Content principles:
  - Only the necessary steps; the right number (not 2, not 60).
  - Skip storytelling; work out the first step ("where do I start?").
  - Much of his work is change management for someone who must make a change at 2 AM.
- Origins: at age 9 he learned English because of Guild Wars. From age 7 to 14 he wrote blog posts on how to build characters (combining skills from a large pool, picking his own set of 8). He started in French, saw almost no readers, noticed the English forums, and switched to English. He sees this as the same thing he does today.
- Team principle: "Consensus kills creativity." He builds very small teams with one decision-maker. He alone writes and decides on his newsletter. He doesn't mind being wrong or failing. He contrasts this with mainstream news outlets that die of consensus and are all copies of each other.

## Newsletter growth and the free-first business model

- Newsletter: "How to AI" on Substack, started May 19 (about one year before this episode). Said to be possibly the fastest-growing newsletter on Substack at the time. Went from a few thousand subscribers to more than 600,000, growing by a few thousand per day.
- He writes two newsletters a week; his team handles the rest. He published about 1,000 LinkedIn posts (two a day).
- He distributes across LinkedIn, Twitter, Instagram, Facebook; estimates 5-10 million people reached daily (his own estimate, hard to pin down).
- Everything is free on Substack. The paid tier is $200 per year only for access to a Circle community; he says on the signup page you can pick $200 or $0, and that most people shouldn't pay. The payment acts as a filter against spam and "buy what I have" posts.
- Rationale: "money finds its way to you." Giving enough free value means a small fraction (he estimates 10,000-20,000 people eventually) pay.
- Business stack: free content on LinkedIn and Substack, then the Circle community, then his consulting firm. The consulting helps companies adopt AI (he calls it "AI fluency") and builds roadmaps. He is deliberately brand-neutral, not tied to OpenAI, Anthropic or Gemini, and picks the best model per use case (for example, he says Claude may be best for a French law firm, Gemini for a Hebrew-language Israeli insurer).
- Advice to new creators: keep a day job. "If you can't handle the pressure of two jobs, your creative work and your normal job, you won't handle the pressure of being a creator and building a business, because they're two different things." Mixing content and business pressure too early hurts both. Paywalls limit growth, which is why large paywalled media subscriptions shrink.
- Example of impact: a student creator at a LinkedIn event told him she had never paid but had learned most of her AI knowledge from his newsletter, and she sends it to many people. He says that is fine.

## Jobs, adaptability and the doom narrative

- Message to people afraid of AI: build skills to build, become entrepreneurial in mindset, and don't stay static in a job. Past generations changed jobs twice in 40 years; now people may change roles many times in a few years.
- Historical analogy: 100 years ago about 70% of Americans worked in agriculture, now under 1%. He expects similar shifts to happen faster (copywriters, lawyers, customer support).
- Skills to keep: adaptability, learning and building. People in the trades (electricians, plumbers) are fine for now. Business owners of restaurants and cafes could use AI too: a Tel Aviv barista asked whether to ask AI for a business plan to host private dinners in a bigger kitchen, and Ruben told him yes.
- On the "AI will destroy humanity" claim he raised at an event: in the year 1000 the Pope wrote that war would end because of the crossbow. Humans love apocalypse narratives. Risks are real (nuclear weapons need rules), but nuclear catastrophe has not happened, and "you have to balance your own pessimism."
- Platform strategy comment: he thinks OpenAI shut down Sora and the Atlas browser to focus on developers and enterprises, copying Anthropic's enterprise approach (forward-deployed engineers). Anthropic and others are investing in them; Palantir and Mistral take similar enterprise approaches. His consulting positioning is as an independent advisor outside that race.
