## Choosing one platform and one persona

- Ruben rejects the "create once, repurpose everywhere" advice. He says running more than one social account at a high level is "nearly impossible" alone, and the few people who do it have a team.
- His approach is one nail, one massive hammer: pick a single platform and hit it as hard as possible.
- He chose LinkedIn because it is non-anonymous, so he can see a person's profile, colleagues and job. Its quality is also high, with few trolls, because people have jobs to protect.
- His platform rules of thumb:
  - Twitter for people in AI, crypto or fast-moving news.
  - Instagram Reels, TikTok and YouTube Shorts for artists or video-savvy people, since the same content works across all three.
  - For founders building a business, he still advises LinkedIn or Twitter, depending on industry and audience.
- Only after reaching millions of views on one platform should you consider expanding, and then with a team.
- He is obsessed with knowing the persona and writes for one person. His Twitter persona differs from his LinkedIn one, so the same content tackles different people.

## Treating content like a startup product

- Ruben treats social media like a startup product because the feedback loop is as fast as it gets. Each post is "kind of like I'm sharing a Google form".
- Feedback signals he reads: engagement, DMs, link clicks and impressions. Impressions are his number one metric: how many eyeballs, and how consistently.
- You won't wake up knowing your persona. Read between the lines, talk to the people who comment (even 20 comments), and ask them questions.
- His first PDF was rough, but people kept asking him basic prompting questions and said they loved the step-by-step guide. That told him to keep doing step-by-step guides.
- He first posted the same material on Twitter as on LinkedIn and it didn't resonate:
  - On LinkedIn he had to get people excited about AI, then make it sound simple.
  - On Twitter, trying to excite people about AI made him look like clickbait. Those readers are already in.
  - Once he got direct (for example "I ran this test of Claude vs ChatGPT and here are my results"), his views "skyrocketed".

## Persona by platform

- LinkedIn: the "everyday guy" with a demanding job and no time to test. Ruben writes how-to guides and frameworks and walks them step by step ("you go on the top right, click the three dots").
- Twitter: a more AI-savvy audience, so he can use terms like LLMs, chain of thought and tree of thought. He has built this for about two months, after a year and a half of content.
- He says the goal is to be the bridge between people and AI, meaning ordinary people and generative AI they can use, not data scientists or autonomous driving.

## Kill your ego: educational content over personal story

- His number one content advice, repeated for years: "kill your ego".
- One employee was surprised after three months that personal branding is not about sharing your own story. Ruben says 99% of his content is purely educational and he stays out of it.
- Successful creators sprinkle in personal stories, but that is not why people follow them.
- Example: his friend Ran Chong, 23 at the time, reached more than a billion people on Twitter in a year. Not one of his posts was about himself. He is just the fastest guy on AI news.
- A person-based brand works, but a logo page would not. People follow it for the useful content, not the person.
- Ruben shares personal updates only occasionally, in his smaller newsletter community (for example after Google's event).
- Business conversations are content too. Talking about the challenges you faced and how you solved them is what people want.

## How to learn LinkedIn and set realistic expectations

- Before posting, spend a week, 10 days or a month studying your favorite creators: how often they post, the format (image or video), and how they write captions. His analogy: to be the fastest runner, study Usain Bolt's routine first.
- Reverse-engineer what works. He cites Stephen King's advice that great writers begin as copy artists.
- Copied templates (a screenshot of a tweet, a few lines, "agree?") still work on the platform, and he is frustrated by that. But it is also your branding, and an expert doesn't need 10,000 likes per post.
- Creating content is his full-time job and his team's. They spend 30 minutes on the first two lines of a tweet. Founders have a business to run and shouldn't expect to become internet celebrities.
- A realistic middle ground: post once or twice a week with high quality content.
- Even a few comments can pay off. Friends job hunting in Tel Aviv got DMs and interview invitations.
- Posting is only about a third of the game. Two thirds is commenting, DMs and engaging with others. His market analogy: selling fruit means knowing the cheese and meat sellers too, and you can't expect engagement if you never talk to anyone.
- A founder who creates content is better at hiring influencer marketers and can talk to them differently.

## Practical AI applications in sales and marketing

- Marketing: he describes paid-ads teams (he worked with Trade Republic's paid team) producing many designs, copies, videos and images to test on Facebook, Snapchat and TikTok, then pouring money into the winner. He says AI plus automation can produce thousands of variations and push budget to the best one. He names a tool by Alison/Quicksilver for this.
- Sales negotiation: he uses a tool (named Substrata in the transcript) on high-ticket client emails. It tells him who has the upper hand, and he uses it almost every day.
- CRM: after training a sales agency from Jason Lemkin in the US, where the team wasn't filling the CRM, he points to tools that connect to calls and fill Salesforce plus draft the follow-up emails. He names Attention Tech as one.
- Inbound content: use ChatGPT or Gemini to brainstorm. He is also building his own tool for this.
- He admits "sales and marketing" is too broad a question, like asking for the real applications of the internet.

## The lean AI-powered company

- Ruben loves this topic. He cites a graph over about 100 years showing the number of employees needed to make a million dollars dropping drastically, using top S&P 500 companies.
- He says some solopreneurs will become unicorns in the near future. He knows solopreneurs running at a 97% margin with one or two team members.
- His own goal is a company as lean as possible, using AI wherever he can.
- Thresholds are falling in content, no-code tools, automation and creativity. Example: Opus Clip turns a recorded live stream into short TikTok-style clips, a job that once needed a hired editor.

## Learning AI and building products in a fast-moving field

- Ruben learned AI from Google, YouTube, Reddit and Discord, and now there are many creators to follow. Free route: consume content from top creators in your niche (he names Nick St. Pierre for Midjourney). Paid route: consulting, which gives current data.
- The industry moves so fast that what you learn may be outdated in six months.
- Last April he sold two masterclasses on ChatGPT and Midjourney for about $150,000. He says that content is already old: it covered Midjourney V4, which is now V6 with many new features.
- He refuses to predict the next five years. Asked at a B2B summit in Paris in June, he said giving a vision would make him "a total charlatan".
- On what to build:
  - He wouldn't build an AI meeting-summary tool, since he guesses Google Calendar and Zoom are already doing it.
  - Don't build AI first. Many companies build chatbots because they can, not because they solve a problem. He calls these "GPT wrappers".
  - Focus on solving the problem first, and use AI if it helps. If it doesn't, "who cares".
