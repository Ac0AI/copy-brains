## Taste is what AI can't commoditize

- Ruben argues AI output is "average," but average is now so cheap that it beats what a freelance writer produced before ChatGPT. The same 2,000-word article that once earned $1,000 now looks like "just some words."
- His line "master AI before it masters you" means this: if you only produce average work, nobody has a reason to pay for it. You have to ask "what is so unique about my vision on doing something," whether you are a writer, accountant or lawyer.
- Lawyer example: contract templates get automated. What remains is service quality and human contact, such as having lunch with the client.
- He says he is open about using AI for his newsletter and records screens of how he does it. People think it is fast, but he spends a lot of time feeding AI his taste and steering its direction.
- He argues AI lets you pivot an article without having to convince anyone, which a human team would resist.

## Turning your taste into a reusable file

- He published an article titled "I am just a text file" the same day. It came from about two hours of answering Claude's questions, after a prompt that said to ask him a long list of questions.
- Result: a markdown file of roughly 800 to 1,000 lines. It holds his opinions, taste, boundaries, how he writes, what he would never say, and his views on AI tools as of January 2026.
- Uploaded to a project in any AI, the model behaves "kind of like me," sometimes blunt and direct, with his little bracketed jokes.
- Idea: condense who you are into an encyclopedia you can carry across platforms, have conversations with, or let others use.
- Takeaway: shaping your taste, then steering the AI with it, keeps you ahead of the tool.

## Decision paralysis when you can build anything

- He points to engineers after Claude Code with Opus 4.5 (end of December 2025). Hundreds of thousands reportedly said they "can code anything but don't know what to code."
- Coding used to take months, so people coded for others. Now it takes hours, so they need ideas, customer conversations and some marketing knowledge.
- He suggests the future skill looks like project management as a philosophy: know the goal, define the steps, and orchestrate multiple AIs with your taste.
- His fix for paralysis:
  - Turn off the AI news. His newsletter is deliberately not about news; it is about how to do something today.
  - Pick one easy thing you want to do and one AI to do it.
  - Spend about 15 minutes finding a reliable source of truth (not SEO articles). Being wrong is fine.
  - Play with it like a kid. His newsletter hero image is a child for this reason.
  - Mastering one thing is good enough.
- Analogy: nobody becomes a pro tennis player without first hitting a ball for 10 minutes with friends.

## Why AI pushes people to be more human

- He told a heckler at VidCon in Los Angeles: "AI will force us to be more human." He also says he can list a thousand bad things about AI, as with the internet.
- Historical argument: in 1900, about 90% of people in the US worked in food (farming, animals, logistics). Freed from that, people could think about electricity, computers, Netflix and Amazon. He expects the same with AI.
- Industries he expects to grow because they are human: health, longevity, events, real human experiences.
- He is not a doomsayer. He says he never fear-mongers about job loss, but he does want people to discuss implications with kids, grandparents and partners.
- Safety example: he built an avatar and voice clone of himself two years ago and called his mother with the French version; she could not tell which was real. He believes video-call scams using fake voices and faces are already possible, and emergency-panic scams would work on him.
- Positive example: he colored black-and-white photos of his grandmother (who died when his mother was 12) with Nano Banana and animated one with Veo for a couple of seconds. His mother cried.

## Slow-build personal brand: keep the day job

- He built his audience with a job. Before ghostwriting he worked at Trade Republic (a Berlin fintech) while running a music label (started at 18, ran about seven years). He moved from job plus ghostwriting to ghostwriting only, then to writing for himself.
- He argues against quitting your job to build a brand. Money pressure leads to decisions that hurt quality: "the more you forget about making money, the more money you make."
- Test he offers: if you cannot handle a job plus the side project, you probably could not handle the side project alone. How much you want it shows in whether you make the sacrifice.
- He cites Justin Welsh as agreeing, and says he had his team read an article on the subject.
- His own "overnight" success was not overnight: the music label and years of curating and writing came first.
- He thinks everyone has potential, but "mathematically very few are gonna make it."

## How he started (pattern: test, then teach)

- As a 9-year-old he was obsessed with Guild Wars. Players had to pick eight skills from a near-infinite set. He tested builds with sticky notes and wrote French blogs about them, then learned English to reach the larger English forum. He did this for about five years.
- Music label work was mostly curating and sharing what he liked, as a "taste maker."
- ChatGPT origin: on November 30, 2022 he was a 25-year-old ghostwriter in Berlin who did not want to hire people. His first prompts were bad, then better. He found only technical material (Python, NLP, papers on chain of thought, few-shot prompting).
- He translated the papers into plain terms:
  - One-shot prompting: give one example before the request. Few-shot: give a few examples.
  - Chain of thought: give steps. Instead of "write an article," say step one outline, step two headings, step three the article.
- On December 7, 2022 he posted this on LinkedIn and woke to a million views. In the comments he asked what people needed and made content from the answers.
- About three months later he had 100,000 followers and went full time, moving from Berlin to Tel Aviv. He cites roughly 500 million LinkedIn views (he is unsure of the exact current figure).
- His method today: test things for himself like a scientist, discard what fails, and only publish when something is useful and not obvious.

## Bots, social platforms and his publishing cadence

- He says the share of real comments on his LinkedIn dropped from about 100% genuine to roughly 50% (he hopes) as AI comments rose. His meme: a monkey handed a rifle.
- He expects platforms to verify humans (he mentions Instagram's CEO discussing ID verification). He supports measures like ID and a paid account.
- Bot cost argument: a bot costs a few cents a month, but at $10 per account per month, 10,000 bots cost $100,000 a month, which kills the economics. He describes this as Elon Musk's logic for paying on Twitter.
- Whether content is written with AI does not matter to him; the goal is talking to real people.
- Substack is his preferred place for effort because it is preserved as high-quality long form compared with LinkedIn.
- Cadence: newsletter twice a week (Wednesday and Sunday), 99% free. His plan is about 100 deep dives a year on one AI topic each. His advice: "skip the noise" and just read the newsletter.
- Example: Nelly, a reader of three years at Philips (12 years there), became the company's AI champion by playing with his content.
