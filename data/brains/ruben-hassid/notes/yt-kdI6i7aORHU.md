## Useful over personal: the core content philosophy

- Ruben's guiding rule for his team: "be useful, not personal." He is "obsessed about being useful."
- He rejects the standard personal-branding advice to talk about your own story, opinions and experiences. His view: "I think no one cares, actually."
- He rarely posts "that's what I think about X." He frames himself as solving a puzzle and passing on the recipe, like a mad scientist handing over the potion so others don't have to spend the time solving it.
- Personal stories have a place in live settings (interviews, webinars, keynotes at VidCon in Denmark and Paris, Google in Tel Aviv), where he connects directly with the audience.
- He argues posts are already personal without trying: he picks the topic, the angle, the experience, the learnings and the conclusion. "I don't have to make it more about me than it already is."
- He says the best creators he follows work the same way.

## Writing for "the guy on the bus"

- Ruben pictures a specific reader: someone on a crowded bus at 7:00 p.m., on the way back from the doctor, kid sick, partner upset, reading on a moving phone.
- He says the problem isn't poor reading skills. Life is simply full of messages and apps (Slack, WhatsApp, Telegram, Twitter, LinkedIn).
- Within 2 seconds the reader must know the context, what they will learn, and how the post helps them.
- By the end of the post the reader should be able to do something better, even if the gain is small.
- If the reader gets nothing in the first 15 seconds, "I'm probably not respecting him."
- Tactics that follow:
  - No long introductions; go straight to the point.
  - Actionable, step-by-step content.
  - No personal opinion pieces.
  - Always check how a post looks on a phone before publishing.

## Posting cadence and the content backlog

- Ruben posts twice a day, every day, on LinkedIn alone. That excludes Threads, Twitter, his newsletter and YouTube.
- His rule: if a post doesn't bring value, don't share it. He isn't trying to fill a calendar.
- The more you publish with the "be useful" filter, the fewer ideas qualify: "I wish I had more bullets."
- He keeps a large backlog of queued posts and still wishes it were bigger. He says people who "don't know what to post today" should flip the framing and ask whether they have enough worthwhile ideas.
- His expansion path: LinkedIn first, then a newsletter, then Twitter and Threads, and YouTube when he has time. He repeats the cycle without a special strategy.

## EasyGen: building a tool from his own ghostwriting process

- Origin: Ruben was a ghostwriter (for a financial analyst, while in Berlin) and wanted a "Mr. Beast-style" assistant who follows you around and absorbs how you work. He couldn't do that while moving between Berlin, Paris and Tel Aviv.
- Solution: train AI on his ghostwriting knowledge and SOPs. He started building it when ChatGPT's first API came out.
- He built it for himself first and says he grew from 8,000 followers to over 400,000 in 2 years using it. He made it public so his team could use it.
- How it works:
  - EasyGen is meant to take you "from 10 to 80, to 10 to 90," not from 0 to 100.
  - You arrive knowing the post topic, and it acts like a ghostwriter you brainstorm with.
  - Inputs include typed topics, audio, and uploaded images such as cheat sheets, from which it extracts the topic.
  - Formats include viral mindset quotes and cheat sheets.
  - You review, preview on a phone, and can generate an image.
- His own usage: he regenerates a post 20 to 30 times before choosing, and generates around 30 posts to pick the best lines.
- "AI is not just one button you press and then poof, everything happens." He still spends time making sure the post is what he wanted.

## How EasyGen sells: proof by use, not growth hacks

- Ruben says there is "no tactic, there is no black hat." He shares content on how he uses the tool, why, and how effective it is for him and his team.
- His team's accounts are the proof. Employees started posting in May, are now at about 23,000 followers each (Anisha is mentioned at 25,000), and their posts are written with EasyGen.
- Affiliates account for roughly 10 to 15% of EasyGen sales. His two biggest affiliates by far are his employees, because they grew their accounts with it.
- He positions himself as the number one user: "No one has ever used EasyGen more than me." He and his team (four people) use it daily.
- His "real black hat" is being useful, showing case studies, and skipping growth-hacking tactics.

## Using AI to be greater, not lazier

- Ruben says the key question is "Am I using it to be lazier or to be greater?"
- His Midjourney analogy: top brands pay a lot for work by creative directors who have taste and experience, and who generate thousands of images with very different prompts and techniques. They build a huge mood board and then pick what works.
- He predicts people with taste and experience will "obliterate" those who just press an automated button.
- Outcome he expects: the mediocre baseline rises, but a portion of people become far better.
- Cross-industry example: a lawyer with an AI contract tool who uses the saved time to focus on the human side and close bigger deals will beat one who simply automates.
- Mindset he recommends: "I finally have the time to do this."
- Outlook: 2022 to 2024 was the chatbot era; next is the agent era, with AI embedded in Slack, CRM, Notion and Google Drive. He notes DeepSeek V3, an open-source model he describes as about 94% cheaper than ChatGPT.

## Turning content into opportunities (keynotes, no ROI expectations)

- Ruben compares social media to the gym: show up daily with the right behavior and have faith, rather than calculating reps and results.
- His advice: "Don't expect any results." If you track ROI on everything, you get lost; if your motivation is being useful every day, good things happen because people see it.
- His own path had no strategy for keynotes. He kept being useful for free and opportunities arrived.
- The problem later flips: "how to say no to 99.9% of the things." He says no to podcasts using a pre-made template reply.
- He ties this back to his music-label days at 17: reach millions online for free, and a person connects better than a company or label. Personal beats made-up company, because a person "has a past, it has a future."

## Business model, revenue and goals

- EasyGen ownership: 20% is held by CTO Pete Sena (a serial entrepreneur who built two digital marketing agencies); Ruben owns the rest.
- He chose a product over a ghostwriting agency because he doesn't want to deal with people or agency pain (tight margins, turnover). Pete told him his agencies' journey "was awful."
- Metrics he cites: about 33,000 weekly users and $500,000 annual recurring revenue ("half a million"), with a target of $1 million.
- Reach claim: his best day of the year was about 4 million people reached on LinkedIn.
- Long-term idea: build for other creators what he built with EasyGen, i.e. train an AI extension of a person's expertise that can serve tens of thousands of users, which no individual could serve alone.
- Guiding principle for decisions: "Having fun," and building like "an endless puzzle."
