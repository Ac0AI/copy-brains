## Using AI to write LinkedIn posts: taste and experience over one-shot prompts

- Ruben argues AI does not make everyone's content good. It raises the floor of what counts as "mediocre", but mediocre output is still mediocre.
- He expects mediocre work to be replaced fast: people whose job is mostly "writing okayish articles", writing average contracts as a lawyer, or writing briefs to designers.
- The replacement will not be a "monster AI". It will be "one guy who can generate hundreds of contracts, hundreds of articles, hundreds of briefs and handles agents" because he has "more taste, more experience and his standards are just way higher".
- His central rule: bad AI results come from "one prompt, one shot" ("write a viral post on LinkedIn"), then judging the tool on that output.
- He uses AI "to be greater, not to be lazier": the aim is to do more, not to finish a quick generation and stop for the day.
- Analogy: the best Midjourney artists are creative directors. They don't chase one perfect prompt. They generate a thousand images, pick the 20 worth deep-diving, then zoom, unzoom and iterate, like a photo shoot where you shoot a thousand frames and select.
- His workflow with his own tool, EasyGen: generate 20-30 posts on one topic, then patchwork the best hook, the best middle section and the best structure. Humans are better as "creator and editor" or orchestrator than as one-shot writers.
- He says people over 30 are mastering AI faster than the 15-25s, who use it for school badly because "it's not vibing with them".

## AI pushes people to be more human

- Ruben's "bold statement" is that AI will push people to be more human.
- Example: two lawyers with equal taste and the same contract AI. The one who handles clients socially gets paid 10x, because clients "do want to talk to a lawyer".
- The same applies to creators: how you behave on a podcast or keynote becomes the differentiator, because people follow personality.
- Historical framing: around 1900 about 90% of the US population worked on food. Within roughly 60-70 years it fell below 1%. He doesn't believe in "no one's going to work anymore". People find new work, and competitive workforces reward those with more taste and volume.
- His advice on trends: "follow the money". He points at the trillions the top five companies are spending on AI as a signal the direction is worth mastering.

## Why professionals should post on LinkedIn (even without becoming influencers)

- He predicts an "uberization" of work: more people become freelancers, hired and fired quickly because skills and needs change fast. Freelancers need choice over who they work with, which means being visible.
- He says SEO articles and ads probably won't work for individuals, and podcasting is hard. So people should talk about what they do for a living, not become content creators per se. This also protects against a company going badly or a job move.
- He is bullish on LinkedIn ("I hate LinkedIn, I love LinkedIn") because it is the only non-anonymous professional platform: you can see classmates, bosses and employers, and DM them. He says LinkedIn doesn't care about creators, which is part of why it is a good place to be one.
- Example: his sister, a gastroenterologist who leads a prominent European effort around ultrasound, hates LinkedIn but needs to get the word out. His advice: not daily posts, but about one post a week on her topic.

## The post pipeline: mission, ideas, then post

- Ruben works backwards from the end of the pipeline. Post creation comes first in EasyGen, with image generation also added. Ideation and mission come next. He says about 80% of a post is the caption.
- Mission: a single problem you are trying to solve. Examples he gives: upskilling teams instead of hiring outside; getting cold emails opened and fixing email deliverability.
- The tighter and simpler the problem, the easier it is. Then solve it in many ways, because there are "way more subtopics than you think". Under "management of teams" alone there are a hundred topics.
- Idea sources: Perplexity, Reddit, Twitter, Discord and newsletters in your industry. He built free "Mission GPT" and "Topic GPT" tools for this.
- Input: you can type a topic, or speak a voice note or upload audio, and the tool turns it into a post.
- Advice on scope: one post should focus on one solution, not a list of several.
- Pivoting is fine ("there is no stamp"), but go all in at the start.

## Starting a LinkedIn account from zero (the playbook)

- Ruben says he'd start again on LinkedIn because it is text-heavy and non-anonymous. He cites having done it four times, including accounts for team members.
- He documented it publicly: he wrote a week of posts for team members using EasyGen, posting every day, recording himself weekly for 24 weeks. One account was at about 6K followers by week nine. He says a person's whole week of content took roughly two days.
- Result he cites: 0 to 14 million views, and over 20,000 followers on those accounts.
- Step 1, first 30 days: post daily and try several formats and angles. It is tough.
- Step 2: analyze the best five and worst five posts. Remake the best five about 30 times over the next month, each slightly differently, and drop the other 25. Don't try to fix a loser. "Always double down on the winners."
- Step 3: repeat yourself a lot. "You don't know how many times I repeated myself."
- Step 4: engage as much as you post, and more in the beginning, on your own comments and on other people's content. His argument: you can't want a community's results and never talk to anyone.
- He says the known pattern holds elsewhere: a YouTuber who regrew a channel secretly did it again because he had the taste and experience.

## Building EasyGen: product decisions and positioning

- EasyGen is a LinkedIn post generator. It is built as a multi-agent workflow, not a GPT wrapper: multiple LLMs talking to each other, connected via APIs.
- Timeline: he built it for himself first and kept it private. He made it public in May 2025 after wanting his team to use it. The talk is from January 2025 per his own references, with about 38,000 users and a figure of 500K that he leaves unspecified (ARR or MRR is unclear in the transcript). He says it is bootstrapped and that VCs are chasing him.
- Product rule: "I don't listen to users." He quotes Henry Ford: asking customers gets you faster horses. He builds it as a tool he uses himself.
- Example of a request he refuses: automated AI comments and LinkedIn post scheduling. He says the minimum risk is a ban and the account is gone, so he wouldn't do it for himself and doesn't sell it.
- Positioning: it is not a one-click solution. Its job is to "make killer variations of posts", and he never promised one-click automation.
- Marketing tactic: he published a blog comparing competitors (Taplio, Magicpost, SocialPilot and others). He ran the same three prompts, one-shot, through each tool and shows the results. He concludes EasyGen is best and invites people to run the trials themselves.
- Naming: both "Master AI before it masters you" and "EasyGen" came from ChatGPT after going through about a thousand names.

## Ruben's path and how he decides to take risks

- Background: as a kid he played Guild Wars and loved "theory crafting" (choosing 8 skills from thousands). He wrote builds and shared them on blogs, and says he learned English from it. This gave him his habit of discovering a recipe and sharing it.
- At 17 he started a techno label that got millions of views on SoundCloud and YouTube. After seven years he concluded the DJs (personal brands) were more powerful than the label.
- He then ghostwrote for a fintech founder and wrote on his own LinkedIn. In December 2022 he published a ChatGPT guide that reached about a million people in two days, which drew requests that led him to produce content instead of taking consulting clients.
- By March he was bigger than his clients, which pushed him to go solo at age 26. He later moved to Tel Aviv, per the transcript.
- His risk method: open an Excel file and write the worst case. How many months of runway (for example four months)? What happens if you earn zero in that time? Could you find a job again? He treats it as an honest conversation with yourself, and says people will call you crazy at every step because most would not take the risk.
- He now has more than 10-15 employees and uses the same worst-case thinking.
- Self-positioning: he's "worthless and yet very impactful": not an engineer or model builder, but someone who teaches non-technical people how to use AI. He wants to be "a step" in people's journeys, not a guru.
- Claims about reach: about 472,000 LinkedIn followers (the talk mentions 400,000 earlier), roughly 200 million views in two years from zero, and a blog sent as a Sunday newsletter to 45,000 people. He plans a tighter Telegram community in Q1 2025.
