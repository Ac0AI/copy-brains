## Why Apple's 30% commission raises costs for users and developers alike

Pavel argues that Apple's 30% App Store commission is added on top of everything else developers already pay. He sees it as a tax that ends up costing iPhone users money, choice, privacy, and app quality. He lists seven reasons users should care.

- **Higher prices.** The 30% sits on top of what users pay developers for apps, services and games.
  - Users already paid "a few hundred dollars more" for the iPhone than it cost to make.
  - His summary: "you keep paying even after you have paid."

## Apple's control of the App Store enables censorship

- Some content in apps like Telegram is unavailable on iPhone because Apple controls what the App Store allows.
- He says Apple controls the store in order to enforce the 30% tax.
- Apple also bars developers from telling users that specific content was hidden for iPhone users at their request.
- He compares it to a web browser deciding which websites you may view, and calls Apple's global censorship attempt "ridiculous".

## Mandatory Apple accounts weaken user privacy

- Installing an app requires creating an Apple account and logging in.
- Every app downloaded and every push notification received is then tied to that account, which makes the user easier to track.
- He says the main reason an Apple account is required is Apple's wish to enforce the 30% commission, so "the cost of their greed also includes your private data."

## Slow app review delays updates

- Users get new app versions days or weeks after they are ready because Apple's review team is "notoriously inefficient" and often delays approval for no apparent reason.
- Big apps like Telegram typically wait several days to publish updates.
- He points out that Apple receives billions of dollars from third-party apps and could hire more moderators, yet apparently cannot.

## The commission makes many apps unviable and leads to fewer apps

- The 30% adds to other developer costs: government taxes such as VAT (about 20%), wages, research, servers and marketing.
- Many apps would be net profitable without the commission, but surrendering 30% of revenue makes them unsustainable.
- Many go bankrupt, so "lots of great apps you could have enjoyed just don't exist."

## Business model effects: more ads and worse apps

- Selling premium services and accepting donations becomes "one-third less meaningful" for developers.
- Many therefore show ads to survive, which skews the industry toward selling user data.
- He prefers privacy-friendly models such as selling additional services to users.
- Billions taken from developers could otherwise fund app quality and R&D.
- Instead, he says the money "rests idly in Apple's offshore bank accounts and does nothing for the world."

## Why the 30% cut has survived for over 10 years

- Pavel notes the situation is bad enough that the cut should be unsustainable, yet it has existed for more than 10 years.
- He says Apple tricked consumers and regulators into inaction.
- He pointed readers to a longer Telegraph post explaining how Apple managed this.
