## VK vs. Facebook: positioning and international expansion

- Pavel thinks Zuckerberg "still underestimates VK in some ways" and suspects he doesn't use VK much.
- VK is not a global Facebook. Several technologies appeared first on VK and later on Facebook.
- VK is popular in Russia, Ukraine and other post-Soviet countries because it is faster, easier to use and has more of the functionality that population wants.
- Going global is not a goal to spend money on. VK translates the product into other languages and sees whether it catches on. If it happens naturally, good; if not, he won't worry.
- On an IPO, the VK IPO was cancelled after Facebook's IPO sent a bad market signal. Market conditions now look good, but he says it is too early to decide.

## Music licensing and copyright on VK

- Pavel disputes that VK is an illegal download service. Users can't download music or video on VK; there is only a streaming service.
- The service is DMCA compliant. Rights holders can send a takedown request, the item is deleted and the user cannot upload it again.
- VK was negotiating with Sony Music on ways for rights holders to earn money from the streaming service. He was optimistic about a solution within several months.
- He confirms this amounts to building a Spotify-like music service.

## Telegram: origin and product design

- Telegram started as a nonprofit research project, built around one question: why must messaging be either fast and easy or secure, never both?
- A dedicated team (his brother led the research and created a new data protocol) built a messenger he claims is faster than the fastest on the market, with strong encryption including end-to-end.
- The project had been going for over a year.
- It is not a business project or an investment vehicle. It is nonprofit and open source, closer to a set of technologies plus apps (e.g. on Android) than a company.
- He says it won't compete with VK. Telegram is a messenger, not a social network, with different demographics and no tie to Russia.

## Telegram's differentiators vs. WhatsApp

- **Security:** "military grade" security. He says WhatsApp traffic can be monitored and the messages easily decrypted. With Telegram, from what they've seen, that is impossible, especially with end-to-end encryption.
- **Infrastructure:** decentralized, with servers spread around the world. Users connect to the closest one to talk with local friends.
- **Openness:** the project is open source and fully documented, and the API is an open platform anyone can use to build their own secure messenger.
- **Speed and cloud:** fast and cloud-based, so messages are accessible from several devices at once. He contrasts this with WhatsApp, where you have to choose one device.
- **Price:** completely free.

## Government pressure, shareholders and Telegram's separation from VK

- VK would have included Telegram's encryption, but some shareholders would not have liked that. Pavel says both major shareholders have ties to the government.
- He says those shareholders made clear they don't want this kind of encryption project. He thinks encryption could be "perceived in the wrong way", which is part of why Telegram is kept separate.
- He sees no direct connection between himself and the government, and says he is doing useful things for the country, such as developing Russian IT as an example for the younger generation.
- He called the April traffic charges a public relations campaign: every major Russian TV channel ran a story, then footage was reviewed and the claims fell apart.
- He took about two months off during that period to figure out what to do next.
- On data requests: if you use end-to-end encryption, there is no data to hand over, and even Telegram's own system administrator can't access private data. He invites people to review the open-source algorithms and check for themselves.
- Telegram had received no requests so far, despite the fact that a Boston bomber was a VK user.

## Privacy, surveillance and transparency

- Pavel says the situation is similar almost everywhere. The difference in Eastern Europe is that governments are open about wanting data, while in the US governments claim privacy is sacred and then it turns out it isn't.
- **Transparency first:** society needs to understand what is collected and why, then debate whether it accepts "big brother", before deciding on a solution.
- **Engineers' responsibility:** it is on IT businessmen and engineers to build technologies that make total surveillance impossible.
- He calls privacy a sacred right, guaranteed by most European constitutions and, he believes, in the US. Russia's constitution protects private correspondence unless a court decides otherwise.
- He rejects terrorism as a pretext for violating everyone's privacy. His argument is statistical: more people die from everyday accidents, like slipping on a wet floor in a bathroom, than from terrorist attacks. By that logic, governments could justify installing cameras in bathrooms for safety.
- He thinks the most popular private-communication companies, above all WhatsApp, should introduce transparent algorithms. The field was neglected for years: "security was number 10" behind new products, which he calls wrong.
- On Snowden, he says Telegram offered him a job doing privacy work. He calls Snowden one of his personal heroes for sacrificing a large part of his life to tell people they were being spied on. He had not met him.
- He sees Telegram as a "fighting project" to push more companies to protect user privacy, including voice over IP, social networking and other tools.
