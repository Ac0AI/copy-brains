## Pavel's arrest in France and how he describes it

- He flew into Paris in August 2024 as a tourist (heading on to Finland, no business meetings) and was met at the airport by police, who read him a list of about 16 charges.
- He was held 4 days in a roughly 7 m² windowless cell with a thin mattress, no pillow and a blinking light, with no phone or contact beyond his assistant.
- He says the charges were about crimes other users committed on Telegram, not anything he did. No one claims he personally took part in drug sales, organized crime or similar.
- He was confused about why police skipped the normal route. As a French citizen his home address is in his passport, and the French consulate is two floors below Telegram's Dubai office. He had met French officials there before and was "happy to just go two floors below".
- He found the public announcements and press coverage unusual. A policeman told him newspapers were writing "everything but truth".
- Hardest part: being cut off from his ill, elderly mother and his children.

## The "failure to cooperate" claim

- French authorities alleged Telegram ignored judicial requests. Pavel says it never received a single legally binding request from France.
- Under the EU Digital Services Act there is a defined process for such requests, and Telegram's privacy policy documents it. Telegram had run a Belgian company handling these requests for about a year before his arrest.
- Telegram discloses IP address and phone number of criminal suspects when it receives a court order signed by a judge. This concerns identification data only, not private messages, which he says Telegram could not disclose even if it wanted to.
- After his arrest, French requests started arriving through the proper channel and Telegram answered them. He says French officials then publicly celebrated Telegram "now cooperating" as if it were new, which he thinks gave a false picture, "bad for Telegram" and worse for France's image.
- He says prosecutors could have found the process by Googling it in "about 6 minutes".

## Complicity theory and Telegram's compliance posture

- Pavel says his lawyers call the complicity theory unprecedented in France. Past cases targeted small niche apps built for criminals, with no bank accounts or audits.
- He argues Telegram is the opposite of that profile:
  - Audited by a Big Four firm.
  - Works with major financial institutions.
  - Issued bonds with JP Morgan's help.
  - Spends millions per quarter on legal compliance and content moderation.
  - Operates in almost 200 countries.
- His logic: a platform used by a billion people (every eighth person on the planet) will always have some bad actors. Blaming the founder for that would be like jailing a president for every armed robbery in his country.
- Current status: not on trial. He is under a judicial investigation to decide whether there is enough evidence for one.
- He is under "judicial control", which limits his travel. He has been allowed to go to Dubai and back, and only has to answer questions in France every 4 to 5 months. After almost 8 months he says he still does not know the exact claim against him.
- Costs: he cannot sign documents for his kids in Dubai, cannot see his mother, and runs the company remotely, less efficiently. France is under 1% of Telegram users (he says about half of 1%).

## Telegram's business model and product record

- Telegram became profitable in 2024, with more than half a billion dollars in profit.
- It reached 1 billion monthly users and is the second most popular messaging app in the world.
- Monetization avoids harvesting personal data for targeted ads. Paid subscriptions are voluntary and the core service is free.
- He claims dozens of features now standard in messaging apps (about 100 by his list) first appeared on Telegram. Examples he gives: replying to messages, sharing links and documents, text formatting. Rivals copied them 3 to 8 years later.
- Telegram doesn't promote channels. A new user sees an empty chat list, and every channel must be deliberately searched for and joined. He calls this neutral: people judge for themselves.

## Privacy, encryption and back doors

- He rejects back doors on technical grounds: no exclusive back door exists, so any back door can be exploited by foreign agents and hackers, endangering all users.
- Criminals would switch to niche apps or VPNs, so only law-abiding citizens would be hurt.
- He cites a French Senate law passed the month before that effectively banned encryption. The National Assembly shot it down, but a French public he says was unaware of it. The European Commission's early-April proposal pushes similar back doors at EU level. He says no country has banned encryption so far.
- He chose not to base his team in the US partly because of gag orders, which he says let the government force an engineer to implement a back door and not even tell the employer. He took US legal advice and decided it was a poor place for a privacy-oriented platform.
- His stance: if a country forced Telegram to disclose private messaging data, "we would discontinue providing services in that country". He says this is why he left Russia.
- No one has demanded "the keys" from him in France, and technically such keys don't exist. If asked, "you'll be among the first to learn about it".
- Biggest privacy risk in his view: state actors compromising the device itself via zero-day vulnerabilities, and spyware like Pegasus (he says he was among those who had it installed on their phones around 8 years earlier, per leaked reports). Checking for known compromises is possible but nothing is certain.
- On quantum computing: encryption has to evolve and quantum-secure methods exist, but he won't claim confidence, since state actors may have capabilities nobody discloses.
- Personal practice: he doesn't carry or regularly use a phone (about a year, no SIM). He sees it as distracting and a privacy risk. He tests Telegram on a device twice a week for product updates, and otherwise works from laptop or iPad to avoid consuming short-form content.

## Governments, free speech and the EU

- On governments' motives, he's measured: officials are "trying to solve the problems that they see using the tools that they have", not necessarily running a conspiracy, even if the result harms freedoms. Interior ministers say encryption is the problem, without seeing two or three steps ahead.
- Recurring justifications for weakening privacy are protecting children and fighting crime.
- He argues freedoms erode gradually when populations take them for granted. People who grew up in the Soviet Union, like him, remember having only three TV channels (all about the Communist Party) and two kinds of ice cream, and so value free markets and free speech.
- He says the DSA requires removing content that one EU member state demands be removed across the whole EU, with appeals taking years and fines or bans for slow compliance.
- He recounts a Latvian border officer telling him the EU felt like the Soviet Union again.
- He still describes France as a country that respects human rights and says he plans to return after the case.
