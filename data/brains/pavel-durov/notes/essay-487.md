## Growth without marketing, and why claims must match product

- In 2021 Pavel said US-based competitors were frustrated that they could not match Telegram's growth, "despite heavily investing in marketing (something Telegram has never had to invest in)".
- His argument: to match Telegram's growth, rivals "have to first make sure their actions match their marketing claims". Until then, he says, data breaches and security issues in their apps are unavoidable.
- In 2022 he cited 700M+ active users and 2M+ daily signups as the reason Telegram "doesn't need additional promotion". He said he was not pushing people to switch and that people can use any messaging app, except WhatsApp.
- Telegram's marketing posture here is to publish verifiable facts and let comparisons do the work, rather than to run campaigns.

## Why he says US-based messengers cannot guarantee privacy

- He argues most competitors could not guarantee privacy "even if they wanted to". Their engineers live in the US and must secretly implement backdoors when the government orders it.
- An engineer who talks publicly about it can go to jail for breaching a gag order.
- He claims agencies often don't need a court order to extract data from apps like WhatsApp, and that court documents are often secret.
- He says some "secure" apps were funded by government agencies from the start, naming Anom and Signal.
- He says the NSA has long shaped international encryption standards to match what it can decipher. Other approaches get labelled "non-standard" or "home-brew", and outsiders are warned against "rolling out their own encryption".
- His conclusion is that apps built on those standards are "plagued with backdoors".

## Verifiability instead of "we're open source"

- He calls "we are open source so anyone can verify" a trick when users cannot confirm the published code is what runs on their phones.
- He says Signal refused to add reproducible builds for iOS and closed a community GitHub request for them.
- He says WhatsApp doesn't publish its app code at all, so its privacy talk is "an even more obvious circus trick".
- His positioning of Telegram:
  - It is the only massively popular messaging service where everyone can check that its apps use the same open source code published on GitHub.
  - For ten years, Secret Chats have been "the only popular method of communication that is verifiably private".
- The principle is to make the claim checkable by outsiders rather than asking for trust.

## Two chat types instead of one compromise

- Telegram has two types of chats: Cloud (100% usable) and Secret (100% secure).
- His claim is that apps trying to deliver both security and usability in one chat type "end up with ugly compromises".
- His two examples:
  - WhatsApp leaks about 95% of messages via unencrypted backups.
  - Signal exposes messages through push notifications. He says the FBI got deleted Signal messages just by checking notification data.
- Secret Chats never show message content in push notifications. This is enforced by design, because turning off previews doesn't help when you can't know whether the people you message have done the same.
- He admits this hurts usability, and says that is why the two-chat split exists.
- Telegram "started the end-to-end encryption revolution" 12+ years ago by launching Secret Chats. He says dozens of apps followed with bold claims, but none has beaten Cloud Chats on usability or Secret Chats on security.
- His summary of rivals: "Their try to mix the two and get neither."

## The WhatsApp backup argument

- His April 2026 claim is that WhatsApp's "End-to-end encryption by default" is "a giant consumer fraud".
- About 95% of private WhatsApp messages end up in plain-text backups on Apple/Google servers, because backup encryption is optional and few people enable it, let alone with strong passwords.
- Even if you encrypt your own backups, your messages still land in unencrypted cloud backups, because 90%+ of the people you message haven't done the same.
- He adds that WhatsApp stores and discloses who you chat with.
- He says Apple and Google disclose backed-up WhatsApp messages to third parties thousands of times per year, while "Telegram hasn't disclosed a single byte of users' messages in its entire 12+ year history".
- In May 2026 he cited Texas suing WhatsApp for lying about privacy, because employees allegedly have access to "virtually all" private messages. He tied this to the WhatsApp founder's remark that he "sold his users' privacy".

## Security track record as a positioning argument

- In 2022 he argued that WhatsApp has had a serious security flaw disclosed almost every year (2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, and again in 2022, when a malicious video or video call could give full phone access). He says these are not accidents but planted backdoors that get replaced when removed.
- He noted that WhatsApp had no encryption at all before 2016, and called it a surveillance tool for 13 years.
- He used Jeff Bezos's 2020 phone compromise as evidence that even the richest person's data was exposed.
- He said he deleted WhatsApp from his own devices years ago, because "having it installed creates a door to get into your phone".
- A pattern in his tactics: he cites a news hook (a report, a lawsuit, an FBI case, a disclosed vulnerability) and links it to a structural claim about the competitor.

## Signal's origins as a trust argument

- In 2024 he amplified a story from Twitter founder Jack Dorsey, claiming Signal's current leaders are activists used by the US State Department for regime change abroad.
- He claimed the US government spent $3M to build Signal's encryption, and that the same encryption now runs in WhatsApp, Facebook Messenger, Google Messages and Skype.
- His reading: big US tech "is not allowed to build its own encryption protocols that would be independent of government interference".
- He said many important people told him their "private" Signal messages had been used against them in US courts or media.
