## Telegram's product principles: simplicity, open platforms, speed

- Pavel says Telegram's edge is being "the most easy to use, the fastest and the most secure" messaging service, and that focusing on the best service for customers is what drove growth.
- He pitches Telegram against rivals on removing limitations. It had multi-device sync, persistent and instantly searchable history (usable "as you use email"), file sharing of any size, and 1,000-member group chats against 100 elsewhere.
- He says it also works reliably on weak connections.
- His design question is "how do we add features without adding complexity".
- The iPhone app has three tabs versus WhatsApp's five.
- His answer is open platforms for third-party developers. Users who don't need a feature never see it.
- Stickers are the example. They launched early 2015 as a user-generated platform. Business users see none, while sticker fans can upload and share packs. He says it "unleashed" creativity.
- On features, he says Telegram ships a major update on every platform every month or month and a half.
- He declines to promise specific features such as phone calls. The roadmap is "fluid" and a small team keeps flexibility to reprioritise when a trend appears.
- He cites voice messaging as a current focus.

## Growth: 100 million users with no marketing spend

- Telegram announced 100 million monthly active users in February 2016, up from roughly 40-50 million a year earlier. He says growth is on every continent except Africa.
- It was at zero about two and a half years before, while WhatsApp already had hundreds of millions of users. He argues it is easier to grow from 100 million than from zero.
- Growth is organic. He says "every day 350,000 people sign up for telegram without any activity from our party. We don't spend anything."
- Growth relies solely on users telling friends to switch.
- He disputes the idea that network effects lock users in. People already use several messaging apps, one each for friends, co-workers and family, so he doubts a single winner will emerge.
- He says users "are eager to switch to a superior alternative". A large share came from WhatsApp and Facebook.
- Outages help. When WhatsApp was blocked in Brazil, a few million people signed up for Telegram that day. He treats that traffic as non-organic and does not rely on it.

## Running a 15-person team at 15 billion messages a day

- Telegram delivers 15 billion messages a day with a core engineering team of 15.
- To keep that going he leans on scripts and "artificial intelligence rather than real human labor" to run infrastructure.
- He credits experience from scaling VKontakte, which at times reached similar metrics.
- The team is distributed and moves country every two to four months.
  - He says three months is ideal.
  - The first week in a new place is wasted getting used to it.
  - Shorter stays distract the team.
- They rent a big house or office plus nearby apartments.
- Travel produces product ideas. In Indonesia (November-December 2015) they saw people using Telegram differently and improved functionality as a result.
- Telegram keeps small offices in Berlin and a legal and accounting office in London. In summer they prefer the Finnish lake district.
- The infrastructure is distributed too:
  - Data sits in multiple data centres worldwide.
  - Cloud data is encrypted with a key split into parts held in different jurisdictions.
  - No single jurisdiction can regulate the data.
- He avoided basing in London after David Cameron's remarks on messaging apps giving law enforcement access. He would not invest in an office only to find the place "inconsistent with our mission".

## Origins: from student websites to VKontakte

- Pavel started at St Petersburg State University by building websites for his department, sharing exam materials.
- The site grew to the whole university (about 20,000 students).
- It then spread to other prestigious Russian universities, all while he was under 20.
- He decided a decentralised social network was needed rather than a portal. He launched VKontakte in 2006 and used his earlier projects as the initial user base.
- Registration later opened to other post-soviet countries and to people with no university degree.
- It reached tens of millions of users within a few years.
- Early on the Russian government paid little attention to the internet. He says Putin didn't use it, so regulating it made little sense to officials.
- After the protests of December 2011, new laws came in and government-friendly funds and oligarchs began buying internet companies.

## Control, pressure and the loss of VKontakte

- Requests from the authorities came as formal signed documents. They asked him to block Alexei Navalny's opposition community and to hand over personal data of Ukrainian protesters. He publicly refused.
- He argued that blocking one network made no sense because the activity would just move to other platforms.
- Armed police came to his door in December 2011 but were not authorised to arrest him.
- That episode planted the Telegram idea. He couldn't be sure his communication with his brother was not being monitored, and that led them to think about an encryption protocol.
- Ownership was the weakness.
  - He and his partners and seed investors originally controlled about 60%.
  - After the investors sold, a fund close to the government secretly bought 48%.
  - Pavel was left with only 12%, so he could not influence his own dismissal as CEO.
  - Shareholders fired him partly to remove the risk his refusals created for them.
- He was in the US when it happened and did not return.
- He says running Telegram from Russia was not possible given the legal framework on private data.
- He sold his Russian company stake at the right moment, before the market crashed, and now funds Telegram himself.

## Funding and business model

- Telegram is financed from his personal cash, with no outside investment.
- He has received many offers from funds and big companies, including the Sequoia type of deal done with WhatsApp. He says he doesn't need outside money now and hopes never to.
- His stated hope is that Telegram can come up with a sustainable business model and cover its expenses.
- He calls it a chance to "give back to society" by keeping Telegram free and independent.
- Runway depends on growth speed. Faster growth raises the monthly burn rate, which may force monetisation sooner.
- At the current growth rate he thinks he can manage "another few years".
- A fallback to buy time would be investment or a loan.
- He wants Telegram independent of big companies. He doesn't rule out a sale in extreme circumstances such as a financial crisis, but he says it is not the plan.
- Monetisation ideas he raises:
  - Advertising does not fit private messaging, but public channels could carry ads, and some media already insert them.
  - A revenue-sharing model for channels is possible.
  - A payment platform for bots, letting third-party services take money from users, where Telegram could take a small cut.
- WhatsApp's move to free and its business services copy Telegram, which was always free. He says its bot and business features were already on Telegram.

## Competition, copying and encryption

- On WhatsApp he says it copied Telegram's features: a desktop app, end-to-end encryption, and bigger group limits (50 to 100, while Telegram went from 200 to 1,000).
- He sees that as a benefit, since a broader market gets better service. He once wrote a blog post, "how to predict WhatsApp features".
- Jan Koum had said in 2013 that Pavel "knows how to copy great products". Pavel counters that the last two years of WhatsApp's evolution have been copying Telegram.
- On encryption and terrorist use:
  - Terrorists use many apps, and Telegram did not invent end-to-end encryption (Apple's iMessage had it).
  - Any popular app will see more illegal use.
  - Telegram cannot read private messages.
  - Backdoors would not work. Telegram is open source, so tampering would be noticed.
  - Bad actors would move to another app, and building one costs only tens of thousands of dollars.
  - The result would be less security for ordinary users while the problem remains.
  - He sees the tool as neutral, like a car maker building the best car.
- Public channels are different. They can be reported, and Telegram takes them down. It blocked around 600 Islamic State channels after the Paris attacks.
- He admits Telegram is "probably not" doing enough, since it is a small team still building tools to automate this.
- He argues that how societies trade privacy against security is for each society to decide, but his Russian experience makes him sceptical of "big brother" moves.
- He holds a free-market, decentralised view. He thinks large hierarchical organisations are inefficient in the 21st century, and that centralisation breeds corruption and inefficiency.
