## Angel investing's "Goldilocks zone" and why Jason bets on founders, not ideas

- **Where angels fit.** VCs have moved downstream. Jason says they now want enterprise companies with $50,000-$100,000 a month in revenue, or consumer products with 10,000-50,000 daily users and months of traffic. They often wait for around 10 million monthly users before a series A. That leaves what he calls the "Goldilocks zone": after the product has launched, before VCs "with their sharp elbows" take board seats.
- **Why that window is attractive.** Nine out of 10 products can reach market for sweat equity or $100,000 or less. Investing after launch screens out the 99% of founders who never ship. He puts the window at roughly three years.
- **Many small rounds.** Startups now raise pre-seed, seed, angel, pre-series A and extension rounds, usually as convertible notes that convert at the series A. Angels can wait and build a relationship over six months instead of panicking to get in.
- **Scale.** Ten years ago there were one or two dozen angels of note. He now estimates 500 to 1,000 people in Silicon Valley doing it as a primary activity.

## Jason's law: back the person, not the idea

- His rule: "I don't need to know if your idea is going to succeed. I just need to know if you're going to succeed."
- His biggest misses were Tesla, Twitter and Zynga. At the time he wasn't an angel, but friends were founding them, and he estimates about $50 million each at his later check sizes. He passed on Twitter because he thought it would be "a cacophony of idiots" and a worse Blogger. He later realized "big things in the world generate tons of wealth" regardless of his taste.
- He passed on Airbnb because he overthought the risks (strangers on a couch, meth parties). He later saw that hotels have the same problems.
- Traits he screens for:
  - passion
  - skill
  - the ability to build a product and a team
  - "they won't give up"
- He says a rational person who understands how hard the job is will quit unless they truly care. Founders must be able to blow up rockets and "still go to work the next day", as he says Elon Musk did.
- Quick diagnostic questions:
  - What are you working on?
  - Why will this work now?
  - Why does this one feature matter?
- He credits YouTube's early team with a clear "why now": syndication plus cheap cloud computing, so embedded videos gave free marketing.
- His list of reasons a startup will fail is long, so he writes it out and rips it up. He asks "what could go right?" and takes a 1-in-50 or 1-in-100 shot if the payoff is larger.

## Risk, portfolio math and sizing

- Dead money: he says people hold too much in bonds, real estate and balanced portfolios returning 4-7% a year. He suggests putting 5-10% of net worth into something highly risky with a shot at 100x or 1,000x.
- Losing six or seven in a row is normal. As a high-stakes poker player, he compares it to losing hands you were favored to win. When he introduced Uber to about 20-25 investors, only three said yes, and some of the greatest investors said no two or three times. Those three hold nine-figure positions.
- Human instinct is conservative (the alligator or lion). "There is no crocodile that's going to eat you if you lose your $10,000." People overestimate the downside.
- Even losing half of a 5-10% allocation is only about 2.5% of net worth, and he argues the network and knowledge gained cover that.
- Track record: six unicorns in his first 100 investments. At book time he had three. The book title promises turning $100,000 into $100 million.
- Fund: just closed a $10 million angel fund of friends' and his own money. He expects roughly 150 more investments to reach about 300. He says angel investing is very hard to scale.
- First-timers vs. serial founders: roughly 50/50 for big outcomes, so he doesn't filter on experience. "All you have to do is hit one."

## How to break into angel investing cheaply

1. Join syndicates (AngelList, SeedInvest, Funders Club, his own "Jason Syndicate") and watch deal flow go by.
2. Start with a fantasy portfolio:
   - Read deal memos.
   - Score conviction from 1 to 10.
   - Pretend to invest $5,000.
   - Log it in a Google Sheet and check valuations every six months.
3. Then use real money. He says money on the table changes your judgment, "like playing poker for $1, $2" instead of fake chips.
4. Put in $1,000 to $3,000 across about 20 deals, roughly $50,000-$60,000 total. That's the price of one angel check, with 20-30 companies' monthly updates to learn from.
5. Treat a $1,000 check like a $100,000 one:
   - Email the founder.
   - Follow them on Twitter and retweet them.
   - Check their job board.
   - Ask every 60 days how you can help.
6. Network via co-investors:
   - Find co-investors on Crunchbase or Mattermark.
   - Put them in a Google Sheet.
   - Send a mail merge, e.g. "We're co-investors in Acme. Any interesting companies you're looking at? I'm breaking into angel investing."
   - About half respond, and some reply repeatedly.
7. Why it works: a round needs 30-50 investors before the series A. Earlier investors have more open slots to fill, and co-investors share deals because "that makes you like family members."

## Democratizing access and accreditation

- He calls the accredited-investor rule (roughly $200,000 a year income, or $1-2 million in assets) unfair. About 95% of Americans are non-accredited yet can bet on sports or buy public stock, but can't buy private startups, where outsized returns are made.
- Europe lets everyone invest as they like. In the US, equity crowdfunding sites (SeedInvest, Republic) let non-accredited people invest around $500.
- Example: early Etsy sellers, eBay sellers, AdSense partners, LinkedIn recruiters and YouTube creators all knew those platforms would be big but couldn't invest.
- Traction: 25 days after the book launched, his syndicate had 1,200 members and 700 more joined. He was seeing about 30 applications a day and accepting about half.
- Cafe X (a robot coffee machine) raised over a million dollars from syndicate investors at $10,000 each.
- He sees crowdfunded deals as so far mostly the ones that didn't clear the market with VCs or angels.
- He thinks investing is bigger than money: angels back the "crazy kids" behind Google and YouTube.

## Advice to founders: stop hacking investors, show engagement

- Rookie mistake: spending 90 of 100 units of effort on hacking investors. He says to spend those on tripling customer engagement, then use the last 10 to tell that story.
- What investors want: someone who can build a team that builds a product users love. Credibility builds over five to seven meetings and email updates, not one pitch.
- Monthly updates:
  - He now requires them in his legal docs.
  - They lead with metrics: months of runway, cash in bank, when the next raise is, revenue and engagement.
  - He tells prospects: put me on your monthly update for three months and I'll decide when it's in my zone.
- Known cost of silence: one company sent him legal docs after 18 months with no contact.
- Know each investor's timing:
  - some invest pre-product (product-driven people)
  - he invests after about six weeks of data
  - VCs want two years of data
- He's happy for investors to lose money "as long as they tried hard."
- He re-backs founders: Raul (Rapportive, sold to LinkedIn, then Superhuman) called him first for the next round, and he put in $1 million. He says this has happened about three times.

## Geography and "pivot"

- New York, LA and SF can all produce great companies. Outside the Valley there is less competition for talent, lower costs (about a third less) and talent stays longer, but fewer unicorns come from there. Amazon and Microsoft are the exceptions.
- Tactic he describes: keep a small HQ or WeWork in the Valley for investors, and put the team elsewhere (Arizona, LA, San Diego, Waterloo).
- To be a great investor, he says you need Silicon Valley access. He did it by flying up two or three days a month and booking all meetings, and hit four of six unicorns that way.
- SF is "the worst-run great city in America", but investors are increasingly tired of SF living costs and open to LA.
- Pivot: he says it means keeping one foot planted and changing direction slightly, not dropping the idea. Example: Uber added ride sharing on top of black cars. Stewart Butterfield pivoted from failed games to Flickr and Slack.
- Side hustles: he generally prefers a clean break and a full-time founder.

## Where he sees opportunity: revisit false starts

- Rule: look at categories where people tried and failed. Investors wrongly reason "it hasn't worked before." Google was the 11th or 12th search engine, just 20x better.
- Examples of timing changes:
  - YouTube: cloud computing made free video hosting viable; earlier, going viral meant a $5,000 bandwidth bill.
  - WebVan to Instacart, UberEats, PostMates and Google Shopping Express: GPS, logistics and unit economics improved.
  - Gaming repeatedly reinvents in waves (casual web, Facebook social, apps).
- AI: past false start, now real. He cites AI for HR, support and legal (contracts, accounting). He expects many white-collar tasks at risk over 10-20 years and expects entrepreneurs to rebuild each vertical as AI-enabled.
- Robotics: past false starts (Roomba, Segway). Now:
  - Cafe X: coffee in under 60 seconds, at about a third less cost.
  - Blockable: robot-built modular housing. He says the real savings is 6-12 months of build time, with the cost possibly 10-30% lower.
- VR: he calls it a possible false start, 4-5 years from mass use, with early VR startups running out of money. AR looks closer, perhaps 10 million daily users in two years.
- ICOs (2017): he calls the first wave scary, like the dotcom bubble. He sees $30-100 million raised on unbuilt products from "dark money" and expects most to go to zero. He thinks tokenized fund LP interests are inevitable within two to three years.
