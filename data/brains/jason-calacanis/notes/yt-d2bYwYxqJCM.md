## Investing philosophy: ask what could go right

- Jason says the way to do well as an angel investor, in tech or in life, is "to not say what could go wrong but to say what could go right," then imagine the world if it works.
- He invested in Robinhood at a dive bar (Antonio's Nuthouse, Palo Alto) after founder Vlad Tenev pitched him. The idea: get millennials to trade stocks, free, at a time when the dot-com crash and 2008 had killed retail investing.
- His reaction: a group with no interest in saving, and a free business model. He said "I'm in because in almost all cases the crazy outlandish ideas that nobody believes in are the ones that have the greatest returns."
- Uber: he introduced it to about 25 investors and only 3 said yes (about 12%).
- Examples of ideas that sound like they can't work: Wikipedia (no one paid, yet it became the largest encyclopedia), Airbnb (staying in a stranger's home), Robinhood.
- Best startups "induce a market to exist." Uber competed with car ownership, not taxis. Airbnb made people take trips (e.g. 3 weeks in Kyoto) they would not otherwise take.

## What to look for when angel investing: product, customers, engagement

- Jason teaches angel investing (book "Angel", the course Angel University six times a year, and a syndicate with about 6,500 members, the largest in the world).
- He says two things cannot be faked: a product built really well (craftsmanship, "transcendent": Calm, Robinhood, Uber, Tesla, Amazon) and delighted customers. A great team usually builds a good product and iterates to delight users.
- Engagement is the tell: "just a fancy word for how much they use it."
- Snapchat example: far fewer users than rivals, but the top third opened the app every hour, with streaks. Clubhouse was funded on hours of use per month.
- Calm was the syndicate's first deal: $378K at a $5M company, about 6-7% ownership. Now worth about $2B; the syndicate still owns about 5%. He invested after Sam Harris explained meditation and UCLA's mindfulness researchers confirmed it was real.
- Calm grew from $10K/month to millions per month over four years, raising only a $250M round in between. He has seen that capital efficiency only 3-4 times.
- He prefers to be the first, second or third check: "I want to meet them on day zero." He turned down mega-fund offers to write $150M checks, saying that is "like being an atm."

## Venture capital math and whether to raise

- Venture money is "jet fuel": only about 1 in 1,000 US startups gets funded. It makes sense only if you want to build something big and fast.
- Jason's own portfolio: 7 of 10 startups he invests in go to zero.
- Fund math: a $300M fund with 30 names at $10M each needs one or two to return 30x-60x. The goal is about 3x cash on cash in 10 years (about 25% annualized); he says the stock market only doubles money in that period.
- VCs are betting on 30 names and need 2-3 to hit, so they push founders to go 100x, not grow 50% a year. Growing 10% on your own revenue is the "bicycle"; VC money is a rocket that can explode.
- The J-curve: bad news comes first (failures die fast), winners take 4-6 years, and it takes about a decade to know if an investor is good. Early on "you feel like an idiot."
- Bootstrapping on customer revenue requires extreme product-market fit and a good price with a healthy margin. Hardware is nearly impossible this way, so it is mostly software.
- Young VCs can be nervous and get in the way of founders.

## Distribution and growth in the modern era

- Market size was underestimated: first 10-20M broadband homes, then 60M, then billions of phones with an app store and a credit card in them.
- Companies now reach global scale in 2-4 years, not decades. Clubhouse went from private beta to the number one app in Germany and Japan in about six months.
- Social networks follow a pattern: about 20 players ever win a category, and 80% is owned by the top 2-3, but the leaders change (Friendster, MySpace, Facebook, Snapchat).
- Jason argues Instagram should not have sold to Facebook ($500M valuation after a Sequoia round); standalone it would be worth about $500B, and Facebook would have been in decline without Instagram and WhatsApp.
- Silicon Valley's playbook for a decade or two is removing friction. Uber removed tipping and the payment step; Amazon had one-click; Clubhouse is one click to listen and two to speak. Unintended consequences follow: Facebook's easy add-to-group feature ended up outing gay students who had joined a choir group.
- Feeds changed from reverse chronological to ranking by likes and comments, which drove outrage-driven engagement. His fix idea: let users bring their own algorithm.
- Amazon Prime: the business was getting people to subscribe (about $13/month or $120/year), not shipping products. He credits Bezos's "two-day shipping" goal as an audacious target that rallied the team.

## Founders, leadership and the "Olympic" nature of startups

- Jason says building a startup is like an Olympic sport or Navy SEAL effort: "seven days a week, 12 hours a day," with no work-life balance. If you want a normal job, do not join a startup or an ambitious company.
- He compares it to people not blaming Michael Phelps for ice baths. The mismatch is people conflating a regular job with an extreme pursuit. Those who sign up to work for Musk or Bezos should know what they are choosing.
- Leaders set the standard. Elon is "indefatigable"; Bezos debated; Jobs was a design genius. A leader's time ends when they can no longer set the standard, and then they should pass the baton (as Bezos did at 57).
- Founders need a purpose: "when you dread going to work that day and you don't feel like solving that problem anymore, that's the tell." People who start only because they want to make money tend to quit around month 9 or 10.
- Common founder traits: charisma (storytelling that gets people, investors and press on the journey), and often hardship or immigrant parents.
- First-principles/"day one" thinking: pause and ask how you would solve it fresh. Elon asks the basic questions engineers dislike. Balance it with pragmatism, goals and dates so you don't over-engineer and never ship.
- Closing quote: "The number one reason a startup shuts down is not running out of money. The number one reason a startup fails is that the founder gives up."

## Skills, execution and mindset advice

- Ideas get zero credit: "it's all about execution." People saying they need a technical co-founder with no skill of their own ("I have an idea") are not credible.
- His career advice: learn to learn new skills. Replace roughly 400 hours of TV (e.g. Game of Thrones plus Breaking Bad) with learning design, UX, development or sales.
- He gets to about 50% of the knowledge in a field (design, writing, sales, development) so "people can't snow you" when he hires. Knowing enough to call out bad work is the unlock.
- Use deliberate practice: he improved at chess by reviewing losses on chess.com instead of just playing more. Tim Ferriss picked up basketball form by studying him closely for a couple of hours.
- Every course from MIT, Harvard and Stanford is free online. He rejects the idea that poor Americans cannot access it, citing smartphone and internet penetration.
- Competition improves things: chess.com at about $100/year, Elon forcing every automaker to go electric, Michael Jordan's "I took that personally."

## Loyalty, crisis handling and the Tesla story

- Jason says investors have three choices when a portfolio company is under fire: fight for the team, go silent, or throw them under the bus. He is "ride or die" and asks the founder first whether to defend them publicly. He did this for Uber and, in the GameStop episode, for Robinhood.
- His view of the Robinhood crisis: it was a liquidity and collateral demand (clearinghouses asking for billions), not a conspiracy with hedge funds. Robinhood raised about $3.5B in a week or so. He says communication is the top issue: put all bad news out immediately, though legal limits can keep founders quiet.
- With Chamath, a competing investor/CEO angle (SoFi), the fight got heated on the All-In podcast. They resolved it privately by acknowledging love and loyalty, and he judges people by intent ("nuance and intent have been totally lost").
- Tesla story (Elon has told it publicly): around the 2008 financial crisis, Tesla had about two weeks of cash. Elon showed him the unreleased Model S clay model. Jason told him "if you make that car you'll change the god damn world," and sent two $50,000 checks to pay for a reservation. He was reservation number 73 and Elon gave him a Roadster; the checks cleared months later.
- Jason's closing advice on gratitude: "Nobody gets there alone." Write thank-you letters; gratitude amplifies joy.
