## Choosing a metric when you're early: show you know the vanity metrics, then lead with raw growth

- For an early consumer product, Jason says investors want to hear retention, viral coefficient and user personas, but only as proof the founder is competent.
- Viral coefficient: every customer who brings in 1 new customer is a coefficient of 1. Anything above that (his example: 1.5 to 1.7) compounds, because each new cohort brings in more people.
- Once you show you understand those metrics, savvy investors "check, check, check" and move straight to "the meat and potatoes": is it growing or not?
- He says that about ten years earlier, founders were obsessed with building the product. After Facebook, Uber and Airbnb grew into unicorns so quickly, the focus became "can you be a growth founder?"
- Investors assume you can build a great product and set up measurement tools. In 2019 he calls building a product "not a big deal" and says tracking metrics is not a big deal either, though it still separates you from half of founders.
- Investors want to know whether the founders are dogged and resourceful, whether they have customer acquisition channels, and whether they can grow the product.

## Pitching a new social network in its second decade

- Jason says a founder entering social networking should expect investors to be burnt out on the space, to have lost money in it, or to think it is insurmountable to beat Facebook. He does not hold that view himself and thinks it is changing as Facebook runs into trouble.
- Investors will also ask how the network gets monetized. The easy answer is advertising, but that needs "tremendous scale".
- He warns against pitching a free, no-ads product that will make money from photo books. Photo books have been tried many times and tend to be a small opportunity, "low millions of dollars". He says that pitch may signal that you are "kind of delusional".
- He says it is "hard enough to build an at scale social network let alone trying to build a new business model". A new business model is "possible, it's just not probable".
- If you want to monetize in a new way, you need "a really compelling case" that either advertising works or that people will pay.
- He gives a similar example for the family-network space: Life360, whose killer feature is finding family members on a map.

## What to tell investors about acquisition when you don't have a growth channel yet

- Pitch that you have no leaky bucket: people want the product, the target persona engages deeply, and you improve the product 2-3% every fortnight (maybe 5-10% a month).
- If the product doesn't churn, the next question is how to acquire customers, and that is where to focus your energy.
- Real-world options he lists: meetups, profitable content marketing, and a pitch to the local press.
- His script for investors: "we know we don't have a leaky bucket, we have a product people love. Here are the seven experiments we're running this quarter, next quarter we're going to run these seven experiments, and then we're going to take the learning from those 14 experiments, whichever three do the best we're going to triple down on them."
- Be honest that you don't yet know the growth technique. Have a list of the ten things everybody knows work, plus a handful of others to try.
- This shows you understand Lean Startup methodology, that there is no silver bullet, and that you are hard-working and humble. He says investors "love to engage" in that conversation.
- He also calls out personas as useful for customer acquisition and design: a stay-at-home parent, a single parent splitting custody days, and urban versus suburban versus rural families all need different targeting.

## Enterprise AI for franchises: sell to the end user first, not the chain

- The case: a startup selling AI drive-through ordering to restaurant franchises, with two buyers. The chain's COO signs bigger contracts covering multiple regions or a whole country. Individual franchisees with 2-3 stores (or sometimes 25-50) close faster because they have autonomy.
- The caller had closed no chain-level deals yet. Jason's advice is to go after the short sales cycle and the more modest reward first.
- Why:
  - Easier sale, and a chain deal "might be a year" for a startup that doesn't have that time.
  - Feedback quality. Frontline operators are like "soldiers on the front lines" who hold the weapon. They will quickly tell you if the product is too expensive or too hard to use, and they will churn if it sucks. That feedback lets you refine the product.
  - Word of mouth. Franchisees talk to each other at conventions, on message boards and over dinner, so one operator tells another.
- His examples: Slack, Yammer and Asana did not try to win the CTO first. They won a group of developers or salespeople, who signed up on a corporate card at $10-25 per person per month and built consensus inside the company.
- When frontline staff tell the manager the tool is helping them win, the manager gives them what they want.
- He contrasts this with 25 years earlier, when a CTO or CEO would go golfing with a Lotus Notes or Oracle rep, cut a deal, and force the software on everyone, with implementations taking years. Now "they take out their credit card, they try it, if it sucks they unsubscribe".
- The goal in years 1-2 is to make the product so indispensable that when you double the price, customers say "I have no other choice". It should save people time or make them money.

## Raise venture money or stay bootstrapped: the tradeoffs Jason lays out

- The case: a content-marketing marketplace that was profitable from day one. Roughly $25K of monthly recurring revenue, a partner, one full-time-equivalent and freelance writers, with investors interested.
- The model as described to Jason: customers pay a $500/month minimum for blog posts, up to $3,500 on a higher plan. The company pays writers $150-225 per post, for roughly 40% writer cost and 60% margin. Billing is automatic monthly retainers, so there are no invoices to chase.
- Jason's math on raising: pick an 18-month view, divide the raise by 18, and that is your monthly burn. A $450K raise is about $25K a month; $900K is about $50K a month. You work for about nine months, then look up to see how close you are to the next round's milestones. Months 10-12 become 3-4 months on the road raising, or you cut toward profitability if the market or the business turns.
- The cost: you add a third constituent. Today you manage your team and your customers; after raising, you also manage investors, which he estimates at about 20% of your time.
- Fast track warning: once you are on it, "it's very hard to get off". Investors want to return 25x, 50x, 100x their money. They do not want slow or methodical growth. They are "looking for their Uber, their Airbnb, their Google, their Facebook". If you take the money, you have to be willing to triple or quadruple revenue year over year for a couple of years until you reach $10M.
- His case for going for it: investors knocking is a signal the business could scale, like being recruited to the NBA (or at least the G League) instead of playing at the YMCA. "Nobody says at the end of their life 'I'm glad I stayed at the YMCA'."
- He compares the human-heavy model to early Uber and Airbnb, which many investors passed on because of drivers and hosts. The people-intensive work was arduous, but the reward was a billion-dollar business.
- The alternative: a lifestyle business throwing off $500K a year is a great business, and he would not judge anyone for choosing it. The worst case for the venture route is a blowup in two years, then you build the next lifestyle business.
- He tells the founder not to be precious about the business: he is young and will have more ideas.

## Pricing tiers: use a decoy "whale" plan

- Jason liked a three-tier layout ("easy peasy" at $500, "fast and furious" at $3,500) and recommends adding a $10,000 enterprise tier.
- Reasoning: almost nobody takes the low tier, and people get steered to the middle. The top tier is a "whale" that may land a customer, and even if not, it makes the $3,500 plan look reasonable ("I don't need the ten, but I'll go 35").
- He adds that one $10K customer at 50% profit could pay for a freelance developer, so one whale could fund the hire the founder was worried about.
- He says the founder was effectively running business model experimentation, with a few customers on each tier.
- On the website: make it look great and tight, because investors and customers can quickly pass on a company that looks corny or not well thought out.

## Relentless focus: scale what works before adding new products

- Jason's stated 2018 lesson: "focus and simplicity and what you don't do is as important as what you do do."
- His restaurant metaphor: if you make the best burger in town, don't start on lasagna. Make the burger more consistent, find more customers and more locations, and sell more burgers.
- At Inside.com he says they perfected the top 10 newsletter format. The plan: go from 30 newsletters to 60 in the next year, and triple from three world-class sales executives with two or three SDRs.
- At Launch he applies the same logic: go from two or three accelerator classes a year to seven, from 20 graduates to 50, and grow the podcast from two advertisers to three and from 100-150K downloads/views per episode to 300-500K.
- Founder types: 0-to-1 people are idea people; 1-to-a-million people are refiners and operators. ADD/ADHD-style founders find it boring to make the "ten thousandth" hamburger, "but that's where all the profit is."
- After product-market fit, "the key to success is scaling and it's a relentless focus". People often get something right, then stop seeing how far it can go.
- He said that even if a podcast or newsletter adjacency looks perfect, the right move is to stay focused until you get there: if 30 newsletters bring in $1M of revenue, 300 should be $10M or more.
- To get world-class investors' attention, you need to scale and set aggressive targets and try to hit them. He points to Zuckerberg's focus on getting users on the platform and keeping them there as his best quality, but warns not to let focus override basic human decency.
