## Whether to raise money at all

- Jason's co-hosts and he open by saying most companies should not raise venture money. Most businesses are self-funded "lifestyle" businesses, and that is fine.
- The test is whether the company is venture-scale. Example given: a $5M-revenue business at 20% margin ($1M/year) growing 10% a year reaches maybe $12-15M revenue in 10 years. That is a $20-30M company, which would return a VC about 3x. VCs want 20, 30, 40 or 50x; angels want 100x.
- Jason's definition of venture-scale in 2019: roughly $100M in revenue with strong margins (30-50% gross margin, software often 80-90%), reached in about 5 to 7 years. Funds run about 10 years, so exits are expected in 5 to 8 years, 10 to 12 at the outside.
- He warns against founder projections like $300K in 2019 growing to $5M in 2024. That is 10x and still not what VCs need.

## Friends and family rounds

- Raising from friends and family can work (examples he cites: Zuckerberg's parents, Gates's parents), but founders must be blunt that the money will probably be lost. Jason says to tell them they will most likely lose it, because he and co-hosts see roughly 7 or 8 of 10 early investments lose everything.
- He argues founders should still ask. If you skip them and build a giant company, they will be angry you never offered the chance.
- Paper everything. Use a SAFE (simple agreement for future equity): the money later converts to shares when a priced round happens. A convertible note is similar but is debt with interest and can run 15-20 pages, too heavy for a first-time founder or a relative to read.
- Have legal documents ready before approaching angels or an accelerator (Launch, Techstars, Y Combinator). Messy paperwork gives investors an easy reason to pass.

## What diligence catches (and how to avoid it)

Items checked: cap table, bank statements, customer references, org structure.
- Founders with no vesting on their own shares.
- Early investors undocumented: who, when, note or SAFE, valuation cap, interest.
- Personal expenses mixed into company accounts (e.g., rent on an apartment used as an office with no allocation).
- A P&L with no cost of goods sold, or expenses swinging from $100K one month to $3K for the next three, or payroll booked as non-payroll expense.
- A $100K bank deposit with no paperwork is a red flag, since the person could later claim 20% equity while the founder says it was a loan.
- Jason's rule: if you are going to do something, do it properly. Learn the accounting software and spend an hour or two in it.

## Angels vs VCs vs seed funds vs syndicates

- By size: under about $1M tends to be angels; above $1M is institutional capital.
- Angels decide faster (no team), but usually have full-time jobs and less time to help. VCs are usually more helpful.
- Minimum check sizes exist to keep the cap table manageable and avoid chasing many signatures later. Common minimums are $25K to $50K, higher for bigger rounds.
- Seed funds ($10M-$100M) usually have 2 to 3 partners and can move faster, but in practice diligence and decision time look much like VCs. Hunter and Satya at Homebrew are cited as a fast two-partner example.
- Syndicate (Jason's, run with Ashley): 250 accredited investors, median about 80 participating per deal, checks roughly $100K to $2.4M. The founder gets one cap table line and needs one signature, from the syndicate lead. Founders send a deal memo with a webinar link; interested investors can contact the founder directly. After closing, the founder should email all members a welcome note and ask for help (hiring, intros).
- Jason's surprise: syndicate members who put in $25K or $50K sometimes lead the next round. His first syndicate deal was Calm.com about five years earlier at a $5M valuation, now reported at a $1B valuation. He tells accredited investors a small syndicate check can be followed by add-ons of "a zero or two" later.

## Choosing target investors

- Read the portfolio. Check for competitors, and flip it: if they funded you first and then a rival asked to invest, would you be comfortable? If not, exclude them.
- Related-category investors are good targets (e.g., fitness investments for a meditation app).
- You will be partnered for years, so you should like the person.

## Accelerators

- Value: fundraising help and a peer network. By week 12 founders become close friends and go through the founder rollercoaster together.
- Skeptical cases: serial founders may not need one. Doing three or four accelerators can signal you could not raise a real round. He compares it to a 40-year-old with four graduate degrees.
- Check who runs it, their track record and their alumni.
- To evaluate a program, ask alumni three or four different ways what they got and whether they would do it again, to get past confirmation bias. He uses the owner who insists their Fisker beats a Tesla as the analogy.
- Jason's claim: accelerator companies get about two meetings out of every three intros pursued, because of intro quality. Without that, expect 5-10% intro-to-meeting conversion. If you convert under 10%, you need better intros.

## Metrics VCs look at

- Revenue growth first. Typical target is 10-20% month over month, nearer 20% being strong. Example: $10K in January, $12K in February, then 20% on $12K ($2,400) for $14.4K in March.
- Marketplaces: both sides, e.g. Airbnb adding about 20 hosts a month, plus how demand was acquired. Liquidity matters.
- Consumer apps: DAU, retention, cohort analysis (January signups still using the product in March).
- SaaS: churn and negative churn. Example of 20% monthly churn: you must replace the base roughly every 5 months. Negative churn means existing customers spend more (e.g., a sales tool team of 10 adds a team of 5).
- Credit to Eric Ries (making startups a science) and SaaStr's Jason Lemkin (SaaS metrics as a science).
- Vanity metrics he does not value: social followers, blog readers, press (Forbes lists), site traffic, unless tied to revenue.
- Do not misrepresent. Putting a former employer's logo (such as Apple) on the team slide when you were retail staff, or calling TEDx Albuquerque "TED", loses credibility. It is "not fraud but twisting the truth," and a slippery slope.
- Pre-revenue companies (deep tech, AI) are judged on the founder: domain expertise, repeat founders help, big-name investors help, and the ability to explain it at a fifth-grade reading level.
- Pre-revenue consumer benchmarks as Jason sees them in 2019: about 10M monthly active users for a Series A; 10-20% of that for a seed round, i.e. about 1M MAU or roughly 100K daily users.

## Venture capital reality

- Venture money is "jet fuel," not coal. Taking it means you are signing up for unnatural, inorganic growth for about 10 years.
- Ashley's cautions he agrees with: VCs pick their target ownership percentage (10-20%) and that determines valuation, and they may eventually push to replace the CEO.
- Jason: they give you millions to build, not to earn interest. He recalls once putting $10M+ into bonds and earning about $600K in interest, which covered about a third of monthly expenses, and calls that unusual.
- Math to know before negotiating: if investors take 20% and you want a $10-20M valuation, you can quickly work out the raise ($2-4M). Terms should be standard.
- Geography: this is Silicon Valley advice; he guesses valuations outside run roughly one-half to two-thirds, China possibly higher with personal guarantees, Europe about half.

## Running the process

- Meetings: say yes selectively. They can distract you if you are not raising, and a VC may only be gathering data on your category (e.g., looking at Skimm and Axios when they email Inside). Sharing metrics is a choice. Buffer is an example of a company that publishes everything, but Jason generally protects metrics because a VC may fund your competitor. He answers with public information, says some things are the "secret sauce," or gives vague counter-answers. He also ignores requests from reporters or junior analysts, and sometimes uses a copy-paste reply such as "I don't speak on behalf of my companies."
- Typical funnel: about 150 qualified targets (not a Crunchbase dump), about 100-125 intros, about 75 meetings, about 10 diligence processes, and typically 3 investors in the round. That is roughly 1-2% of targets. Raising is a full-time job; delegate the day-to-day.
- Start with top-tier firms (Silicon Valley or leading hubs) and aim for the top 10%. Lower tiers can be less professional and overpromise.
- Raise enough for at least 18 months so you do not have to raise again, and have plan B and plan C.
