## Why Jason sees the post-ZIRP unicorn backlog as a portfolio-management problem

- Jason frames the startup market as three eras: peak ZIRP ("money's cheap, go for the growth"), the post-ZIRP collapse, and the AI era. He says the "game on the field" changes roughly every 3 years, so founders get conflicting advice at once.
- He points to a chart showing roughly half of unicorns growing 0-20% or declining. He calls this "that backlog": companies valued on "performance yet to come" that did not deliver.
- On the Rule of 40 (growth plus profit margin), he notes few unicorns clear it. He cites Palantir's CEO talking about a "rule of 80".
- He uses Uber as the example of the shift. Under Travis, it was growth at any cost ("we can lose $3 per ride. Who cares?"). Later it posted large profits and announced a $20 billion buyback, about 12% of the company.
- He notes the same shift in public markets, where capital allocation is now questioned (for example, Apple's $700 billion in buybacks).

## Seed investors: the 1-2-3 portfolio buckets and "pods"

- Jason built an investing system for very large portfolios, with the basic numbers: roughly 50-70% of seed companies fail, and only a small share become huge.
- At his fund, 11 investment professionals plus analysts, researchers and associates (young people hired out of school and trained) are organized into "pods" of about 30 companies.
- The pods meet monthly. About 10 of the 30 show up, so founders solve each other's problems.
- Companies are put into three buckets:
  1. **Clear breakouts** (examples he gives: Robinhood, Superhuman). They need little help. Senior people manage the board relationships, and the team "pops champagne."
  2. **Figuring it out.** They have traction, customers and investor interest. These are the ones the pods help most, for example with churn, product-market fit or wrong pricing.
  3. **Struggling.** They cannot get customers, are not raising, team size is flat, and runway is under 12 months. Jason calls this "hospice care": help the founder decide whether to shut down, sell, pivot or start over, and invite them back to the accelerator with their next idea.
- The three signals he looks for in a company: getting customers, investor interest, and traction.
- Hard conversations with founders about wrapping up should start with a plan question: "what's your plan?"

## Selling secondaries: the evolution of Jason's philosophy

- Jason describes four chapters in his own thinking:
  1. Never sell a share.
  2. Didn't sell, and regretted it after offers were declined and companies later went to zero or lost value.
  3. Sold roughly 10-20% in secondary transactions. He never felt like an idiot for selling (for example, some Uber at $30 a share, which he used to buy a house that doubled in value, while Uber later traded around $90).
  4. Proactively pushes founders to sell. When a company he invested in at roughly $10-15 million reached about $500 million, he asked the founder about a secondary plan.
- Chapter 4 tactic: he proposed a $20 million secondary in an oversubscribed round, with the founder selling roughly $5-7 million of stock (1-2% of shares) and the fund selling about 10% of its holding pro rata.
- The structure he describes is an auction-style tender: everyone says how much they want to sell, and everyone gets their share. A 10% holder would get about $2 million of a $20 million sale, and a 40% holder about $8 million.
- He says he wishes he had sold 20-40% in some past cases, especially around 2021. He tells founders to take money off the table after years of work.
- He regrets that he wasn't sold enough in some cases, and he regards educating founder-partners about secondaries as part of his role.
- He agrees that fund managers should manage end-of-life portfolios through partial secondary sales or by helping portfolio companies reach M&A.

## The state of M&A, IPOs and exit timing

- Jason thinks a big part of the logjam was the four-year freeze on small and mid-size acquisitions (roughly $50 million to $500 million). He says companies like Meta and Amazon had small acquisitions scrutinized or blocked.
- His example is that the freeze affected even small deals, not just high-end ones such as Figma.
- He shows a chart of startups buying startups: 46% of VC-backed M&A deals had a VC-backed buyer on a full-year extrapolation. He reads this as consolidation and a way to wrap up old funds.
- On IPO thresholds, the number he hears is that a company needs roughly a billion in revenue growing 20-30% a year, though he adds he is not sure it is true. He also mentions half a billion to a billion in revenue as another version of the bar. Figma, which he cites, was around $700 million growing 45-46%.
- He notes 70% of unicorns may not actually be unicorns, and are growing under 50%.

## Advice for a stuck, slow-growing, profitable-ish company

- Jason lays out the options and trade-offs for a company with revenue but low growth:
  - Sell to private equity. EV-to-revenue multiples are around 2-4x for classic software.
  - Reposition as an AI company if the company has the balance sheet and wherewithal.
  - Run it for profit, possibly with debt.
- On the debt idea, he argues a company doing about $100-200 million in revenue with 10-20% growth and profitability "might be a great company to borrow 20, 30, 40 million at 10% and run the business." He also says: sell to PE later, maybe after doubling the business's value.
- He raises the conflict of interest in this decision: venture investors targeting 100x have to sign off on a loan sitting ahead of their preference. His summary of the question to ask them: "do you want zero dollars or do you want 50 cents on the dollar?"
- He notes a PE sale can be good for founders who own 40-60% of the cap table, and for employees.
- On shutting down a company, he frames it as an experiment ("failed experiment") and asks whether the founder has "another experiment you want to try with a clean cap table." He notes that cap tables get convoluted when a company struggles.
- He points out a company with $30 million of capital in it will probably not return that on a small exit, while one with $2 million could.

## Capital efficiency, AI-first teams and recruiting

- Jason's observation from his accelerator: founders come with little or no capital raised (or a $125,000 raise) and are quickly at a $500,000 a year run rate. He says AI tools make startups cheaper to run and sell.
- He argues that capital-constrained teams look for "the cheapest, fastest, best way to do something," which is increasingly AI.
- What he sees in startups: people who are not AI-first are being cut and replaced with AI-first people. An AI-first executive who knows Zapier and large language models has leverage of 2-4x the person sitting next to them.
- He also points to using international employees at a fraction of the cost.
- His example product is Athena, an AI/virtual personal assistant service he invests in.
- Key quote on hiring: "Recruiting people is painful. You wind up hiring the wrong person half the time. It takes three to six months." His conclusion is that talented people who can automate a job function beat adding more headcount: "I don't need to solve every problem by adding a body."
- He compares this to when startups stopped having to stand up their own servers: abstracting a function away rather than staffing it.
