## Down markets are the best time to invest, and "tourists" leave

- Jason says "fortunes are made in the down market, they're collected in the up market." He calls the best time to invest a down market.
- In a downturn fewer people start companies and fewer people invest in them. That gives time to meet companies and make "a really thoughtful investment, not out of fear or FOMO, but out of really doing your research."
- The founders and investors who stay active in downturns tend to be the most resilient. He calls them "the crocodiles, the sharks, the cockroaches, the people who don't die."
- He describes the 2016-2022 boom as "tourist season in Silicon Valley and startup land for five, six years." People who are not committed or full-time go home when the market turns, and he says he has "seen this movie before."
- He first scouted for Sequoia around 2009, at the tail end of the Great Recession. He says he lived through two intense recessions as a professional.
- Recessions make everyone focus on what matters. He calls the 2022-era correction "a great flush": weak companies and weak projects get flushed out and the strong ones remain.
- He planned to invest more in startups over the next year or two than in the previous two years, after pausing on overpriced deals.

## Valuations reset: what overheated looked like and where prices went

- He blames the overheated market on stimulus, low rates and cash piling up during COVID: PPP, extra unemployment and stimulus checks, with nowhere to spend it. Then came inflation, Ukraine and China lockdowns.
- Late-stage valuations were cut roughly in half. A billion-dollar valuation became about 500 million. SaaS multiples of 100x to 200x revenue fell to something like 30x to 50x revenue.
- In private markets, companies had been raising at 25 to 50 million valuations with no launched product. He calls that "over."
- A standard seed round used to be around 5 million, then went to 15 to 20 million, and he expected it to come back down to 6 to 12 million.
- He sat out deals he couldn't justify, like paying 50 million for a startup that hadn't launched, or 25 million for one with 500K in revenue.
- His rule: "Entry price does matter." Investors were crediting companies "for work not done" by valuing them as if they would execute perfectly for five years.
- He cited the Peloton example. At roughly $30,000 per subscriber of valuation, you had to bet on 10x more subscribers to make it back.
- He says everyone got entitled: employees, VCs, public-market investors, crypto investors and founders. "Everybody thought this was easy. It's never been easy."
- Hedge funds and public-market investors dipping into private markets, and investors who skipped diligence and copied what Sequoia or others did, were part of the overheating.

## Sell on the way up and lock in downside protection

- Jason says he is always selling on the way up as an angel. If he invested at 5 or 10 million and a secondary appears at 250 million, 500 million or a billion, he takes it or considers it deeply.
- His example schedule: sell 10% at 250 million, 10% at 500 million and 10% at a billion. He accepts that he may look stupid if it goes to 10 billion, because the proceeds get redeployed into more startups that could also go up.
- On his math: if you sold 20% of an investment before an asset fell 90%, you would not be down 90%. "You'd have that 20% as a floor." He says "a little downside protection never hurt anybody."
- He says many investors were locked up in Coinbase, Peloton and similar positions and "never realized it." You also have to be able to capitalize on a win, not just make it.
- Selling part of a position while keeping some ownership is a theme across his alternatives too. For example, he could sell half of his Tesla Roadster through Rally Road for 125K against a 150K cost and still own half.

## Portfolio construction: the Goldilocks window and milestone-based follow-ons

- Jason invests in a "Goldilocks window, not too cold, not too hot."
  - Too cold: an idea, passion and a white paper.
  - Too hot: $20 million in revenue at a $200 million valuation, selling 1% of the company with only a quarter percent available to him. He can't build a position.
  - Just right: product launched, about 250K in annual revenue (for example 20K to 30K last month, times 12) at a reasonable valuation multiple, buying about 5%.
- He starts at roughly 3% to 6% ownership and aims to build a position of about 15%. For an exceptional founder he may go straight to 10%.
- Follow-on plan: if revenue triples and valuation triples, go to 10% ownership. If it doubles again, go to 15%.
- He has learned to love the milestone-based process in Silicon Valley: "You don't need to buy all the shares in the seed round. You just need to be a great partner to the founder."
- He discusses follow-on rights with founders up front: pro rata, and super pro rata if the company is growing. If it goes sideways he takes his pro rata at most. If it is going poorly, he does not do bridges.
- He tells founders it is their job to keep the company growing if they want more money from him.
- Deal sizing at his syndicate: the average investor puts in about $7,000, with roughly 100 to 150 investors per deal. He likes lots of small bets and doubling or tripling down where he has conviction.
- He has invested in well over 300 companies. His firm invests about $100 million a year in early-stage startups, and he runs a 21-person company with podcasts, conferences and an investment team.

## Process over prediction: write it down and review it

- His core advice: "Process, have a process, write it down." You can't predict the market or the outcome, so you focus on what you control.
- Track the funnel: how many companies apply, how many he meets, how many second meetings, how many follow-ups, how many bets, how many double-downs, how many times he can liquidate.
- Write down why you said yes and why you said no, then go back and review. He recommends the book "Superforecasting," and compares it to poker players who review their decisions (outs, expected value).
- Review passes, too. His own example is passing on Indiegogo when he was only writing 25K checks and had no team, and passing on MasterClass at the first round because of valuation and because he discounted the founder's access to Dustin Hoffman. He says the lesson was that the access was itself a signal of ability.
- He spent two hours on two consecutive days with his investment team refining process: how many companies to meet, what to ask, when to say no versus "not yet," and how many days to wait before following up if a founder doesn't book a meeting. He says they debated day three through day six and concluded any would work.
- He follows up with almost every company he passes on to look for signals that he was wrong.
- "If you want to get good at something, you better be a [maniac], because you're competing against people who are [maniacs] like me."
- Book recommendations tied to this: "Thinking in Bets" (Annie Duke) and "The Checklist Manifesto" (checklists for discipline and process).

## How to spot a breakout company

- He says you probably can't pick the unicorn at the earliest stage, and warns about survivorship and confirmation bias afterward. So make 20 to 30 bets, monitor them and watch for breakout signals.
- Signals he looks for: over 10% month-over-month growth (doubling about every 7.2 months), revenue tripling year over year, heavy hiring. Those signals are "undeniable," and he double-clicks to find out why.
- A company is "about three things": the team that builds the product, the product, and the customers who consume it. Get that flywheel going and it will "work out fine in all likelihood."
- Things that distract founders: total addressable market, competitors, culture. He focuses on the three core signals instead.
- Product velocity check: a software company should push a release about every two weeks. During diligence he asks, "When was the last update? Can you share the release notes?"
- Customers: you have to "delight" them, which takes a great product, which takes a good team.
- Red flag: founders who are better at raising money than building products, who convince investors to give them money at a higher rate than they convince customers to. He says it is not insurmountable but a major flag.
- He passed on crypto deals with no product because "I'm a product person." For crypto he only sees a team and a white paper, and cannot talk to customers.

## Serious founders: what he wants, and what he doesn't

- He doesn't like "pre-seed" rounds where founders ask for 250K to quit their job, hire a dev shop and go to conferences or crypto parties. He sees it as entitlement.
- Preferred path: do consulting three days a week and work on the side project four days, work seven days a week, get the product launched or to 10K a month in revenue, then raise a proper seed round. That avoids dilution and shows commitment.
- Founders who skip steps, and investors who skipped diligence and customer calls, were both part of the problem.
- He says a downturn sorts founders. "If you're not serious about it, this isn't like a hobby, it's a vocation."
- The generation that found accelerator admission (Techstars, Y Combinator) the hardest step, with seed, Series A and Series B coming easily, now has to catch up. They have the valuation of a later stage but the traction of an earlier one, and need to "fill in all of that credit you got in advance."
- Equity crowdfunding: Inside.com raised about 700K from its users on Republic. He likes the discipline of having hundreds of $100 investors, because it makes him feel the responsibility more than a VC writing a check that is 1% of a big fund.

## Hiring in a downturn

- In the boom, mediocre people held 10 offers and CEOs had to negotiate pay packages that didn't make sense.
- In a down market: "You're going to have a great person with two and a mediocre person with none." You can say the package doesn't make sense and offer one that does.
- After the dot-com bust and the Great Recession, people said "I have nothing else going on, sure, I'll work at your startup," and he says "great magic happens" when that occurs.
- Talent who can't get through hiring freezes at IBM, GE or Google will "take a flyer on a startup."

## The syndicate, the podcast and where he sees opportunity

- His syndicate (thesyndicate.com) was the first on AngelList. It has done over 250 SPVs, run with SureFund management. Roughly 11,000 to 12,000 syndicate members.
- First deal: Calm.com, invested 376,000 at a 4 to 5 million valuation. The company is worth over 2 billion, a nine-figure position for them, and they have sold some shares for returns while keeping the majority.
- Writing a deal memo for thousands of accredited investors makes him sharper. He compares it to playing in the NBA finals: every turnover is remembered, so he is more careful than he would be "bawling in the street."
- Podcasts as a deal-flow and learning tool: over 1,300 episodes of This Week in Startups and about 80 of All-In. He has portfolio founders on as guests, which helps them and brings in more deal flow. He compares it to "getting an MBA every year."
- He says podcasting isn't required for every investor. Writing, conferences or one-on-one research can also work, whichever the person is best at.
- Sectors he likes: B2B SaaS (subscription pricing that is affordable and high-value), industrial companies (he cites Blockable, which builds homes and factories), and consumer subscriptions that are singularly focused. His subscription bets: Calm (meditation), FitBot (workouts), Steezy (dance), Musician (popular music), ToneBase (classical music) and Fluent Forever (languages).
- He expects the next big wave of companies to be business-to-business and industrial, citing Stripe and Flexport as likely next big IPOs, rather than consumer-facing like Google, Facebook, Uber, Airbnb and Tesla.
- He remains a large holder of Uber and calls it undervalued, pointing to network effects favoring the winner over Lyft.

## Alternative assets: what he owns and how he thinks about them

- Focus: startups are his main asset class ("the greatest asset class available"). Elsewhere he makes many small bets to learn and goes deeper only where he has an edge.
- Crypto: he bought Bitcoin at about $100 and did well, but is not active. He stays "crypto curious" and has a handful of investments. He passed on token deals like Filecoin because there was no product to evaluate.
- Cars: he owns the 16th Tesla Roadster (a signature series, among the first 100) and the first Model S ever made (signature number one). He was offered 250K for the Roadster on Rally Road after paying 150K. He thinks both will be worth low millions in 10 years. He might keep the Model S until he is 70.
- Art: he owns a fractional piece of a Basquiat via Masterworks. He says he doesn't have an edge buying a whole painting and would be too concentrated in one investment. Platforms he uses and thinks curate well: Masterworks, Republic, SeedInvest and Rally Road, plus others he named like Rcrowd.
- Real estate: three properties (primary home, Tahoe ski house, one in the Bay Area), bought for use and as a hedge, not speculation. He tried Airbnb on a previous home and sold the house after a party caused $5-6K in damage and neighbor complaints.
- He likes fractional ownership of homes (for example, Picasso) and shared planes among friends, and notes the hard parts are cleaning, maintenance and scheduling.
- Yield: he doesn't need distributions from alternatives because he has lumpy angel exits plus steady media revenue from This Week in Startups.
