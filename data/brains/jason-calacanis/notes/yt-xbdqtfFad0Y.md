## Early media career and the zine playbook

- Jason grew up in Brooklyn. His dad owned a bar and lost it in the 1987 market crash. He went to Fordham at night and fixed Laser Printers during the day.
- On the subway he became obsessed with magazines and asked how people got on the cover, what an editor-in-chief and a publisher do.
- He started two zines at 22 and 24. The first was Cyber Surfer (five issues, about online services before the web browser). The second was Silicon Alley Reporter, which began as a 16-page photocopied newsletter.
- Silicon Alley Reporter grew to a 300-page glossy mailed to 100,000 people, with $12M a year in revenue and 100 employees. He built it "all on my credit cards."
- He was on the cover of the New York Times, appeared on Charlie Rose and got a New Yorker feature.
- He ran an early event called Ready Set Pitch in 1996. Seven companies pitched Microsoft and AOL at Josh Harris's loft (sudo.com, an early online radio network).
- He had a choice between joining Fred Wilson and Jerry Colonna's Acme Ventures (he read business plans for them, including Beverly Hills Internet, which became GeoCities) and keeping his magazine. He chose the magazine.
- After the .com bust he sold the company to Dow Jones for two years of salary.

## Learning skills fast and using media as a power lever

- He taught himself PageMaker, writing and photography.
- Principle: "a big part of being an entrepreneur is being fearless about adding skills." Break down what skills a product takes, such as writing, selling ads, taking pictures, layout and distribution.
- You can get to 60-70% proficiency in almost any discipline by putting your mind to it. That is enough to figure out who has the 80-90% you can hire.
- Media made him powerful quickly. He went from outsider to having Harvard and HBS founders lobbying to get into his magazine.
- He created the Silicon Alley 100 ranking deliberately. He put Esther Dyson at #1 over DoubleClick (the company with the most funding), justifying it because she "was here before them and she set the stage." The point was to provoke debate.
- His takeaway: lists are meaningless and exist to tweak people. When founders ask for help getting on "30 under 30" lists, his answer is "it's meaningless, get back to work."
- Media-plus-investing is now a common playbook, and he describes himself as an entrepreneur who learned to be a journalist and then started angel investing.

## Weblogs Inc and the Engadget exit

- After the bust he watched his former employees make about $10K a month each from blogs (Jenny Jordan on Boing Boing, Rafat Ali on paidContent). That made him realize blogs were a business.
- Around 2003 he started Weblogs Inc to build business-to-business blogs, with the idea of doing "like 100 of these" across niches. He used subdomains (wifi.weblogsinc.com, apple.weblogsinc.com), influenced by About.com.
- Nick Denton attacked the idea publicly, saying Jason would ruin blogs by commercializing them.
- To compete with Gawker he tried to hire talent. A friend told him he had targeted the wrong blog, because most of Gawker's money came from Gizmodo.
- He took Peter Rojas out to dinner and offered double pay, a MacBook Air and equity. Rojas had been promised equity at Gawker and never got it. Together they built Engadget, which grew to about 10x the size of Gizmodo.
- Mark Cuban was his only investor. He sold to AOL (Ted Leonsis and Jim Bankoff) about 18 months after starting, for roughly $30M, in 2006. Rojas, Jason and his partner each made millions.
- Bankoff later took the playbook and built Vox Media and The Verge.
- It was his first big win and his "second act" after Silicon Alley Reporter.

## Podcasting: This Week in Startups

- Podcasting took off in 2007-2008 once RSS enclosures could be downloaded into iTunes. He was in that RSS crowd (Dave Winer, Ev Williams) and recorded his famous question to Steve Jobs about open podcast RSS feeds.
- He started with "Calacanis Cast" for about 30 episodes, then renamed it This Week in Startups after appearing on Leo Laporte's This Week in Tech.
- First guest was Brian Alvey (2010). It was weekly, then twice, then three times, then four days a week. His rule to his team: sell out the ads three to six months ahead and he'll add an episode. He eventually reached six days a week and hired Molly Wood in January 2023.
- Early setup: Skype calls plus a roughly $20,000 TriCaster video switcher.
- He did it for personal enjoyment first. He grew up on Charlie Rose and Howard Stern and thought daily radio was the most fun job possible.
- The show feeds his business. You can buy ads on TWiST but not on All-In. People meet him for investing and start with talk about the podcast.

## How the podcast changed his fundraising and deal flow

- He raised his fourth fund (a 506(c) public raise). He mentioned it on All-In and TWiST, did a couple of webinars, and got about $44M in interest.
- After five hours of webinars, with no road show and no pitching existing LPs, he had $40M in commits. The last fund was $44M.
- Deal flow has shifted from fighting to get into deals to oversubscribed deals fighting to get him in "because they perceive some value in it." He cites that as the real difference once a podcast has a few years behind it.
- Two founders offered him an allocation in an oversubscribed round if he could get them two All-In Summit tickets. He gave up two of his 15 tickets.
- His advice on consistency: do 100 episodes and it becomes "second nature," like basketball or poker.
- He sees the platform as one of the great paradoxes of his career. A non-commercial, shared-with-three-other-people project is among his biggest successes.

## Running All-In: format, moderation and the besties' rules

- All-In was never supposed to get this big. It reached roughly #26-27 on the overall charts. His selfie requests in San Francisco went from a few to 10-20 a day.
- The four besties agreed on rules of the road: just do the podcast every week, no Summits, meetups or brand extensions early on. Aim for 50 episodes a year and 100 every two years, with a high-quality show.
- He bases the format on The McLaughlin Group: heated but friendly argument among smart people. "Just enough heat makes it interesting"; too much takes away from the experience.
- His role as moderator: "let's play like the Warriors, let's pass the ball." He gets each bestie their touches, built a science corner for Friedberg, who zoned out during politics, and let Sacks have politics.
- His techniques: a finger twirl "wrap it up" signal, passing to another person, asking questions to clarify positions for the audience, and moving between altitudes (explaining acronyms, then going into the weeds).
- He lets disagreement play out. He and David Sacks disagree on a lot but are close friends, and their families vacation together. His message is that people can disagree and still be friends and make progress.
- On expertise: the group claims expertise in capital allocation, startup formation, technology and markets (over 100 combined years). On politics and social issues they are like much of the audience, and everyone should discuss hard issues.
- He cut his X replies to followed accounts only for a while after brigading from MAGA and crypto fans who misread his clarifying questions.
- Lesson: he has caught "lightning in a bottle" about half a dozen times, but 20 other projects didn't hit.

## Tech and the press, and platform responsibility

- Early on, tech press was collegial because tech was niche. Now coverage is cynical, and one company's mistake gets 100 stories while 99 good companies get none.
- He argues there's a long-standing grudge because tech gutted media's business. AOL, Google and Facebook took ads, eBay and Craigslist took classifieds, and Apple intermediated subscriptions.
- He thinks podcasts and Substack are a rebalancing, with podcasts giving more nuanced, accurate coverage than many traditional outlets.
- He defended Away founder Steph Korey. He sees the "work hard at Christmas" message as a normal CEO rally cry that was unfairly spun, and thinks anonymous sourcing is overused.
- On Palmer Luckey: he said it was an unforced error for a CEO to fund anonymous extreme trolling, with collateral damage to employees and families. Several game studios dropped Oculus development. He later hosted Luckey on the All-In stage, asked why he was fired, and apologized for the harshness.
- On platforming harmful people, he argues it's a mistake to give a megaphone to someone going through a manic episode (Kanye West). At a large audience scale, even 0.001% of listeners is thousands of people.

## Personal operating principles

- Stay normal. In Silicon Valley some people get rich or famous "and they stop acting normal."
- Be fearless about adding skills, and don't be afraid to be an outsider (night school, no Stanford).
- Embrace being public and extroverted. He scores 96-100 extroverted on Myers-Briggs and enjoys the micro-celebrity.
- Combine roles. His edge comes from being an entrepreneur, a journalist and an investor at once.
- His goal now: squeeze everything out of "this amazing orange that fell in my lap."
