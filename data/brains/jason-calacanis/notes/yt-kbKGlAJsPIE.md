## Jason's path: from Brooklyn printer repair to media, then angel investing

- Jason's dad lost his bar to the feds over unpaid taxes after the 1987 crash, about six weeks before Jason started college. He told Jason to take care of his mom and said he might go to jail.
- Jason worked days fixing laser printers and took 16 credits a semester at Brooklyn College, six to nine PM four nights a week. He called himself an underperformer in academics.
- His early hustles were cracking and copying software to sell for $10, and selling hot VHS tapes.
- He published zines, starting with Cyber Surfer, about dial-up services and CD-ROMs.
- He then built Silicon Alley Reporter and Digital Coast Reporter in LA: two magazines, two conferences and two email newsletters.
- He grew that business to $10 million in revenue "off my credit cards" with 75 to 100 staff at age 27. He taught himself ad sales and magazine operations.
- Fred Wilson and Jerry Colonna at Flatiron paid him $1,000 plus sushi to read business plans. He wrote about GeoCities and told them to invest. Flatiron made a fortune on it, and he chose to keep the magazine over a VC career.
- Weblogs Inc was sold to AOL for $30 million. Mark Cuban put in $300K for roughly 10%.
- When the money landed, Jason cried. He said his family would never have to worry about money again, because he had spent his life worried about it after his dad lost the business.

## How All In started and why it works

**Origin**
- Chamath and Jason knew each other from the AOL era. Jason kept inviting Chamath onto his podcast. Chamath said yes only after he left Facebook.
- They played cards together, traded notes, and became close. Chamath later called after an interview and asked to do a podcast with just the two of them.
- Jason suggested the name "All In", a poker name, and they added Sacks and Friedberg. They had a poker game that had been referenced on air for years.
- Jason expected it to last 10 to 20 episodes. The pandemic made people want information and perspective, and it took off.

**His read on why it works**
- Real friendship and rapport around a poker table, and the willingness to disagree on air.
- Sacks and Jason fight on the show, and Jason says the fights are real and unscripted. He says the world wants the hosts to be enemies, and he likes the "best of enemies" dynamic of Gore Vidal and William F. Buckley.
- Jason says he deliberately cast characters. He called Friedberg the "Sultan of Science" and gave Sacks his red meat in politics. He plays the point guard who shapes the conversation.
- He studied the McLaughlin Group to learn moderation and interjection. He later realized Sacks is a relentless debater, so he now works to keep it a conversation rather than a debate.
- He plays audience proxy: "Can you unpack that?" He asks what an acronym means on behalf of listeners, even when he knows it.
- Listeners get an information edge from people who see 20 or more founder meetings a week, plus the "billionaire porn" appeal and likeable characters.

**Format craft**
- He wants podcasts that are "candy and vegetables" at once: personality and entertainment alongside real depth.
- Guests need to be intelligible. He lets them go deep but makes sure they "take the stairs" rather than jump straight into brain surgery.
- He says Howard Stern is the playbook: build characters, brand them, and do live events. Jason says he is starting a reality show in the Gordon Ramsay vein, helping founders.
- He says All In deliberately did not monetize. His partners agreed to keep the show pure rather than sell it to a network, though the second-order value of deals and brand is huge.
- The All In Summit made a small profit on a couple million dollars and was the number one tech event of the year, by his account. He plans to run it again, either as All In or under a new brand if the others vote no.

## Working hours, team, and how he runs the Angel business

- Jason says he works harder now than ever, and with more energy than in his 20s. He says he finds meaning in the work after the death of his friend Tony Shea.
- He says he gets "great joy" from the daily show and from entertaining an audience, and he treats it like going to the gym.
- He dislikes legal, HR, accounting, operations and tax work. He does not enjoy negotiating term sheets either.
- To handle that, he brought in Mike Savino, his first boss from his 20s, as president to run the company. Jason does the podcast, founder meetings and LP fundraising.
- The team is about 22 people, 10 on media and 12 on investments. The investment team does about 60 first meetings a week, heading toward 100 total meetings a week across 10 to 12 people.
- He moved investment team meetings to two sessions a week, Tuesday and Thursday 2 to 4 PM, rather than one long Monday meeting.
- He tells every hire he works 60 to 70 hours a week and says "keep up". He looks for a solid 50 to 60 hours from them.
- Most staff stay two to four years and then leave to start their own funds. Jason is fine with that.
- He says the Angel Syndicate is the largest in the world. He has deployed about $185 million as an angel and now deploys about $50 million a year. The syndicate has roughly 11,000 angels.
- He says he does not aspire to be a billionaire. If he did, he says he would raise a late-stage fund. What he wants is to be happy and to do what he loves each day:
  - the podcast
  - skiing
  - time with his kids
  - meeting early founders
- His investing ambition is to be on the "Mount Rushmore" of angels and to be in the conversation among all investors.

## Raising Fund IV in public (Rule 506(c)) and democratizing venture

- Jason's funds grew quickly:
  - Fund I was $10 million, raised from friends including Bill Gurley, Dave Goldberg, Tony Shea, Sacks and Chamath. It took about five years to deploy.
  - Fund II was $11 million and took about two years to deploy.
  - Fund III was $44 million and took about two and a half years to deploy.
- Raising time dropped each time. Fund I took six months to a year, Fund II three to six months, and Fund III three months. He expects Fund IV to raise in the first ten days what earlier funds took months to raise.
- For Fund IV he used a 506(c). That lets him publicly say he is raising, and he runs webinars for prospective LPs. He tweeted about it and about 1,000 people signed up.
- The limits he cites are 250 accredited investors for up to $10 million, and 2,000 for larger sizes, which are QPs. He cites a $5 million investable-assets test for QPs.
- He believes accreditation will eventually become a test you can pass by taking a course, rather than purely an income or net worth test.
- He says the hardest part of raising was the grind. He cites Magna VC (see Jason's interview with him), who took "50 meetings a week" to build a fund, and he thinks anyone willing to do that can do it.
- His thesis is: "now is the time to democratize venture capital." Accredited investors who have never been in a venture fund should at least consider the asset class. He warns that it is high risk and high reward, and he says he is in about 20 venture funds himself.
- The institutional view he reports is that big endowments say they rarely add funds and that a solo GP is a hard sell. One of them told him to put one more fund on the board and come back. He decided to see which QPs come out of the woodwork rather than chase institutions.
- Alternatives he considered:
  - invest only his own money in each deal and Syndicate it, with deal-by-deal carry and no LPs, audits or admin
  - raise a fund and be accountable to LPs
- He chose a fund because of what he finds meaningful. He especially wants an endowment like Memorial Sloan Kettering or a cancer foundation, since both his parents are cancer survivors.
- He says Sequoia's annual founder dinner showed what foundations did with the returns, which made the work feel more serious.

## Angel investing: Uber, Calm, and his pattern-matching checklist

**Early angel path**
- He became a Sequoia scout and learned about Syndicates and SPVs from Naval Ravikant at AngelList.
- His first Syndicate deal was Calm.
- He was an early angel in Uber, with Travis Kalanick at Dogpatch Labs. He had worked with Dogpatch Labs through Open Angel Forum.

**Calm**
- He invested $378K for about 6% at a $4 million valuation, before the company raised again at a roughly $250 million valuation. That meant no dilution in between.
- He says Alex and Michael later told him they were about to shut down. They had pitched about 40 investors who all said no. They debated whether to burn his money, and took it because he insisted.
- Pricing advice he gave them: they planned to sell the app at $10 a year. He asked what a meditation class costs; he says it was a $20 suggested donation per class. He asked how often someone should practice, and the answer was daily or weekly. He said that implied around $80 a month, and asked about $10 a month. They went with a monthly price around that idea (he recalls it landing at about $60 a year) and he says it became "a money printing machine."
- He tells the story as proof that pricing and product-market fit are often a few questions apart.

**His investing checklist (teaching founders and his team)**
- He is training his team to score companies against "nine" criteria and an "anti-list" of things that kill companies. He gave only two:
  - World-class design. Meaning the best, or one of the top 10, in the category. He cites Calm vs. Headspace and Robinhood vs. E-Trade. A website-builder template with stock photos does not count as world class; that is "serviceable."
  - Product velocity. Ask what changed since a June meeting by July. He looks for a changelog or roadmap, and cites Superhuman's constant shipping. He says a founder like Travis would take a call and walk you through what they are debating that Sunday.
- He says his team must tell him how many of the criteria a company hits, and how many red flags it has.
- He is trying to avoid meetings with companies he will not invest in. He basically does not take a pitch unless he intends to invest, so his team front-loads the work.
- He says he has never made a pre-product-market-fit investment now worth over a billion except Calm.
- He runs Founder University: a 12-week course where founders pay $700 and get it back if they finish. About 90 percent finish, roughly 200 people have gone through, and he invests $25K in some of them. He uses it to filter talkers from walkers.
- He rejects the idea that you must have gone to Stanford. He says maybe five percent of his founders did. He says nobody cares where you went to school or live; they care about product, metrics, team and growth rate, which he calls "as beautiful of a meritocracy as I've ever seen."

## Public markets, Adobe-Figma, and valuation logic

- Jason says he is buying equities and trading publicly (he mentions Adobe, TSMC, Twilio and Shopify as names he likes, with a "not investment advice" caveat). He says it balances his understanding of public versus private success.
- He argues Adobe buying Figma was the right move even at a huge premium. Adobe was unlikely to rebuild Figma without a full rewrite. Buying it removes the risk that Figma builds Photoshop, and puts Figma into Adobe's distribution channel.
- He reasons that the market's drop on the news was a discount on a good decision.
- Valuation logic: a company with strong network effects inside an organization deserves a meaningful revenue multiple, like a castle with a bigger moat. Figma extends to non-designers, and everyone from BD to the CFO to legal comes in.
- His Weblogs Inc lesson: AOL paid $30 million against about $200K in cumulative revenue. People thought that was crazy because they did not realize AOL's distribution drove huge ad revenue to the blogs.
- He says an acquisition that looks absurd can look like a bargain five years later. He cites WhatsApp, YouTube and Instagram: "leave it alone, don't screw it up."
- He gives a rough take on All In's value: a top-40 podcast is worth at least $50 million on its own, and possibly $50 to $100 million. He points out that one extra Uber-type deal would add more.

## Views on capitalism, wealth, and learning to build

- Jason says the world is unfair but that in America it has never been more fair. He says anyone can learn almost anything for free:
  - YouTube
  - MIT OpenCourseWare
  - Google's free career courses
  - founder-built channels
- He says term sheets used to be secret. Now there are many videos on how to negotiate one.
- He argues people create jobs and are rewarded for it. He supports capitalism and criticizes attacks on founders.
- His example: Bernie Sanders and Elizabeth Warren attacked Bezos, and Amazon then paid about $22 an hour, with benefits and college. He says the free market delivered what the politicians could not pass.
- He criticizes San Francisco politics and a supervisor who celebrated taxing founders. He points out that one Slack exit generated about a billion in taxes for the state.
- He thinks California will be damaged for a decade or two.
- He describes himself as an independent and moderate, a fiscal conservative and social liberal, and says he is writing a book about wealth and money.
- He wants to be on a venture Hall of Fame list. His own picks for impact include Don Valentine, Arthur Rock, Paul Graham, Doug Leone and Mike Moritz, YC, Ron Conway and Bezos.
