## Fundraising as a numbers game: building the investor target list and funnel

- Jason says investors should be found by working backwards from adjacencies. If a startup is an on-demand pet grooming app, he looks at who invested in Uber, Instacart and other on-demand businesses, plus consumer technology and e-commerce.
- He tells founders to use Crunchbase, PitchBook and Google to build the list.
- Target the specific partner, not just the firm. Check each partner's profile and Twitter to see whether they talk about SaaS, consumer or commerce. Emailing the wrong partner can get the lead forwarded and ignored, "burning" it.
- Also check stage. Some partners do seed, some Series A, some growth, and scouts write the small checks.
- His launch accelerator suggests a curated list of 150 investors. He says generic emails to 1,500 investors are "the wrong way".
- Funnel as he describes it:
  - Roughly one in three or one in four targets gives a first meeting, depending on how hot the company and category are. Maybe 40 meetings from 150.
  - Maybe half of those convert to a second meeting.
  - Perhaps 10 go into diligence, about 5 into lunch, and a few produce one to four term sheets.
  - That is about one in 50 to one in 75 targets giving a term sheet. "Everything is a numbers game."
- He also says to check whether an investor can actually invest: ask how deployed their fund is, when they raise the next one, and what their follow-on strategy is. Some are between funds or only doing follow-ons.

## Jason's pitch rules: the five golden rules

Jason says he built these from running pitch events since 1997 (Meet the Alley) and the Launch accelerator, after sitting through thousands of pitches.

1. **Get to the product in 15 seconds.** Show it fast so the audience's "neurons fire".
2. **Examples matter.** Use a specific user story with numbers. His Airbnb example: Susan has a $1,200 spring break budget. Paris hotels start at $179 a night, so 8 days blows the budget. A $60-a-night room makes it about $500 for the room, plus a $379 flight, and she can afford Paris.
3. **Synchronicity.** What you say must match what is on the screen. Reveal Susan's picture, then her budget, then the options in step with the story, not a slide of projections while you tell a story.
4. **One slide, one message.** A slide with four paragraphs of business model is a "deal memo". Give each business model its own slide, and keep slides moving and breathing.
5. **Show, don't tell.** Show the product, data and customers instead of vague descriptions.

Other pitch advice:
- The slide is a guide, not a script. Don't memorize the deck; the slide should trigger what you say.
- Practice deliberately: record yourself, get a transcript, highlight the "um"s, and redo it.
- Be able to explain the startup in one jargon-free sentence. Examples: Airbnb lets people rent rooms; Uber is everybody's personal driver.
- Keep three decks: a teaser deck of 10 slides or fewer to email (no confidential info), a 20-40 slide deep dive, and an appendix. A rule of thumb is about one minute per slide, and he says some people go 20-30 seconds a slide.

## The cold email and "loom, zoom, room" sequence

- The cold email should be short and specific. His template: reference something the investor said (e.g. "we saw your interview on This Week in Startups where you talked about your investment in Robinhood"), say what you do ("something similar in Latin America for millennials, plus crypto"), give traction ("1,400 customers", "$25 a month for unlimited trades"), and link a demo or a Loom.
- He describes the email as a movie trailer: "The trailer is not the entire movie. The trailer is to get you to buy a ticket."
- Sequence: **Loom, then Zoom, then room**.
  - A custom Loom walkthrough of the deck, addressed to the person, plays on reciprocation: they took time for me.
  - Every Loom is also another rep at your pitch. He suggests 10 a day at 10 to 15 minutes each, and reading the replies for feedback (for example "you didn't talk about your customers").
  - Lead with whatever is strongest: product, traction or team, such as "three engineers from Facebook, Amazon and Google".
  - Then ask for a short Zoom, offering flexible availability and a calendar link for 30, 45 or 60 minutes. Offering 10 to 20 minutes shows you respect their time.
  - The in-person meeting comes after, if it fits.
- He says he likes emails from people who name a specific episode or tweet and give a traction number, such as $25,000 in monthly recurring revenue.

## Running the first meeting

- Start with investors you care less about for about two weeks to get reps and feedback. Move to top targets in weeks two or three.
- Bring two decks: a 5 to 10 minute, 10-slide version and a 15 to 20 minute, 30-slide version, with an appendix. Ask the investor which they prefer. He says this shows you think about the other side of the table.
- When he is the investor, he asks the founder what format works best (10 minutes of pitch and 10 of questions, or a product demo).
- Be high energy and positive. Answer questions concisely, "like ping pong". An answer should take about as long as the question.
- His accelerator records founders answering questions and times them. Example: "tell me about your team" at 70 seconds may be too long, while "how many employees" should be about 7 seconds ("five full-time employees and three contractors").
- Close with clear next steps. Ask directly whether they want to invest, go to diligence, present to their partnership, meet customers, or meet the team. He says he likes founders who say "you're our dream investor, would you lead the round?" because it shows the confidence he wants in someone selling or hiring.
- Know the revenue answer cold, for example "$27,000 in recurring revenue", rather than hedging.

## Follow-up, urgency and investor updates

- Don't be passive after the meeting. Follow up on anything unclear: for example send the Notion page with the hiring plan he asked about, or add an answer on a competitor. "Never lie, tell the truth."
- Create urgency honestly: "We have two term sheets, we plan to decide on Friday." Add fresh progress, such as closing two enterprise customers or an upfront payment of $40,000.
- Send concise monthly investor updates with a chart up top. Put non-investors who passed on a BCC list or mail merge too. If growth proves his concerns wrong (he gives "we grew 17% this month" as an example), he may invest later, perhaps at a higher price or in the next round.
- "People invest in lines, not dots." Many updates over time build the line and the founder's credibility.

## Diligence on investors and SEC rules

- Founders should do "dark references": email founders the investor backed, without telling the investor, and ask about them. Jason says email about 100 and expect around 99 positive answers; a few clashes are normal.
- Know what kind of investor you want: hands-off check writers, or deeply involved ones. He cites a past episode where a founder described a nightmare VC experience, and notes there are two sides plus the reality.
- Rule 506(b), the most common route, means no public talk about the raise. With 506(c) you can raise publicly but must verify accreditation (W-2s or letters from lawyers or accountants). That costs more, and founders often use a service; he names AssureFund Management, in which he is an investor.
- Only take money from people who understand the risk and can afford to lose it. $50K from a friend with $50M is fine; a third of someone's retirement account is not. He says a small share, around 3 to 4 percent of a retirement account, may be fine if they really want to support you.
- Use a good law firm. He says he trusts Republic and SeedInvest as platforms and doesn't trust other ones. For private raises, he says lawyers will tell you to stay private if you can.

## How to get on his podcast (and why not to pay for it)

- This Week in Startups doesn't take PR pitches. His team replies "we don't take pitches for the podcast" and he penalizes pitchers.
- His guest criteria: do something interesting, be successful, have a hot take, or write a great blog post. Strong performances on other podcasts also get noticed.
- He wants real answers, not media-trained ones, and says "don't give me the media trained answer."
- He says booking services claiming to place people on the show are lying, and he warned off one that implied an inside line.
