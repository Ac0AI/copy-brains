## Hiring a founding team: co-founders, vesting and first hires

- Jason says a founding team should have complementary skills. At least one co-founder should be technical, since "being an engineer is not a skill you can just learn overnight". Another should cover sales, marketing or operations.
- Founders almost never all stay through an IPO, and often not even through seed and Series A. So he has founders vest their equity, typically a one or two year cliff with a four year vest.
- Example: three co-founders hold 20% each, 10% is in the option pool and 30% is with investors. If one leaves after a year, they keep only 25% of their 20% (5%). The unvested 15% is retired, and everyone else's share rises proportionally. A board can also steer some of it back to the founders who stepped up.
- Typical founding teams are two founders, sometimes up to five. Splits are common but not always equal. Someone who joins in year two may get a smaller share. A founder who puts in $500K of seed money may hold 20% as a founder plus about 10% as an investor, usually as preferred shares.
- Jason's rule of thumb: until about 10 employees, hire generalists. One person does five jobs, then five people do five jobs, then five people each do one job, and that is a department. Early staff must be hardworking, tenacious and need little management.
- After roughly 10 employees, hire where you personally spend 30-50% of your time and can hand it off. There is no fixed formula for who is hire 11.
- Experience level by stage: around Series A, 2-5 years; around Series B, 8-10 years. Senior people often wait for product-market fit and escape velocity (roughly 2 years of runway and $2M+ in revenue, with a known investor) before joining.
- Jason wants "missionaries not mercenaries".

## Remote vs office, and in-house vs outsourced

- Remote suits solitary work such as writing and coding, if people can manage their time. Meeting-heavy work (investing, product conception, sales culture) works better in one room.
- Pick one model, all remote or all in the office. A few remote people on an in-office team become second-class citizens.
- Remote developers should do quarterly off-sites, about one week every quarter, in person.
- Geography limits the talent pool, and Jason notes many people no longer want to live in San Francisco.
- He tells founders to keep long-term vision, strategy and customer feedback in-house, especially before product-market fit. Enterprise sales and client success should stay in-house.
- Outsource repeatable, administrative work: accounting and taxes, payroll, HR (via a PEO), office space (WeWork). He notes four of the classic first ten hires (office manager, accountant, HR, receptionist/sysadmin) no longer exist as internal roles.
- He advises against dev shops. "You don't accrue the knowledge into the company", and shops want "the least amount of work possible for the highest amount of pay". In-house developers give a much faster cadence.
- A remote developer team on your payroll, where you are their only customer, is different from a shop. Prefer SaaS services over single contractors for redundancy.
- Tools named: Zendesk, Rippling, Gusto, Zenefits, TriNet, LinkedIn Talent Solutions, Breezy HR (run hiring like a sales pipeline), Comparably (salary benchmarks).

## Running a hiring process

- Keep a hiring plan about six months ahead, because it takes about six months for a hire to settle into a role.
- Keep the team at about 80% capacity, not 100-110%, so people don't burn out and there is room for special projects. He compares people to CPUs.
- Be proactive: reach out to about five candidates a day instead of posting a job and forgetting it.
- Candidates should hear back within a day or two. Not replying is "bad karma" and bad for the brand. Keep the job board current.
- Founders should write job descriptions themselves, with personality and a mission. He also blogs and posts on social media about what he is looking for.
- Be opportunistic: if talent shows up, create a role. That is how launch hired Sam.
- Ask your network "do you know anyone?" when reaching out. It doubles as a soft pitch to the person.
- Start hiring for diversity and inclusion early, and be intentional about it.

## Structure, ownership and explaining the "why"

- Give each early employee 3-5 things they own and report on. Each employee reports to exactly one person. With multiple founders, split the reporting lines to avoid "permission shopping" (asking another founder for a different answer).
- Draw an org chart and update it as structure changes. Example: a portfolio company moved to "sales pods" of an SDR, an account executive and a customer success person.
- Always explain the outcome and the reason behind it. Example: he wants 50 companies in the accelerator's final interviews, to accept only 7. That way, he says, "investors coming get addicted to our deal flow", and the batch has no duds.
- He argues younger employees want to know why. Managers must "take the time to explain why this is important" or lose purpose-driven Millennial and Gen Z talent.
- He says more people want part-time or freelance work, and part-time job postings attract more applicants than full-time ones.
- He prefers a small team (about 15 people) because you can know everyone, and he notes that founders will copy the seriousness you set.

## Performance, firing and communication

- Firing should never be a surprise. Sequence: written note about slipping performance, then a verbal conversation, then a performance improvement plan (PIP). Jason says about 80% of PIPs don't work out, so a PIP is effectively a final warning.
- Judge the work, not the person. High performers can bend rules a bit (for example arriving late), but low performance plus lateness means the person is out.
- Document everything. Follow up verbal talks by email. This protects the company on unemployment claims and legal issues.
- Trust your gut and fire fast. "If there is any doubt, there is no doubt." Founders are good at picking people but cowards at cutting them.
- Be kind, firm and unemotional. Jason suggests giving 1-2 weeks of severance even without a formal policy, because "enemies accumulate" and Glassdoor will punish a bad exit. He once laid off 50 people at once.
- Use plain language ("you are being fired", not "let go"). No debate, and you don't have to give a reason in an at-will state, though he says to take legal advice when it's sticky.
- Defer an exit interview if they push back. The manager does the firing, and the team is told afterward directly, without gossip.
- Example of firing immediately: a sales hire was moonlighting for a customer and emailing the company's list from his work email.
- He is cautious about references and offers to be a positive reference for people who didn't work out.

## Compensation and equity

- Make comp "transparent, fair and generous" where possible, without endless negotiation. Underpaying makes people keep a foot out the door.
- Startups shouldn't hire people seeking maximum compensation. Those people should go to Facebook or Google. Startup equity has a "70-80% chance of zero".
- Promise a pay review at the next round. Example: seed-stage $5,000/month draws can move to about $100K after a $3M seed, around a 10% raise when the next round closes.
- Salaries vary hugely by city. Miami salaries can be a third to half of San Francisco's, and Toronto about 25% less. Yelp moved support jobs out of the East Bay for this reason.
- Non-cash perks: remote days, team lunches, a company retreat (launch's is the Whitsundays, about 1% of annual budget), birthdays off, and mission alignment.
- Offer an either-or menu such as $80K + 1% vs $90K + 0.5% vs $100K + 0.2% so people choose their own cash-equity mix.
- ESOPs are typically 10-16%. A 409A valuation comes first. Common is worth less than preferred. Standard vesting is a one year cliff with a four year vest.
- Jason would prefer back-weighted vesting over 5 years, with about half in the first three years and half in the last two.
- He wants longer exercise windows (30, 60 or 90 days is too short). His idea: after three years, a 10-year window, or window length equal to double the years worked. He recalls employees at Mahalo spending real money to exercise and says he feels guilty about it.

## Culture and boundaries

- Don't hire only people you'd have a drink with, and keep professional distance from reports because you may have to fire them.
- He says workplace dating is no longer the norm; people have dating apps. Know the chain-of-command rules, and report and separate reporting lines if a relationship exists.
- He says serious founders model the seriousness they want. Company parties and trips to Vegas "do not end well", and are a bad look in 2019.
