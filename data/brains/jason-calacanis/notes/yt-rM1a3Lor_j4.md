## Angel investing: picking founders, constraint, and portfolio math

- Jason says founders who raise huge pre-product rounds lose their edge: "the whole team gets off their game, entitlement goes off the charts."
- His analogy: it is like putting a chef in a Costco, or giving an artist a mural-sized canvas and truckloads of paint. "Constraint makes great art."
- Money distances the core team from the customer. They end up arguing over chairs, the receptionist's desk, or laptop RAM instead of shipping.
- A founder who needs $100K-$250K to build something that can now be built in a weekend has "signaled to the market that you are a permission asker."
- Jason invests after a product exists and he can play with it. His examples are Superhuman (Raul had done a company before, so a decent seed of several million, under $10M) and serial founders like Mark Pincus.
- Serial founders tend to raise about $10M for 10%, a 5-10x premium over the average for good reason.
- He tells founders to treat investors as "somewhere between a checkbook or ATM and a coach or a close friend who can give you candid feedback."
- Y Combinator requires a technologist on the team. Jason's view is that if you can't build, "what's your goddamn excuse?" Free resources exist (freeCodeCamp, Lambda School with income-share agreements).

## Vetting founders in a world where everyone knows the playbook

- Jason has done a thousand-plus episodes, so founders now pitch him exactly what he has said he wants. Some fabricate personal-itch origin stories.
- One pitch cited a dog killed by a car as the reason for the startup.
- Since stories can be faked, he leans on traction: "The secret weapon is to just talk to the customers." Customers are hard to fake, and he knows the techniques people use to fake them.
- He sees friction reduction (incorporation, cap tables, terms sheets, we-work space in a day for about $1,000) as a net positive. It also means authenticity is what separates founders.
- He compares this to NBA vs golf/tennis/skiing: accessible sports (a ball and a hoop) become the world's sport.

## Smart money vs dumb money, and why VCs get the best deals

- Jason says some advisors tell founders to avoid sophisticated investors in the first round, take "dumb money" (dentists, naive investors) at the highest price with no governance, and roll over later. He has seen this as a deliberate strategy.
- He says founders wind up regretting it. You want the smartest people on your team, and the incremental 20% valuation is not worth it.
- Skin in the game matters: a dentist with $50K in will shrug when it goes badly. An investor like Aileen Lee with $500K in "is going to go to war with you."
- Examples of sophisticated seed investors he names: Aileen Lee (Cowboy Ventures), and Hunter and Satya at Homebrew.
- On non-accredited and crypto raises, he proposes that regulators let projects register and raise capped amounts from retail (for example, a $1,000 cap per non-accredited investor). His logic is that losing $1,000 is not the "risk of ruin."
- His existing SPV constraints: 250 investors, up to $10M, accredited only, with the syndicate lead on the hook.

## ICOs, crypto, and why he calls them out

- Jason says he was never against crypto, only the ICO scams. He stayed cautious because he had something to lose and did not want to go to jail.
- He says that if ICOs became legitimate and legal, "the syndicate comm will be an ICO token and we will go all in."
- He notes the pattern in ICO-funded companies: no product, no hundreds of employees, despite $10M-$250M raised. He cites a pitch where investors simply sent crypto to a wallet and filled in a spreadsheet, with no KYC.
- He gives credit to Ethereum as a real outcome, and thinks Huobi is real.
- On Bitcoin, his view has shifted over time because it has not been hacked or shut down. He previously gave it a "majority chance" of going to zero or being replaced by better tech.
- He now says he would consider putting about 1% of his net worth in Bitcoin as a hedge. He lost an early ten-Bitcoin stash to an exchange hack; his wife bought a batch at $300-$800.

## Platforms, antitrust, and competition

- On Amazon Basics: Jason says the problem is not making a competing product. It becomes a problem when Amazon gives its own product preferential search placement or cuts the third-party seller's traffic.
- Using Amazon's public data to pick products is fine if the same data is available to everyone (reviews, search terms).
- On Google: he says Google claimed organic results didn't change while moving the first organic result down about 400 pixels beneath its own boxes (shopping, travel, stocks, local). "90 percent of the clicks are above the fold."
- On lower prices: he argues rampant competition is good for society, because $4 Amazon Basics cables beat $30 Apple ones and Airbnb stays in Kyoto at $50.
- On the Apple App Store: he calls 30% reasonable compared with retail, where the store took half. It drops to 15% in year two for subscriptions.
- His advice to startups: "pay the Vig to Apple." Playing nice may get you promoted or featured, and subscriptions through the store put you on paid ranking lists.
- He thinks the principle should be equal treatment: if Netflix and Spotify get a different deal, that is where it gets murky.
- Zuckerberg vs Bezos: he calls Zuckerberg more cutthroat, citing "just copy Snapchat" and says Bezos tends to think "day zero" from first principles.

## Positioning, enemies, and audience

- Jason cites Basecamp/DHH's Hey launch as a textbook case of picking an enemy. He quotes the Rework chapter: "Having an enemy gives you a great story to tell customers... people get stoked by conflict."
- He praises Hey's screener feature (approve or reject first-time senders) as a killer feature, and says he told Superhuman it should have it.
- On media: he argues "he who holds the audience has all the power." Elon Musk can't be cancelled because he has a bigger audience than any media corporation.
- Companies increasingly build the audience first, then the products (Instagram moving into shopping and ads).
- Barstool: he wrote a March 2017 blog post saying it would be a billion-dollar company, after Chernin bought about 50%. He expected it to expand beyond sports.
- Dave Portnoy "is the best in the world" at entertaining and "understands who he is as a character."
- On Robinhood: he was an early investor. When the founders pitched him, he objected that millennials are commitment-phobic and retail investors had been wiped out twice, but liked "free" as the answer to how they would make money.
- He views retail speculation as a silver lining because young people learn finance and wallets instead of betting on sports.

## Portfolio construction, ownership, and confidence

- Jason's advice to new angels: reach about 30 investments, invest only 5-10% of net worth, and expect that "the top two or three deals" make about 90% of the returns.
- He tells syndicate members he is "swinging for the fences, not singles and doubles." Most people need 50-100 investments to hit an outlier.
- His ownership has evolved:
  - Sequoia scout, ten years ago: $25K-$50K checks, well under 1%.
  - Fund one, about seven years ago: 1-3%.
  - Superhuman: about 2%.
  - Uber (via syndicate): he put in $378K and owned about 5% at the time.
  - Accelerator: takes 6%, with a target of 10-20% in winners and a board seat where possible.
- Example: Fitbod, a subscription app doing over $10M a year, where he would follow on from the syndicate and fund. He says such companies might get 10x-20x revenue multiples (anywhere from 5x to 30x).
- Uber: he sold some shares at $50-60B valuations and still holds. Calls the dip to $15-16 during the pandemic a "gut check" but he did not sell.
- Confidence: he says he is "at peace with never hitting an Uber again" because he can make a career from hits like Robinhood, Wealthfront, Trello, Thumbtack. "You don't need to be Michael Jordan, you could be Kobe or Shaq."
- Sequoia's edge, in his view: "they show up for [work]" and meet founders every day.

## Remote work and content as deal flow

- Jason treats his podcast as deal flow. He originally insisted on in-person guests but moved remote.
- He says the quality of guests improved and the loss in conversation is maybe 5-10%.
- Remote tech check: This Week in Startups sends guests a mic and headset, and an ethernet dongle (about $100 and a producer's hour). They run a speed test and a screen share. Accelerator founders must use a wired Ethernet connection and a proper headset, with no AirPods.
- He says this lets them avoid most technical issues, with failures roughly one in 50 presentations.
- On dream deals: he said he would love to buy the Knicks.
