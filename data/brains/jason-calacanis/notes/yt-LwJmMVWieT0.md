## Low-cost marketing beyond social media and Google Ads

- Jason's core point: social and search ads get very expensive in some verticals. Around the 2015 to 2017 app boom, app installs cost $50 or more, or $1 to $10 per click.
- Real-world meetups and events are a cheaper way to acquire users. His example is a dating app: have the whole team invite single people to a cocktail hour, buy each a free drink (about $3), and ask them to install the app and give feedback. That works out to roughly $3 per install, or less.
- Other offline tactics he lists:
  - Door-hanger flyers, as for a Nextdoor-style app.
  - Yelp Elite style meetups.
  - Direct mail postcards, with the CMO citing a service called Postie.
  - Vehicle wraps. He is an investor in Wrapify, which went through his accelerator, and says it is hard to scale but worth testing.
  - Local sponsorships. He cites Squarespace on the Knicks, and notes you can test it by comparing New York signups to markets where you didn't advertise.
  - Small podcast sponsorships, which he says you can negotiate hard on.
- Content marketing as a lead channel: if you sell a $5,000-per-year product, a podcast at $500 per episode only needs one client per ten episodes to pay off, even with 1,000 listeners, "if it's the right thousand people."
- Street teams: take a five-person team to a busy spot like Fisherman's Wharf and ask people to install the app and give feedback for a $5 Starbucks card. This gives installs plus product feedback. He notes that founders are often scared to get feedback, and he likes forcing it.
- He favors high-engagement tactics that create "super fans," because each super fan tends to bring one or two more users.
- Rule: test, test, test, then run another set of tests.
- Platform mastery: his CMO says basic proficiency on a platform like Instagram or LinkedIn takes a couple of weeks and expert level takes about a month and a half to two months. Jason's hiring advice is to stop spending 3 to 6 months searching for a social media expert. Find someone with aptitude and attitude and train them for about six weeks.

## Protecting a startup idea

- Jason's main protection is silence: don't tell people about the idea before you execute. He calls broadcasting it a "rookie mistake."
- He pays close attention to the question's wording: it asks how to protect the idea, not the business.
- VCs stealing ideas: he knows of roughly two accusations in venture history, Cosmo vs. UrbanFetch being one. He argues VCs "do not want to work hard" and won't run with your idea.
- The real risk is on the margins. If a VC has a competing portfolio company, they may forward public information such as a page on your site, so don't expect public information to stay protected.
- He says to avoid meeting a VC who already backs a competitor, since you may hand them a roadmap for their company.
- Patents are "kind of meaningless in our industry except on the edges." Copyright, trademark and service marks exist but are secondary.
- Analogy: you don't go on Letterman to talk about an album coming in six months. Talk about what you have done, not what you plan to do.
- Credibility: he says investors split people into talkers and doers, and he wants founders to be in the second group. "Put your head down, shut up, get some goddamn skills, build the product."
- If you tweet or blog your idea and someone else builds it, "you really don't have a basis to complain."

## Where the next Google comes from, and accelerators

- Peter Thiel is a contrarian who likes the unpopular side, and Jason treats the "next Google won't come from Silicon Valley" line as provocation meant to make you think.
- His view: $100 billion companies have only come from the US and China. He expects Airbnb, Lyft, Uber and Palantir to become $100B "super unicorns" and says future ones will likely come from China and the US because of market size.
- China argument: its growing middle class (he puts it in the low hundreds of millions, on track to exceed the US population) gives native-language companies a shot at trillion-dollar valuations. He says Google is number one in search in every country except China, Taiwan, Russia and Korea.
- Accelerators: Y Combinator started in 2005, and unicorns from accelerators take about 10 years to emerge, citing Dropbox, Reddit, Airbnb, and SendGrid from Techstars.
- Rarity framing: a unicorn needs about $100M in revenue and a "decacorn" over $1B in revenue. Netflix has over 100 million paying customers, so millions of paying customers is possible but not easy.
- Serial entrepreneurs like Evan Williams generally skip accelerators because they can self-fund.

## Keeping momentum for a niche social app

- A niche gives you a natural audience, and the narrower the niche the easier it is to identify and reach the people in it.
- His examples: a women-only professional network (Sophia Amoroso's Girlboss network), and a network for women in finance on Wall Street, where there might be 100,000 people who mostly know each other and are on LinkedIn.
- Expand in stages, like Facebook (Harvard, then the Ivy League, then other colleges, then everyone).
- Use data, not opinion: plot features on two axes, how often each is used (daily, weekly, monthly, yearly) vs. what percentage of users use it. Put about 90% of attention on the top one or two features.
- Instagram example: the posting experience and the feed get most of the polish. He notes his birthday post got about 600 likes vs. 100 to 200 for a typical brisket post, which hints at why birthday content might be prioritized in an algorithmic feed.
- Re-engagement: emails and notifications that pull people back, such as missed tweets on Twitter, YouTube recommendations, or product update emails like those from Superhuman's CEO.
- Expand beyond the niche, but "don't do that too quick."

## Non-technical founders and focus

- He says you don't need tech skills to found a tech company, but you do need tech knowledge and an understanding of what the technology can do. He points to Airbnb's designer founder, and says Gates and Jobs didn't write their core code themselves.
- Still, a technical co-founder is strongly preferred. Without one, you run out of runway: if you pay a dev team $10K to $15K a month, work stops when the money does. A technical co-founder "will never have to stop writing code."
- His example: at Weblogs Inc., Brian Alvey built the platform while Jason landed Peter Rojas and Mark Cuban. Jason sketched a 4x4 grid of 16 blogs at the bottom of every page as an SEO hack, Brian shipped it in 48 hours, and it gave a 15% traffic advantage.
- Founder's job: collect ideas from the team, prioritize them, and put the rest on a "not right now" list to avoid shiny object syndrome.
- Rule: understand what value users get from your product and "double down on that until it stops working."
- Focus: when a founder gets 300 people who love the product, they often start a podcast, event or book instead of growing 300 to 3,000 to 30,000.
- Compounding: small daily progress beats silver bullets. 1% a day compounds, and 3 to 7% weekly or 30% monthly is huge.
- Improve many things a bit instead of one thing 2x. Five parts of the show each made 25% better double the overall result.

## Transparency about funding, and getting fired

- A question about being fired after 5 weeks for asking about funding: Jason notes the person was an employee, not a founder.
- He thinks founders should share the basics of funding with the team, because it gives employees certainty. He runs the business with 18 months of runway in the bank and tells people so.
- A reasonable middle path when fundraising is ongoing: "we're opportunistically looking at different funding scenarios," which avoids distracting staff or signaling weakness.
- He uses funding as a recruiting advantage, since rivals will tell candidates a startup is running out of cash. If the business is shaky and you brush off questions, staff will start questioning things.
- Personal lesson: he was fired from a computer store (Wolf Computers) for being late once or twice, despite selling many printers. The company went out of business six weeks later. His takeaway: don't give an easy excuse, and overperform so a single mistake isn't decisive.

## What entrepreneurs miss, and unique ideas

- Entrepreneur mistakes he cites:
  - Overestimating the downside: you can go back to a job if it fails, so just start.
  - Lack of focus.
  - Underestimating small daily gains.
- Market creation: great companies often induce new demand rather than just competing. Airbnb brought in people who couldn't afford $150-a-night Paris hotels at about $50 a night, and Uber and Lyft got people to go out who would have stayed home.
- For his Jason syndicate project, he says the opportunity is the roughly 14 million of ~15 million US accredited investors who have never made an angel investment, not the 1 million who already have.
- You don't need a unique idea. YouTube was reportedly the 500th video site, but it differed in execution: free hosting, ad revenue share, plus falling bandwidth and storage costs. Facebook copied Friendster's look but kept its servers up, and Instagram Stories copied Snapchat.
- Ask "why now": reputation systems (Yelp, Amazon reviews) made Airbnb possible, and a pre-smartphone text-based taxi service never took off.
- Ways to find a new angle: new technology (GPS, phone cameras, AR/VR) and new business models (SaaS subscriptions like Slack versus $50K-a-server enterprise software; a $50/year meditation app vs. a $5,000 transcendental meditation course).

## Raising kids as entrepreneurs, and when to quit

- Kids: he talks to his 8-year-old daughter about business using real terms (inventory, margin, rent) and takes her to his incubator. His own training: working every job in his dad's bar, including counting the money.
- Ideas for older kids: after-school jobs, entrepreneur clubs, and small projects like the laser-cut earrings idea. He also wants to buy a candy store or ice cream shop for kids to run, and recommends entrepreneurial movies such as The Founder and autobiographies.
- Reason: jobs change fast, and even big-company employees are expected to be entrepreneurial.
- Running out of money after 5 years: a day job or part-time work is fine to pay the bills. He says if it hasn't worked in five years it likely won't, unless you have data or intuition that year six is the breakout.
- Test the business: email your free users saying you plan to shut down or will charge $1,000 a year to keep it going, with options of yes, maybe or no. Anyone who isn't a "yes" tells you what you need to know. He also suggests asking users to vote on a maintenance window to see if anyone engages.
- Equity crowdfunding: unlikely to work, because investors will ask why a five-year-old product has no business model.
- Pricing: don't be afraid to charge. Creatives pay $100K a year for school, so a $25 a month app for them is reasonable, and early charging gives you data.
