## Why Jason wrote the angel investing book and how wealth is built

- Jason's book *Angel* (2017) draws on 150 angel investments, six of which became unicorns. Desktop Metal, a metal 3D printer maker he backed in its first round, announced a raise of over $1 billion the day of this interview.
- His agent had pitched him "for Dummies" books for a decade. He argues books should come from subject-matter experts, not from inexperienced people who write them to become experts. He says he was a good entrepreneur, not an elite one, but has real credibility in angel investing.
- His core thesis: wealth in the 21st century comes from getting on the cap tables of high-growth companies, "specifically in Silicon Valley, because the hit rate there is so much better." He says the numbers there are "add a zero or two from any other market."
- He argues the 20th-century path (save, pay down a house, brown-bag lunch) has broken. His parents' Brooklyn brownstone cost about $45,000, under one year of their combined income. Today it is a $1M-$2M house against roughly $300,000 of household income, with student debt of up to about $150,000 on top.
- His hope: if 100,000 people buy the book and 5-10% start angel investing, maybe 100 get an incredible result. If the rest land within plus or minus 50% of their money, that is still a fine outcome.
- He says the book also works in reverse for founders. It shows how angels and VCs think, so founders can sync with them rather than "game" them.

## What angels and VCs expect, and the "add a zero" idea for small profitable businesses

- Return expectations:
  - A regular angel wants 5-10x in about seven years.
  - Professional angels hope some companies hit 50-100x.
  - Jason himself looks for companies that can do 100x to 500x.
  - VCs invest millions and aim for about 10x, with an outside chance of 100-200x.
- Taking outside money is "lighting a fuse" or "hitting a starter pistol." Your life changes: you send updates, answer questions, and are now expected to scale.
- He frames what small, profitable "grinder" founders (e.g. a one-person plugin or template business at $1M a year) already have and lack. They have product sense and customer sense, but not yet scale sense.
- His method for adding a zero: study your customers. If the top 5-10% share a need, double or triple down. Example: three of 1,000 customers are financial companies asking for the same features, so tell them you are building that product and charge them $25,000 a month.
- Illustrative cap table math: $1M revenue, $5M valuation, 5M shares at $1. An investor puts in $500,000 for 10% and the company deploys it on five salespeople ($50,000 plus commission) and two developers.
- His advice: "just add a zero." He says this is what he tells founders, and when they do, he says "let's add one more and we're done."

## How to pitch angels: a profitable small business is an asset

- Some investors read a bootstrapped $1M business as "thinks small." Savvy ones see a "dude business" (a founder-run $500K-$1M business) as a sign of smart, lean operators who learn a lot.
- Examples he gives:
  - Phil Kaplan (DistroKid) runs lean companies of himself plus freelancers up to millions in revenue.
  - MailChimp reached about $400M in revenue without outside money.
  - SurveyMonkey took investment after scaling off revenue.
  - Both paths can work.
- His pitch template for a profitable company: "We made a million dollars. We can grow 20% a year for 10 years and make $10 million. We want to build a billion-dollar company; here's the opportunity and why we need $1.5 million for 15%."
- If revenue has been flat for five years, you need a credible story for why it will accelerate. Example: you never had outbound sales, so add a team. Investors would want to test that theory and might give you $500K to do it.
- First-contact email: send about 10 angels fewer than five sentences. Say what the company is, how it makes money and for whom, include a revenue chart (weekly, monthly or quarterly) and a product demo link. He says angels click the links and then take the meeting.
- He criticizes the opposite approach: "Most people write their life story and what they plan on doing, the talkers, the tourists."
- Founders who reach $1M can reach $10M. He is unsure whether they can reach $100M, but a $10M business is worth 5-20x revenue, and investors can tell you which people you need to hit the next milestone.
- Recent example: his first cannabis investment, in a company making about $1M from advertising, cannabis tourism, magazines and festivals. He took the scrappy revenue base as a good starting point.

## Take more risk, and treat funding as a starter pistol

- He argues most founders earning $500K-$1M "don't take enough risk" because they protect an affluent lifestyle. If you never risk it, there is no chance of outsized success.
- Failure is recoverable: if you fall climbing "the startup mountain" you have more credibility and start over at the bottom. "You put another quarter in the machine, you get to play the video game again."
- Having built a million-dollar business before, you can build another.
- On Shark Tank-style thinking: money is not the prize, it is "the starter pistol." Investors want a return on capital, and finishing matters.
- Why fear the downside when "it's not life or death"? Bringing in investors gives you several people working for your success with skills you lack.

## Investor updates and planning discipline

- Jason asks for a monthly update from each founder. It takes about an hour to write and covers key metrics, challenges, wins, losses, and where investors can help.
- Writing it creates discipline, and sending it to about 10 investors and the management team opens up dialogue. Companies that do this "go a lot further."
- He believes in planning. Example: plan to go from $1M to $3M in year one, then $3M to $9M after. If you only hit $2M and $6M, you will likely still be further along than someone without a plan.
- Founders who resist giving updates signal to him that they are not serious about the business or the relationship with investors. He likes people "serious about the business" who "want to do the business right."
- How involved he is depends on the founder: involved if they want help, less if they do not.

## Own the direct relationship: email, paid tiers and ads (Inside.com)

- Platform risk lesson: Google featured Weblogs, Inc in its first quarterly report as an ad partner (over $2,000 a day), then cut about 90% of the traffic after algorithm changes. Calls went unreturned, despite a 10-year relationship.
- He argues Google should have paid content partners a licensing fee (e.g. 10 cents CPM) for content shown in its answer box.
- Same pattern with YouTube: it changed the rules and cut ads for creators with under 10,000 views, so he left the platform.
- Facebook example: its API is allowed until you gain traction pulling people off Facebook, then it is shut off.
- His response: go direct with email. "Use any of these other platforms if it gets you customers, but own a direct, deep relationship."
  - "I can't tell you how many people I know who have apps and have no emails."
  - Email "is the big growth hack" (Twitter's "here's what you missed" emails). If you are not collecting emails everywhere and giving subscribers massive value, "you're doing it wrong."
  - He also began experimenting with SMS.
- Inside.com numbers:
  - 200,000 subscribers across 26 newsletters, targeting 50.
  - LAUNCH Ticker: of 27,000 subscribers, over 1,000 pay, about 4%.
  - If 4% of 200,000 pay, that is about 8,000 paying subscribers, roughly $1M a year, funding about 20 journalists working from home.
  - The paid tier launched about three to four weeks before the interview with 1-2% of free users as the target.
- Staffing: remote journalists with 3-5 years' experience for $40,000-$50,000 plus benefits is a strong offer outside New York and San Francisco.
- Ads versus paid: LAUNCH Ticker let about 1,000 paid users turn off ads by clicking a profile setting. Only 10-20 did, because people like targeted ads. He thinks about 20% of people will pay a few extra dollars (his Hulu example: $10 with ads versus $13 without), so a paid tier can still carry ads.
- Idea he floats for Netflix: sponsored free days (e.g. a Mercedes-sponsored free Saturday with an ad and survey) as onboarding.

## Run experiments like probes, and cut apps for email

- Mental model: send 100 probes to 100 planets and see which return a signal. Crossing planets off the list produces data. Entrepreneurship is "triangulating around a signal," where revenue and engagement (such as open rates) are the signals.
- Test cheaply and quickly. Ask, "what's the least costly and quickest way to get the signal?"
- Inside.com example:
  - The news app had hundreds of thousands of downloads, but under 1% used it daily.
  - Emails get 30-50% opens, with two sent a day. He calls this a 50x improvement on an old technology.
  - The app team of about eight highly paid people could be redeployed to hire about a dozen journalists.
  - He says no news app has worked, including ones he invested in such as Circa.
- Daily Brief test: 40-50% opened it in the morning, but readers said the news was stale the next day. He sent an afternoon update to about 1,000 people, then told the whole list they were moving to twice a day, with unsubscribe as the option. Only a few complained.
- Listen to metrics over vocal customers: "Sometimes people listen too much to their customers." Use data and experiments, not "emotions, feelings, predictions, or who's in charge." The restaurant analogy: you can charge for bread; if someone does not like it, they can go elsewhere.
- Cart-before-horse lesson: his Inside Drones video series was paused because the audience engagement was not there yet. His fix is to work backwards and build engagement first.
- He recommends *The Lean Startup* (Eric Ries).

## Podcast networks versus independent shows

- His experiment seven years earlier: he and friends put $100K each into a network of shows (This Week in Startups, Kevin Pollak's Chat Show, Mark Suster's venture show, a Mad Men recap, Pensado's Place). Only two shows broke out.
- Lesson: credible hosts succeed and emerging hosts rarely break out in 3-4 years. The credible ones do not need a network, since an episode costs about $500-$2,000 to produce.
- He argues great podcasters stay independent as $1M-$10M businesses (Leo Laporte, Joe Rogan, Sam Harris, Adam Carolla). Networks struggle because hosts leave for life events or bigger deals. He believes Rogan and Laporte are above $5M.
- His own podcast started as recordings of two lunch conversations and turned into nearly 800 episodes and about 150,000 downloads per episode. He calls it a networking tool and a way to find founders to invest in.
- Open-source and defensibility: pick where you make money and make that part defensible. Google open-sources everything except its search algorithm; Facebook open-sources hardware but not its ad network or social graph.
