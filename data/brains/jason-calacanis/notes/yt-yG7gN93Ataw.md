## Surviving a downturn: the first moves Jason demands of founders

- Jason says to be brutally honest about the state of the business, get the data, build a plan, communicate it to the team, then execute. "Standard leadership stuff", and people respect transparent leaders.
- Without revenue and with a short runway, he says the company will likely die unless it acts. He puts the target at 18 months of cash. Six months of cash is a likely death.
- He uses a foxhole image: "we've got like six bullets each", so the plan can't be walking through the front door.
- Cutting costs is not optional. When a founder said they didn't want to run the company if they had to lay off two out of three people, Jason's reply was that they never should have started a company. His analogy is the pilot who lands the plane in the Hudson: "You don't have a choice."
- A founder's job in a crisis is to save as many jobs as possible by saving the business, so jobs can be brought back later.
- Take any available government money, such as PPP loans. In his words, if you don't apply "you're insane" because it is "essentially a grant".
- He advises weighing the employee tradeoff openly: with a million in the bank and a million a month burn, either everyone takes a pay cut or you cut people. His move is to survey the team on the maximum pay cut they could take, possibly traded for extra options, then decide.
- Furlough rather than agonize. "You can always hire people back, but you can't always save the company."
- He warns against "white knight syndrome". If an investor "is getting the term sheet together", set a deadline (he suggests Wednesday) and make the cuts if it doesn't arrive.

## Services revenue and survival tactics when revenue is thin

- Revenue is revenue. If a SaaS company can bring in consulting revenue, he says to take it.
- For a company with customers that have money, the move is to offer custom software or outsourced development, such as building the features a big customer asked for. His illustrative figure was a project of about $300,000 for the year.
- Normally he would reject a services business on margin terms, but in a crisis he says "you've got to eat".
- For R&D-heavy and hard-tech companies (med tech, biotech) that may run out of cash before revenue, he suggests consulting to potential customers, for example offering to run a customer's lab while the startup works on its own idea.
- Do not be "too precious". Survive at all costs.
- If the founder thinks of it as four to six weeks of cash rather than four to six months, the behavior changes. Do that mental exercise.

## Raising capital in a downturn

- Sequence matters. Make the cuts, extend the runway and drive revenue first, then talk to investors. Investors sort founders into people who want to win. Going in saying "I need a million for six months" with two payrolls in the bank gets "I'm not catching that falling knife".
- Show seriousness: you have cut, you took a 50% pay cut, co-founders took 60% and 10%. This reads as fundable and serious.
- Know your investor type. Angels investing from a balance sheet that just fell 30-40% will pause. Syndicates should be expected to take about twice as long to close and fill for about half the amount, possibly at half the valuation.
- Funds with dry powder get conservative and will double down on existing winners and thin the herd. He triages his own 100+ active portfolio companies first and says that will take him three to six months, so new investments will be "few and far between".
- Don't be precious about size or valuation. A founder who planned a $20M raise may end up raising $1M at $5M post. He says that may be the reality "and that's okay".
- Treat raising as sales: you are selling equity. If you can convince a sophisticated investor, you can sell customers and hire. He ranks the difficulty: investors are hardest, customers medium to hard or easy depending on price, and employees were hard but will become easy in a downturn.
- Trial close at the end of a pitch. He cited a founder who closed his laptop and asked the investor what he would need to see to write a check; the investor named milestones, and the founder returned later and got funded. A related close: ask "when should I come back to you, at what milestone?" and push for a benchmark.
- Prefer a fast no over a long maybe, and cross investors off if they are not a fit.

## What investors want to see now

- The era of growth at all costs is over for now. Investors want sustainable growth, cohorts of users who really use the product, and unit economics that work.
- He describes a pilot's checklist: look at airspeed and altitude, then go down the checklist. For a startup that means, for example, spending $6,000 to acquire a customer via Facebook and Instagram ads, then looking at what churn those customers show. Artificial growth gets discounted.
- If half your customers are at risk of churning and don't show true product-market fit, consider raising prices to push them out and focus on the top half.
- The pitch should be unvarnished: here is the reality, what is working and what isn't, the challenges and how you'll overcome them, and why you will come out the winner. "Figure it out later" won't fly.
- Don't spin or hide early numbers. Show weekly or monthly revenue and metrics rather than a quarterly view that hides bad months. Investors expect early numbers to be unpredictable, and he says the point is to help founders make them predictable. They will find out anyway. "Here's what we figured out, here's what we need help with" is attractive to people who like to solve problems.
- He recommends emailing him a revenue chart by week or month, plus a PDF deck, with no links to unknown files.

## Valuations and deal terms

- Jason predicts that seed valuations roughly halve. He remembers investing in Uber, Thumbtack and other eventual unicorns at about $4-5M valuations when they had products in market, and thinks we go back to that.
- His rule of thumb: half of the previous market rate. YC companies might drop by about a third, for example from $10-14M caps. Accelerator companies at roughly $6-10M caps might go to roughly $4-7M.
- Series B and breakout companies may take about a 30% haircut.
- Don't benchmark against anomalies (Zoom, Slack, Box, or Tom Cruise's pay). Breakouts are unusual. Uber looked like a money-losing business but in its early days the Lincoln Town Car service was printing money with near-zero CAC through over-the-shoulder virality.
- Founders who optimized for valuation are now telling him they over-optimized. He criticizes six-month notes with bumped caps (moving a cap from eight to twelve to raise another $600K for six months) and says raise a proper round with 18 months of runway. He calls the downside "risk of ruin".
- On safes and notes: he says the safe was almost a way to "troll investors" when it has no expiration date or cap. He recommends an expiration date, which forces everyone to sit down, re-evaluate and agree to extend, convert or return money. A note that never converts is bad for both sides, especially if the company breaks out. He cited TopTal as a case where investors were hurt because no equity round was ever done.

## How Jason picks investments and what Launch looks for

- He is sector agnostic. His biggest hits defined sectors that had no names yet: Robinhood and Wealthfront defined fintech, Uber and Airbnb defined the on-demand economy. His own Calm investment looked silly (people wrote about him losing money on a meditation app) until it worked, so he avoids trend-chasing.
- He looks for founders with resilience and leadership, who are self-possessed, confident, and will fight for their opinion. His test is to ask a question like "why consumer and not enterprise?" and see whether they defend their view. He says he is not trying to change their mind. He credits Uber's decision to be full-stack consumer as a decision where an investor pushing an enterprise-software model would have been wrong.
- His line: tactics matter less than what customers think and what the team sees in the results. The people on the front line have insight he won't.
- Launch's "Goldilocks zone" is about $250K a month in revenue, or a consumer product growing about 5% week over week. If a company has a competitive Series A going, it doesn't need Launch; if it has only an idea and no launched product, Launch is not that helpful. Launch helps with the bridge to a roughly $1M to $5M round. Its real contribution, he says, is "anointing": the Launch name signals a real company.
- Check size in the discussion: a $100K check, wired quickly once a company joins the accelerator.
- Launch plan at the time: more cohorts run virtually, with the goal of 100 companies a year at seven per cohort, around twelve cohorts a year.
- A prototype with no customers is not fundable for him. Free users are "freeloaders, not customers", so charge. He wants a couple of paying customers first, though he'll talk earlier.
- Jason notes that Travis Kalanick demoed Uber at his Open Angel Forum, where people watched a car pull up. His lesson: back people who take action, and outsiders who are authentic.

## Staying in touch with investors and cold outreach

- It is always a good time to contact investors if the business is good. Build a list of people who said no and send a short monthly update.
- Format: very short and tight. Lead with a chart of the true metric, then new customers, new hires, a press link, product screenshots or a video, a customer testimonial. "Less text, more visual, more data." Nobody cares about long narratives.
- Cold email example structure: acknowledge they are busy, mention something specific from a podcast or post of theirs, give one-line traction (for example 1,600 customers growing 20% a month, with a chart), offer to update, and state when the next monthly update will be sent.
- Add context: follow them on Twitter and connect on LinkedIn with a personalized note. Play the long game.

## Pivot versus persevere, and testing cheaply

- Persevere or pivot is a judgement call from the heart, the data and customers. Twitter meandered and then caught fire; Angry Birds came after dozens of tries. He worries about founders who pivot too often and also about those "banging on a door with their head" when it's a brick wall.
- He wants founders who stay for the mission, not mercenaries asking about exit. He still holds his Uber stake and thinks it is a trillion-dollar company.
- To pivot well, run the laziest, smallest test. Example: a landing page for an iMessage-based virtual admin for executives at $50 an hour or $500 a month. Advertise to 10,000 LinkedIn CEOs, take a credit card with charging to start June 1, and measure conversion and CAC before hiring anyone. Do not hire a team to pretend to be the product before you know demand.

## Market outlook, pitch decks and practical tips

- Do not time the market. It is never a bad time to start a great company. After 9/11 and 2008, Jason says the world mostly went back to normal with small tweaks (the TSA, then PreCheck and Clear as the "antibody"), so expect remote work and food delivery to accelerate rather than a wholly new category.
- He expects a "Yolo" rebound in travel, cruises, events and hotels, and likes competing in sectors that are getting crushed right now.
- Silicon Valley: roughly 80% of Launch companies are from outside the Valley. He sees Launch as an Ellis Island: sort the winners, anoint them, introduce them, and send them back to a low-cost location. Being close to capital helps in a crisis. He thinks commuting is a dumb cost, and that remote work will reduce pressure to take the company bus.
- Pitch deck: don't plaster it with COVID. Focus on customers, metrics, acquisition and stickiness. Include COVID only if it is relevant (his example is off-grid travel).
- Zoom pitching: use wired Ethernet (turn Wi-Fi off, buy the $10 dongle), a real microphone, good lighting and a quiet room. Set up a separate "presenter" user account on your computer with no notifications, no Spotify, and a clean desktop.
- If the business benefits from the crisis (online education, entertainment, telepresence), raise, invest in people, product and customer acquisition. Customer acquisition costs on Facebook and Google are probably down.
- On apps: the golden era isn't over. Consumer subscription changed things. A company can reach scale with about 15 to 30 people (he cited Calm and Instagram as small-team examples, and Fitbod among his own), but iOS developers are expensive.
