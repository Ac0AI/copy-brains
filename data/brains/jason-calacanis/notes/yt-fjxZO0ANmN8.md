## Angel investing: start small, start with revenue, learn by doing

- Jason's rule for a first-time angel: make your first 20 investments only in companies that already have a product in market and generate revenue. If someone pitches a pre-revenue company, "don't do it. Wait."
- Invest the minimum check you can. If the minimum is $5,000, ask whether you can do one or two slots instead of a full check.
- On his syndicate, a $1M allocation is split into roughly 250 slots of about $4,000. People ask to take a single slot and he says yes. He has had investors who wanted to put in $500 and he says yes if there is room.
- He encourages newcomers to take their time and learn how it works rather than rush in.
- He compares it to learning poker: you can learn just as well at a thousandth of the cost, so don't learn at a $25,000 buy-in table.
- He requires people in his syndicate to take his Angel University course (about $300, with the money donated to charity) and read his book (about $10-20). The fee is meant to stop people from burning seats.
- His first investment for a new syndicate member is typically $4-5K, which he calls effectively a small charitable donation as the cost of learning.

## How Jason evaluates startups: product and customers over charisma

- He bets on team, product and customers. Early on he looked at the product and the customer base more than the founder's personality.
- On charisma: "You can't fake a great product. You can't fake happy customers." He argues charismatic founders can be coached to fake charisma, so he discounts it, and says it will get easier to fake still.
- Examples of how he judged early bets:
  - Uber: he had known Travis for a while, the product was a done deal and customers were very happy.
  - Calm: he met Alex, the valuation was $4.5M, revenue was only about $10,000, and no one else would invest. He liked the product, and believed meditation could be huge. People thought the bet was foolish.
- Jason says he "wrote the million-dollar homepage" comment in jest about the Calm founder's homepage; the point is the product was excellent despite thin revenue.
- His investing team has 10-11 people. They take about 70 meetings a week, 50-60 of them first meetings, and invest in about two companies a week, usually one follow-on and one new. They have gone from 10-20 thousand pitches and thousands of meetings down to about 50 companies.
- He keeps questioning his own investments and uses writing (his book, deal memos, public trades) as a way to hold himself accountable.

## Deal memos and risk framing

- Jason writes a deal memo for each syndicate deal. He syndicates a $500,000 allocation across several hundred angels on his platform, now over 10,000 investors.
- His syndicate runs with over 11,000 accredited investors and more than 260 deals. He has invested in about 350 companies.
- He is deliberately candid in memos: founders can lie, take the money to Vegas, defraud a vendor or be incompetent. So "invest as little as you can."
- He uses the word "bet" on purpose so readers understand it is a bet, and he states how he is placing his own bet.
- He only lets accredited investors in, to follow the rules, even though non-accredited people would be 25 times more potential participants. Average check is now about $6-7K, up from $3-4K.
- On accreditation, he thinks it should work like a driver's license or a PADI scuba certification: read a book, take a test, spend 10 hours and $100-200. He doesn't think net worth should be the test, and finds it absurd that a non-accredited person can buy a leveraged-mortgage ETN through a brokerage but not invest in a friend's startup.
- He runs Angel University free for people who want help getting accredited.

## Cycles, downturns and preparing for a bust

- Jason says he warned in December 2018 that a long bull market was ending. His advice was to make sure you have a path to profitability, hold a healthy amount of cash in the account, and raise money at the top of the market. He admits his timing was off by a year or 18 months and takes little credit: "It's a pretty basic piece of advice."
- He calls the pull-back a stress test of everything: stocks, NFTs, crypto, real estate. He expects countless lawsuits, citing the dot-com era when litigation ran for years.
- On valuations, he points to companies valued at $100M with 4,000 users and no revenue, then $1B, then $4B, which did not make sense. He credits Clubhouse for taking a reset to about $250M and then executing.
- On large LPs: pension funds and endowments are sophisticated, diversified across cycles, and can handle losses in some funds. He worries less about them than about unfair outcomes for consumers, which will drive the most litigation.

## Crypto and Web3: what he thinks works and what doesn't

- Jason bought Bitcoin at about $2-3, then again at $100-200, and has made seven figures. He lost the early coins because he didn't have the keys and the site was hacked. He says it is not that he doesn't understand crypto.
- He says Bitcoin could go to zero (hack, ban, denial-of-service style attack, black swan) and that all technology is eventually replaced by something better. He was attacked for saying so.
- He calls 99% of ICOs and crypto projects things that never became anything reasonable, and says a significant portion were scams, incompetence or both. In startups, by contrast, you get paid after proving something.
- On retail losses: he suspects some VCs structured token deals (foundations in places like Panama, legal spend about 100 times normal) so they could exit positions before product-market fit. He frames this as speculation, not something he can prove. He notes that it was the retail buyers who ended up holding the tokens.
- He expects district attorneys, who want higher office or to keep their seats, to go after crypto fraud, and says the "this is not financial advice, do your own research" disclaimer will draw regulator attention.
- What he finds promising:
  - NFTs tied to real rights, such as club memberships (Soho House, a golf club) where a smart contract sets resale rules, e.g. return of the original investment plus a split of profits with the club.
  - DAOs for shared ownership, like a Tuscan farm held through an LLC with weekly use shares, or a scholarship fund where members vote on applications.
  - Gaming and prediction markets, such as online poker or an oil-price bet settled through an oracle, with no intermediary.
  - Free-to-mint NFTs, e.g. The 100 Thieves minting for about 700,000 people to celebrate a championship, as a way to avoid the need to sell.
- What he is skeptical about:
  - Blockchain as a database: slow and always behind modern tech.
  - Fully decentralized companies and DAO governance: "when things go badly... you need a general who can make decisions quickly."
  - Decentralized versions of eBay, PayPal, Stripe or Airbnb: he thinks most customers prefer a trusted central brand and want refunds when hacked.
- Pricing point: to compete, a crypto payment product should be about 10 times cheaper than the best existing deal. If Stripe is about 3%, aim for about 30 basis points. Gas fees are "ridiculous."
- He says Web3's real believers got drowned out by people there to flip assets, like the dot-com era, and hopes the crash pushes out 70-80% of players so those who can ship products remain.

## Jason's "J-trading" public-market experiment and edge

- Jason has historically held index funds (Vanguard, Wealthfront) plus real estate, a barbell with high-variance startup investing. He recommends most people use Wealthfront-style auto-invest: he got his whole family on it at $500-1000 per month.
- He set aside a few million dollars to trade public stocks, announcing trades on This Week in Startups with the hashtag #Jtrading. The plan is about 20 positions and about $1-2M, hold for 10 years, then publish the results and pick his best seven ideas.
- Trades he mentioned: Stitch Fix (he bought about 5,000 shares because he knew and trusted Bill Gurley, who bought a million shares), Disney (he thinks a bundle of Disney, Hulu and ESPN+ could reach 1 billion subscribers), and Amazon.
- His screen for a beaten-down stock: revenue, price-to-sales, user growth, cash on the balance sheet, and whether it is losing money. Example discussion on Snap: about $4.5B revenue, about $16B market cap, about $5B cash, so roughly 2-2.5x sales net of cash, with a possible acquirer such as Microsoft or Google. He called Evan Spiegel a product genius.
- BuzzFeed: he calls journalism a bad business with no viral growth path, thin cash and quarterly losses, so a falling knife. He would own Snap or Peloton before BuzzFeed.
- His edge principle: "I only want to bet money where I have an edge." He has an edge in private markets and poker, none in crypto, and is trying to build one in public markets. If he wanted a crypto edge, he'd stop other investing, start a crypto startup and hire 10 crypto developers and architects. He frames the trading money as his MBA tuition.
- Poker lesson: two small changes (tighter ranges out of position, folding weak suited hands under the gun) took him from losing three of four home games to winning most.

## Building a media business with friends (All-In) and starting new shows

- Jason says working with friends is great, but All-In is a special case: four alpha personalities, "like Game of Thrones."
- The show began when Chamath asked him to do a podcast; they added the two Davids. Jason focused on being a good moderator: challenging people, interrupting, forcing them to explain terms.
- The show grew to regularly rank in the top 30 podcasts. He says they got offers but none large enough to change their lives. About $5M a year in revenue, which he produces, and he says he created the name and format and does most of the work.
- Tension arose over the All-In Summit. Jason says the event could have made millions but they gave away half the tickets and spent heavily on a $600 gift bag. He asked for 2 extra equity points (31 for him, 23 each for the others, as CEO) to run a bigger media company, and said his 20-person team was pulled off investing work to produce the event.
- The four had different goals (Sacks wanted just the weekly podcast, Chamath and Jason had ambition, Friedberg had worries). They settled on 25% each and just the weekly podcast, no more events.
- They agreed on three things: they love doing it together, listeners love it, and they believe they are doing something that matters. He calls the third the key.
- His moderating approach on politics: steer toward first principles (fiscal conservatism, government staying out of personal lives, guns) rather than tribal fights about Trump, Biden or DeSantis.
- He is launching two more podcasts in September: Founder University (weekly, a 15-minute lesson on scaling a business, taught to his three portfolio companies already) and a roundtable with guests who can't join All-In because the All-In crew appears weekly, with Jason again as point guard.
- His method for building skill: pick a new skill and aim to reach roughly the top 10% (70th to 90th percentile). Moderating has led to TV deal inquiries.

## Staying motivated and spending money on experience

- Jason says he always wanted to be a millionaire and stop worrying about money, but he is not driven by needing more; he plays for fun and love of the game.
- After two friends died young (Dave Goldberg and Tony Hsieh), he reassessed what he actually enjoys. A friend pointed out that nearly everything he did was for others.
- Changes: he bought a ski house and skied 40 days in a year (more than the prior decade), bought a roughly $9,000 Specialized e-bike, and spends summers with his kids around Tahoe, keeping his calendar clear.
- His view: life is a collection of "best experiences" you laugh about with friends, so he tries to create more of them with his kids, wife, friends and business partners.
- He is learning to spend money on things that raise his happiness, which goes against his Irish Catholic Brooklyn upbringing. He has his chief of staff test-drive bikes and find an instructor.
- He has a 12-year-old and six-year-old twins, and tells people to enjoy every moment.
