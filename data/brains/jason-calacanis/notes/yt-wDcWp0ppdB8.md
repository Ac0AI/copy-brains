## Distribution comes from a product people won't shut up about

Jason's core claim is that virality starts and ends with a great product. His examples: Slack, Uber, Tesla and Snapchat.
- Tesla owners tell friends to drive Teslas. Snapchat users annoy friends by showing them how to use it.
- Uber users hand a friend a referral code so each gets a free ride.
- He says "the great products people can't wait to tell their friends about them."
- His order of operations: build the product that delights customers, then build everything else (press, brand, marketing spend) around it.
- A great product also "buys you more time to make mistakes" on marketing and hiring. Companies that are good at press, raising money and culture but weak on product waste that effort.
- His example of that failure is Fab.com: great at fundraising and design, but the product was not essential, so it never got a strong business model or margins.

## Testing for virality with Net Promoter Score

Jason uses NPS as the test for whether customers are truly engaged.
- Question: how likely are you to refer this product to a friend, on a scale of 1 to 10.
- 9 or 10 is an advocate (+1), 7 or 8 is indifferent (0), 6 or under is a detractor (-1).
- Products with the highest NPS are the ones people won't shut up about.
- A product stuck at 7 to 8 can still be a good business, but it lacks virality. It "better have margins or monopolies or something."
- At the early stage he doesn't need everything figured out. He wants a founder who studies the few 9s and 10s to learn why they love it.
- His hypothetical: a streaming startup finds its few superfans love documentaries, which are cheaper to license, so it uses documentaries as its beachhead against Netflix.
- He credits this lean-startup style: form a thesis, measure it, and "if you measure it you can manage it."

## Referral incentives and the half-life of marketing tactics

- A "give $10, get $10" referral is a timeless channel because people feel good giving friends a discount.
- Economics: if acquiring a customer costs about $30 on Facebook, a $10 give plus $10 get can be cheaper. Without referrals Uber would pay more through radio or other expensive channels.
- Most marketing tactics have a half-life. Of 20 tips he could give, half would stop working in six months and the rest would work half as well.
- Infographics are his example: once everyone sent them to journalists, they became "the opposite of what journalists want to see."
- Marketing channels burn out, so the durable foundation is the product.

## Fund marketing from a profitable core, and pick the right press

- Marketing spend is only available once the product or business makes money. Then you can afford to lose money on marketing.
- His own case: Launch events run at breakeven because the firm makes money by investing in companies. That let them spend on Instagram, Facebook and Twitter ads and give away about 1,500 free tickets for Launch Festival Sydney.
- Big brands buy bus and outdoor ads without proof it works. They spend 5%, 10% or 20% of profits on advertising to start a positive flywheel.
- Rule of thumb for experiments: if it works, double the spend; if not, cut it. A $100,000 radio test at $10M in revenue is 1% of yearly revenue.
- Match press to vertical. TechCrunch or Mashable may come by default from raising money, but you may need a trade publication for lawyers if you sell legal software.
- Ring needed local news in high-crime areas to move doors out the door fast.

## Spend frugally early, then deploy capital where it pays

- Decide each purchase by ratio, not by principle.
- A $20,000 sales deck is a no-brainer if the company prints money and customers spend $250,000. It's a bad idea for a startup with $50,000 in the bank and two customers, where it is 40% of the capital.
- Early on, learn a basic deck, buy a template for about $50, and call it a day.
- He used to handle his own legal work, then outsourced the parts that needed a lawyer.
- Be frugal early so you can deploy capital correctly later, then loosen up once you have money.
- His "6 out of 10" logo rule: spend about 20 hours learning to make a decent logo, then hire a professional once you've raised.

## Resourcefulness and starting before you feel ready

- He argues most people "overestimate what it takes" and set up their own roadblocks. His shorthand is "RTFM," read the manual.
- He looks for resourceful founders. Reaching about 60 to 70% of an expert's skill takes roughly 50 to 100 hours, so the skill to have is acquiring new skills quickly.
- Podcast gear is the example: roughly $150 microphones are fine, since only the top 10 to 15% of listeners notice a $3,000 mic. Content comes first, but bad audio will lose people.
- He fixed his tennis-ball machine and pool heater by looking up the parts online, so he knew the board cost about $300 before hiring a plumber.
- Podcast origin: he says he recorded for selfish reasons, to talk to people he wanted relationships with, and the audience was secondary. He did Calacanis Cast first, then This Week in Startups.
- Kevin Pollak started his chat show by simply being handed the studio keys on Sundays. The lesson: do the behavior and you are that thing.

## How Jason evaluates founders and finds deal flow

- He backs the founder on objective evidence: what they built, how they built it, what customers think. Examples: a Google sales VP making sales tools, or a founder whose product spread across eight campuses.
- He asks short questions and listens to how well-considered the answers are, such as whether they interviewed incumbents' staff to learn the real margins.
- Common thread in winners (Uber, Thumbtack, Robinhood, Wealthfront, Desktop Metal, Datastax): passionate, resourceful founders who care about their market.
- Randomness still applies: timing, competitors, and whether technology is ready (YouTube before cheap bandwidth, ride services before GPS phones).
- He is a founder-focused investor, not industry-focused, because nobody can define the next big category on demand.
- Deal flow is a funnel from his podcast, events, Founder University, social media and book. Reputation for being helpful matters, so founders recommend him.
- He travels to cities (Miami, Orlando, Iowa, Sydney) and finds two or three good companies per trip.
- He says about 20 to 30 great companies emerge each year, and his "Goldilocks zone" is post-idea, pre-Series A: a small team, a minimum viable product, and a few customers, often with $1M to $2M raised.
- His first filter for investors reaching out is a built product, since ideas are cheap and "execution is far more important."

## Advice for new angel investors

- Use bankroll management: decide a percentage of net worth to deploy (e.g. 10%) and don't blow past it with bridge rounds into a friend-of-a-friend's startup.
- Go where the deal flow is: Silicon Valley, New York, Seattle, Boston and other top hubs. Investing in your home town usually means weaker deals.
- Make small bets of $10K to $25K and learn the game, like entering a $20 poker tournament instead of the $100,000 buy-in table.
- Don't invest pre-product. Plenty of companies have finished products and are raising, and you can be the fifth or sixth investor and follow signals from better investors.
- Be wary of charismatic founders who take $250,000 and pay down debt, and never ship.
- His syndicate at Jason.syndicate.com has about 2,400 members who can join deals from $1,000 to $3,000 up to $20K to $40K, starting small and growing.
- Liquidity advice: sell secondary shares along the way instead of waiting for a single exit. He sold Web Logs Inc. to AOL after about 18 months for $30 million, having turned down a $20 million exit earlier in a prior company. He says the quick way to get "poor" is to sell too early, and founders should think in decades.
