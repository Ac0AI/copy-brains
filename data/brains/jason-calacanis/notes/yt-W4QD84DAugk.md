## Fundraising: why "too early" really means "wrong investor for your stage"

- Jason says to judge an investor by behavior, not words: "the best way to judge a person is to look at their previous behavior."
- Check what stage they have actually invested in. A family office that writes $30M private-equity checks for friends' deals will not write a $125K-$250K seed check, however nice the meeting felt.
- Investors say nice things in social settings to everyone, so he tells founders to "divorce the nice things they're saying from the reality of what they do."
- If an investor says you're "too early," go to the programs built for that stage first: Y Combinator, Techstars, Antler, a16z, Pear (summer program), Sequoia Arc, and his own Launch Accelerator.
- Take the $25K, $125K or $250K check there, get your first five customers, then return to the bigger investors.
- His own programs target year zero: Founder University runs six cohorts a year across three geographies (Riyadh, Tokyo, US), wants two or three founders, and does not require incorporation or customers.
- The accelerator investment wants you incorporated.

## The 2026 seed funnel: contact 150 funds to get two term sheets

- His numbers for a seed round today: contact 150 seed funds, get about 50 meetings, convert about 15 (10% of the list) into second meetings, and get two term sheets.
- Judge only second meetings. People are not obligated to take one, so a second meeting is a real signal.
- Stories of founders who "instantly get funded" without trying are memorable because they are not the norm.
- Treat it as a sales funnel and qualify each investor:
  - Do they invest at your stage?
  - Are they actively investing, or in maintenance mode between funds?
  - What check size do they write?
  - Do they lead or follow?
  - Do they take cold email, or only intros?
- Fundraising is a full-time job. With three co-founders, one handles product, one sales, and one corporate development, which "is another fancy way of saying raising money."
- Build deep relationships with investors over time.
- Same lesson as job hunting or content creation: you fill out 100 applications for one job.

## Finding the investors who fit your company

- Investors differ in thesis and process. Jason knows a True Ventures investor in his 60s who only takes meetings via another VC or a portfolio founder.
- Jason's Launch (launch.co/apply) meets about 10% of applicants. Some weeks bring 200 to 500 applications, and they meet about 25 a week (it used to be 140, which was too many).
- Per year that is 3,000-5,000 meetings and about 100 investments, from 10,000-20,000 applications.
- Firms have sector preferences. They don't do biotech, and they have been looking at military tech.
- Research method: use a Crunchbase account, follow firms on social media, and build a list of about 150 firms that invest at your stage in similar companies.
- Pitch the pattern match. Example: he did Thumbtack (marketplace) and Robinhood and Wealthfront (fintech), so a fintech marketplace pitch fits him.
- If an investor only takes intros, meet their founders first. To reach 10 target firms, email about 50 founders, get about 20 meetings, and hope to reach 3-4 firms through them.
- Launch has a tool called Whisper Network where founders search and request introductions. Even with it, "you're hunting, you're pecking."
- Creative visibility works too. One founder put "AI B2B startup raising pre-seed, product is live, come say hi" on his laptop in a San Francisco cafe, and several investors came up to him.
- Jason's view is that the Bay Area's culture of helping people without expecting short-term returns is why being physically there matters.

## How the cost of starting a company collapsed, and what it changes for investors

- The biggest shift versus 10 years ago is the capital and time needed to reach product-market fit.
- Web 1.0 era (1990s):
  - A seed round was about $3M, with 20-30 people.
  - Reaching market took about 12 months.
  - Spending went on a PR firm (about $10K/month for 18 months, roughly $180K), an office at about $25K/month, a $250K letter of credit, about $100K of legal, and HR, accounting and legal hires (about $250K).
  - He totals this at roughly $3M before launch.
- Cloud era: about $300K, three people eating ramen at home, a startup standing up in under 6 months.
- Today: about $30K for servers, often with free credits. Founders vibe-code a product, get a first customer, and then apply to YC, Techstars, Antler or Launch Accelerator.
- A typical applicant now says they "vibe coded a product, got to 100K in revenue" and then applied.
- Idea to first customer (a light form of product-market fit) is now weeks or a weekend.
- His example is the bounties program (twistartups.com bounties): the real-time fact-checker bounty drew 15 submissions, about three really good ones, in under 30 days. A second $5,000 bounty was for "annotated."
- He wants to hire winning bounty founders and incubate the product as a real startup.

## Building a moat against OpenAI, Anthropic and other frontier labs

- The big LLM interface will always leave out features, because of "clutter and confusion."
- His example is Rome, a travel startup that built an interface on top of a model. It returned money to investors after ChatGPT absorbed the basic function, but its approach points to the real moat.
- Build features and community around the model output that the labs won't:
  - Multiplayer mode: a group researching a Tokyo trip together, seeing each other's picks, voting, and adding to a shared agenda.
  - A virtual travel agency: an AI-assisted human planner paid by the hour.
  - Ground support: matching travelers with a local guide, such as a food guide who skips the tourist-trap lines.
- His summary: they "are just going to give you your basic output," so own the community, marketplace and human service layered on top.
- His example of AI plus a human service layer: AI planning that ends in someone on the ground who gets you in line, translates, and arranges a back-kitchen ramen visit.
- Tax is another vertical he names where this pattern applies.

## Hardware is back as a moat

- Investors flipped on hardware: it used to be "hardware is hard, I don't want any part of it," and now it is "one of the only moats left."
- Hardware gives lock-in and raises the fidelity and usefulness of the product. His examples: Plaud, and Whoop.
- His own hardware investments: Terra Cafe and Cafe X (both still going; he says Cafe X is doing well), plus a Butterfly (a Dropcam/Ring competitor) and an early smoke detector that failed. Most fail and a few succeed.
- For a bootstrapped hardware founder nearing profitability, he says to raise money from investors who are comfortable with the capital needs.
- Work backward from the many existing hardware investors.
- Kickstarter and viral ads are still viable for the first thousand customers paying far above eventual retail, since early adopters "just want to be the first to have it."

## Robotics business model idea: price per hour, lease to consumers

- Sell robots like labor: if Amazon pays about $27/hour (about $35 fully loaded with insurance and workers' comp) to sort packages, a robot maker like Optimus or Figure could offer a robot at about $4/hour with on-site maintenance.
- At about 8,000 hours a year, that is roughly $32K a year per robot.
- His guess is that commoditized robots will ultimately cost "a dollar or $2 an hour."
- For consumers, he would abstract it like a car lease: about $299/month for 500 hours, with overage at a dollar an hour.
- He likens security robots to guards who earn about $20/hour: the robot never sleeps and records 360 degrees.

## Training and credentialing: associate program and a possible Founder University degree

- His firm is hiring six associates-in-training in June for the venture side: researcher, then analyst, then associate, over about three years, with pay in the $60K-$90K range.
- He hires about two to three times as many as needed because one or two will opt out or be let go, and about one-third become stars.
- His framing: "instead of paying for a degree in venture capital, we pay you."
- Plan: two professional-training sessions a week (Monday morning, Friday end of week) with the rest of the week spent in a business unit. His goal is to make it good enough to charge about $50,000 for a two-year version.
- Benchmark: Kauffman Fellows charges about $80,000 for two years, which he calls overpriced and elitist; he thinks he could run a better one with 10 people at $50K each ($500K) and two full-time staff.
- He believes his version would be better because fellows go to Saudi Arabia and Tokyo and interview companies.
- Legal constraint: he can give a certificate or diploma but not a degree in the US without a partner university, though UK universities may allow a UK-accredited degree.
- Ideas: a two-year Founder University that combines the founder and venture tracks (major one, minor the other).
- He would add media skills (newsletter, social, podcast), because many brilliant founders and investors are "dead air" on a podcast.

## Personal wealth and who Jason criticizes

- He puts "F-you money" at exactly $10M: at 5% that is $500K a year, or $400-500K after tax, enough to "just exist."
- His criticism is of behavior, not personal feuds. On Mark Zuckerberg, he says every time he had a chance to do the right thing, he "picked his own self-interest."
- His examples are teenage girls and Instagram, user privacy, and how partners and founders were treated in Meta's partner program.
- He also cites blatantly copying others' ideas, and optimizing platforms to keep people online.
- He says it is not personal; he hasn't spoken with Zuckerberg in person in a decade.
- On Sam Altman, he says he does not like what he did to Elon Musk, whom he calls a friend.
- He notes both men are among the most successful in business history and that self-interested, burn-bridges decisions can be "a quick path to extraordinary success."
- On the media, he distrusts press pieces and viral clips. He says that when he reports news, a low-double-digit percentage of the time it is not exactly accurate, so he adds "allegedly" and disclaimers, and thinks before reacting.
- He declined a New Yorker request to comment on Altman, with no business interest and a policy of not talking to the press.
- He also says he regrets how harshly he reacted to a military-tech founder who was fired, calling it kicking a man when he was down.
