## Early ventures and the path from IT to publishing

- Jason grew up in Brooklyn, the child of a bartender and a nurse. He describes the family as "barely middle class and scraping by month to month."
- His parents couldn't afford college. He studied at night at Fordham for five years (about $10,000 a year) while working days fixing laser printers, then moved into IT, setting up Novell networks.
- At Sony in the early 1990s he built "Jason's News," a Lotus Notes app that pulled news from LexisNexis. He emailed it to senior executives, and it ended up being read daily by the Sony leadership.
- He cold-searched a Sony director's press history, sent him the file, and got invited to lunch. That relationship led to the Sony jet and an exec-level internet advisory role at age 23.
- He started Silicon Alley Reporter at 25. It peaked at 70 employees and $11.6 million in revenue, built on credit cards.
- After the bust it shrank to 10 employees and $600,000 in revenue. He says: "I learned more on the way down than on the way up."
- He later started the blog company (Weblogs Inc.) from an idea on the night of Patrick Ewing's retirement ceremony. He proposed 10 or 20 vertical blogs, his developer partner said it would need 100, and Jason replied "let's do it!"

## Perseverance, resilience and resourcefulness

- He names perseverance, resilience and resourcefulness as the most important entrepreneur traits, with resilience probably first: staying positive while "getting your ass kicked."
- At Mahalo, "resourceful" and "resilient" are two of the 10 words in the culture document, and he uses them to hire. His test for resourceful: someone who can "get a dollar out of a nickel" or solve a problem nobody has solved in 10 years.
- If you're about to give up, he says you may not be cut out for it. If you persevere through two or three attempts and learn each time, "you will succeed."
- "You miss 100% of shots you don't take." Ask whether in 20 years you'd rather say you tried and failed than say "I had the same idea for Twitter, I just didn't do it."
- Even "the silliest entrepreneur on their worst day" is more impressive than someone punching a clock.
- He says entrepreneurship is typically a younger person's game. Someone with two kids working 40 hours a week on a startup has it harder than a founder who doesn't sleep.

## When to listen to advice and when to ignore it

- Knowing when to listen and when to ignore is one of the hardest things for young entrepreneurs. "Consider the source."
- He now avoids ideas most people immediately understand: if the majority likes it, it has probably been tried, everyone has the idea, or it won't be big.
- He wants an idea where about 5% say it's brilliant, 10% say it could be interesting, and 85% don't get it.
- His Human Power Search (Mahalo 1.0) idea failed. Tell-tale sign: he wasn't using it himself. He realized that even if Google has 3-4 spammy results in 10, there are 6-7 good ones, and hitting back is low effort. "People just adapt to imperfect situations."
- He calls it his Newton: "too early," but some people were jazzed about it.
- Product check questions: Are people getting value? Do you use it yourself? Do your employees? Do people use it repeatedly? Are you part of someone's everyday life?
- "All of my success in life has come when I've focused on the product." He cites Silicon Alley Reporter, TechCrunch, Autoblog, Joystick and the Mahalo video pivot.

## Timing, iteration and small decisions

- Success is "all timing and iteration." YouTube won after thousands of failed web-video attempts because bandwidth dropped, storage collapsed in cost, Flash made playback easy, and syndication made sharing possible. "3-4 things unlocked that magic."
- Facebook was not the first social network. It was the first with a good clean UI.
- Friendster had a similar UI but its servers weren't kept up. MySpace could have won with reliable servers and a more professional look. LinkedIn might have won with public profiles and messaging. If Twitter hosted images, Facebook might be smaller.
- His point: "little decisions along the way can make a big difference."

## Search, switching costs and not playing games you can't win

- Beating an incumbent search engine requires being 2-3 times better. "The mental cost of switch is high, people think it's low." Nobody switches for something 10-20% better.
- Swap the logos on search results and testers can't tell the difference. Put the logos back and they say the brand they know is better. He calls that a lesson in brand.
- Google's relevancy relied on domain age, inbound links and page count. Demand Media bought an 11-year-old domain, added millions of pages and built links. J.C. Penney bought links at scale. The "emperor has no clothes" exposure makes it easier to game.
- He predicted Google would move to social signals, time on page and browser data, turning the index upside down. A year earlier he pivoted Mahalo out of the content-farm race for that reason.
- On partners: "the worst thing you can do to a partner is make them look stupid." eHow boasting that its algorithm outsmarts Google invites retaliation.
- "You never get high on your own supply." Know the game you're playing: "Me vs. anybody at Google in poker, I'm rich; I will win." He would not play chess or write an algorithm against Google.
- When he can't win, he changes the rules. He would publicly show a $1,000 page next to the three pages ranking above it and campaign publicly for the problem to be fixed.

## Content economics: why depth loses in SEO and how Mahalo pivoted to video

- Ad-supported content has a perverse incentive: the better your page solves a problem, the less people click ads. Spending $10 on 100 thin pages beat spending $1,000 on one deep video series.
- Jason spent $500-$3,000 on a series of videos with an expert in HD, with Q&A and articles, and still got beaten by thin pages. He described the 100-page strategy as roughly 300-to-1 odds against a single deep page.
- His Mahalo pivot was expert-led educational video, in the style of Khan Academy (one teacher, 3,000 lessons) and Lynda.com. Targets: music, food and video games, then language, math and science.
- Claimed numbers at the time:
  - 26,000 videos produced
  - 30 million views the previous month, 12 million site visitors
  - 105 staff, planning 100 more video editors
  - 800-900 videos a week, planning about 2,500 a week with more studios
  - A target of 500,000 videos in 2 years
- His video unit economics: a guitar video ("How to Play Hotel California") cost about $100 and got 500,000 views in 6 months. At a $3 CPM that is about $3,000 a year and $10,000 over 3 years. Related songs cross-link and compound.
- About 95% of views came from YouTube. Even with TubeMogul distribution, "YouTube is where it's at." His rule: when something is working, focus on the platform that matters rather than building for five.
- Casting: he picks teacher credentials over presentation skills when forced to choose.
- Evergreen topics (a quadratic equation is the same tomorrow) and translation make each video reusable. He expected US internet companies to make two-thirds of revenue outside the US.
- His vision: one brand that teaches everything, with video, articles and expert Q&A together. Lesson plans and exercises come next.
- He says design matters less than assumed: eBay, Craigslist and Huffington Post are ugly. "Community and content trumps the look and feel."

## Running Launch as a founder-first conference

- His conference strategy was pricing as disruption. DEMO charged about $20,000 for a six-minute slot, TechCrunch50 charged startups nothing, and at Launch "I pay you."
- The Launch ticket tiers: $1,000 for non-bootstrap startups, about $400 for regular startups, with 10-25% off for people who follow him on Twitter and read his newsletter, and a "sob story" email route.
- Initially he expected to lose about $150,000 and was running at a loss or break-even, with any profit reinvested in the companies.
- He gave away roughly 10 free Demo Pit tables (a $1,000-$1,500 value each) via Hacker News and Quora, asking only that attendees "kick ass."
- He kept the focus on the 40 startups. He kept his own company's presence off the event and opposed staging fights between big names (the Disrupt direction), so the headline would be the startups' moment. "Everyone can win, it's not a zero sum game."
- Mandatory rehearsals: he scored each rehearsal and wouldn't let anyone onstage under an 8, but wouldn't let them drop out either. He compares himself to the man at the back of the run with a stick. "It's so awesome to watch companies go from having a great product but a terrible presentation to having both."
- He asked rehearsing companies for their number one need ("venture capital and angels"). He then invited the open angel forum members free and personally emailed whole VC firm teams a form letter ("Will no one come as my guest?"), which got replies. He then offered VIP party access.
- On conference pricing generally: SXSW grew from about 500 to 5,000 people by staying affordable ($450-$800). He now sends his best developers to conferences as a reward.
- He wants more builders (developers, product managers) rather than only CEOs, who he says give "bullshit press releases." "There's an inverse correlation of how much money you've made and how freely you speak."
- Origin: TechCrunch50 came out of a 2006 dinner where he told Mike Arrington to run an event that picks startups on merit instead of selling stage time.

## Fundraising, exits and financial realism

- He took on consulting income while building businesses rather than going all-in: "fuck you I have to pay my rent!" A severance from his Venture Reporter sale (19 months remaining on his deal) funded a year of building Weblogs Inc.
- He says it's easy to say "it's not about money" once rich. Venture capital means "venture" plus "capital," and exits are the point. "Anyone who says this industry is not about money is lying."
- He sold Weblogs Inc. after 18 months with $10,000 left in the account and a $30 million offer: "what would you do? Take the money!"
- "If they offer you $100 million, take the money for God's sake!" He thinks young entrepreneurs get bad advice not to sell. Turning down a disproportionate offer is "in a way saying you'll never have another good idea."
- His first company got a $20 million offer that he declined; he later got under $1 million. That stung for a long time, because it was the money meant for his parents. He now plays long and aims for a top-25 site.
- Selling makes sense at the peak when the business can't get much bigger (his example: Huffington Post). Keep the chips on the table when growth is visible (Facebook, Groupon), where a big stack lets you "play some marginal hands." Know when to cash in.
- He saw angel rounds overpriced: valuations double or triple what they should be. Angel rounds should be $1-3 million, not $6-8 million. He was sitting those out unless he received an advisory stake.
- He argued 2011 was not a bubble: Groupon, LinkedIn and Facebook were profitable, scalable businesses. Mahalo's 105 staff were funded by revenue and he didn't need to raise more.

## Positioning, press and fighting as an underdog

- He deliberately embraces the outsider and underdog role: "I like it better when I had to fight my way up." He picks fights on behalf of startups against entrenched players that prey on them (DEMO, pay-to-pitch forums).
- Angry emails and attempts to blackball him are a signal: "when people start trying to blackball you or get you fired or shut you down, that means you're on the right track." If people in power get concerned, you're disrupting something.
- Press advice for founders: ignore it. Whatever is written is "in the rearview mirror," writers often don't understand the business, and headlines are written separately from the story and optimized for clicks.
- He criticizes journalism for hating on everything and writing anti-success stories. His newsletter is deliberately a celebration of what's great.
- Location: he based Mahalo in LA because he met his wife there. LA lets him be the big fish in a small pond for hiring, since no one leaves Mahalo for a local rival. The big Valley companies pay about 50% more for the same people. He thinks New York funding may be easier than the Valley because many East Coast VCs won't take a long flight.
