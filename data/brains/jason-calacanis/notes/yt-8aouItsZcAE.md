## How the LAUNCH Fund Started

Jason's first fund came out of the LAUNCH Festival, his free startup conference. At the 2013 festival, David Sacks suggested Jason pick the top five conference startups each year and said he'd put in $50K each, a mini fund of about $250K. Jason says he had already been forwarding founders to investors for free, and he first doubted angel investing. His thesis was that entrepreneurs in their prime should put all their money into their own company and stay focused. Roelof Botha and Alfred Lin then showed him Sequoia's Scouts program, which gave $25K to $50K checks. At that time, $50K into a $5M company bought about 1%.

## Raising LAUNCH Fund One

- The plan: raise $10M, make roughly 100 investments of $100K to $250K, and take a 20% carry.
- He ran it as a side hustle while running other companies.
- He first collected $50K checks from friends, including poker friends.
- A mutual billionaire friend then allocated $5M, half the fund. Jason calls this the big moment: "you don't know where your luck's gonna happen."
- He closed the $10M quickly and deployed it over about three years. He then ran LAUNCH Fund II and says Fund III was wrapping up.
- He announced it on September 24, 2013 by email to his big list, saying he would invest in 100 to 200 startups over five years.
- His advice is to say yes to things. A few a year lead nowhere, but the good things in his life came from taking that leap.

## Syndicate rules and economics

- Naval taught him how SPVs and syndicates work. In Calm, he put $50K from the fund, shared it with about 150 syndicate members, and $328K came in.
- That was about 6.5x extra capital on a $50K check, so he describes it as a $10M fund plus a $60M fund. It didn't happen on every deal.
- The rules of the road:
  - The fund gets 100% of deals. No deal is syndicated unless the fund is in it.
  - LPs are the highest class and have access to every deal.
  - Once the fund hits its target check, any leftover allocation is offered to the founder to syndicate.
  - Founders choose; about 50% of them syndicate, so syndicate members see about half of all deals.
- Founders decline for these reasons:
  - Their round is oversubscribed.
  - They don't want the 4 to 6 week process, which requires a deal memo and a webinar to the syndicate (then about 300 members, later about 9,500).
  - They already have other funding sources.
- Founders who do it get these benefits:
  - In Calm, Jason went from owning 1% to 5% through the syndicate.
  - About 150 people at roughly $6K to $7K each are voting with their dollars. They become advocates who reply to asks like needing a CFO, an intro to Disney or a patent attorney.
  - It adds 150 investors as one cap table line (the SPV), a way to turn a company's supporters into loyal advocates.
- By 2022 his biggest syndicate deal was $6M. Early deals averaged $200K to $300K, so one deal now equals about 20 early deals.
- He says people claimed syndicates, equity crowdfunding and micro funds would never work, and "innovation in venture capital, everybody's like this can't work and then it inevitably does."

## Mistakes and fixes

- He charged no management fee, only legal and accounting costs, and the media company paid for everything else. He now calls that "a dumb decision."
  - A 2.5% fee is about $250K a year, enough for a couple of support staff.
  - Fees come out of the returns: on a $10M fund returning $20M, carry is $4M, and four years of $250K fees would be netted out to $3M.
- He believes a16z hired rich partners so they needn't take big salaries, which let the firm fund marketing and HR departments.
- LAUNCH was first to add a fee to every syndicate deal: $5K on small deals, $10K up to $1M, and 1% over $1M. It is taken out of the money invested, so it doesn't affect the founder.
- The fee pays for support staff who answer the phone about K-1s, updates and trust name changes. He says many syndicates failed because they had no support and no fee, and compares it to a hotel with no front desk.

## Events, deal flow and the accelerator

- He split from his former partner in the TechWrench 50 conference, which he calls "the greatest thing ever." Once he controlled the event, he made it free, even the demo pit tables. The largest SF one hit 15,000 attendees, and he ran two years in Australia.
- Lesson: if you pick a cynical, dark partner, things trend dark.
- Format: 50 companies, 3 minutes each, with pitch coaching and an invitation to invest.
- Many founders couldn't close with investors, so he started the LAUNCH Accelerator, first called an incubator. He says incubating means hatching an idea, while accelerating means an MVP plus a couple customers, then adding customers and capital. He has run 24 classes and says it is a better product than YC's for founders.
- He doesn't want to scale it far: going from 7 to 14 to 21 companies per class "is not fun for me, it's not magical."
- Deal flow was his early edge. Now it is a burden: about 200 qualified contacts per week and 50 meetings a week as a firm.
- He sends founders to the team first, with the goal that your conversation with him is already a fit and starts with your business.

## When he knew it worked, and non-consensus bets

- Signals: people asked to buy his positions in Uber, Thumbtack and Robinhood. Up rounds followed.
- Calm was the best example. He watched revenue and cash balances grow, especially after Apple moved apps from a one-time $10 charge to subscriptions. The company later converted at about $250M and offered him a chance to sell some shares, locking in 50x.
- The Calm team had met 40 investors, all said no, and LAUNCH was the only yes. They said they would shut down without it. He says non-consensus bets that work are what make you an investor.
- He says Travis Kalanick credited him on Uber, noting he supported founders for 10 years and brought three of the first five investors.
- He knew he'd be good at it from day one because friends he referred to VCs built multi-billion dollar companies, and journalism gave him a sense for people who are bullshitting or media-trained.
- Advice to new investors: watch 100 pitches and see the pattern. Compare revenue, valuation, headcount, and customer types (pilot, Kickstarter, real) and business models (SaaS or one-time hardware). Founders self-select for charisma, so ask for margins, customers and the books.

## Fund manager commandments

- Build a wide LP base so you aren't dependent on one LP. His first LP was 50% of the fund.
- Make capital calls less annoying. Funds like Sequoia call money when needed, because IRR starts when the money is drawn. Twelve calls of $4K each annoy LPs, so he now tells LPs to expect larger calls like 25% at a time.
- Take your time, and concentrate. Top LPs told him he was an idiot for not following on, and that you need 5 to 15% of a big winner rather than 50 basis points.
  - His targets and examples: 10% ownership, Calm about 5%, Superhuman 2%, Grin 12%, and 6% in another.
- Get a board seat or observer seat. Without one you miss the next round, which closes before you hear about it.
  - On boards he tells founders to skip the funding tour: "last round was 10 million, I think the company's worth 20, would you like us to put a million dollars in at 20."
- Have a multi-fund strategy and find your "Goldilocks zone" in stage, founder type, sector or geography, then press that advantage. YC doubles its accelerator rather than adding a Series A fund.

## Scaling the team

- He no longer takes most first meetings. Most founders are too early, so they are routed to Founder University (a 12-week or 2-day program) or the Accelerator.
- He delegated legal, accounting and similar work because his superpower is communication, not spreadsheets.
- He is moving to where the team makes accelerator and Founder University acceptances, and he meets companies once they are in.
- He compares it to sports: stars need a team in the playoffs. Breakout success comes with a team, and he enjoys training people to do the job.
- He is the pioneer of the journalist-plus-investor model and expects more.
