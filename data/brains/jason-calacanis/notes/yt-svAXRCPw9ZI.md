## Interviewing: listen, then double-click on the last answer

Jason studied Mike Wallace (60 Minutes), Oprah, Howard Stern and Charlie Rose as interviewers. His main rule: "the next best question is embedded in the previous answer."

- Many interviewers are slaves to a question list. They get nervous as an answer ends and queue the next question instead of listening.
- His fix: take a breath, listen, then double-click on something in the answer. This keeps the interviewer in sync with the audience, who don't have the question list.
- He thinks about the audience throughout. He asks "can you unpack that?" and re-explains acronyms such as GDP. He argues slowing down can speed things up because it takes people back to first principles.
- A period is sometimes the right end to an answer. Not every answer needs a follow-up.
- On Charlie Rose: his rambling, multi-angle questions gave the guest several directions to go and time to think.
- On Oprah: she used silence and empathy to draw out emotion.

## Media and content playbook: treat every new medium as a sandbox

Jason has started roughly seven media companies over three decades. His approach: "what if it works?"

- When a new medium appears, he assumes it will work and goes in early. If it fails, he says nobody remembers the failure.
- He started Silicon Valley Reporter in 1995 as a photocopied zine. He made photocopies at a copy shop and handed them out at a party. The back page said "Subscribe for 100 bucks to 10 issues a year", and hundreds of people tore it off and mailed him $100. That was tens of thousands of dollars in subscriptions, enough to quit working for others. It grew into a 300-page glossy that hit $12 million in revenue in its top year, built off his credit card.
- Weblog Inc.'s Engadget became the number one blog in the world. He credits Peter Rojas, Ryan Block and Brian Alvey.
- Mahalo used a wiki to build search-result pages ("SERPs") with images, answers and content. It reached $10 million in revenue. Google then de-indexed it, even though Google was its ad partner, and revenue fell to about $500k. He says this is what Perplexity does today.
- He says content creators are being hurt by AI. The New York Times, the music industry and Reddit need to hold the line and insist on being paid to be indexed.

## Where he sees media opportunity now

- Video is the current currency. Short-form video that pulls viewers into long-form is one opportunity. He says he doesn't see anyone doing it well yet.
- Vertical micro-dramas (mini soap operas for phones) are big in Asia, mostly aimed at middle-aged and older women. Great content often starts as genre, so he expects "a Tarantino of vertical short storytelling" and an award-winning version.
- He expects new IP (a Star Wars-style franchise) to come from VR or AR rather than books or film.
- He cites KPop Demon Hunters. Singalong screenings drew people into theaters.
- He points to Dasha and Anna's Red Scare podcast, Brett Easton Ellis and Pete Pachal's Stay Tuned as successful paid or fringe podcasts. He pays for these. His point is that authentic creators who aren't "trying to do a podcast" resonate.
- He thinks the podcasting space is saturated, and he finds podcasters interviewing podcasters self-referential.
- He expects "isolation vs. experience" to be a big theme. Examples: live music, vinyl bars, and young people carrying flip phones or point-and-shoot cameras to be more present. He stacks phones at dinner.

## How All-In started and runs

- Chamath called him during COVID, after leaving CNBC, and wanted a podcast. They added Sacks and Freeberg, who joined for the COVID discussion.
- The format is modeled on The McLaughlin Group. The moderator is "full contact", pushes guests to answer, and breaks chops, which Jason ties to his Brooklyn roots and the poker game.
- His mental model for guests is a four-quadrant grid:
  - Axis one is knowledge and lived experience.
  - Axis two is whether the person cares about others' opinions.
  - Low-knowledge people don't belong on a podcast. Knowledgeable people who are scared to speak aren't useful either. The show is built on knowledgeable truth-tellers with "FU money".
- Topic selection: each week every cast member submits URLs to a producer, Lisa. Topics are ranked by how many cast members want them. The top news story of the week goes first.
- He says the audience is millions a week and includes heads of state and CEOs. The show stays high-level and doesn't dumb things down.
- He doesn't think the pod got Trump elected. He cites two reasons for the loss. One was Biden's cognitive decline. The other was replacing him with a "DEI candidate" picked without a primary. He says Democrats should have run a six-week primary and that Harris avoided unscripted podcasts.
- His rules for following Trump: wait 72 hours before reacting, since many statements aren't repeated. When something is unpopular he course-corrects, so the 2025 tariff crash led to a walk-back.
- He doesn't think founders should copy Trump's marketing. His advice for startups is to talk about customers, celebrate wins, show and explain the product, make launch videos, and appear on podcasts.

## Angel investing and the Sequoia scout program

- He began by advising friends on fundraising after selling Mahalo to AOL and raising from Sequoia. He introduced Sequoia to deals for Tesla, Zynga and Twitter, and Sequoia didn't do the early rounds.
- Roelof Botha asked him to be the first Sequoia scout. The program is the first of its kind. The terms:
  - Scouts invest their own deal flow with $25-50k checks.
  - Carry is split 50/50.
  - The goal was expanding Sequoia's surface area after missing Facebook and Twitter.
- Other early scouts included Brian Sugar, Seth Sternberg and Sam Altman. Altman invested in Stripe. Jason invested in Uber, DataStax and Thumbtack. He says it was Sequoia's highest-multiple fund, not the highest in dollars.
- His view of venture:
  - The middle stages (Series A to C) are overcrowded and overvalued, which makes those portfolios hard to run.
  - Opportunity sits in early stage, which needs many bets, and in late stage.
  - About 80% of seed companies go to zero, and he cites about 60% pulling through to a next round.
  - Many companies stall out at $500 million to $2 billion because later rounds overvalue them, and that is the time to take chips off the table.
  - Decacorns are very rare and most VCs never hit one.
- His two big wins: Uber and Robinhood, both invested pre-launch.
  - Uber was about $5 million pre / $4.5 million post, pitched at an angel forum he hosted.
  - Robinhood was about $15 million.
  - Both were two of his first 100 investments.
  - He now invests in about 100 companies a year and expects about 150 a year at Fund 5.
- "Third or fourth" in Uber: he told Travis he wanted to be first, Travis said he was third or fourth, and it became a meme (via VC Brags). He doesn't know the true order.
- Why he invested in Uber and Robinhood: he asks "what if it works?" at the idea stage.
  - Uber: one VC told him to sell software to cab companies. He says the cab companies were the problem and Uber took the economics from them.
  - Robinhood: the objection was millennials have no money. He said that if it works they will have 401(k)s, 529s and Roths.
  - Airbnb and Canva: both were laughed at and both "manifested a TAM".
- Angel forums: he hosted free, invite-only dinners with about 10 angels taking five pitches each, and he said people could commit on the spot. He started them to destroy the "Keiretsu Forum", which charged founders $5,000 to pitch. His rule is "don't pay to pitch your startup to investors."
- Tesla: he was friends with Elon, whose cash was nearly out. Jason wired nothing but handwritten checks for two Model S cars at $50k each. He got reservation #1 after noting "you're the first person to buy it". He also owns Roadster #16.

## Launch and Founder University

- The funnel: about 20,000 applications a year, or 400-500 a week. The team (22 people, 12 on investing) meets the top 100. They invest in roughly 1 in 150-200.
- Half the applicants were pre-incorporation, so they started Founder University.
  - It is a 12-week course with a speaker plus a pod of 20 companies.
  - About 350 teams were in the latest cohort and about 30 get funded.
  - Terms: $25k at a $1 million valuation, plus the right to follow on with $100-250k in the next two rounds.
  - He says about 40-50% mainly want the $25k to incorporate, and about 150 checks have been written.
- Results: about 60% of companies raise a further round at a higher valuation within a year. He doesn't want that rate above 50-60%, because the aim is to bet on crazy ideas.
- Fund economics: $25k checks are an experimental budget. A loss of $2.5-5 million across 100-200 deals, in a roughly $45 million fund, is fine. Winners are built up to about 7-10% ownership.
- Competing programs: apply to YC, Techstars, Antler, Neo and Launch together. Acceptance rates are under 1%. He thinks the top 10% are all roughly equal and the selection at 1% is imperfect. Some A16Z Speedrun companies came through Launch first.
- Founder feedback loop: he asks every applicant to rate the process. He reads every score of 6 or below.
  - The most common complaint was "you didn't understand what we pitched."
  - His fix is for callers to repeat back the founder's vision before moving on. Ratings improved.
- Advice for founders: fundraising is a volume game. You need two or three yeses from about 100 meetings, so talk to low hundreds before a Series A. Don't give up after 20.
  - Traction matters. A team with $300k in revenue beats three teams with none.
  - Don't set a sky-high valuation. Someone with a million in revenue growing 50% a year can win the investor over.
- VCs are "terrified human beings" making bets on incomplete information, "like poker".

## AI and jobs

- He thinks AI could reduce headcount at the large tech companies by 2030 while earnings grow.
- Twitter after Elon: about 85% of staff were removed and the product shipped faster. He called the old setup "the greatest heist in the history of business". He gave an example of lunches costing $400 each.
- He sees a 90% chance this displacement is faster than past ones, over 4-10 years rather than 40.
- His advice to young people: "nobody's coming for you. Help is not on the way." Find two or three talented friends, learn all the skills and start a company.
- He is an investor in Athena (assistants). He pays about $3,000 a month per assistant, or $72k a year for two, with 80 hours of weekly coverage. He argues outsourcing beats hiring one assistant who leaves within 6-18 months.

## Elon Musk and personal habits

- Elon's underrated strengths: he is a real engineer, and he is one of perhaps three or four people who understand factories as well as he does.
- His work ethic: at Twitter he took meetings until 1:30 or 2am with an NFL meeting at 10am the next day. Jason calls it "preternatural".
- GLP-1s: he gained two pounds a year for 20 years. He went from 213 lbs to 170 lbs at 5'8½". He says he wasn't open about it at first because it was experimental. Side effects he had: indigestion, and vomiting if he ate a lot of pizza or sushi after a dose. A benefit: less "food noise". He is now a paid spokesperson for Ro, with no discount code.
- "Comparison is the thief of joy." He says to stop comparing yourself to others, since there is always someone richer or more successful.
