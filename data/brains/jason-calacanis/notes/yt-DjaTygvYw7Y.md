## Hiring the first sales people: founders sell first

- Jason says sales solves everything. He cites Mark Cuban from the Weblogs Inc. days: "sell solves everything", because with great sales you have resources to fix every other problem.
- Founders should be on the first sales calls themselves. They are always selling anyway: to investors, partners, press, customers and new hires.
- Hiring a super senior sales leader too early is dangerous:
  - They expect big event and marketing budgets, an assistant, a designer and SDRs.
  - They often are no longer active sellers and won't get on the phone.
  - They become resource consumers, and the founder sets them up for failure.
- His preferred first hire is a line Account Executive working in parallel with the founder, not a VP.
- The founder and AE should aim for ten phone calls a week. After five to seven weeks they learn the objections, who picks up and takes the call, and which title to target.
- He wants those decision-maker details made transparent in the organization, not opaque.
- He notes that senior hires tend to have wild comp expectations of hundreds of thousands of dollars.

## Sales compensation structure and rules of thumb

- Early salespeople are inefficient. Contacts don't know you, there are no marketing materials, and they reach the wrong person first and navigate the org through several calls.
- Jason tells founders to count half of an early salesperson's comp as R&D or market research. Example: a $100K package ($50K base, $50K commission) is $50K of market research and $50K of sales.
- Selling something new is harder. His example is Balloonr (ideation software), where there is no "VP of ideation" to target, unlike customer support for Zendesk.
- Commission-only is legally risky in California. Labor boards default to the employee's position, so someone who works 50 hours a week and sells nothing can claim pay.
  - Contractor deals (1099, e.g. paid per closed deals) can work.
  - Ad reps and real estate brokers are paid this way. At Silicon Alley Reporter, a Chicago rep got 30% of ad sales, so a $10K back cover paid $3,000.
- His preferred model is base salary plus commission.
  - Paying a draw or advance against commission works to cover ramp-up. Example: $1,000 a month for the first six months against a $50K commission plan.
  - Pay commission when the money comes in the door. If a customer pays twice, the rep earns twice. Cancellations and refunds mean no commission on that money.
  - Some cash-rich businesses pay on signing and claw back later.
- Typical split is 50/50 base to commission. If they crush it, two-thirds can come from commission.
- Red flag: a candidate who wants less than a third of their comp from commission. "That's not a good sales executive."
- "Base pays your rent."
- Examples he gives:
  - Yelp reps in Arizona, per what other salespeople told his team for Inside.com: about $30-35K base, about $20K more at target, so roughly $55-60K total.
  - Silicon Valley SaaS reps: $100-150K base plus about $100K on target.
- Founders should expect salespeople to out-earn them. He wants the founder to be around the sixth or seventh highest paid, and calls three reps making $200K while the founder takes $100K "success". Founders hold 10-30% equity while sales hires hold a fraction of a percent.
- He warns that salespeople can be the worst to deal with internally, and the better they sell, the worse they can be. Build a clear culture with proper incentives.

## Outsourcing and structuring prospecting (SDRs)

- Prospecting is the research and data gathering of leads. Founders do it first, then hire SDRs, and ideally outsource it.
- Define target demographics first: industry, company size, roles.
- Why outsource SDR work: people hate the job and burn out, with turnover around every six months. Many SDRs become account executives or leave.
- Outsourced SDR firm test at Inside.com: $3,000 a month for two SDRs (offshore).
  - They expect to find about 2,000 leads, roughly $1.50 per lead.
  - They expect 10-20 calls a week, about 50 a month, so around $60 per phone call.
  - He compares it to a roughly $35K a year employee at about $15 an hour.
- At Launch, an in-house Toronto team spends about one hour a day finding leads for the two Silicon Valley account executives. That is roughly 10 hours a week, 50 companies a week with about four contacts each, or 150-200 names. They enjoy the work, which is why it stays in-house.
- Jason is open to up-work style offshore help. He notes a Serbian designer at $15/hour versus a $100/hour San Francisco quote.
- Build an "autonomous sales machine":
  - Set an SDR weekly goal, such as 200 contacts.
  - SDRs load leads into a CRM as cold leads, and CRM flags duplicates and existing clients.
  - Spot check and assign leads, then reps send outreach.
- CRMs and tools named: Zendesk Sell, Pipedrive, Salesforce, HubSpot, Outreach.io, Mixmax, LeadIQ.
- Decide which skills your AEs need from SDRs. Some reps want leads queued up for calls and others want to write their own outreach.
- At Launch, SDRs only load leads. Salespeople assigned leads randomly do the outreach with templated sequences and customize the first line.

## Email sequences and outreach tactics

- A sequence is a series of automated actions, typically three to five emails, with follow-ups spaced about three days apart. Newer tools also add Twitter follows, LinkedIn connects and voicemails.
- Jason's team approach: new reps write a couple of sequences and test them, so they learn themselves and might find something new. Proven templates are introduced later.
- Another approach at Launch: three emails within two weeks, then move interested contacts (those who opened more than once or clicked) into a slow-drip sequence of one email every month or two across a year, with reminders to connect on LinkedIn or Twitter. Don't put non-openers in it.
- Jason dislikes sequences with five, six or seven follow-ups.
- Best practices from the discussion:
  - Keep cold emails short and valuable, and make it easy to say yes.
  - Offer specific windows of time.
  - A/B test subject lines, body, day of week and time of day.
  - Mix very short "did you see my last email" notes with informative emails or demos.
  - A chart, screenshot or looped video of the software works; goofy gifs don't.
- He finds the "hey, can I ask you a question?" opener annoying: say what you want upfront.
- Scheduling links (Calendly, Mixmax, Google Calendar) let prospects pick a slot. He doesn't think cold calling happens as much, and says he doesn't answer unknown phone calls (Pixel call screening).
- Track and compare reps' open rates, calls per week and close rates to find what works.

## Closing: the BANT framework

- Launch uses IBM's BANT: Budget, Authority, Need, Timeframe.
- Budget: try to get an exact number. Knowing how frugal or flush a company is changes how you pitch. Think of ways to evolve your product to capture open budget.
- Authority: identify a champion and the decision-maker, who may differ, especially in larger companies. Accounts Payable may also need to approve. Tailor the pitch (ROI, fit in budget) to each.
- Need: find the prospect's pain points during nurturing and show how you make their life easier or beat competitors.
- Timeframe: use urgency, such as budget that must be spent this quarter or a holiday deadline. Move quickly on your side.
- Jason notes personal motivation matters. Advertisers on his show buy for different reasons: fandom, leads, closed sales, or the quality of the read.

## Upselling, renewals and client success

- Separate sales (closing) from client success (renewals and upsells) once you have the money to do so.
- Pay attention to what the product does for customers and add features or tiers that capture more of their open budget. Example: a $99 per month product adding offers worth $100-200 more per month.
- Double down on "whales", your largest customers. Founders should handle them at first, then pass them to account managers. Get their feedback, build rapport, and thank them with experiences such as a dinner or a Warriors game.
- Renewing and upselling is much easier than booking new clients. Keeping customers from quitting matters as much as or more than winning new ones.
- Create monthly recurring revenue on top of one-off services or products. Founders often take off when they learn to turn this on.
- Jason's own example: on This Week in Startups, he and the team improve content and guests to keep advertisers. In 2019 they had ten unicorn founders on the show.

## Bribes, gifts and ethics in sales

- Jason was offered $100 for taking an expense software demo, another $100 if he got his boss to close that day, and $50 more for requested info. He calls it a bribe and "dirty", even though he says it was the best product he'd seen.
- His test: would you be comfortable if your boss, or the New York Times or TechCrunch, saw it?
- Gifts and coffee are fine. Above about $25 you should tell your boss immediately.
- Corporate bribery can bring jail time, and US companies are held to the American standard abroad. He cites Walmart's bribery in Mexico and Sony sales junkets (Cabo, business class) as examples.
- Thanking a customer with an experience after they've spent money is different from a bribe to win a decision. His example: a bank taking 25 VCs and 25 founders to Turks and Caicos for kitesurfing.

## Sales tools and getting the team to adopt them

- Tools named: Mixmax and Outreach for sequences, Zendesk Sell and Pipedrive for pipeline visibility, LeadIQ for lead data.
- Use the same tool for each function so reporting stays in one place. Switching costs are high (about $100 and team training when Launch changed CRM).
- Use free trials and ask for extensions. Negotiate hard in competitive tool markets.
- Budget roughly $300-400 a month per sales executive in tools, about $5,000 a year. A $10K sale pays for it.
- Getting adoption:
  - Let people opt in and have early adopters report results at team meetings, so others see the wins.
  - He argues email sequences let you invite 100 investors to an accelerator with one push of a button.
  - Jason's rule: if a lead isn't contacted in the CRM within 60 days, it opens to anyone (some companies use 30).
  - For old-school reps who refuse to use the CRM, he gives about a year of amnesty, but by the third or fourth salesperson they must adopt it.
  - "The people who don't adopt these new tools are no longer competitive."
