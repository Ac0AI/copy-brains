## Why Jason started angel investing

- Jason first refused to invest in friends' companies because he trusted only his own judgment, and he got burned. He passed on Zynga after telling Mark Pincus that virtual poker chips make no sense ("a $25 million mistake").
- He gave Elon Musk advice on Tesla when it traded around a dollar a share, but he wasn't angel investing then.
- He turned down Twitter when Evan Williams showed it to him, predicting "a cacophony of idiots." He calls that a $50 million mistake.
- The fix was to decouple his opinion of an idea from his ability to pick great people. He now invests in friends and smart people, not in ideas he personally likes.
- His first 15 to 20 investments were made with Sequoia's money. He had introduced Sequoia to companies, they gave him a small pool, and the returns were split 50/50. That pool outperformed Sequoia's own parallel fund.
- Sam Altman's Stripe position and Jason's Uber and Thumbtack positions (about $200 million) drove those returns. He then raised his own fund and has done about 150 investments.
- He wrote the book because he's one of very few people with more than 100 investments and several unicorns (he says six). He believes it can change people's economic mobility.

## Why he thinks getting on a cap table beats the old wealth playbook

- The old advice was banker, lawyer or cop, marry someone with a good job, buy a home, and leverage it. That broke when houses went from roughly 1 to 1.5x household income to 10x or more, and graduates carry about $100,000 in debt.
- He lists four ways onto the cap table of high-growth startups:
  - **Founder:** one big bet, but you might get three or four tries in a lifetime. Most people quit after one.
  - **Employee:** small equity that rarely pays off.
  - **Adviser or consultant:** work for half cash and half stock, vesting monthly. He suggests young people advise about five startups a year with a skill like design or development.
  - **Angel investor:** increasingly accessible through syndicates and Reg CF/Reg A style rules for non-accredited investors.
- Pay attention to where the value gets created. Outside the Valley, a $10 billion or $100 billion company is very rare. New York has had billion-dollar companies like Tumblr, Etsy, Kickstarter, DoubleClick and iVillage.

## How a beginner should start (low-cost, high-learning)

- Make many small bets. Put about $1,000 into each of the first ~10 deals through syndicates (AngelList, Seed Invest, Republic, his own Jason Syndicate) and act as if you had put in $10,000 or $100,000.
- Treat it as an education and networking cost:
  - A founder sees you as a helpful, engaged investor regardless of check size.
  - You can see who else co-invested, build a spreadsheet or list of those co-investors, and email them for coffee when you travel.
- Plan for 20 to 30 startups over 2 to 4 years, diversified, with small bets first and bigger bets later. Double down on the winners.
- Never put much of your net worth into one deal; he suggests a fraction of 1% per investment.
- Total downside is small. Risking 5% to 10% of net worth across a portfolio is survivable, and a lost 5% is outweighed by the network and education. "Most people overestimate the downside risk" (a line he attributes to Elon).
- Year two is the worst. Maybe 7 of your first 10 are running out of money while you're making your next 10. People who bet too big too early quit right before winners pay off. If 1 to 3 of 10 survive, something special is going on, and the payoff should be far more than 5x.
- Know your own psychology. From watching poker players, he saw some love to lose and rage-quit, while winners step away, eat a sandwich and come back disciplined.
- Alternatively, find the top 10 angels by track record and put $1,000 into their syndicates. He frames this as having their experience work for you.

## How he evaluates startups and founders

- He invests in the arc of a founder's career, not a single idea. He expects the first investment may return zero, the second 2x and the third 100x. He tries to be the first call and first check for repeat founders.
- He now writes down the reasons something will fail, then often tears up the page and lists only the one or two reasons it could work. Examples: Airbnb sounded like it was "for serial killers and meth heads"; Google was roughly the 11th search engine; YouTube was about the 50th video site; Uber and Lyft weren't the first to try hailing cabs by phone (Vindo texted for cabs).
- "What if it works?" is the core question. If success is obvious, the price is probably wrong; the upside lives in ideas that seem crazy.
- Separate your limited ability to judge the idea from your ability to read the founder's passion. Check whether five crazy early customers could become the 90% who use it. Founders in true billion-dollar opportunities will know more and care more than you do.
- Look for craftsmanship and obsessive attention to detail, like Barry Diller's staff stripping labels off a Fantastik bottle so it looked clean. Great founders can seem stubborn, annoying or even jerks because they believe the world must change. Thumbtack's Marco Zappacosta impressed him with rich, passionate, detailed answers.
- He sometimes seeks out the founder a VC finds "uncomfortable," because the discomfort may come from strong conviction.
- Don't judge a company by disgruntled ex-employees. He compares the New York Times piece on Amazon to asking people who washed out of Navy SEAL training about the SEALs.
- Run due diligence. The first 50 deals he skipped it. In the next ~100 deals he caught two founders in outright lies and wrote about them in the book.

## Timing: when to invest (his 2017 view)

- He doesn't recommend investing before a product has launched and has a few customers. Many teams can build an MVP with friends-and-family money and sweat equity, so waiting eliminates about 97% of failures.
- Of launched products, perhaps two thirds die within two or three years, but you get that time to see whether anyone actually uses it. Even a few customers show the founders aren't "tourists or [bullshitters]."
- After a hit like Instagram, a me-too app has to be about 5x better, not 10% to 20% better, to get people to switch. After "The Social Network" came out, a wave of tourists arrived.
- He stays at the very early stage (5 people, 6-month-old companies) and turned down offers to do later-stage $25 million to $50 million checks, because that's the most exciting point.

## How to be valuable to founders (and build your network)

- Be of service without expecting return. He built goodwill at Silicon Alley Reporter by delivering magazines himself on a luggage cart, putting people on the cover, and inviting them to parties, then tapped that goodwill later.
- Advice for founders or would-be investors: study the deal flow, then offer help (for a salesperson, rewrite a job description for a company that's hiring). "Be of service" is his main networking tactic.
- Treat angel investing as an arms race of who adds the most value. He tries to give the same value to failed startups and to companies he passed on.
- Passing on a round isn't the end. Ask to be on the monthly update list and reply helpfully. He gets about 100 updates a month from companies he passed on, but only about 75 of the 150 companies he did invest in send updates.
- For companies that come back for a bridge, he adds a side letter requiring monthly updates. No updates usually means a 99% chance of failure; even Uber sends quarterly updates.
- Tell founders up front that most startups fail and that if it happens, shut down gracefully and call him first for the next idea. Once, a founder who sold his company early agreed on the phone to make Jason the first investor in the next one, and similar promises have paid off two or three times.
- He tells founders to call him when the news is too scary to tell their team (they'd quit) or other investors (they won't fund again). Once a founder called after throwing up in the shower. Jason told him he could shut down, take a big-company job for two years, and he'd be the first check back.
- Communicate candidly. He prefers "candid" to "honest" (an idea from Ed Catmull's "Creativity, Inc."), because asking for honesty implies you weren't being honest before.

## Syndicates and cap table leverage

- About half of the companies he invests in agree to syndicate their rounds. He moved from AngelList to his own Jason Syndicate, with about 1,300 people.
- Founders benefit because syndicate members can open doors. Members work at places like Google and Disney, and include authors and athletes. For Cafe X (robotic coffee), the question "does anyone know anybody at Google?" gets answers.
- Backers invest as little as $1,000 per deal, and the whole group sits in one LLC with paperwork handled.
- He says it breaks the idea that angel investing needs $100,000 checks. A recent syndicate for a company he works with raised over a million dollars and broke the platform's record.

## Motivation, goals and marketing tactics

- He's driven by an addictive, competitive personality and sets audacious public goals. Next goal: 300 total investments and being the best angel ever.
- The chip came from growing up in Brooklyn, feeling "bridge and tunnel," and wanting to be the person who decides who goes on the magazine cover.
- Silicon Alley Reporter's Top 100 list was a growth hack:
  - The first year only 60 people could be found, so he asked each person to name others (accountants, headhunters, lawyers) to reach 100.
  - He ranked by tiers (most important, least important, middle).
  - He deliberately ranked a loud player (DoubleClick) at number 2 and Esther Dyson at number 1 to provoke controversy. After the list, the invites to Knicks games and parties poured in.
- Book promotion tactic: a raffle ticket in the book to win dinner with him and James. Winners tend to be entrepreneurs, and he said he'd probably invest in one at dinner.
