## Why Jason says this is the best moment to angel invest

- Jason argues downturns are when great angel entry prices appear. He made his best bets in the Great Recession years.
- Uber, Calm and Thumbtack were valued at about $5M, $5M and $4M when he invested. He puts the combined entry at roughly $14-15M.
- In recent years, companies with less performance than those were asking for $12-20M valuations.
- His portfolio split in the crisis: some companies doubled usage and revenue, others fell to zero revenue.
- He was investing more going into the crisis than before it.
- He says creative energy peaks in a crisis and many great companies are founded then.

## Valuation discipline and shots on goal

- Jason tells founders and new investors to "be valuation conscious". Entry point matters even if you later hit a $10B outlier.
- The reasoning: a fixed pool of capital buys a fixed number of swings. $1M for 1% of one company could instead be ten $100K checks for the same total ownership.
- Cheaper entry means more shots on goal for the same money.
- He also argues that a team which breaks even on its bets still wins. Staying in the game gives a chance at an outlier, and the market intelligence is valuable.

## Portfolio construction and pro rata

- Jason describes early startup investing as roughly 80% of bets going to zero.
- He describes three of four companies failing and about one in 30 or 40 becoming an outlier.
- The edge he lists: proprietary deal flow, many bets, spotting which ones break out, then putting 10x more capital behind them.
- His book's rules, as recapped on the show: make 30+ investments, invest only in startups with product in market and revenue, start with very small bets, then go 10x on winners. Spread the 30+ over about three years so you learn along the way.
- He now insists on pro rata rights or he doesn't invest. In his early $25-50K check days he never thought about them.
- A team member tracks the quarter-over-quarter and year-over-year performance of about 150 active portfolio companies.
- When a company is about to raise, Jason makes a preemptive offer. Example: $250K or $1M at a $15M cap.
- Companies sometimes say no and go to market, but he says they win the deal about 80% of the time.
- This moves his ownership from about 6% to 12-20% in winners.
- Ownership history he gives: under 1% in Uber, about 2% in Superhuman, 5-6% in Calm, now 9-15% in newer winners such as Fitbod and Steezy.

## Advice for family offices

- Jason says family offices usually see deals only after Silicon Valley has passed on them. His poker analogy: the best hands get played by others and the rest get passed down.
- Founders see family office money as not valuation sensitive. He says to them: "So what you're saying is you want an investor who is not smart."
- Before buying a deal, he says to check it wasn't passed on by everyone else. He doesn't want you "splitting the arrow".
- Better routes for family offices:
  - Back fund managers who know what they're doing, then try to get their pro rata allocation downstream.
  - Invest in companies that already have $10-50M in revenue. You then check in every 6-12 months instead of monthly or weekly, and you avoid the 80% mortality.
- A family office with real appetite could hire a young Jason-type, paying salary plus bonus, and put about 1% of the fund to work per year.
  - Example: a $100M office deploying $5M over five years.
  - The intelligence gained is worth it even at break-even. A person bringing one strong deal a week would make the whole organization smarter about the future.
- His view is that he sees the future about 10 years before everyone else.
- He keeps his public-market money in a low-fee Wealthfront account. His reason: he has no edge competing with high-frequency traders.

## How he evaluates a startup

- Core idea: judge potential through performance. The question is whether the company can reach $50-100M in revenue.
- Capital is a zero-sum competition. Funding one company means another doesn't get the check.
- Founders are highly charismatic, so he separates charisma from performance.
- Talk to customers:
  - Ask what they'd do if the product went away.
  - "I'd be upset" is one level of product-market fit.
  - "Can I buy a license for $10,000, this is mission critical" is a stronger level.
- Check craftsmanship in the product. He cites the iPhone, Tesla, Wacom and Uber. If he can see detail in the product, customers can too.
- He asks founders: "is there some other idea you have that's better than this one?" Some answer yes, and he moves on.
- He reads whether founders hold firm on their business model or ask whether he'd invest if they changed it.

## Where venture capital fits

- He defends the VC model. He says VCs forgive losses by calling investments "experiments", and the system works when you can make 100-300 bets.
- Venture backs rocket-ship outcomes, not steady growth. A pizzeria or dry cleaner growing 10% a year (doubling in about 7.2 years) is a "lifestyle business".
- He says that term isn't an insult. The owner keeps 100% of the profits and the shares.
- Accelerators, seed funds and platforms have taken over the "picking apples" stage that VCs moved downstream from.

## Building a syndicate and an angel brand

- His first AngelList investment was Calm, a $378K syndicate check.
  - The company went from $5M to $1.4B.
  - His syndicate sold about 10% of shares at a $250M valuation in a secondary, booking roughly a 3x cash-on-cash return in under five years while keeping 90%.
- He left AngelList after about 40-50 investments and created his own syndicate site, to control the experience and have a direct relationship with investors.
  - About 4,000 members, around 2,000 active.
  - The top 250 are in a Slack workspace helping with projects.
- Goal: 10,000 active members, so 250 people at $40K each fund a $10M round.
- Distribution engine:
  - His podcast, running 11-12 years with 1,000+ episodes and 6-7 full-time staff, is "top of the funnel" for deal flow.
  - Listeners email him and strangers pitch him in the street.
- He teaches Angel University, three hours for a $100 charity donation. Cohorts of 150-250 people raised about $50-60K.
- He ran Open Angel Forum, now remote demo day, where about seven startups pitch investors.
- Uber came through being helpful: he introduced Travis Kalanick to investors. He had known Travis from interviewing him as a journalist.
- On his own record he says: "I got lucky 12 times."

## Work from home and the economics of talent

- Jason sees remote work as an efficiency shift that will help pull the economy out of recession.
- Bay Area workers carry a $20-50K salary premium; he expects pay to reset by location. He cites Zuckerberg's policy of cutting pay for people who move.
- Facilities spend is often the number two or three line item, so shedding offices can make companies 20%+ more profitable.
- Remote work exposes low performers through daily stand-ups and end-of-day reports.
- He doesn't believe a $50-500B company can be built fully remote. Culture and in-person collaboration matter for those.
- He expects commercial real estate to fall at least 50%.
- He expects employees to value flexibility, so employers that refuse will struggle to hire.

## Skills over degrees and the education shift

- He expects a homeschooling and subscription boom of $10-500 a month, naming Brilliant and Khan Academy. Rich parents may hire a teacher for a small pod of kids.
- He expects second- and third-tier colleges to struggle and gap years to rise.
- Hiring is shifting to skills. His test: rate yourself 1-10 at a specific skill.
  - A 6 is unneeded, an 8 is tolerated, 9-10 is "employable forever".
  - "Business degree" is not a skill.
- He thinks Harvard "picks" smart people rather than making them smarter, and he sees his own role as picking, not making founders 10x better.
- He passed on investing in MasterClass and calls that a mistake.
