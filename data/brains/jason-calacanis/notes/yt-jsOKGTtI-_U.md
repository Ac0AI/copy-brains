## Starting a business with no money, and the "ask" that unlocks it

- Jason grew up in Bay Ridge, Brooklyn. His dad's bar was padlocked by the feds and the tax authority in 1988 when Jason was 18. He calls it one of the great traumas of his life.
- He went to college at night over five years, paying his own way by fixing laser printers and building local networks before the internet.
- He started Silicon Alley Reporter as a photocopied zine. He had no money to print it, and the overnight manager at the print shop let him pay $100 on a $1,750 bill.
- Jason's takeaway: "when I ask people things that many times people will do what I ask them to do." He learned early that you can ask for outrageous things and sometimes get them.
- He photocopied about 1,000 copies of the zine, then handed 200 out at an internet party at Roseland. By the end of the night, everyone at the party was reading it.
- The next morning he made postcards offering a subscription: 10 issues for $90. He had seen Esther Dyson charge $1,000 for her newsletter, so he picked a price far below hers.
- Within two or three months, subscription envelopes of $90 each were piling up at his office share. He quit his jobs.
- He frames the experience as a sliding-door moment: a nobody became important in about 60 days.

## Get to a new medium first

- Jason says he has always been fascinated by new mediums: "if you get to a medium first you can exploit it." That covers zines, the early web, blogging and podcasting.
- His reasoning: the people who tinker with a platform early get disproportionate credit and attention. The platform's value then rises around them, like buying a loft in an unfashionable neighborhood before everyone else arrives.
- Silicon Alley Reporter worked because he covered the internet when browsers couldn't show graphics. He says the timing "was so insane."
- When the dot-com bust hit, he tinkered. He called people to learn what was happening and watched who was building things: Flickr, Delicious, Wi-Fi, early blogs.
- He wanted a read on the landscape after the crash, because everyone had quit and gone to find jobs.

## Rage after the bust, and Weblogs Inc.

- By 1999 the magazine ran about 300 pages, with almost 100 people working for him. He was making about $7-8 million a year from the magazine and a couple of million from events, and he turned down a $20 million offer because the business was his identity.
- The bust wiped out the business. He sold the remains to Dow Jones with a roughly $500-600K employment contract. Dow Jones then fired him and handed him a check for $500,000.
- He says the emotion in that moment was a "hulk-like rage." He felt he had to prove it wasn't a fluke, got an office the next day, and started whiteboarding ideas.
- His early ideas included Mercury Club, a $100-a-month delivery service for rich New Yorkers using Vespas and an 800 number.
- His next business came from two observations. An employee, Rafat Ali, was moonlighting on a blog (paidContent.org), and his event producer, Shani, was making far more from a blog than he had paid her.
- He concluded blogs were "faster, better, cheaper," and decided to build business blogs at scale.
- Weblogs Inc. details:
  - Co-founded with Brian Alvey (technology).
  - 87 blogs in 18 months, about 450 bloggers, 200 of them active.
  - Bloggers were paid per post: $2-3 at first, then $5, then $10, and the economics were modeled in a spreadsheet.
  - Peter Rojas was the missing piece; he insisted on distinct, branded blog names.
  - Mark Cuban invested $300,000 for about 20%, and Jason says he gave Cuban $6 million back.
  - AOL bought the company for $30 million.
- His conclusion: "once you're lucky twice you're good." The sale proved the first success wasn't a fluke.

## Events and community as a business

- Jason says he loves hosting events because "if you host the party those people get to meet new people create some relationship or moment that then changes their life forever."
- His event "Open the Kimono" (OTK) is still remembered by attendees as a peak experience years later. It included a wine-making competition, and the winning wine was bottled for attendees.
- He says events are far more tiring than podcasting for introverts, and that he is highly extroverted (86 and 100 on two tests).
- For a hosted event, he suggests a format where the host interviews someone on stage, recovers backstage, and then takes audience questions.
- He frames the purpose as manifesting a community in a physical space and creating shared memories.

## All-In: how a podcast with partners works

- All-In started as a spinoff of poker games during Covid. The four hosts are Jason, Chamath Palihapitiya, David Sacks and David Friedberg.
- He modeled the format on the McLaughlin Group: opinionated, interrupting, full-contact discussion.
- Chamath proposed it first as a two-person show, and Jason suggested the name "All In" because of poker.
- Jason acts as the producer and moderator. His routine:
  - A shared Google Doc "docket" each week, built with his producer Nick, filled with links and bullet points. The group chats on Signal.
  - He feeds each host topics suited to them: science for Friedberg, politics and law for Sacks, markets and startups for Chamath.
  - He compares his role to a point guard: set up each partner to score.
- His moderation techniques:
  - Give private notes after episodes. He tells a partner, for example, that they made four strong points and should make one or two, then ask what another host thinks.
  - Use a circling finger to signal "wrap it up."
  - Say "okay" to break in on monologues, a habit he picked up from Sam Harris.
  - Drop a joke when a debate overheats; he cites Bill Maher doing this well.
  - Explain that when he challenges a partner, he's helping clarify their position rather than disagreeing.
- He says the show works because viewers like seeing friendship and unguarded conversation.

## All-In: conflict, ads and the Venture fund

- Jason says all four hosts were opinionated people who'd never had a boss, so the show repeatedly came close to breaking up over the event, spinoffs and advertising.
- The group chose to stay together because the audience loved the show.
- His partners refuse to run ads. Jason says that leaves roughly $25 million in advertising on the table (about $6-7 million a year, and he'd get a quarter).
- Instead he used the show's reach to raise his next venture fund:
  - He used a 506(c) designation, which lets a fund solicit publicly.
  - He mentioned on the podcast that he was raising a fund and asked people to email him.
  - About a thousand emails came in, and five webinars produced $44 million in commitments. His prior fund was also $44 million.
  - He describes the return as having to deploy the money and earn about 25% of the profit, and notes every LP meeting begins with a request for a selfie.
- His rule for the show: the partners should root for each other, and a hard debate (he cites Ukraine arguments with Sacks) shouldn't damage the relationship.

## Money, success and friendship

- Jason says that past a certain point, wealth stops changing daily life. He points to a night with billionaire friends: the same restaurant, the same ski trips.
- His real scoreboard: "did you wake up and couldn't wait to get out of bed."
- He says he drives a Model Y rather than the Plaid and prefers a New York strip to wagyu.
- He says people treated him very differently after he became a millionaire: "like all of a sudden I got like 30 more IQ points and I was two feet taller."
- He argues for loyalty. His dad taught him and his brothers to always have each other's back.
- He says his two best life moves are "have great friends and build cool stuff."
- On friendships, he says as you become more successful your world gets smaller, and a trusted inner circle matters most.
- He describes a friend's loss, and the deaths of two poker friends, as the point where he started to reassess what matters.
- He is writing a book about money and wealth. His theme is what happens when you "catch the car."
- He advises:
  - Take chances: "what if it actually works out."
  - Be kind, and have productive disagreements with a big open heart.
