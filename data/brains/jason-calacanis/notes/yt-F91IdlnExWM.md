## Early-stage angel investing: take the bet, don't hunt for red flags

- Jason rejects "negative art" screening, where one red flag means pass. His method: list everything that could go wrong, then on the other side list "but what if it works?" If it works, he rips up the red-flag side, because those flags were only a map of what to fix or avoid.
- His driving analogy: there are a million ways to die on the way to the Warriors game, but you focus on staying in your lane. Race drivers are told "don't look at the wall, look at the road."
- He distrusts consensus: "If there's consensus on the idea, it probably should exist already." He introduced Uber to about 25 people and 3 invested. Ninety percent passing doesn't make an idea good, but it can mean an outlier idea with outlier results.
- He never underestimates a founder because early Steve Jobs pitches were raw too: "everybody who starts in this business is awkward and has weird ideas and is unrefined."
- He claims the human brain is pessimistic by default, because scared people lived longer, so investors must correct for that bias.
- Hit rates: he asks successful angels how many investments it takes to hit a 25x, 50x or 100x outlier. The usual answer is 20 to 50. His own record is roughly one unicorn per 50 investments, and earlier in his career closer to one per 25. He has made over 300 investments (about 30-40 a year).
- He teaches this in Angel University, a 3-4 hour course taught with Mike Zavino. Proceeds go to charity (about $150-175K donated), and he charges so attendees have skin in the game. Each class adds 300-500 members to his syndicate.

## Syndicates: structure, economics and why they work for angels and founders

- A syndicate is a group of people investing in an LLC that invests in a company, with a lead (Jason). His started on AngelList for the first 30 deals, then moved to thesyndicate.com.
- Starting one is cheap: Ashure Fund Management (which Jason invested in, and which was AngelList's back end) sets up a syndicate's legal documents for about $15K.
- Scale: 68 deals closed in 2021 for $51M, the largest syndicate in the world, with about 9,500 members adding a couple hundred a month (about 1,000 when his book came out, with a goal of 10,000). He does about two deals a week.
- Minimum is $4K per deal and the average member puts in about $7K. His average deal allocation is about $750K, often oversubscribed ($1.5-2M of interest), so he trims members' requests.
- Why members join: finding deal flow alone means years of building a brand and roughly 25-50 meetings to find one qualified company, and direct checks usually start around $50K. A syndicate lets you do twelve $4K checks instead of one $50K check, choosing which deals and how much, unlike a fund where you get a year-end statement and learn the result a decade later.
- Quality varies: there are no rules for syndicates, and some leads do no diligence. His does diligence, checks legal documents, and hosts a webinar with the founder.
- Founder benefit: one line on the cap table instead of many angels, plus 100-200 syndicate members who can give feedback, introductions and help finding employees.
- He leaves tiny syndicate deal-term debates to a team and standard terms (see below).

## Deal terms: keep the cap table clean and everyone aligned on share price

- Jason's firm takes preferred shares, not common, and won't waive pro rata rights, because "we have LPs" and need pro rata to run a successful fund. Founders have asked for common about 10 times and the answer has always been no.
- He avoids funky structures. East Coast VCs sometimes add odd terms, and the next serious investor sees it and may be scared away.
- His principle: the share price is the aligning device. A founder with 40%, a seed investor with 7%, a Series A at 15%, a Series B at 10%, employees at 15%: everyone sees price go from $1 to $4 to $12 and wins together.
- How preferred works: it converts one-to-one to common at IPO or acquisition. Before exit, its protective provisions stop founders from taking the money and shutting down. Example: $5M invested and a $3M sale means investors get paid first (60% whole).
- Examples of structure he warns about: a $1B valuation with a 2x participating preferred (sell at $500M, the $100M investor takes $200M, or 40% of proceeds); or a distressed $500K bridge with a 4x liquidation preference on a $5M company.
- Down rounds mean selling more shares to raise less, which can start a "debt spiral" or "death spiral" if the cocktail is too complicated to unwind. Funky terms don't doom a company but cause pain later.

## Radical delegation: spend time only on the highest-leverage behaviors

- With 300+ investments, he says you must "be ruthless about delegation," and "go fast alone, go far together."
- Example: he used to meet every accelerator applicant (hundreds per cohort, then the last 50 and follow-ups). Now his team applies his rubrics and heuristics to the check of about $100K, and he watches a pitch video or reads coverage only.
- Deal structure and legal debates are delegated using a standard-deal rule set. His team may vary terms about 10%, and tells founders what they can approve and gives his mobile number if they want to escalate.
- The two things he keeps: hanging out with founders he's invested in and meeting new elite founders, and his podcast, which helps him meet startups and promote portfolio companies. "You got to look at the highest leverage behaviors and then get rid of everything else."
- Benefit of the podcast as deal flow: it also builds the brand that feeds the syndicate.

## Picking founders: the broken, driven Jedi, and why leadership is a later skill

- He looks for a founder with "something to prove," a chip on the shoulder, and an energy level he says is easy to discern. "If you show me a balanced person, I will show you a moderate outcome in startup land." He cites Jobs and Travis Kalanick; he thinks a founder working 70-80 hours a week needs this drive.
- Founders often start as great individual contributors (writing code, marketing, selling). Success later demands leadership: "What got you here will not get you there." The brutal standards founders hold themselves to cannot be unleashed on their team. He tells them to inspire people, ask good questions, "make them feel 12 feet tall," demand excellence and empower them.
- His Michael Jordan reference: Jordan's savage standards won titles but left him miserable and estranged from Scottie Pippen. The team-builder analogies: Draymond Green as the glue for Steph and Klay; one superstar scoring 40 can still lose.
- On experience: young founders (Zuckerberg, Gates, Jobs, Larry and Sergey) show audacity and a drive to prove something, but serial founders have a much higher success rate and higher aggregate returns. They avoid unforced errors, like a veteran who knows how to set a pick, because games are won by a few points. Nobody has studied whether young hits stick with a company for decades, so he notes both can be true.
- He tells serial investors to look at the anti-portfolio and forecast critically, and to keep asking whether success was causation or correlation.
- Founders survive on resilience: "The great ones are like, 'Yeah, I lost a limb… I'll go on the next mission.'" After more than a decade of an up market, he sees soft, entitled founders. He tells them there's "no timeout" when they have ammunition left.
- Crucible moments make founders: Uber and Robinhood both had hard stretches that battle-tested their founders, and he argues a founder who survives is stronger, not flawed.

## Startup failure modes: burger quality, runway shame and crisis behavior

- Product-market fit means consumers love the product. His burger metaphor: founders change the furniture, name of the store and everything but the burger. "Make another burger and make it 10% better and do that 10 times." Do it seven times and compounding makes it better.
- Product velocity needs strong, focused product people. Focus beats creativity: he says Zuckerberg copied features and won through focus, speed and simplicity.
- Failure often comes from shame and silence. Young founders hide it until two weeks of runway remain. He wants to hear everything early, being "the safe space" for a founder crying in the shower before brunch.
- In a crisis, never concede while you have capital left. Options he's seen work: ask staff to defer salary with a signed promise of repayment plus interest, cut pay in half, or move to minimum wage for two months.
- Market cycles: he lived through 1987, 1994-95, the dotcom bust and 2008. In the shakeout after 13 years of up markets, easy rounds based on a good deck or network end, and investors now want performance. Rounds 'sell the promise' first, then performance.
- Founders at 500K revenue with four months of runway who quit because "I'm not enjoying it" show the wrong mindset. He tells them to keep fighting.

## Compounding, the Rule of 72 and many small product gains

- He teaches the Rule of 72 in his accelerator: divide 72 by the growth rate to get the periods to double. At 10% a month you double in 7.2 months (not 10), and at 10% a week in 7.2 weeks. Many founders don't see compounding.
- He says making one feature twice as good is very hard, but making 10 features 10% better is easy, and users feel it. His burger example: bun, cook, cheese, plate, box, sauce, order process, speed.
- He tells founders to think in decades: Elon's electric cars and SpaceX are in their second decade, and "how long can you suffer through a neverending series of challenges" is the lesson of success.
- On Uber's economics, he used simple math: if they lost about 50 cents a ride across billions of rides, raising price by $1.50 could produce $2B more, and even if 10% of rides vanished the result was $1.8B. He saw the lever as obvious once the competitive dogfight eased.
- Superhuman example: Rahul Vohra asked how to beat free Gmail. Jason's math: if Superhuman saves 10 minutes a day, that's about 60 hours a year, worth $60,000 at $1,000 an hour, so $360 a year is about 0.6% of the value. He invested $500K in the seed round, having earlier backed Reportive (a tripling when LinkedIn bought it), after Rahul promised Jason would be his first call. Jason says he also invests in people who return his call.

## Holding winners and building a founder network

- He advises riding winners and taking some chips off the table as you go. Selling Uber early at about $5B would have missed another 12-20x. He sold some Uber and kept some, and has not sold any Robinhood (once $30-40, then about $10) or Square, which he says he will never sell, citing Jack Dorsey.
- His reasoning for holding Uber: the on-demand super app (rides, food, experiences, trucking/logistics). He thinks Uber, Airbnb and DoorDash are takeout candidates for Walmart, Amazon, Google, Apple or Facebook.
- Capital allocator advice: start small. His wife could invest 1-5% of net worth, learn from losses and then concentrate on winners.
- Billionaire paths: allocate capital, found a company or be among the top 50-100 investors in an outlier. He says wealth past $10-20M doesn't change much, and he'd choose 40 ski days over 10 more investments.
- Friendships: be a loyal friend and give relentless support, even public "air cover" (defending Travis and Robinhood on CNBC). His friend Fritz taught him to call friends when alone. He urges people to double down on relationships as their circles shrink with success.
- Founder University: a 12-week course for $700 refunded if you finish. About 90%+ complete, versus about 6% for typical online courses, because of skin in the game. Concept: 100 graduated first, 200 are in the current cohort.
