## Venture capital as rocket fuel: who angel and VC money is for

- Jason separates ordinary businesses (design agency, PR shop, pizzeria, dry cleaner, consulting firm) from venture-scale ones. Ordinary businesses are great, can employ people and give the owner independence, and need little money. His example: you can start a consulting firm for $0 with a free website and an email to a thousand people.
- Venture capital is "jet fuel" or "rocket fuel". You wouldn't put it in a Vespa. It is for outlier companies that can grow at a velocity others find unrealistic and reach hundreds of millions or a billion people (Uber, Robinhood, Cal.com style bets).
- Those companies are more complex, have a lower chance of success, and mostly fail hard. If they succeed, they can "change the fabric of society" (Google, Facebook, Uber, Airbnb). He calls it an Olympic-caliber sport.
- Founders of ginger beer, tea or "noble purpose" companies often don't understand why they are not fundable: they don't have a one-in-a-thousand or one-in-10,000 shot at being an outlier.
- Illustrative portfolio math of ten investments: six go to zero, the seventh and eighth return about half their money, the ninth returns two to three times, and with luck the 10th, 20th or 30th returns more than 30x. He calls this a unique kind of gambling that even insiders struggle with psychologically.

## How Jason sources deals and why brand matters

- He meets founders largely through his podcasts. Among founders he's as known as a celebrity; outside the industry he's a nobody.
- He says tech investing is "really a popularity contest amongst investors". Investors have brands, and founders want to be affiliated with them, like a talent agency (CAA, William Morris) or record label. Affiliation validates a startup and gives others permission to engage with it.
- Podcasts, events and books are one thing: a way to meet people in the industry and let them share knowledge, so founders remember his name. The second thing is investing in their companies. He says everything his team does falls into those two buckets.
- Example: he met the founder of Cal.com on the podcast and invested because of it. He calls the podcast a tool and tactic that is in his wheelhouse as the company's leader.
- His firm Launch has a stated goal: be the best investors in the world. The plan he describes works backwards from that: support founders early, invest in 80 to 100 companies a year, reach 1,000 investments in ten years, and invest about ten times as much in winners as in failures.
- Funnel: roughly 10,000+ pitches a year, 2,000 to 4,000 meetings, about 100 investments.

## Expecting failure and making many bets

- Most startups fail: he cites two out of three or three out of four. So a founder needs only three, four or five tries to get a likely success. He tells founders whose first company failed that Travis Kalanick's third company was Uber and Elon Musk's third and fourth were SpaceX and Tesla; the third or fourth attempt is often the sweet spot.
- First-time home runs (Gates, Bezos, Zuckerberg) are rare but memorable, which makes them seem common. He calls that confirmation bias, and also cites survivorship bias (the WWII planes: bullet holes only appeared on planes that made it back).
- Nobody knows which companies will win. He did not expect Cal.com to become the second-best company in his portfolio. He expected Uber to be a billion or two billion dollar company, not $60 billion, so he was off by 30 to 60x. He didn't think Robinhood's first idea would work, thought the second or third would, and was "delighted to be wrong".
- He doesn't think he's a great picker: "I think I have a great network and I have great relationships." One Uber hit doesn't prove skill, so he warns against concluding that.
- Because outcomes are unpredictable, he treats every portfolio company as amazing and supports them as much as possible. His summary: find the best people you can, invest in them, support the heck out of them. If it fails and they did a good job, pat them on the back; they can do another one.
- Even internally, if 12 team members each picked two companies he doubts the majority would pick the winner.

## Where an investor's time goes: the failing companies

- About 90% of his time goes to the companies that fail or struggle. Winners hit their numbers and get high-fives; there's no point "screwing up your formula". Scaling winners still needs hiring, watching competitors and building infrastructure.
- With over 200 investments, he says one recent week was chaos at four companies (about 2% of the portfolio).
- Typical crises: running out of money, Google making your product free, a well-funded competitor selling "hundred dollar bills for 50 bucks" (as Uber did and had done to it), and hardware or shipping disruptions with only six to twelve months of runway.
- He uses a rocket metaphor: many startups have no finished rocket, no jet fuel, or no astronauts, and have to fix that before orbit.

## Leading a founder through chaos: goal, plan, people

- He sees his job, and any leader's, as convincing everyone that there is a plan and that sticking together and executing gives better odds. He likes the lighthouse-in-waves image for this.
- His framework with a founder: what does success look like (goal), what is the plan to get there, who do we need on the team to execute? Then tackle tactical issues (lawsuits, leases, competitors) without losing focus. He says many founders have neither a goal nor a plan.
- He uses calm-under-pressure metaphors: firefighters and ambulance crews triaging, samurai and Jedi (Qui-Gon meditating and conserving energy), Gladiator's "fight together as one", and Black Hawk Down. The lesson: block out the non-essential, work together, and focus.
- Background: he grew up in 1970s and 80s Brooklyn in his dad's bar, did martial arts and marathons, and worked on an ambulance, which he credits for his discipline and fighter mentality.

## Fight or fold: how he handles struggling companies

- He admits his weakness is a never-say-die attitude and that it probably hurts returns. Many VCs calmly stop funding, give up the board seat and move on, which he calls more efficient and rational; he can't do it easily.
- He's seen founders figure something out at month 18 or month 30 and go "supernova", so he keeps fighting. Sometimes, though, the idea or the market is wrong: "you can't swim your way out of a tsunami".
- His approach: ask the founder what they want. "Do you want to keep fighting or do you want to close this chapter?" If they want to fight, he fights with them. If they want to shut down or sell for parts to Google or Facebook, take a salary for two or three years, rest, and then come back, he'll back them again.
- Focus matters: founders can't run three or four companies or projects at once and still produce an outlier.

## Founder traits, superpowers and filling gaps

- He says no single founder archetype wins. Introvert or extrovert, sales, marketing, engineering or visionary, none is a common factor. What the successful ones share is that they are self-possessed, focused, care about the idea and don't give up.
- He asks founders to know their superpower and lean into it, because the rest can be hired. Examples he gives: Elon is an engineer and material scientist; Steve Jobs was a packager, brand builder and experience designer. His own superpower is talking.
- Check for introvert or extrovert energy: after an interview, do you have more or less energy? He comes out with more.
- Immigrant founders over-index: he cites the many Indian Americans running big companies and sees the work ethic and risk-taking of immigrant families as a driver. He thinks restricting immigration is "bonkers".

## Markets he wants founders to attack

- Look at big broken markets where technology has had little impact and ask which hard problem you hope a founder takes on.
- Robotics outside factories, especially replacing manual labor. Cafe X (robotic coffee) is his example, seen as the first frontier because it was the only robotics company he found that ran fully without human intervention; he expects robot arms to add steps each year (a cold foam, a donut) and eventually move to pizza or burgers.
- Housing and robotics building houses, including modular housing (his investment Blokable).
- Self-paced or small-group online education, in a US system where students take on huge debt. Examples: income share agreements, Steezy (dance) and Brilliant (math), vertical sites that teach one thing very well.
- On public tech stocks: he's not a stock picker, but notes mega-caps got more resilient by acquiring new business lines (Facebook with WhatsApp, Instagram and Oculus; Google with Android and YouTube; Disney with Pixar, Marvel and Star Wars) and that companies now stay private longer, until years 10 to 13.
