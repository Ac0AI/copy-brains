## From "good product" to realizing marketing was the problem

- Dagobert was a software engineer and consultant who left a good job to build a startup with his wife. They expected to be profitable within a couple of years.
- Sales didn't come. Customers' feedback and his own conclusion, after a couple of years, was that the product wasn't the problem, marketing was.
- Neither he nor his wife saw themselves as marketers. He had to choose between quitting or doing marketing anyway, and he kept going until he found a channel that worked, which turned out to be Twitter.

## Paid and sponsored channels failed first

- He assumed he could put money into Google Ads and get customers out, which he calls the dream of anyone who isn't into marketing.
- He spent $600 on Google Ads and got about 600 visits and zero sales. He found it humbling to see how much work it takes to convince people beyond buying clicks.
- Sponsoring newsletters also failed.
- That left organic reach. His reasoning was "I'm not going to keep paying, I have no choice."
- On Indie Hackers he found a voice, got on the front page a couple of times, and made a few sales. He then tested Indie Hackers, Reddit, product discussion boards and Twitter side by side.

## Why he picked Twitter over Indie Hackers

- He started on Twitter about 10-11 months before this talk, purely to promote his link. He searched for "what are you working on today" and "Indie makers, what are you building" posts and found an excuse to mention his product.
- He expected social traffic to be low quality, but found it similar in quality to Indie Hackers traffic.
- One of those promo tweets got about 60 likes, 2 sales and roughly 1,000 visitors to his site.
- His main argument is that Twitter is an open system with no ceiling. A great Indie Hackers post tops out at about 3,000 views and maybe 500 visitors to his site. On Twitter, reach keeps growing as followers grow.
- Over time he saw fewer sales from Indie Hackers and more from Twitter, and chose to focus on Twitter.

## Measure the channel, don't over-optimize it

- He tracked where each sale came from. Once he knew buyers came from his Twitter, he took that as proof the channel worked.
- He has lots of event tracking on his site but says "the simpler the better sometimes."
- He wasted a lot of time earlier overthinking tiny funnel optimizations that he hoped would double his conversion and that changed nothing.
- His view is to measure everything but not put too much hope in small optimizations, and to go partly on instinct.

## Story tweets beat one-liners and memes

- **What failed:** he first tried to sound like a Twitter guru, writing polished one-liners like the ones he saw succeeding. Nobody engaged. He believes a good one-liner is very hard because every word has to be perfect, and you need real authority to pull it off.
- **What worked:** he stopped pretending to know everything and shared his own experience with more humility ("this happened to me today"). That is when traction began.
- **The format:** a "story tweet" of about five to six short lines separated by blank lines, usually with an emoji at the end of each line. It follows a short arc of context, problem, solution, then the emotional ups and downs and what he learned.
- **No product pitch:** he talks about his startup without naming it, so the takeaway reads as a universal lesson.
- **Results:** at around 1,000 followers, story tweets often got about 500 likes. One got 1,600 clicks to his profile and about 300 new followers.
- **Memes:** he makes them himself, and began making them for fun, not as a strategy. They typically got 30 to 100 likes. People laugh and keep scrolling without clicking his profile, so they convert far worse than story tweets, even though memes are what people mention when they describe him.

## What to share when building in public

- A post needs a lesson learned or a strong emotion: excitement (for example a big sales day) or a hard day, as long as it isn't just depressing and gives people something to relate to or hope from.
- Most often he shares a lesson from something he went through.
- Turn a mundane update into a story: "Today I had this problem, I found this solution, and it gives me hope for X." A flat "I did this today" isn't useful to readers, but a problem-solution story lets them learn and recognize themselves in it.
- His rule: share what you went through, then what you got out of it and what you learned, so others can learn too.

## Personal account vs brand account

- Both can work, but he recommends using your own face and name.
- His example: would you rather follow Apple or Steve Jobs? Most people say Jobs, because they want to hear from the visionary and not product updates.
- To build an emotional bond and turn people into fans who trust you and spread the word, people need someone they can relate to, which is much harder to do with a brand.
- Valid reasons to hold back include wanting anonymity or not wanting to mix in personal or political content.
- If that applies, he suggests starting a fresh account. He did this himself after years of not using an older, family-oriented account, and a dedicated startup account made it easier to be himself without mixing in personal topics.

## Getting likes: engage first, and you don't need a huge audience

- **How the algorithm works:** your feed first shows people you have DMed, replied to or liked before. To get your tweets shown first to others, engage first with a handful of people you resonate with.
- **How to do it:** reply with value to their tweets, build a relationship and interact regularly. They will engage back, Twitter will show your content to them first, and your tweets get early likes and a chance to spread.
- **Be specific:** he agrees with advice to avoid random tweets. Pick a clear theme and post consistently.
- **Audience size:** you don't need millions of followers. He argues that the more followers someone has, the less successful they often are in business. Some people he once looked up to turned out to be struggling and broke.
- **What matters:** he says what matters is trust and deep connection with the right people, so that they buy when they need you. If you connect deeply enough, a small audience is sufficient, and it depends on your product and its price.
- **His own numbers:** he said he was making about $3K a month at the time. He attributed that to his product not being the best fit for his audience, not to a problem with the audience.
