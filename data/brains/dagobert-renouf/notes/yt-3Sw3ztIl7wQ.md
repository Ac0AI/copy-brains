## Money chasing, burnout, and the move to a startup

- Dagobert started making money online in high school with fan sites. One for a French Idol contestant ranked first on the contestant's name in the domain. He placed a Google ad directly against the download link and made about $150 in one night around the final.
- He then freelanced as a web developer and, at 27, moved to San Francisco to get a US engineering job. He landed a dream job at $100k a year.
- After a few months of buying whatever he wanted, he got bored. His realization: "all this time all I wanted was to create stuff and be on an adventure," and with nothing left to chase, he lost that.
- He now says he doesn't want money for its own sake: "it's empty." He chased it for almost 15 years.
- At 29 he left the safe job to build a startup with his savings, having tried freelancing and employment. His view: staying in a safe job leads to being miserable, so the risk is worth taking to find some happiness.
- He describes being "both fulfilled and full of anxiety" while living off savings. In February 2022, selling about one logo per week, he considered going back to a job, then rejected it ("remember what it's like having a boss") and went all in again.

## Finding the idea and building the first product (2018 to 2020)

- In September 2018 he saw a founder on Indie Hackers say he was "making a killing" with Logo Joy, an automated logo generator. His wife is a logo designer and he is an engineer, so they decided to build Logology together.
- Their plan was to start from scratch rather than copy Logo Joy. They had worked together before: she designed websites and he coded them.
- Progress was slow. His mother died in November 2018, which cost him about three months of productivity. He also "indulged" in engineering and building the perfect product.
- They launched in April 2020, about a year and a half after starting. He barely slept and kept perfecting the site overnight.

## Why the launch failed

- They announced to a waiting list of 100 people, expecting roughly 50 sales. They got one sale, from a friend, then no traffic and no sales.
- His diagnosis: they were "psyched up about our own product thinking it's so amazing" and had no marketing plan beyond announcing it on Reddit and Indie Hackers.
- A third co-founder joined for about a year to do marketing. He posted on Reddit and Indie Hackers, and a few hit posts brought some sales. Then it died out, and people started complaining about repeat posting ("you just posted that last week").
- Once launch channels were exhausted, they did what he says every founder does without a marketing plan: built more features to fix it.
- Costs: they were about $50k down in savings. His father told him to stop and get a job. About 50 people had bought a logo after a year and many loved it, and he did not want a job again, so they kept going.

## Marketing he tried that failed

- He avoided marketing because it's hard and he did not want to "write a sales letter" and convince people. He wanted the product to speak for itself.
- Google Ads produced zero sales. Sponsoring a few newsletters produced zero results.
- Realization in 2021: the missing piece was marketing. He still hated it and did not want another co-founder.

## What worked: spamming, then Twitter in public (May 2021)

- His first approach was brute force: put the link everywhere. On Twitter he searched for posts like "what are you working on today?" and replied with Logology about 20 times a day. He did the same on Product Hunt discussion boards, Indie Hackers and Reddit. For a couple of weeks nothing happened.
- First break: someone on Twitter asked which CSS technology he used for his website. As a front-end engineer he answered and shared his link. It went viral, with about 1,000 visits in 24 hours and 2 sales in a day.
- Second break: a visitor on the website live chat said the logos were too expensive and that he could just screenshot the free branding course his wife spent a month writing. Dagobert, who had about 200 followers then, posted the screenshot on Twitter and it went viral. He also got a sale, possibly out of pity.
- Lesson: sharing what he was going through, the real struggle, worked on Twitter. He went all in, spending about 10 hours a day on it.

## Results and diversifying beyond Twitter

- Revenue went from almost nothing to nearly $3k in his best month. It now hovers between $2k and $3k a month.
- He did not get more out of Twitter after the initial jump.
- Downside of Twitter: sales depend on posting. "If you don't tweet for one day, nobody comes to your website," which he finds exhausting.
- He is starting SEO. Twitter feeds it indirectly: podcast interviews (like this one) and articles written by people who found him on Twitter bring links to his site.
- Goal: spread sales across more sources so he can relax with "a good system."
