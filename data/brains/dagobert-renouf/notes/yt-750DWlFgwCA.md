## Why Dagobert argues indie making is a lottery, and what he sees as the "scam"

- Dagobert posted that indie making is a lottery and says he took heavy criticism for it. He compares it to telling a believer that "Jesus isn't real".
- He stands by it mostly. His point: Comp AI "got incredibly lucky to stumble upon this at the right time". Timing and market matter more than anything.
- The "scam" he describes is not entrepreneurship itself. It is the indie-making culture of "hey, I made millions by doing this, here's my MRR every month", where people mostly sell to other indie makers.
- It works like a pyramid: a few people at the top (he names Pieter Levels and Marc Lou) benefit as others buy their books, courses and boilerplates. He says these people do cool work and monetizing followers is fine.
- Survivorship bias: social media shows only the winners, so "it seems normal and easy". He calls selling to the indie-maker community "one of the hardest ways to be an entrepreneur" if you want to get rich.
- Criticizing a big name brings their fans after you, which he sees as unhealthy, almost cult-like.

## How he says to pick a market instead

- His main mistake over seven years of failing: he came "from the perspective of an artist and not an entrepreneur", building things that would be "cool" (a designer-quality $90 logo, a Product Hunt alternative that cares about founders), never asking "what's a growing market right now."
- Once you have the right market, "you don't have to do much". Years of indie making already gave him the skills (SEO, product), so the market is the missing piece.
- His method:
  - Do SEO research for underserved markets where people are searching.
  - Browse G2 and Capterra to see which products work or fail.
  - Build an alternative with a clear differentiator, such as different pricing.
  - Reach buyers through traditional channels: sales calls or outreach.
- He calls this "way better and way easier" than social media, especially for people who aren't naturally good at social.
- He sees opportunity in "old traditional business" sectors outside social-friendly and SEO-friendly verticals. At Comp AI he sold to a freight cargo shipping boat management software company using AI to disrupt. Incumbents have no competition, so they build poorly.
- He says Comp AI won because its founder came from indie making: a Next.js product against "[bleep]" competitors.
- Those niches can sustain SaaS priced at "100 times more than $9 a month".

## Sponsoring his wedding suit as a distribution stunt

- Context (July 2025): nearly out of money, launch day wasn't earning, and he wanted to marry his Russian partner partly because of visa limits. The smallest wedding was about €4,000, more than his remaining runway.
- Daniel Vassallo offered a $4,000 loan. A joke from the creator of Prompt Watch ("€500 if you put my logo on your suit") turned into a real offer, and he opened the suit to logo sponsors.
- Execution details:
  - He hired a stylist who makes his own coats, designed a monochrome white-logo suit, and made the suit good rather than a joke.
  - Prices ran from about €300 for small spots to about €1,500 for the large back area.
  - Hiring a videographer cost nearly €2,000, so he made little profit. He says people who paid most got the worst-placed spots, since the viral coverage focused on the cheaper ones.
- It went viral far beyond Twitter: newspapers, a relative's elderly grandfather in a Russian village, and now a Japanese TV documentary.
- Sales lesson: some people who "pledged" didn't pay until pushed. He publicly shamed one for not following through. People often assume you are joking, so you must ask for the money.
- Logo tip from his five years running a logo startup: a good logo must work in one color, and should carry the brand name unless you're Nike.
- Real purpose: to "feel the support of the community" when he needed it. About 90% of sponsors were people he knew.

## How he got into sales at Comp AI and what he earned

- Selling suit sponsors showed him he loved sales ("you talk to someone and in five minutes they send you 500, 1,000").
- Comp AI CEO Lewis Carhart, who had sponsored his earlier launch product, saw the suit and they started talking. Dagobert offered to help sell, and joined around August 2025, before the October wedding.
- He had interviewed about 70 founders for his launch platform and says Lewis was the only one he thought could become a billionaire.
- Comp AI sells compliance (SOC 2 and similar) to startups. It reached roughly $400K ARR within months of its Product Hunt launch, partly through the founder's own selling.
- Results:
  - Sold about $200K of annual recurring revenue per month at peak, mostly from inbound demos booked as the brand went viral.
  - Earned about $30K/month pre-tax.
  - Closed about 250 deals over the year, with only about 4 churns, the lowest churn on the team. Later hires outperformed his close rate by roughly 10-15%.
- Team grew from about 5-6 people when he joined to about 30 when he left, and he lost the autonomy, the small-team feel and some of his usefulness. He was also in France while the company hired in the US.
- He says he cannot discuss the company's viral social persona and was not involved in it.

## His sales approach: be real and push people away

- Before this he saw sales as a "job for idiots"; he was afraid his style would not work.
- His method, once in a flow state: "push people away". He tells prospects up front "I don't think it's for you, man" and documents why.
- Why it works:
  - It protects your energy at about 10 real conversations a day.
  - Telling someone who actually needs the product that it isn't for them builds immediate trust, and "you win" against competitors.
- He says you don't have to be the "used car salesman"; being genuine leaves everyone happy, which also explains the low churn.
- He argues many people avoid sales because of that stereotype, so skilled, honest sellers are scarce and valuable. He had multiple strong offers right after leaving.

## Lessons on money, ambition and working alone

- Making 30K/month changed his view of indie making: he was "fighting for my life" for a $5K/month goal. He now says that after 70-hour weeks with no safety net, $5K/month is "way too low" and "not worth it". He wouldn't get out of bed without a shot at around $50K.
- He says succeeding for one year at Comp AI taught him far more than seven years of failure, and that glorifying going it alone is "completely stupid": solo, you cannot take a bad day or a free-minded vacation.
- He wants co-founders now: someone builds, he sells.
- Ideas he favors:
  - Take a high-paying job first. He says earning $200-300K is easier than succeeding as an entrepreneur, and raises your standard before you return to building (Daniel Vassallo earned about $400K at Amazon before indie making).
  - Ship quick fun side projects with zero revenue pressure to keep momentum, while the real bet is a B2B product that uses sales.
- Perfectionism: at his peak he took nine months to ship a one-page site (launch day), and had never launched on Product Hunt. He spent about six months vibe-coding an AI therapist partly to work through it.
- He cut his Twitter use because the feed distorts thinking: "The best way to have good tweets is to not be on Twitter." He worries about playing the spicy, controversial role for engagement.
- Side project: a tool where people sponsor his Twitter profile, which made $3,000 in about five hours of work. He notes it feels like "nothing" now versus the past.
- Current plan (September 2026 interview): about 9 months of runway with a baby due, building B2B with co-founders while shipping fun projects on Twitter.
