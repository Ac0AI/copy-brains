## Burnout comes from too many open threads, not too much work
- After pushing hard on his Twitter course (Dominate Twitter), Dagobert hit a down period over Christmas.
- He says the cause was mostly lack of clarity: "when I get too many things I get lost and I lose direction and when I lose direction I lose productivity and I burn out quickly."
- The pile-up included course improvements, a Logology version 2, tweet writing, and company admin and accounting he had ignored for months.
- His fix:
  - Clear the neglected admin.
  - Decide the course is "good enough" and stop improving it, with only small updates a few times a year.
  - Write two months of content ahead so he can move on to something else.
- Once the backlog was done he felt relaxed and free to start something new.

## Batching: a week of writing for two months of tweets
- Dagobert's usual system is one concentrated week of writing roughly two months of tweets and memes ahead of time.
- He had skipped it for a stretch, and the tweets posted in that gap were low quality. They got far fewer likes even though impressions held up.
- He would rather stop posting than "post low quality just to post."
- For the next batch he plans to:
  - Add long-form threads.
  - Add a few meme threads.
  - Write shorter, more authoritative advice-style tweets, "better" versions of the platitude format.

## Threads: value, not a growth hack
- Dagobert was long wary of threads because they feel like a growth tactic, and most of them are "boring as [ __ ]."
- He has written only two in his whole time on Twitter, and people loved both. He says that is because threads are a different way of bringing value, not because they are a hack.
- Planned thread: his full strategy for launching the course, start to finish, since many people asked how it succeeded.
- He also plans more meme threads, because one went very popular a few months earlier.

## Follower count changes how people read you
- Reaching 10K followers was, for Dagobert, the biggest shift in how people perceive him.
- Sales of his product rose "overnight" because 10K made him look more credible.
- At a larger size, his old long personal "story tweets" stopped landing. Followers now expect advice from him, not just stories.
- He says a tweet from someone with 100 followers carries a different weight than the same tweet from someone with 50K.

## Why polished content goes stale: build, fail, then tweet
- Early on, Dagobert was deep in building Logology, so his tweets were raw, lived and resonated.
- Over time he refined tweeting into a craft, so it became less spontaneous. He thinks readers can feel that it is "rehashed stuff that I already said last year."
- Diagnosis: he spent a year mostly tweeting and had nothing new to say. "You can't [ __ ] people," he adds, and he isn't trying to.
- His remedy is to stop spending all his time tweeting and go build Logology v2: get "slapped again by reality," experience failures, and tweet about those stories.
- Rule he draws: spend time building or experiencing things, then tweet about them.

## Links in tweets: get engagement with or without the link
- Mechanism: a link doesn't hide the tweet. People click it, leave, and don't engage with the tweet, so it gets no extra visibility and dies.
- He gives three ways around it:
  1. Make the tweet itself interesting and curiosity-provoking, then put the link in a follow-up tweet. People engage first, then go looking.
  2. Add an incentive to engage even after clicking. For his course's Black Friday sale, the offer was 40% off, with 60% off if you retweeted.
  3. His usual approach: keep the link in his bio and make people curious enough to click his profile. For example, "here's a new episode of my podcast doing this and that." People who want to support him will engage and visit his profile.

## Video clips with subtitles
- Dagobert recalls a podcast he guested on two years earlier. The host posted very short (about 15-second) clips with highly visible subtitles.
- A clip of him explaining the Twitter algorithm got about 150 likes and brought the host many listeners.
- His takeaway: a short, valuable bite with subtitles can do well and make people want to tune in to the full show. Without subtitles, it won't work as well.

## Validating a product by selling it publicly
- Dagobert's takeaway from watching a product tested on Twitter: share the product with an audience and see instantly whether people want it.
- If they don't, you avoid wasting time. If they pay, "they validate it with their money" and it is clearly going to be a successful product.
- He adds that showing a real product being built is more exciting to an audience of indie makers and startup founders than content about services, because they all want to create things.
