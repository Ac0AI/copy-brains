## Why Dagobert and his wife built Logology

- Dagobert is a French developer who started in web development and design at 15. He later specialized in front-end engineering (React, JS) and worked for French, Russian and US companies.
- He describes himself as "pretty easily bored". After a job he liked, he asked "is that it?" and concluded he had "no other idea except to try" building his own company.
- The idea came on his honeymoon road trip through the US South. He and his wife, a designer, saw someone talk about success with a logo business and decided to build one. The idea dates to roughly three years before this October 2021 interview.
- His wife has designed logos for 10+ years, including for successful French startups. She charged about 2,000 euros per logo.
- Many people loved her work but had only about 200. She had to say no, or she wasted time on low-paid jobs.
- The insight: automate her process so the same quality costs about 100 dollars instead of 2,000.

## How Logology works

- Logology is a website that generates logos. The logos are real designs, not icons pulled from a database.
- Dagobert's wife has designed more than 600 logos over roughly 2.5 years. The catalog is organized by startup industry (productivity apps, communication apps, cleantech, biotech, marketing and more), with logos meant to carry meaning for each.
- The flow copies how she works with clients. She asks about vision, values and target customer, then proposes ideas. Customers rarely say "do a bird", so the value is in what the designer brings.
- Users answer 11 questions, which take about 3-4 minutes. The questions are abstract, like "if your startup was a movie, what would it be?".
- Logology then matches the user with logos, fonts and colors and sells the result for about 100 dollars.
- It also describes the user's brand personality. Users say "that's me", and some told them to sell just that.
- Some users find the abstract questions off-putting. They are looking for ways to make it easier.
- They A/B test the homepage button text. They are cautious about testing the button color because their visual identity is strong.

## Getting the first customer

- The first customer came a year before the interview, in late April, right after they announced the product. She was a loose acquaintance from a friend group.
- She came to the site, found "the logo I've always wanted", and bought it within hours.
- Dagobert's takeaway: the product is very good for a few people, who fall in love and buy instantly. The hard part is repeating that with everyone.
- He says a friend buying still counts, since it is still value exchanged for money.
- Early on they used FullStory session recordings to find UX problems and watched the first buyer's session live. He later removed it because it felt "very creepy" and made them stop working.

## When a startup should invest in a logo

- He used to tell founders not to spend 2,000 on a logo when they have 10,000 to build the product, and to find profitability first.
- He still believes a startup shouldn't spend real money on a logo. That is why Logology is deliberately cheap rather than pivoting upmarket.
- But his view has shifted. Early internet products (Google, YouTube, Facebook) could win on innovation alone. Today, with no-code, "hundreds or even thousands of products" launch daily, and landing pages and logos all look the same.
- Branding is more important than 5-10 years ago for standing out. A distinctive brand gives people "a bit of a pause" instead of a skip.
- The product, copy and differentiation still matter, and may matter more.
- His rule of thumb: with zero customers, don't bother. Once you have about 10-20 people who love what you do, a logo is a useful next step.

## Tools and recommendations

- Daily tool: Roam Research, for networked notes and connecting ideas (related to the Zettelkasten method).
- Books: "Zero to One" by Peter Thiel is his go-to. He also recommends April Dunford's "Obviously Awesome", which helped them figure out their positioning and the problem they had communicating the product.
- Indie hacker to watch: Danny Postma, who executes fast on varied ideas. One example was turning a landing page site into a tool for building landing pages from inspiring blocks.
