## How Dagobert built and validated the Twitter course

Dagobert's Twitter course came from the same process he'd use for a SaaS: find the problems first, then build.

- He wrote the landing page before recording anything, to test whether the course could be engaging and solve a big problem.
- He asked his Twitter audience about their main problem with Twitter and compiled the answers into the value props.
- Core problems he targeted: people don't understand why they get no engagement, they waste time on Twitter, and they can't come up with tweets. So the course has an engagement section, a time routine, and a writing framework.
- His rule: "focus on problems, find two or three core features", exactly as he would for software.
- He first planned a book, spent a month writing it in Lisbon, and found each chapter took two days to make good. A friend suggested video because it tolerates more mistakes.

## Production process and cost

- Five months of full-time work on the course and nothing else.
- Unscripted on purpose, to avoid sounding robotic: he worked from an outline of 23 chapters in the extended version. Each chapter took about two hours of takes.
- Over 30 hours of footage and about 60 hours of editing, a first for him.
- He spent a week watching about 30 hours of YouTube on lighting and sound.
- Gear cost about 500 dollars: three or four lights, three stands, a big sheet of paper for the background, t-shirts. He filmed on an iPhone rather than buy a 2K camera, since a failure would have cost too much.
- Work rate ramped from about 32 hours in week one to over 70 hours a week for two straight weeks before launch.
- Reception: people say it's the most in-depth Twitter course they've taken, and word of mouth now drives sales.

## Launch playbook: relationships first, then ask

- During the five months he replied to people whose Twitter questions he'd answered, offering to DM them when the course came out. That built a list of 150 people.
- He had a list of about 50 mid-to-large accounts he'd supported for a year. He DMed them a week before launch and again on launch day. He was "blown away" by how many retweeted.
- His explanation: "you just give give give give and then eventually you ask", and they say yes because you gave first.
- Early access: three weeks before launch he sold about 10 copies to hardcore followers and used their feedback to improve the course. He gave a few to bigger accounts for free.
- Timing: he launched the last week of October so a Black Friday promo would fall at least a month later. Closer than that, he feared full-price buyers would feel burned.
- Result: about 10K dollars in the first week. Then 5 to 20 sales a day through November.

## Black Friday mechanic

- 40% off, plus another 20% off (60% total) if the buyer retweets the Black Friday tweet.
- The aim was engagement, since tweets with links get less reach.
- Result: about 30K dollars in November, roughly 10K+ in December, and about 3K in the first 12 days of January.
- Why he expected it to work: he ran a 50% Black Friday for Logology the year before. It created awareness, and sales stayed above normal at full price afterward. This year Logology made about 1,800 dollars in that week.
- Sales now run about 2 to 5 a day. He mails stickers to buyers, who tweet about them, which spreads the course.
- Main version costs 80 dollars. A cheaper compressed version exists, but about 99% of buyers take the big one.

## Why the course sold: traffic was already solved

- Logology already got 5K to 10K+ visitors a month from his Twitter account.
- He knew creators like Daniel Vassallo and Arvid convert far more traffic with a course. So with a link in his pinned tweet and bio, a killer product on top of the same traffic should sell.
- He rejected the claim that courses only sell at launch: he gets steady monthly traffic to it, as with Logology.
- He saw his audience grow from about 5K followers to roughly 50K in under a year.
- His lesson for others: find what your audience needs and build it for them.

## Pricing, perception and what people pay for

- Indians bought the course far more than they ever bought logos. He first thought Indians were cheap, but they just don't value logos. A logo is far from money. A Twitter course promises more audience and sales.
- His rule: "the closer you are to the money, the higher the value". Communities and logos feel far from money. Sponsoring a podcast whose listeners buy your product feels close to it.
- Courses sell partly on the feeling of progress. Buying already feels like moving toward the goal, even if the buyer never watches.
- Design test he proposed: does your product let someone feel they gain something even if they buy it and never use it? Example: he paid 300 dollars for a one-time-fee maker community because it put him near successful makers, then left after a couple of months.
- He estimates a logo is seven times harder to sell than a Twitter course. French customers are the hardest to convert.
- Idea for Logology: sell a guide to designing a whole startup identity, something people feel they'll always need.

## Treating the course as a product

- He treats it like a SaaS with long-term updates. Plan: a new video (about 20 minutes) a few times a year on algorithm changes and what to change in your strategy. He sends free updates and bonuses.
- Gumroad stays his platform despite roughly 8% plus fees:
  - It handles affiliates (over 5K dollars from affiliates out of about 50K at the time).
  - It also handles newsletters, file delivery and updates, and visible ratings.
  - Its Discover section brings 2 to 3% of his sales.
  - He sees the fee like Stripe's 3% on Logology, as a convenience cost.
- Ratings: about 88 of 90 are five stars. Complaints are rare.
- Success case: Kyle was stuck at a couple thousand followers for years. After applying the course his impressions and startup sales rose about 4x and he neared 10K followers.

## Twitter risk, breaks and staying flexible

- He takes breaks: he hadn't tweeted for four days at the time. He says that after a break you lose some reach because others filled the gap, so he spends extra time reconnecting when he returns.
- He now needs about two hours a day on Twitter, down from 10 hours when he started.
- Twitter is a "public square", not an email list. Impressions reach followers plus strangers.
- He doesn't plan a newsletter backup. He prefers the conversation on Twitter and is comfortable with uncertainty after years on Logology.
- He expects Twitter to keep improving under Elon Musk and cites the removal of nft spam bots.
- Business mindset: "everything will end eventually." Build savings and keep learning. The skills (courses, audience building) transfer to any platform. At the time the course revenue covered about a year of full salary, and he didn't feel they'd "made it".
- Logology is not abandoned. He wants to grow it to at least 10K a month and later teach that.
