## Dagobert on handling uncertainty and spontaneity in content

- He avoids scripting and recitation. For his Twitter course he records each chapter from a title and a few notes, talks for about 30 minutes, then cuts it later. He says scripted delivery "would feel like kind of dead," while spontaneous delivery carries energy and emotion.
- He deliberately avoids preparing for podcasts. When hosts send questions in advance, he purposely doesn't read them, because he wants to train himself to be spontaneous.
- He was inspired by Lil Wayne, who stopped hiding his lyrics and got better by doing hundreds of songs a year.
- He says he has more fun speaking English than French because it feels less serious and mistakes feel more forgivable.
- When cutting video, he plans to cut where he can show an image, so only the audio cuts and the edit isn't obvious.

## Big dreams, small goals, and why ambition causes procrastination

- He started his startup (Logology) with his wife four years ago and says he got comfortable with uncertainty only recently.
- He realized he was procrastinating because a huge ambition creates a fantasy of what must be achieved. Since the outcome can't be guaranteed, he fell into perfectionism, reassuring himself by polishing tiny details for months.
- His fix: "keep having big dreams" but "focus on small goals." For the course, he would be legitimately happy selling "a couple dozen copies" to people he can help, and that mindset made recording possible.
- He gave the same advice to a founder (Shield) earning a couple hundred dollars a month who wondered whether to quit: try reaching the next goal, such as $500, then $1,000, then $10,000.
- He says dreaming can't fail, while doing always involves struggle, sometimes not even your fault (market, customers). But "when you're in it you have a chance to get better, so get in it as fast as possible."
- He still holds big dreams (1 million Twitter followers, a course making $50k in its first week) while accepting he doesn't control outcomes, and he stays grateful for the small steps.

## Too much consumed knowledge fuels perfectionism

- He stopped reading books, listening to podcasts and reading most articles. In his 20s he consumed so much that "nothing was good enough" and nothing checked all the boxes he knew existed, which fed perfectionism and fear of launching.
- Too much information leaves him unsure which way to do things. He says it's time to start doing, experiment, and learn for yourself.
- He would read a book only when facing a very clear problem it could solve, not for reading that only confuses.

## Twitter growth: the novelty curve and refreshing your content

- He went from about 150 followers in May 2021 to hundreds of thousands of people exposed, after a few months of figuring out the algorithm. He calls Twitter free, high-potential distribution that can expose you to people who don't know you, which a newsletter can't do.
- Growth curve: about 1,000 to 10,000 followers between September and December 2021 as "the hot new thing." Like fashion or a hit song, novelty fades and people get tired.
- His read on big accounts complaining about lost engagement: they repeat the same ideas for six months, so people get bored. He says he did the same, and the same joke told a hundred times gets less funny. He names Jack Dorsey as an example of someone with few likes relative to followers who doesn't complain.
- His response is "season two": new, deeper, more philosophical content still related to startups. In year one he spent hours a day thinking of content, then kept repeating it to capitalize on it.
- His Twitter course targets the first stage: getting your thoughts heard when "you tweet some things and nobody hears about them," via strategy and the algorithm. A possible later course would cover maintaining success.
- He expects waves: audiences come and go, algorithm changes shift content, and bad months pass. He ties this to startups ("there's a new wave coming up at some point").

## Twitter and newsletter work together

- Twitter is for spreading ideas and reaching new people. Most followers are not fans; they saw something they liked and rarely return.
- A newsletter is a deeper, long-term relationship with real fans. People who complain about Twitter engagement often built large newsletters because of Twitter.
- He says you need both. He has no active newsletter beyond a product-specific one.
- He loves Twitter as a social space ("my own personal co-working space"), which is why he doesn't move to TikTok or LinkedIn.

## Acquisition channels: keep trying, don't get stuck

- Twitter is his main acquisition channel, so lower reach cuts Logology sales. He is responding by starting SEO and trying more channels.
- Affiliates: he opened the product to affiliates about a year before this interview. For roughly a year it produced almost nothing and cost about $30 a month. In the last couple of months, 10 to 15 percent of sales came from affiliates (about $400 a month), because some affiliates finally found a good way to talk about the product.
- His rule: don't give up too soon (he could have stopped affiliates after three months), but don't stay stuck repeating what isn't working. "Keep doing different things."

## Personal brand versus company brand

- He says it is about 10 times easier to build an audience for a person than for a brand. People want the founder, the vision and behind-the-scenes content (his example: you'd follow Elon Musk, not SpaceX product updates).
- Exceptions: if you plan to sell the startup, or run very different products in unrelated industries, a brand account makes more sense. If most of his traffic came from a Logology account, it would be easier to sell; his traffic comes from his personal account.
- If you build several products around one topic, he recommends a personal brand. He plans more products (a Twitter course, possibly others), all related to marketing and branding, which fits one audience.

## Small, enjoyable business versus chasing a unicorn; finding the middle on effort

- Logology numbers: about $3K a month revenue against about $5K needed for two people, plus roughly two years of runway from savings. He says he is grateful not to have a job.
- He criticizes founders who earn enough but feel unhappy because they chase big lifestyles. He cites a solo founder making about $3,000 a month who pivoted after seeing competitors earn more, neglected the product, and lost Dagobert as a paying customer.
- He cites Peter Levels (about 60 products before one made millions) as an approach that works for some, but says he prefers a small business he enjoys. He thinks many "good enough" businesses never exist because people quit when they don't make millions in three months.
- On effort: he warns against both extremes. Perfectionism cost him about 20 months to launch plus nearly a year more to find real traction. A weekend launch that fails also avoids pain because there is no attachment.
- His advice: with some pre-validation, spend one to two months giving it "a real go," not perfectionist, then see how people react. For his course he spent about $400 on lights, a black background and a mic, using an iPhone instead of buying a camera.
- Both extremes avoid the pain of failure. He says embracing that pain is fuel to keep improving, and his Twitter course failing would hurt him.
- He says early confidence was arrogance based on fantasy. Real confidence comes after launching, failing and learning, like learning to swim by going in the water.
