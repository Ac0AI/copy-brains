## Putting logos on a wedding suit: turning a funding problem into community and a job

Dagobert Renouf was a bootstrapping entrepreneur for seven years (no outside funding). Over the summer of 2025 he launched a solo startup that wasn't going well, and he was running out of money. He wanted to marry his Russian fiancée, who he met almost two years earlier, and visa issues pushed them to speed up the wedding. He couldn't afford a wedding but refused to give up on either the wedding or the startup.

He posted to his social media community about his entrepreneur journey: "I have to find a way to pay for my wedding. No idea how I'm going to do it." He said the minimum wedding he could do was 4,000 euros.

## How the idea came out of an audience post

- A famous entrepreneur friend in his community offered to loan him the 4,000 euros, with no obligation to repay if he couldn't. Dagobert says the friend "just wanted to support" him.
- Someone joked that if he needed help, he should put their logo on his suit. Dagobert notes that in an entrepreneur community "everybody wants to promote themselves. That's what we do."
- He took the joke seriously. His reasoning: after years in business, "You've got to get out of your comfort zone. You try crazy shit if you want to make it."
- He posted the offer on X, and dozens of people said they wanted a logo on his suit.

## Selling and pricing the spots

- He sold spots at about 300 euros on average.
- In total he sold about 10,000 euros' worth of logo placements.
- 26 entrepreneurs ended up supporting the suit, and he knew almost all of them personally.
- Selling was harder than he expected, in part because he had to convince people that his fiancée was on board.
- Many backers told him to keep the money and skip the logo. He insisted on doing the suit anyway. He describes it as a wedding gift between entrepreneurs.

## Making the idea acceptable to his fiancée

- She hated it at first. All she saw was people on X joking about it and making fun.
- What changed her mind was reframing it: the logos were a way to bring his community to their wedding.
- Her condition: only companies he loved, mostly indie companies and people he knew.

## Executing it so it looks good

- Once committed, Dagobert wanted it to look good, not like a joke. He hired a talented but expensive stylist friend, quoted at about 5,000 euros.
- The final logo list was set at the end of August, with the wedding in October.
- Printing logos directly on the suit would have required prototypes and more time, so they used embroidery instead.
- The result was a high-quality suit that "doesn't look like a joke" in person, and family and friends loved it.
- During the wedding he was more stressed than his wife because he felt he owed sponsors visible logos in the photos and videos.

## Persistence through public criticism

- Dagobert says it took months of posting about the suit, and half the responses each time said things like "This is so bad. This is disgusting."
- He sometimes felt he was the only one who saw the beauty in it, because entrepreneurship is his life: "There's nothing on the suit I don't like. I can almost see the face of every founder associated with their company."
- His takeaway: "At first, they think you're crazy, and then it seems obvious." He says this was the first time he experienced that pattern as an entrepreneur.

## The unexpected result: a sales job

- The cofounder of Comp AI, an acquaintance, kept replying to Dagobert's suit posts asking to put his company's logo on it.
- Dagobert had also realized he could do sales. The cofounder told him, "Let's get you a sales job."
- Dagobert now works in tech sales at the startup Comp AI, based in Lille, France.
- He says he couldn't see any way of living other than entrepreneurship for years, and that without the suit, "I would have never gotten this job."
