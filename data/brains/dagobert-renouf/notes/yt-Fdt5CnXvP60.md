## Bootstrapping with a spouse: building Logology

- Dagobert and his wife, an experienced logo designer, started Logology in September 2018. He had saved money from a software engineering job; she was a freelance brand consultant.
- The problem: startups came to her wanting a brand, but projects started around $3,000 and they only had $100-200. Her answer to them was "no solution".
- The idea was to automate her process into three steps:
  - A five-minute quiz that finds the brand personality. She used to do this in a one-day workshop with post-it notes.
  - Matching logos from a pre-designed library. She designed more than 700, after about 15 years of experience. Focusing on startups let them split the market into brand personalities and industries.
  - Customizing font and color within boundaries she set, so the result can't look ugly.
- He describes it as "an automated version of her". The wife does the creative work ahead of time and the product delivers it instantly.
- The name: the first choice, "Logo Stories", was already trademarked in France. After about three weeks of brainstorming they landed on "Logology" (the study of the meaning of symbols). They didn't like it at first, now love it.
- They bootstrap on purpose. They want to get rich, but mostly want freedom to work on their own terms, and he sees investors as a compromise.
- When money ran low, his wife took on occasional custom logo contracts, about one every two months, to keep them afloat while Logology grew. At the time of this interview they were close to profitability.
- Extras he mentions: saving the quiz process gives a free branding course (colors, symbols, fonts, casing) with five versions, one per brand personality. A guide on applying the brand beyond the logo was in progress.

## Growing on Twitter from 150 to 15,000+ followers in eight months

- Context: after about three years, the product was good and people loved it, but almost nobody knew about it, so sales were near zero.
- He had tried ads, blogs and communities. A reply that went viral and brought a few sales pushed him to commit to Twitter, with "nothing else to lose".
- Eight months before the interview he had about 150 followers; at the interview, over 15k.
- His content: daily stories about his startup journey, plus memes. Memes started as fun for a few friends (his first got 2-3 likes) and later became video memes.
- The mistake he says many people make is treating Twitter "like a TED talk", broadcasting thoughts to the world. For a normal founder it's about one-on-one connection:
  - Visit people's profiles, look at what they post, engage constantly.
  - Connect authentically, not in a fake networking way. He sees Twitter as more relaxed than LinkedIn.
- Why it sells: startup founders are his target customers. They relate to his story, connect with him, and come to Logology when they need a logo.
- Party analogy: you don't skip hello and then say "come to my party tomorrow". Mingle first, plug your product when it's relevant.

## What counts as spam, and what to avoid on Twitter

- Never use "Promote" on your tweets. Even paying $10 for reach is, in his view, a red flag, because viewers can no longer trust the likes and engagement were real.
- Spam is not binary. The same reply can be spam or value depending on context.
- Good: replying to a productivity tip with something that adds to the conversation, then mentioning your productivity app with some humility.
- Bad: dropping your crypto project link under tweets about startups.
- The test: is it relevant, is it humble, and are you adding value or just plugging?

## Why bootstrappers should focus on marketing and ignore big startup books

- He read many startup books before launching (he names ideas like crossing the chasm, unfair advantage and big markets). He calls the concepts good but written for VC-funded startups.
- That advice assumes money to "throw a million dollars on Facebook ads" and run experiments. A tiny bootstrapped startup can't use it.
- He says there is very little material about starting when you are tiny and unfunded.
- His advice:
  - Experiment yourself.
  - Find communities of people like you.
  - Don't read too much into the big startup books, because they can make you overthink and try to answer questions you don't need to answer yet.
- His main tweeting theme is that founders should focus on marketing more than on product.

## Product Hunt strategy

- He had not launched yet and says to take his view "with a grain of salt", based on watching friends launch.
- Two ways to launch:
  - Launch at the start to get first users and feedback. You may be disappointed by traction, but it kickstarts a new project.
  - Launch later, once you have a solid product, happy customers and some audience. His chosen approach.
- Why later: existing momentum can propel you to top spots of the day, and the push can go viral in a way that is nearly impossible without it.
- Follow-on benefits: the "product of the day" badge helps conversion, you can contact media, and the launch becomes part of your story for PR.
- His framing: Product Hunt is step two, not step one. Step one ("hey look at my product") fails because nobody knows you.

## Logo and branding opinions

- Branding is about meaning, not just aesthetics. A logo should convey the company's values and story.
- Calendly's new logo: he disliked it. It's a blob that "doesn't mean anything", and he preferred the earlier simple calendar-with-a-C.
- Alphabet's logo: a plain one is fine because it's a holding company and not customer-facing, so marketing doesn't depend on it.
- Similar logos: every simple logo has probably been done somewhere, since there are limited shapes. What matters is whether two companies compete in the same space and cause customer confusion (a legal issue). The simpler the symbol, the likelier overlap, so the story around it matters more.
- Reading a logo: orange and pointy reads as aggressive and action-oriented. Good for coaching, wrong for baby diapers. An upward arrow signals progression and aspiration, and a dark corporate palette signals business.
- A designer's rule of thumb (his wife's view): a logo with too much detail breaks when shrunk onto a tiny sweater tag. Font weight should balance the symbol.

## Approach to new tech and crypto

- He holds a small amount of crypto for fun, using money earned from it. He bought Bitcoin about five years earlier, it roughly 10x'd, and he sold enough to recover his original money.
- He admits he doesn't really understand crypto, but makes small bets so he doesn't miss out.
- In his 20s he jumped on every new technology. His current rule: ignore new tech at first, and if it is still in the news about three years later, pay attention. He says you aren't really late, because you are still early.
- On accepting crypto payments at Logology: no objection, but it needs implementation time and tax handling. He's focused on what helps customers and sales, and doesn't think crypto does yet.
- On adoption: businesses mostly aren't for it because he sees no clear problem it solves for them.
