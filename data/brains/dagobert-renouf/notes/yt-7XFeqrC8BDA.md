## Burning out on social-media growth, then rebuilding around honesty and community

Dagobert was a freelancer for about 12 years before founding a startup. He ran Logology (a logo generator) with his then-partner for 5 years, burned out in the process, and recovered over roughly two years. He treats that burnout as the turning point for how he builds now.

- He first burned out at around 25, when freelance clients finally arrived all at once. The survival adrenaline vanished once money came in, and he refunded about half the projects.
- He assumed his own startup would not burn him out because "motivation comes from inside". He was wrong.
- The second burnout hit right after success. Logology made about 4-5k a month and his Twitter course made about 10k a month, so about 15k a month combined, after 5 years of mostly zero and 2 years of 2-3k.
- Reaching the 10k goal solved nothing. Taxes in France ate the gains, he could not afford an apartment, and he realized he was unhappy in his relationship.
- Symptoms: a racing heart (resting normally 55-60, but 90 sitting and 130 standing), near-fainting, and being unable to get out of bed. He thought it was long COVID for 5 weeks. Naming it as burnout and depression improved his symptoms about 50% instantly.

## What changed after the burnout

- He now focuses on purpose more than money.
- He no longer builds on a "if we hit X, everything will be fine" milestone, because the 10k milestone proved empty.
- He stopped sacrificing his vision to grow: "[ __ ] money." He accepts the risk because last time money did not fix his deeper problems.
- He spent 8 months in a salaried job at a Paris startup, Abbyal, which gave him financial security and let him recover.
- He observes that many products he loved turned bad once they sold or chased growth. He refuses to follow that path with Launch Day.
- He keeps Launch Day affordable for indie makers and will not charge more than about 100 euros, even though that limits revenue. He also keeps doing the long founder interviews.
- He is looking for side income because the model is not yet sustainable financially.

## Twitter/X growth: the strategy changes by stage

Dagobert distinguishes how he grew from how he posts now.

- **Early (about 4 years ago):** he replied to people asking "what are you working on today?" with a link to Logology, roughly 10 times a day. One reply took off and brought 2 sales in a day, which was huge for them.
- **Growth phase:** he then did about 250 replies a day for 6 months to a year. It was effectively networking: engaging, making friends, and finding relevant content to post.
- **Results:** Logology went from about 200 a month to 3,000 a month in 6 months. He went from 0 to about 6,000 followers, and he grew from about 10K to 50K after that. His course covers this path, updated for the new algorithm.
- **Cost:** it was exhausting. Being dependent on social media is stressful because one algorithm change can kill you.
- **Now (large audience):** he posts whatever he wants, including very personal things. He lost some followers for a while, then found people liked the authenticity.
- **His view on sequencing:** he said in his course that once you are big you should stop trying to make friends and start being yourself, and he thinks starting with zero followers and full authenticity is very scary.
- **Takeaway for newcomers:** he is not sure everybody can start authentic. He would not have had the confidence to do this at zero followers.

## The wedding suit as a sponsorship product

- Dagobert needed about 4,000 euros for a bare-minimum wedding (20 guests) while he had about 6,000 euros left and no family backup.
- He tweeted about it. Daniel Vasalo offered a 4,000 loan, the first money he had ever accepted from anyone.
- Someone then joked they would pay $500 to put their startup's logo on his suit. He retweeted asking if anyone else wanted to, and about 20 people said yes. He says: "that's product market fit."
- He took it seriously after that. He hired a stylist (a designer who had made thousands of suits) he had previously paid about 3,000 euros, and worked with him for a week on the design.
- The suit costs about 3,000 and has logo spots sold to indie makers. The best back spot is a bidding, with the top bid at 1,600 euros. One friend bought a large spot for 800 euros.
- He planned a QR-code option, a spot on the sleeve/heart area at 100 euros or less, so low-budget makers can take part, since many only had 50-100.
- He framed it to his fiancée as bringing "my people" to a wedding where he has almost no friends attending. It also makes the wedding feel interesting to him instead of boring.
- Wedding presents can be framed as business expenses, which he calls a joke.

## Why community matters to him

- Indie makers often live in small cities with nobody to share the journey with. He has one close friend who is not on the same path.
- The community moved from indiehikers.com (5 years ago, now dead) to Twitter, which he says is less lively than 3 years ago because some people left over Elon's politics.
- "You need people to be able to share the journey with... just being alone doing that it's too hard."
- This was what helped him through the burnout and why he wants the wedding to include the community.

## Launch Day: a product launch event, not a Product Hunt clone

- **Origin:** in September 2024 he was fed up with Product Hunt. He thinks it is still worth launching on (only upside), but says almost nobody actually visits it. He also pointed to press articles aimed at investors saying that, because Product Hunt is owned by VC, its portfolio companies get pushed, so "it's not a fair game."
- **Validation:** his first tweet pitching a better version drew huge traction. He sold about 60 spots at $29 in one week. He then waited 9 months to launch because of his burnout and divorce.
- **Why he changed the format:** copying Product Hunt would be useless. What he knows works is showing yourself and telling your story, so people care and then buy.
- **How it works:**
  - It runs once or twice a month, for 48 hours only.
  - About 15 products take part, each with a unique, limited-time discount. The limit creates fear of missing out and he says people don't buy without it.
  - He negotiates hard for the biggest discounts.
  - Each founder gets a long interview (half an hour to an hour), published as the Launch Day podcast. That averages about one interview a day.
  - Two months in, it broke even at about 3,000 a month, so he can stay at zero.
- **Selection:** he rejects founders who won't open up or who won't give a real discount, and he is honest about fit. For example, an education-for-kids product might not sell because his audience is mostly indie makers.
- **The anchor-founder tactic:** each launch includes at least one "star" with strong social media skills (e.g. Sergey, the screen-recording app maker) who carries the less experienced founders. He says: "I'm just trying to move the boat forward."
- **Community layer:** he opened a Slack with a channel per launch where founders boost and support each other, and it now has messages daily.

## Interviewing and content format

- He does no prep and no editing, starting because he lacked time. Listeners told him they prefer it because it feels like being with the person.
- He finds hard cuts in other podcasts annoying. His approach is a free-flowing long conversation, with 30-second shorts cut separately for discovery.
- He argues "there's value in not trying to focus on value": listeners are at the gym or driving, so it need not be dense.
- He advised the host to publish unedited, since editing (the host spends about 12 hours per episode) is the reason he quit his own earlier podcast.
