## Selling a Twitter course in a crowded market: positioning, emotion and pre-built promises

Dagobert is a software engineer who left a $100K job to bootstrap a startup with his wife, a designer. The startup is Logology, a website that generates logos automatically. They spent years and almost all their savings on it. The product wasn't the main problem. Marketing was, and neither of them is a marketer. He still sees himself as a startup founder first. The course is a side project that could bring in cash while they work on the startup.

## Why sharing his journey beat promoting the product

- He first spammed his product link on Twitter, replying to people who needed a logo. It brought a couple of sales and little else.
- He then switched to sharing his founder journey, which is relatable to his customers (other startup founders). He didn't talk about logos, because his wife is the logo expert, not him.
- His reasoning is about volume. A product tweet might get 10 likes and 30 visitors. A story tweet gets 10 to 100 times the engagement and followers. Even if only 1% check out the product out of curiosity, that is far more people.
- His first viral tweet was a screenshot of a live chat in which a visitor said "I just stole one of your logos". Startup founders rallied behind him, he gained many followers, and it led to a logo sale.
- His conclusion: "most important thing is the emotion", because emotion is what gets engagement and action.
- With 50K+ followers, he still mentions the product only once or twice a month, for big milestones or features.
- He says the memes are low-touch and sell less directly, but laughter builds instant trust and goodwill. They make a large-scale audience feel like a personal connection.
- He advises choosing the format that is natural to you (jokes, visuals, expert threads). Authenticity is rare on social media, so genuine content gets attention.

## How he learned Twitter

- At that point he made about $300 a month and had a Twitter following of about 150, mostly inactive school friends.
- He spent 8 to 10 hours a day on Twitter for about six months, including weekends.
- He ran many experiments on posting frequency, engagement and content. He deliberately tweeted good tweets at bad times to test his timing theory.
- After about two months he started to see the pattern. After about four months he had a repeatable daily process.

## Positioning the course against a saturated market

- He avoided courses at first. He distrusts most course sellers, assuming they know only that courses make money.
- He changed his mind after helping people in DMs who asked how his small account took off. That showed him the value was real.
- Competitors included big names (he mentions Daniel Rosalo and Avid), with some courses reportedly making around $200K in a year. People in communities told him a course wasn't worth doing.
- His edge was a mix of strategic growth and authentic connection. Most courses offer only one: "be authentic" without growth, or hacky growth tactics without connection.
- Few competitors had a strong marketing position. Some had only a Gumroad page, so there was room to stand out.
- He named it "Dominate Twitter". In a poll of three titles, "Winning the Twitter feed" got the most votes. But those who chose "Dominate" felt strongly about it, while nobody loved the winner. Others called it too aggressive.
- He went with the polarizing name. His rule: "if I don't stand out I'm dead", and a strong emotional reaction beats a mild approval.
- His goal for the name was to get people to open the landing page, even if they thought they would hate it. The page then does the convincing.
- His wife's branding (strong black and white, a bold font) supports the aggressive tone, balanced with humor.

## The emotional sales page

- He started designing the landing page before recording the course. He wrote about four versions first.
- He monitored Twitter complaints. The recurring pains were not understanding the algorithm, invisible tweets, and watching worse content go viral.
- The page follows an emotional arc:
  1. Push on the pain: you fail, you have zero engagement, people make fun of you (including a meme of someone tweeting daily with no result).
  2. Relief: it's not your fault, it's the algorithm.
  3. The way out: understand the algorithm and the pain goes away, via the course.
- His credibility comes from the algorithm. People in DMs told him he knows it better than anyone, so he built the course around it.
- An earlier headline was "the algorithm is killing your growth". He later changed it to a benefit-focused title about growing your startup with Twitter. He wants to A/B test the pain version again, since it opens with pain and emotion.

## Build the promise first, then the product

- He did not pre-sell. DMs and competitors' sales were enough validation, and he was confident in the page.
- He designed the course to fulfill the landing page. He asked what chapters would make the page's promise true. For example, he added a tweet-writing section he hadn't planned, after hearing it was a pain point.
- Results: $8,073.30 in the first 24 hours, 700+ buyers and five-star reviews. Because it delivers on the promise, he now gets word-of-mouth sales as well.
- He contrasts this with the usual order of building the course (camera, lighting, months of recording) and then looking for reasons to sell it. He calls his approach about 10 times more effective.
- He is applying it to Logology: rebuilding from scratch by asking Twitter about branding and design pain, writing a landing page as strong as the course's, and then building the product to match.

## Market research without a big audience

- Start with an industry or topic you care about (for him, design for startups), not a fixed product idea. This keeps you authentic and open to better ideas. He notes the real pain in his niche is how to design everything for a startup, not logos specifically.
- With few followers, monitor Twitter and Reddit communities for recurring problems. Twitter polls get extra visibility even on small accounts, though closed questions are less useful.
- With a large audience, he asks his audience and customers directly.
- Ask about pain, not ideas: "don't ask people what's your idea, ask people what's your pain". Ideas are casual and cheap. Real pain gets honest answers.
- About 20 in-depth conversations with people in the target market are enough.
- To validate, put up a landing page with a buy button and an early-access discount. Sales prove the pain is strong enough. His summary: "if there's pain there's money".
