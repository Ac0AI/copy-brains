## Dagobert's burnout: how it happened and what he changed

- Dagobert's burnout was driven by perfectionism, ego, and a fear of failing, more than by the work itself.
- He spent 6 months building useless features because he wanted the product to be perfect and was scared of failing. He says a therapist could have saved him those six months.
- His last startup failed partly because he tried to do everything perfectly and spent months on things that could have taken weeks or days.
- He admits he wanted to be CEO and kept control of everything, telling himself it made life easier for his then co-founder and wife. In reality he was "so scared of losing control", and everything sat on his shoulders.
- When he burned out and collapsed, everything collapsed with him, because he had put all the responsibility on himself. This was a big part of the end of the relationship and the startup.
- His body kept the damage. He says he spends hours a day consciously blinking so his eyes don't hurt, from years of staring at screens too long, too focused.
- He used to think burnout was all mindset and that he could push through. He now says you can push until something physically breaks.

## Therapy as a founder tool

- Dagobert has been in therapy for about six months. He says everyone told him to go and he first thought he didn't need it, because he already journaled and meditated.
- His point: knowing something is wrong is different from having a trusted professional tell you "this is the mistake you're making". It "changes everything".
- He compares it to the gym: "it's like mental health", so there's no reason not to do it.
- His therapist is Argentinian and based in northern France. A session lasts 1.5 hours:
  - About 40 minutes of normal talk therapy.
  - Then she asks where he feels the issue in his body (usually chest or neck) and works on it for 30-40 minutes with a kind of massage meant to heal and release what's blocked.
  - Then a 10-minute debrief.
  - It is so intense he can only go about twice a month.
- He says the body work is the "magic part": for two weeks afterwards his posture and body change without effort.
- A therapist helps founders because most mistakes are not knowledge problems. "We know what we need to do but we don't really want to do it", because of ego or pride. Someone outside the indie-hacker bubble can show you that.
- On the second session, his therapist showed him what he had said and pointed toward leaving his wife. He says the answer was "right there" and felt too scary to say out loud.
- Social media is an echo chamber where everyone repeats the same things to support each other. A therapist outside it makes things feel less big.

## Rules for his current work rhythm

- Dagobert goes to a co-working space and gets a rhythm and friends from it. He takes the long French lunch break with them, even when he feels like he should keep working.
- He walks 20 minutes each way, puts on AirPods and sings in the street.
- That walk helped him drop a stressful problem: Twitter wouldn't let him upload an episode before a recording. He decided "I'll figure it out tomorrow" and arrived calm and present instead of rushing and multitasking.
- Lesson: "we need more quiet and more calm in our lives." Stress leads to wrong priorities, rushed work and mistakes.
- If he started another startup with a co-founder, he would do couples-style therapy with them at least monthly. He sees co-founders as a relationship like a marriage.

## Saying no instead of yes to everything

- Dagobert says that at his peak he replied to every Twitter reply and DM and said no to nobody, because he feared missing out.
- Now he ignores free offers (free videos, free content, cold emails). His reason: "it's not free. I have to spend time getting into it", and it diverts focus from his own agenda.
- He found he lost much less engagement than he feared. He credits a strong community who care about him.
- He thinks the fear came from childhood-style fear of being abandoned: if you stop being active, people forget you.

## Money versus following his heart

- Dagobert had just left his job with only a little money saved. He had also just separated from his wife, so he had little support system.
- He was offered paid work. Daniel Vassallo offered him $4K per month to be an ambassador for Small Bets. Others offered a lot of money for a few memes.
- He says saying yes to money feels like the safe way, since "I can't fail". But if it's not what your heart wants, you "end up not in the right life", then burn out and become unhappy.
- Since the burnout he is "not willing to compromise" on that anymore. He is trying to balance where his heart wants to go with financial stability.

## Fixing perfectionism in his podcast and getting joy back

- Dagobert paused This Indie Life for a month after the Nico interview. The reason: editing the Moo episode took 3 days, and the pain of that made him procrastinate. He had recorded 5 more interviews meanwhile.
- After posting about it on Twitter, people told him he didn't need a perfect edit. He now does a clean edit and publishes, which takes about 4 hours instead of 3 days, so he can do one episode per week.
- His view: the value of the podcast is the content and the original, unfiltered conversations with founders, not production polish.
- He tells his sponsor he'd rather not take the money some weeks: "this week I'm not feeling it", because he wants to keep the joy.
- He wants to code again just for fun, with no goal of making money. He had started coding at about 15, and it was fun until it became a freelance job. He is a Ruby on Rails fan.
- His idea: build something useless, like a movie-ranking app, only for himself. He notes the trap of turning every hobby into a boilerplate, template or product.
- Rule of thumb: when a hobby becomes a way to make money, it gets hard to have a hobby again.

## Distribution and platform choice

- Dagobert's lesson from burning out on Twitter: he went all in on one platform and depended on it for traffic and sales. When Elon took over, things changed and it was very stressful.
- This time he diversifies, but he says what matters is not the type of content but who uses the platform: "who's listening and where are they hanging out."
- His audience is founders, small entrepreneurs, startup founders and indie hackers. They are not really on Instagram or TikTok.
- His best Twitter memes got hundreds of thousands of views but only about two likes on Instagram.
- Threads surprised him: with only about 100 followers there, one meme got about 60 likes, and he enjoys it.
- He has about 85K followers on Twitter.
- Tip: if you fail on one platform, maybe your people are simply not there. Go where your audience is.

## Authenticity on Twitter

- Dagobert says posts where he shares his struggles do very well because they are engaging and real.
- He warns that authenticity can become clickbait too: "I'm starting to figure out how to make people cry."
- He wants to stay mindful of that trap, and thinks the newer algorithm rewards human, emotional or funny content over only showing off revenue.
- On comparison: he says that opening Twitter and seeing other founders succeed while you fail makes you feel bad, and that LinkedIn and TikTok are even worse.
