## Dagobert's journey: from burnout to divorce to rebuilding

Dagobert opens the episode by explaining the pivot of This Indie Life toward "the dark side of entrepreneurship". His most successful episode in year one was the one where he described burning out with his startup, which then led to his divorce.

- He says constant success content ("I just made $10,000 this month") makes him "start to feel like a failure real fast".
- His goal is a podcast where founders are honest that "nobody's above everyone else": everyone is struggling, in doubt, and having ups and downs.
- He had been with the same partner 12 years (from 21 to 34) and is now divorcing her. He describes the aftermath as "brutal", lonely and scary, with rejection "in your face all the time", but also as a period of fast personal growth.

## Why his past logo startup lost to an AI-generated competitor

Dagobert's previous startup sold logos. He contrasts it with a competitor that used AI image generators, produced dozens of logos instantly, charged a low price and promised no particular result.

- The competitor made about $85K in 3 months (roughly $20K from the app plus a ~$65K exit), more than Dagobert's startup made in 5 years.
- His own mistake: "we went after a market that doesn't have much money" and used the highest quality, with every logo designed by hand.
- Few buyers in that market appreciated the quality, so it was "kind of useless".
- He says the competitor spent about 20 times less and made about the same money. The lesson he draws is that people wanted the cheap, instant result at that price point in that market.

## Emotional attachment, family-restaurant thinking, and planning an exit

Dagobert describes how he built his company with his wife and treated it as "our baby".

- They thought of it as "a family restaurant" and said they would never sell.
- That attachment led them to try to save something that was sinking. He calls it "too much emotion and attachment".
- His current view: if you want to stay indie, you need to plan an exit. If a product takes off (he uses $100K/month as an example), it attracts competitors and big players, and a solo founder who wants to avoid hiring ten people cannot fight them. "You either die or you exit."
- He sees exceptions like Peter Levels as very uncommon.
- Commoditization risk varies by product. Community and reputation products are harder to copy. He notes that a Twitter course built on being the reference in the indie maker world can't be copied.
- He cites Kevin O'Leary's idea that entrepreneurs who do it for money give up quickly, while those who do it for freedom keep fighting. He frames what founders want as freedom, not money.
- He also points out that a developer could reach roughly $200K+ at big US companies within about 5 years if money were the only goal.

## Working too hard: 70-hour weeks and the wrong product

Dagobert explains why he stopped lifting and training after his first two years as a founder. Early on he did 5x5 StrongLifts at home with weights and plates, but food, sleep and training time all add up.

- His realization after about a year and a half: he needed to give "150% to even stay in the chance", so he trained less and then stopped for about two years.
- His peak was roughly 72 hours of work in a week, which was when he was close to burnout.
- His key lesson: "if you need to work this hard for this long, maybe it's the wrong product."
- For sustainable indie income (he uses $5K/month as an example), he argues you don't need to overwork. Once you have years of experience and skills, you can find a different product or revenue source without destroying your health. When you're 18 you should work your ass off to get skills.
- He believes that with his current experience he could get a few sales quickly on a new SaaS, whereas beginners face the same large quantity of things to learn (copywriting, marketing, shipping) no matter what.

## Indie as "hard mode": no assists, no cushion

Dagobert compares indie life to the Doom nightmare difficulty.

- On hard difficulty you cannot stop moving, your brain has to change, and the game takes about 100 hours instead of 20.
- As an indie "we remove every assist, every helper". If you don't see sales on the Stripe dashboard, "then you suck. That's just how it is."
- This is why it feels like a roller coaster: when things go well it's all on you, so the highs and lows are extreme and hard to manage. One day you feel on top of the world and the next day, with no sales and no explanation, you feel like you suck.

## Handling the highs and lows: illusions, cockiness and stoicism

Dagobert says the same pattern shows up in both sales and dating. You imagine the win will take you to "this ultimate place of freedom and happiness", then realize hours later that life is the same.

- Danger of the high: you get cocky and "stop doing the things that brought you here in the first place". He says this applies whether the high comes from a woman or from your company.
- His antidote is to accept the pain without collapsing and to not become cocky when you succeed. He calls it "kind of like stoicism".
- On bad days he says it happens to everyone, "even to the best". Not feeling like crap sometimes may mean you aren't challenging yourself.
- He uses the line "some days it rains, some days it's sunny".
