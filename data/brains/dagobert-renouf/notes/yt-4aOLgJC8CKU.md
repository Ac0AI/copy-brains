## Founding with a partner: Dagobert and Lucy on logology

- Dagobert and his wife Lucy built logology together. Lucy is the designer, he is the developer and business/marketing side. Roles formed organically, so they rarely need to work on the same task.
- They kept money separate for about 10 years, then merged it when they started the startup, after roughly 8 years together.
- He says the real test for a co-founder is: "pick someone you could have a baby with." If you couldn't resolve parenting disagreements with them, a startup will surface the same conflicts, because skin in the game makes it "crisis mode for a long time."
- A previous co-founder (about one year) didn't work out. That person pushed for funding and YC; when Dagobert and Lucy prepared for it, they realized it wasn't who they wanted to be.
- Commitment mismatch was their early crisis. In year one Lucy worked about two days a week and he worked about three hours a day, expecting a "three hours a day and make millions" outcome. When he saw it failing he pushed harder, while Lucy kept other commitments (teaching design, radio show, writing novels). They had to explicitly decide to both go full time.
- Work-style differences: Lucy uses long task lists and meets deadlines; he focuses on habits and treats goals as "a carrot in front of a donkey," often missing deadlines. He lost interest before merging her finished work into production. His fix: ship what you did this week, and don't move on until it's shipped.

## Why they kept going when it wasn't working

- They started logology after seeing a logo generator making money and assuming "we can do better because we have better logos." He calls this looking at one dimension of a successful startup and thinking you can beat it there. They were weak at marketing, validation and process.
- After about two years they had spent a third of their savings and made around $70 a month. He chose to go all in, spending down to the last roughly 10K, keeping enough runway to find a job.
- His motivation was the alternative: he didn't want to return to client work or a job, so "it's still better than the alternative."
- For a few months they had "literally no idea" how to succeed and kept trying anyway.
- Hard events stacked up: his mom died two months after they decided to build logology, the co-founder split got ugly, and money stress followed. He lost motivation after about two years, for the first time in his life, and developed insomnia.

## Perfectionism: where it hurt and where it paid off

- Wasted perfectionism: roughly six months spent reinventing the tech stack and "the wheel for everything." He says the tech stack doesn't matter.
- Useful perfectionism: Lucy refused to compromise on logo quality. Feedback consistently included people saying it beat everything else, because a designer crafted each logo rather than a random database.
- A strong landing page also gave people a reason to check the product out. He doesn't claim it's the optimal way to get to market.

## How logology works and where it's headed

- Lucy has designed more than 800 logos by hand; users get logos generated from her pre-made designs. Dagobert estimates 2,000 as the minimum for a good answer for anyone and 5,000 as ideal. Early focus was startups/tech; food and hair salons need more designs.
- They aim for timeless designs that last "100 years," not trends. Lucy dreams of publishing a book of her designs.
- Logology reached about $3K per month using Twitter and other marketing, with SEO work in progress.
- Insight from the course launch: people don't care about logos as much as the whole brand. The direction is to offer logos plus illustrations and a full vibe for buttons, navigation and landing pages ("Logology 2").

## Twitter as acquisition: authentic but strategic

- He doesn't tweet about design because Lucy is the expert. Since his target market is startup founders, talking about his own startup journey makes them curious enough to check out the product. Some story tweets sent around a thousand people to his profile.
- This suits him because he dislikes feeling salesy ("I'm always shaking when I promote").
- Content process:
  - Step zero: engage with other people first; replies produce the best ideas.
  - Step one: turn the best replies into tweets. He built a make.com automation that pulls his most-liked replies from the past week and turns them into tweet drafts.
  - Step two: turn tweets into memes. He has made over 400 meme templates and posts about one per day.
  - Batch day: one or two days a month he spends 2-4 hours screenshotting about 100 meme templates from Reddit, 9GAG and other sites, then makes about 30 memes for the month.
- He says he mixes "authenticity with strategy." "Just posting whatever I want" is often a cop-out that avoids work that could fail.
- Being helpful is both real and strategic: it builds a brand as "someone nice who isn't trying to sell." His rule: "selfless in the morning and selfish in the evening" (engage with others' content in the morning, promote his own at night).
- He frames authenticity as a mix of selfless and selfish. You need to take care of yourself first to help others, and supporting other course creators (like Daniel Vassallo) also helps your own position.

## Twitter and mental health

- Engaging on Twitter every morning pulls him out of his own head, since he's thinking about others' stories, and he reliably feels better afterward. It replaced the isolation of working alone.
- Seeing successful people can inspire him, or crush him in the wrong dose. He advises focusing on your own progress and not comparing, since you don't know others' full stories.

## Lesson: start with an audience and their problems, not a product

- Logology was built the "genius idea first" way: build, then ask who to sell it to, then build an audience. His Twitter launch course was the opposite and worked far better.
- Course process:
  1. Build an audience on Twitter.
  2. Send tips by DM and learn what people need.
  3. Ask more precise questions about what they want to know.
  4. Write and iterate the landing page copy before building anything.
  5. Build the course, share early access, collect feedback, improve, release.
- Course revenue was easier partly because courses sell more easily, but he says part of the gap came from the process.
- Plan for Logology 2: start from his audience's problems, design the landing page and copy first, and only build once it's appealing.
- His summary: "Stop thinking of products, start thinking of audiences, people you care about, people you want to help," then build credibility and build what they want.
