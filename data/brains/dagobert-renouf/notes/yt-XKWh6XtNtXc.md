## Why Logology started: accessible branding for early-stage founders

Dagobert co-founded Logology with his wife Lucie Baratte, a freelance logo designer of 10+ years, and he builds the product as the developer. The idea came on their honeymoon.

- Lucie kept hearing from founders who loved her style but could not pay about $3,000 for a custom logo ("I only have a hundred").
- The mission is to make professional brand design accessible to entrepreneurs at the earliest stage.
- Logology is not a logo generator. It adds brand advice and a design-thinking approach, so a customer gets a meaningful identity rather than a random icon. The site's meta description is "get your brand identity within five minutes".
- Every logo in the catalogue is designed by Lucie, who chose each color and font combination in advance. Logos are not randomly generated.

## Turning a service into a product (automation with a personal touch)

- The first six months went nowhere because Dagobert and Lucie tried to design the product "in our heads". The fix was to copy exactly what Lucie does with her freelance clients and automate it.
- Lucie's method is design thinking applied to visual identity, run as interactive workshops that define the logo.
- Lucie wanted about 1,000 keywords so users could describe themselves precisely. Dagobert judged that too service-heavy to automate. They settled on roughly 10 brand personality types, mixed with the customer's industry.
- Lucie spent about a month writing the post-purchase branding-advice email sequence. It is tailored to the buyer's personality type, so the automation feels personal.
- Dagobert treats a $49 logo buyer like a former freelance client. They write handwritten cards and email every customer, and he is often on live chat.
- Both founders spent years in service work. They now want to build a product, which they find more fun and more valuable.
- A guest customer said the whole flow (questionnaire, color and font suggestions, follow-up emails, guidelines) is why he would pick Logology again over generators and freelance designers.

## From spammy promotion to "participating": how Dagobert approached marketing

Dagobert held a bias against marketing for the first two and a half years, and Logology sold few logos. His reasoning is that marketers are often "bullshitters", so people picture something shady and salesy.

- He started by posting the product in every online community.
- On Twitter he searched for indie hackers and "build in public" people answering "what are you building today?". He replied "I'm working on Logology today" about ten times a day for weeks. He calls this spammy and low quality.
- The shift came from participating sincerely: listening, engaging and bringing value. People became curious about Logology on their own.
- His view: "Real marketing is about connecting with people, listening to people", and promoting the product only when it is useful.
- He says reframing marketing as participating also made it more fun and easier. He now calls himself more of a participant than a marketer.
- He considers early spamming a normal first step for most founders, because marketing is experimentation.

## Organic beats paid at this stage

- In early 2021 Dagobert spent about $600 on Google Ads and got zero sales. Hundreds of people clicked, but nothing converted. Budget was limited, so they stopped.
- He also sponsored newsletters aimed at startup founders and indie hackers. That went nowhere either.
- Twitter then started working. Today almost all of Logology's sales trace back to Twitter, directly or indirectly.
- His reasoning: people have defenses against ads because they know you paid. When they see something they like from a person on Twitter, they trust it more.
- Retargeting ads to remind people who started a logo are a possible future test. He thinks getting paid ads right takes at least a few thousand dollars, and they do not want that risk yet.
- Lucie is now writing blog posts on design and branding to build a resource that can bring Google traffic, which is still organic.
- Dagobert acknowledges the tradeoff: when you have no money you invest time, and once you have money you can save time with it.

## Advice to first-time founders: pick work you enjoy and keep going

- Dagobert says most founders expect to make millions because they have big ideas. When that does not happen they are disappointed, and that is where the real work starts.
- Positive motivation ("change the world") stops helping when you have no customers. In the hopeless stretch around 2021 he used negative motivation instead: "I don't want to go back to a job." He preferred to keep trying things until something worked than to return to work he wanted to avoid.
- His main advice is to build something you enjoy doing even if it fails. Lucie will always be happy designing logos, and he is happy building product and talking to founders on Twitter.
- If you only chase a "good idea", it starts to look like a bad idea the moment it gets hard. Enjoyment is what keeps you going until you "climb back up".
- Both founders stress mental health in the hard stretches. Lucie says a bad mental state "will shadow your vision". Dagobert suggests meditation, yoga, a therapist or a coach.
- Lucie's advice on marketing: stay genuine, talk to customers, and listen to their problems. Do not just do whatever they ask.

## Where Logology goes next

- Lucie and Dagobert plan to rebalance between marketing and product after a period where Dagobert spent most of his time on marketing.
- The product will expand beyond logos to fuller brand identity: guidelines, more colors, more fonts and more files.
- They plan to introduce parity pricing across products.
- Lucie is writing blog articles on branding and on how customers can manage their brand and tell their story.
- Context: after about three years of work, Logology only became sustainable in the last six months before this interview (October 2022).
- Possible future direction: working with other designers and paying them fairly. Dagobert and Lucie say Logology is the only online logo solution built to pay designers fairly.
