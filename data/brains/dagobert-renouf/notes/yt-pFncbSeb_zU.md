## Twitter as the only channel: authenticity over networking

Dagobert Renouf co-founded Logology (logology.co), a logo generator for startups, built with his wife, who is an expert logo designer. For about 13 months it averaged one user per week. He turned it around by sharing his founder story on Twitter. His follower count went from under 100 to thousands within weeks, and he says revenue quadrupled.

- He describes himself as introverted and bad at traditional networking. On Twitter he doesn't try to network; he shares what he is going through.
- He argues authentic, vulnerable posts connect on a deeper level, even when some days get little engagement.
- Other founders identify with the struggles, so they come to care about him personally, not only the product.
- He contrasts this with "resource thread" accounts that grow to 100k followers but never connect on a human, personal, emotional level.

## Community turns into referrals, and many referrers never bought

- Around August or September 2021, he saw someone ask for a logo designer and get about 30 replies. Nine of them recommended Logology, from people he didn't know and who weren't customers.
- He says nine out of ten people who recommend Logology never bought anything. They like him and what he is doing, so they recommend him.
- His view: the more vulnerable and authentic he is, the stronger the community gets. It works like friends supporting each other, so he also helps and supports other founders on Twitter.
- Kevin C.W. Hellon's tweet is the example: he thought of logology.co when someone asked for a logo because of Dagobert's consistent meme posting and presence.

## Content process: reply-first ideas, scheduling, and mixing tone

- He builds a bank of ideas by engaging with others daily. Most tweets now start as a reply that got likes, which he calls "an MVP for my tweets", then repurposes as a standalone tweet because it is likely to do at least moderately well.
- He keeps his next 30 days of tweets written and scheduled.
- After a stretch of "brutal truth" memes about getting zero users and zero money, followers said it was too depressing. He now mixes the week: some painful or funny, some happy, some celebratory, some about how hard it is. He doesn't want to run all depressing content Monday to Friday.
- He is both very transparent in what he shares and strategic about how he uses Twitter.

## Memes and tools

- Image memes: Imgflip (img-flip.com). He started with its templates and added his own captions. He likes Spongebob memes because they work well, not because he is a fan.
- Video memes: Pinata Farms, a kids' app with caption tech that tracks the character in a video. It is US-only. He switched his App Store region to California with a fake address (after cancelling his subscriptions) and downloaded it. When an update forced a redo, he contacted the team and they gave him the private TestFlight beta build.
- Scheduling: Hypefury, chosen because it was the first he heard of. He still pays for it. Many scheduling tools offered him free accounts, but that would add "scheduled by" attribution under his tweets, which he didn't want. He likes that it is simple: a schedule of slots you fill ahead of time, plus best-time calculation. He also likes evergreen re-posting of popular tweets.

## Twitter algorithm: use the exact topic keyword

- He says the Twitter algorithm doesn't understand synonyms, only topic names. To trend in marketing you must literally use the word "marketing"; if you discuss it without the word, it doesn't work.
- Where to find topics: the "trending in..." topics in the home feed, or Twitter settings under content preferences, which lists every topic and subtopic (for example business, with startups, funding, entrepreneur, small business beneath).
- Use at least one topic keyword in every tweet you want to reach beyond your followers.
- Two ways past your circle: retweets (rare and a big investment) or a keyword plus a fast initial burst of likes. He recalls roughly 5 likes in about 10 minutes being enough, but isn't sure anymore.
- It worked at 300 followers. He says many people with great tweets get zero likes because they lack the early boost and the keyword.
- He now gets at least one tweet a week with more than 2,000 likes, and says that wouldn't happen without keywords.

## Paid channels failed; going all-in on Twitter

- Google Ads: he spent $600 and made $0.
- Facebook ads: skipped, believing a meaningful test needs at least a couple of thousand dollars.
- Newsletter sponsorships: about $200 across roughly four indie-hacker newsletters, hard to track, no visible effect.
- His takeaway: as a builder who disliked marketing, he assumed he could just put money into ads. He says you can't pay money and make money without a lot of budget for testing; you have to hustle.
- Affiliates: he tried and failed before Twitter.
- He then spent about 8 hours a day on Twitter for six months, with no product changes for 10 months. He has since found processes that need less time and is building features again.

## What Twitter brings besides sales

- SEO backlinks: he gets about one new backlink per day with no dedicated effort, from stories, memes, interviews and "accounts to follow" lists. His wife is starting SEO content this year, and he did the keyword research. He expects the two together to compound.
- Partnerships: Chad (CSAcorn), founder of Better Legal, which helps people form US LLCs, DMed him after Dagobert shared a struggle. Every Better Legal signup now gets an email recommending Logology for logos, and Dagobert pays him a commission.
- Opportunities like podcast interviews, and credibility.
- Community and mindset: finding hundreds of close peers and thousands of like-minded founders made him feel less alone and less negative, and showed him every founder makes mistakes.
- Logology costs $100, versus about $2,000 for a custom logo.
