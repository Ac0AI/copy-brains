## Why Dagobert shut down Logology after five years

- **Outcome:** Dagobert and his wife Lucy closed the logo design startup Logology after five years and almost all their savings. It had reached 3K per month in revenue, plus 5K per month from his Twitter course "Dominate Twitter".
- **Real problem:** the cost of living and running a business in France made that revenue "barely enough to survive". Profitability reached a few months earlier had already slipped back below survival level.
- **Recurring pressure:** the logo product had no recurring revenue, so they needed to find 100+ new customers every month. They considered a recurring model plus SEO, but AI uncertainty (both for acquiring customers and for designing logos) made them doubt it would be enough.
- **Why not raise money:** hiring a couple of people was another option, but after five years of fighting they were exhausted, out of motivation and out of money.

## Burnout and what overwork cost

- In February 2023 Dagobert suffered a massive burnout. His heart rate went wild every time he stood up, and he spent weeks in bed, thinking it was long covid.
- Years of screen time without rest damaged his eyes. He gets dazzled by bright light (screens, traffic lights, sunny days) and will need glasses permanently.
- He worked around 80 hours a week for five years, with no time for hobbies, fun or romance. Even on rare vacations he kept thinking about getting the next customer.
- He says they "sacrificed everything we cared about just to reach success at any cost", and the dream of living on their own terms got lost along the way.

## Lesson: a good business matters more than a good idea or hard work

- He admits they were idealistic and believed "having a good idea and working hard was enough to succeed".
- His conclusion: "all that matters is: is it a good business?" That means:
  - Is there demand?
  - Are customers paying enough?
  - Can you acquire customers at a good enough cost?
- He calls this "the boring stuff" and says founders avoid it in favor of the fantasy of building a cool product (his example: "that would be so cool if there was a new AI copywriting tool").
- Only after finding a good niche or market should you ask whether your idea is good and how hard to work. Look at competition and risks first.
- Logology competed in a crowded logo niche and struggled to make ends meet from launch day. They also made bad strategic decisions that forced constant overwork.
- His summary: "we're constantly swimming against the fact that it's not a good business to be in." He insists he can't be blamed for lack of effort: "I really tried my best."
- Even a successful solo business can go down in three months, so starting from a weak business makes the vulnerability worse. He says a founder's work is "never over".

## Denial and the Twitter feedback loop

- He says he and Lucy were in denial for at least two years. Some denial can help (he sees value in faking it till you make it for about six months), but theirs went on far too long.
- Twitter made it worse. Good feedback, interactions and networking made it feel like they were progressing, and his Twitter success let other people back up the belief that they were doing well.
- The burnout was the wake-up call that ended the denial.
- He now wants to tweet about mistakes instead of successes, because Startup Twitter is almost entirely success stories that friends leave because they stop being useful. He had about 30 drafts of mistake tweets ready.
- He also described stopping tweeting for weeks because he felt like "a loser and a failure", and his fear that people would lose interest if he stopped sharing motivating tweets.

## The next step: product manager at a small startup

- Dagobert went back to a job, starting the following week on a trial period. He had avoided this for five years and said he never thought he'd do it.
- He used to be a developer but was bored by having little control, "just here to execute". Researching roles, he found product management, which he describes as the "CEO of the product":
  - speaking to customers
  - defining features and strategy
  - working with developers (easy because he is a dev) and designers (easy because he is a designer)
- He deliberately chose a small startup, where a product manager can oversee the whole product and focus on growing it, unlike big companies where PMs handle a single feature.
- Expected benefits: an end to the crazy hours and constant financial pressure, and work that can become enjoyable again. In France the standard week is around 35 hours, which he notes is less than what he worked on "vacation".
- He admits fears: losing the no-alarm-clock lifestyle and being stuck five days a week working for others. He says he knows to "curb my enthusiasm" because he has bootstrapped startups.

## Plans for the future

- He expects to come back to startups someday but has no motivation now and needs time to recover and live a simpler life.
- Next time he wants to build with at least one co-founder who is "completely different" from him and Lucy. Logology felt solo even as a couple because they lacked business skills.
- He wants to stay in France and network locally for co-founders, noting he has many Twitter followers but almost no networking contacts in France.
- Lucy keeps running the site and may return to custom logo design.

## Takeaways on community and money shocks

- When he hit rock bottom and stopped tweeting, follower count "wasn't worth much". The friends he made on Twitter, dozens of whom supported him over DMs, gave insights and perspective and "made it 10 times easier to find a way out".
- On financial volatility, he says that as an indie founder an unexpected cost hits you directly (e.g. losing 5K to broken equipment), unlike an employee whose boss pays. His view: "it's never over, you always have to hustle... you can rest for like three months tops."
