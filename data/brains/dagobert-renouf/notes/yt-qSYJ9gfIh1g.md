## Why Dagobert left a dream job to build Logology

- At 15 he taught himself web development. He freelanced as a designer and engineer for almost 10 years.
- At 28 he got his "dream job" as a senior front-end engineer: remote, US salary, good team. After a couple of months he felt it was "the end of everything", with no more challenge.
- He concluded that what he loves is "the challenge" and "the adventure". Starting a company was the only thing left that excited him, even though he never wanted the stress and risk.
- Three years in, he says he still has trouble sleeping and is uncomfortable. But he hasn't been bored once, and he has "grown way more than in the eight years before".
- He argues many people stay stuck in jobs they dislike because they fear doing something else.

## How Logology came about

- His first startup idea was "WordPress for mobile apps". He couldn't find anyone interested and lost energy after a couple of months.
- While on his honeymoon he saw on Indie Hackers a founder making good money with an automated logo service. He and his wife (a logo designer) had had the same idea about four years earlier. Back then they never assumed people would pay.
- She was also bored of 10 years of freelancing. They decided to build it, with "no plan", just the skills: she does logos, he does websites.
- He saw existing logo generators as doing a poor job. They make the user pick the symbol, font and color, so no designer is really involved.

## Product design: automating a $3,000 designer workflow

- His wife charges about $3,000 for a custom brand. She had to turn away many prospects who only had about $200.
- Her real process has three steps: a workshop on values, vision and customers; five design proposals; then the client picks one and she finalizes it. Logology automates each step.
  - **Workshop becomes a quiz.** It is a five-minute "personality quiz" for your brand, with questions like "if your company was a superhero, which one would it be?". It does not ask "blue or red?". It assigns one of 10 pre-designed brand personalities.
  - **Proposals become pre-designed logos.** His wife designed about 700 logos by hand over three years. The tool matches roughly 30 of them to the user's personality and industry.
  - **Finalizing becomes curated choices.** Colors and fonts are hand-picked by her.
- Because every path was vetted by the designer in advance, "you can't go out of Logology with a shitty looking logo".
- One repeat customer bought five or more logos in a year. He now takes the quiz and picks a logo in about 20 minutes.
- A key customer testimonial says it is quick, affordable and high quality, the three things you're "not supposed to have together".

## Positioning, niche and pricing

- He deliberately targets startups and indie founders, mostly SaaS and online products. Making the logos takes so long that they had to pick one vertical. Startups are likely to try something new and are easy for him to reach because they are like him.
- He plans to expand later to other businesses (hair salons, car mechanics).
- Main logo package: $100. Average revenue is about $70 per sale. He had about 200 sales at the time.
- Planned roadmap, each step building on the last:
  1. Logo only (today).
  2. A full brand guide (fonts, colors, spacing, dark mode, how to use the logo), planned at about $100. Early adopters already asked for it, and one paid for early access.
  3. Generated assets: social posts, landing page design, pitch deck templates. About $200 for everything, or possibly a subscription.
- Together, steps 1 and 2 would be about $200 per customer, so he could double revenue per customer.
- He sees "branding" as seen as something only for big companies. His hope is to make a real brand identity available to anyone with $50-100, and perhaps become a "branding API" that tools like Canva could plug into. He says the market is "still one step ahead".

## From 20 months of slow launch to growth on Twitter

- It took 20 months to launch. Then more than a year to reach 100 sales, about five sales a month. He says they "didn't do good marketing" and had no idea how.
- What kept them going: early customers said Logology saved them, and he knew from his local entrepreneur circle that many others would care. He accepts that most people don't care about branding, and argues a small, well-defined audience that does is enough.
- Failed channels: Google Ads, sponsored newsletters, Indie Hackers posts (a few sales).
- Turning point (around May 2022): he started replying to "what are you working on?" tweets with Logology, about 20 times a day. One reply went viral, sending about 1,000 visitors in a day and a couple of sales.
- He dropped Reddit and Indie Hackers and focused on Twitter. He spent about 10 hours a day on it, reading guides, tweeting daily and sharing his founder story.
- Tweeting only to grow felt inauthentic and disconnected. So he also posted memes, since he is "the guy at parties who makes jokes". The memes took months to gain traction, but they now bring more people to his profile and website.
- Result: sales went from about one per week to about six or seven per week from Twitter alone. Monthly revenue was about $2,000-2,500. Followers were about 9,000, and he expected about 40k in 12 months at that rate.
- He now gets tagged whenever someone asks for a logo.

## Focus, feedback and priorities

- **Listen only to paying customers.** People who didn't buy kept suggesting things like showing all logos in a catalog. Buyers want the opposite: "even less choice", more guidance. Following non-buyers would turn the product into a "stupid catalog" and lose the people who pay.
- **Marketing before features.** His priority is to survive and make a living, so he forces himself to keep focusing on marketing rather than building features. He builds new things only when he has spare room, such as a week with posts already written.
- **Stay focused.** He turns down unrelated ideas. They always seem easy and then bring unexpected problems, leaving "two weak products instead of one strong one".
- **A related side project:** "Memeology", a website collecting all his memes so people can reuse them. He sees it as another way to feed traffic to Logology.
- **Stop consuming, start building.** He used to read many books and built nothing. Now he says to stop reading and learn from experience, because real situations differ from what books describe.
  - His influences are Peter Thiel and the Basecamp founders (the "It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work" and "Rework" authors; he mentions a book on remote work), plus a few Twitter friends such as Tony Dinh.
- **Advice to his younger self:** set aside about two months, or even a few weeks, to build something you think is cool, and try to get one customer. Don't over-analyze market size. It teaches more than anything else and shows how different reality is from imagination.
