## Starting a startup after hitting a wall in a job

- Dagobert built websites from age 15 and worked as a developer, freelancer and consultant for years, often partnering with founders. Around age 28 he concluded the only remaining option for freedom and self-expression was building his own company, which he had avoided because it looked stressful and overwhelming.
- He quit his job as a software engineer for a US company (working remotely from France) with some savings and no firm idea.
- With his wife, who also wanted to stop freelancing, he spent a bit more than a year building Logology, then launched to nobody. They had assumed "building a good product would be enough" and neither was good at marketing.
- He argues that unless you build "the next Facebook," a product will not take off by itself; if you are not putting eyes on it, nobody cares.

## Finding Twitter after other channels failed

- After the launch, he and his wife spent months and years trying Google Ads, sponsoring newsletters and writing blog posts. None worked.
- Out of desperation he began replying to tweets and promoting his startup with almost no followers. After a couple of weeks, one reply produced a few sales and visits. At the time Logology struggled to make more than one or two sales per week, so two sales in a day was big.
- Since everything else had failed, he went all in on Twitter.

## How Twitter scaled, and what it brought beyond sales

- Unlike blogging, Twitter scaled roughly linearly for him: more followers meant proportionally more sales.
- Working Twitter basically full time for about three months took Logology from roughly 300-500 sales per month to about 2K, later stabilizing around 3K per month directly from Twitter.
- Side effects he values: podcast invitations, articles written about the company, and a PR effect he says he would not know how to do deliberately.
- SEO benefit: hundreds of websites now mention Logology because they found him on Twitter and liked his story.
- He calls Twitter "as much content marketing as it is networking" and says networking makes partnerships and problem-solving "10 times easier."

## Getting from 0 to 1,000 followers

- He says zero to 1,000 is the hardest stretch; after that, social proof and engagement create momentum. Under 100 followers is hardest of all.
- The common mistake: tweeting daily to nobody. With no audience to like and propagate your tweets, "you might be hiding the best tweets in the world" and still get nothing.
- His switch: early on, spend time connecting rather than tweeting. Reply to and add value on tweets from people in your niche.
- Why replies work: on Twitter, a reply is as valuable as a standalone tweet and can go viral the same way, unlike a comment on YouTube or Instagram. Replying to people who already have an audience gets your content seen.
- Target accounts with a few hundred to a few thousand followers, not millions.
- Routine: about 30 minutes a day finding people who inspire you in your niche and engaging with their posts. He says this is usually enough to reach 500-1,000 followers. As you grow, shift more time to your own tweets.
- Proof of his own approach: he started about a year and a half earlier with around 100 followers and reached about 60K.

## Why he avoids AI tweets and copied viral content

- He considers tools that resurface successful tweets from big accounts for you to lightly reword "pretty unethical," and says they fail for two reasons: copying content does not work without an audience, and it builds the wrong audience.
- He has seen accounts that started alongside him and grew faster (some over 200-300K) with viral lists, threads and recycled ideas. When those people later tweet about their own product or interests, they get almost no engagement because followers came for information, not for a human connection.
- His sales and opportunities come because his tweets are real. Some of his tweets still fail, because he keeps sharing his own thoughts and refining his thinking.
- On follower count: "between two times more and ten times more it doesn't change much, what matters is how many people care."
- ChatGPT can help match your voice, but the thoughts must be yours.

## Using Twitter as a founder: personal brand over company account

- He argues Twitter is about people. People would follow Steve Jobs over Apple, Elon Musk has far more followers than Tesla or SpaceX, and LeBron James has more than the Lakers.
- Best use for a CEO: connect directly and remove the distance marketing creates. A founder talking about building a product is more emotional than an ad or a brand account.
- He admits many CEOs want "easy marketing that scales" (set a budget, click a button, get a return) and says Twitter does not work that way; the payoff is trust and authority as a person in your niche, which then boosts anything you build.
- His Twitter view after Elon Musk's takeover: he launched his Twitter course the day Musk took over and was stressed, but found the changes smaller than announced. He cites bot fixes (he had been getting three NFT-scam bots tagging him daily until they fixed it) and says he avoids following Musk to protect his peace.

## Memes as a trust-building tool

- Memes connect emotionally: laughter creates trust. Humor alone makes people trust you but not take you seriously, so he balances memes with valuable content that shows he knows his field.
- He has made 400+ memes over more than a year, aiming for one per day, as a self-imposed challenge. Each takes about 30 minutes on average. It became what people know him for.
- Process: the image matters most. "If it's funny by itself you already won half." He initially used mediocre images with jokes and it did not land.
- Find images on meme sites such as 9GAG and Reddit until one is very funny, then adapt it to your niche.
- Content angles: (1) make fun of a real pain your audience shares (for him, startup founders), turning a tough situation into a joke; (2) deliver a simple truth in a light way, such as his repeated lesson that founders need to do more marketing, not just build a product.

## Persisting through three years without income

- He originally expected to make millions. After years of struggle, goals became realistic: just making a good living.
- After about two years they had spent roughly half their savings and nearly quit, then decided to commit fully rather than go back to the work they no longer wanted.
- His reason to continue: nowhere to go back to. "I'm not going back... this is the life I chose."
- He argues a deeper motivation than money is required, since money alone is quicker to earn with a job, savings and real estate.
- Advice on platforms: spreading across many platforms thins you out and makes real relationships harder. Pick one and stick with it; he stays on Twitter and rarely uses LinkedIn.
- At recording he said Logology was pivoting, with a goal of reaching 10K per month that year.
