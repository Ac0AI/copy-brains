## Starting out: building websites from age 15

- Dagobert started building websites at 15, initially wanting to make video games. He joined a small teenage team that needed a website, and the feeling of turning an idea into something people could use got him hooked.
- He learned PHP from a free French tutorial site. After about three months of struggling and copying tutorials, a loop finally clicked: "it's kind of riding a bike". He was still a noob afterwards but could now build anything.
- His first money came at 16 from a fan site for the French Idol TV show. He bought the domain people would search for and offered the show's videos as free downloads, with an ad placed directly next to the download links (zero pixel margin) so many visitors clicked it by mistake.
- Results: about $150 in one night (the final), around $1,500 over a few months.
- The next season he built separate sites for every candidate, with comments and ratings. The TV channel's hosting provider shut it down once the channel launched its own online video platform, and he took everything offline.

## Freelancing, jobs and the pull toward his own thing

- He left college after about a month, having already made about $2,000 from a single website.
- He freelanced for most of his professional life: web design, engineering, and mostly SEO on the marketing side. Early on he was paid very little, later thousands of dollars per site.
- Freelancing let him work directly with founders and find the ones he liked. Two examples:
  - A year in Moscow at 20 for a client building an e-commerce site.
  - A year helping a US friend build a social network startup.
- Bored of restarting with new clients, he chased money instead. He set a goal of a $100k/year US job. That also got boring.
- His realization: at 16 with the Idol site he already had what he wanted (own project, own terms, fun, money as a bonus). He had been chasing money instead of that.
- He says working for others always lacked something: "it's not your vision". Employment also spares you the anxiety of figuring it out yourself, which is the trade-off.
- He wanted to speak freely instead of being "a bit fake" for clients, and left jobs because managers blocked his attempts to build his own features.

## Logology: what it is and why it works

- Logology gives a startup a designer-quality logo in about five minutes. It was co-founded with his wife, a graphic designer who did logos for 15+ years. Her custom logos cost around 3,000 euros, and many startup founders loved her style but could not afford it.
- Flow:
  - A roughly 5-minute questionnaire mirrors the questions she would ask a client (vision, values, target audience, industry, "which movie/superhero is your brand").
  - It outputs a brand personality (about 10 types, such as visionary, kind, playful).
  - It matches the user to logos from the 750+ she has designed.
- Pricing history: they first planned unique, one-time logos but that would need about $500 per logo. Customers said they would just hire a designer at that price. They moved to about $100, with packages from $50 and a $15 entry price mentioned at the end.
- Logos are licensed from a catalog, so another customer could in theory get the same one (he says it happens very rarely).
- Upgrade path: come back later and pay only the difference to get a unique version. Lucy spends an hour on the phone customizing it, so it can be trademarked.
- He argues customers who value branding see even $100 as cheap. Nobody from the US or Europe called it too expensive, and some said he should charge more. A testimonial from a well-known indie hacker said it was better than a 400-euro custom designer.
- Copying risk: they cannot stop screenshots, and a watermark would "kill the experience". Their protection is that a logo is public, so a thief is exposed, and they show the copyright and could sue.

## Branding for small startups

- He agrees you do not need great branding to succeed. Google, Facebook, YouTube and Hacker News all started ugly.
- But those had revolutionary products. A small indie product usually does not, and people give you about five seconds, so he recommends a clear message, a fast site, a decent logo and consistent branding.
- A good brand gives the founder confidence and an edge. His landing page design draws compliments and makes visitors trust them quickly.
- Observation: the earlier Facebook example, slow app for years, only worked because Facebook was Facebook.

## The cost of bootstrapping and what keeps him going

- Both he and his wife quit their jobs. They are living on savings: about $80,000 down over three years, around two days of vacation in three years, and a father-in-law who wants him to get a job.
- Timeline: spent 6 months deciding what to build, launched in early May 2021 after about 18 months, then sat at roughly five sales a month for a year. They need about 60.
- He passed 150 customers at the time of the interview.
- Early traffic came from friends, Reddit, Indie Hackers and startup directories. A big early mistake was requiring signup before showing any logos. Removing it produced the first real sales.
- What keeps him going is not confidence ("confidence will leave you") but something closer to faith. One sale a month plus a customer saying they love it is enough to feel "it's not hopeless".
- Paid tests that failed: Google Ads (~$500-600), a newsletter sponsorship ($200) and Reddit ads ($100), all with zero sales.

## The Twitter breakthrough

- In late May he was desperate, so he searched Twitter for "what are you working on" style posts and replied with his link. With about 180 followers, one reply got around 100 clicks in 24 hours and 2-3 sales.
- A second viral post came from posting a screenshot of the creepy "I'll screenshot your logo" chat. That led to DMs with big indie hackers, including Arvid Kahl.
- He then put roughly 99% of his time into Twitter for three months and went from about 200 followers (June) to nearly 4,000, aiming for 10,000 by year end.
- His core rule: spend about 10 times more time interacting with other people's content than on his own tweets. After a viral post, he found that replying only on his own thread made the next day's tweets get no reach.
- Daily routine:
  - Morning: open the feed on "latest" (not the algorithmic view), scroll back to where he stopped last night, like and comment on lots of tweets (about 30 comments in an hour and a half), then reply to all his notifications.
  - Two scheduled tweets a day: one text, one meme.
  - Evening (around 9 pm): repeat the same interaction round.
  - About three hours a day total.
- Why it works, per him: when you interact with someone, their content and yours tend to show up in each other's feeds within 24-72 hours, and more interaction raises the ranking.
- He follows only about 250 people and wants real connections, so he does not outsource it. He notes that Twitter beats Instagram for conversion because people can click the profile link easily (a friend gets about 10x fewer link clicks from Instagram for similar views).

## Marketing mindset

- He says marketing still feels mysterious: with programming he felt he could build anything after three months, but with marketing he cannot see how traffic maps to sales.
- He dislikes optimizing for algorithms rather than users, but accepts it as the cost of using social platforms.
- Spamming tweets as a last resort felt wrong, and he describes it as having "nothing to lose".
- Social media warps founder self-image: headlines about $30k MRR in a year teach nothing, and he used to feel like a failure for not making millions in two years.
