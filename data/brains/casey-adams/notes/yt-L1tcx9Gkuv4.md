## Getting interviews with big names by getting creative and showing up

- Casey has run his podcast for almost five years, with 400+ interviews including Larry King, Rick Ross, Grant Cardone and Elon Musk's mom and sister. He started at 17, in his bedroom in Virginia, on a pair of Apple headphones, with no connections.
- Grant Cardone story: Casey DMed Grant on Instagram, was told to email his assistant, and emailed saying he'd "be in Miami next Wednesday and Thursday" and would love to do it in person (or on Zoom if easier). He had never been to Miami and had no plans to go. The assistant confirmed a time and studio address, so he flew out from Virginia with friends, including his business partner Kieran, and did the interview.
- His lesson: "putting yourself in positions to win and to create opportunity starts by getting creative with how you think about things." He threw the ask out first and figured out the logistics once they said yes.
- In high school he took about 80 days off school across junior and senior year, flying to LA on a Friday and back on a Monday, to make this kind of thing happen.

## How the Larry King interview happened (show up, act on small opportunities)

- A friend invited Casey to a dinner for Tilman Fertitta's book promotion (about 20-30 creators). His business partner ended up seated next to Larry King's son Chance. Casey introduced himself to Chance afterward, invited him to an event he was hosting in LA the following weekend, and Chance came.
- The next weekend, Casey spotted Larry King himself at Century City Mall. After 10-15 seconds of hesitation he walked up alone, said he'd just met Chance, admired his work, and had hosted his own podcast for about three years. He got a selfie and sent it to Chance.
- About six to eight weeks later, around Thanksgiving 2019, Chance replied to a holiday text that his dad would be interested. The interview was recorded December 10, 2019, under two weeks after that text. Casey was 19. Larry was recovering from a stroke earlier, so an earlier ask had been bad timing.
- Larry passed away later; Casey was among the last people to interview him, and BBC World News DMed him that day to go on air.
- His lesson: "never have an expectation of opportunities coming up, just get out there and put yourself in a position to win." Accept the dinner, the event, the coffee invite, because you don't know which one turns into something. A lot of opportunities cross your plate and people don't act on them.

## Pitching a hard-to-get guest (the Rick Ross approach)

- Rick Ross told Casey on air that he "presented yourself like a boss." Casey's message had these elements:
  - Referenced someone Ross respects: Casey had just interviewed Robert Greene, whom Ross constantly cites (48 Laws of Power).
  - Tied the ask to Ross's own goal: a book he had coming out that the interview would promote.
  - Asked for very little time: only 10-15 minutes.
- It took repeated DMs over time. Ross saw them, didn't respond, then eventually said to email or text it, and it got done.
- Principle: busy, successful people value their time. Understand what matters to them and how you can help.
- Casey advises thinking about who influences the person you're approaching: "who does this person follow, who do they care about," and use that in the pitch. It applies beyond podcasting.
- Before approaching someone at an event, he checks Instagram for mutual follows or connections and context, so the conversation starts past surface level. In DMs, context is how you break through "just another DM."
- He keeps re-applying advice from his own past wins, since "if you're not practicing the same things that got you to where you are then you're not going to grow as fast."

## Building and nurturing relationships

- Casey calls relationships his superpower and says it comes down to "caring about someone": paying attention, caring about what they care about, finding ways to help (for example offering guests his Vegas studio).
- Presence matters. He cites Gary Vaynerchuk: even a 30-second interaction was fully dialed in, not distracted.
- Staying top of mind: instead of watching 50 Instagram stories, reply to a few with a quality message about their day (a tactic from Nick Dio, who works with Gary). He says this restarts good conversations, even with people he hasn't talked to in a long time.
- About 80% of his time on Instagram is in DMs: 10-20 short messages a day, asking questions and reaching out to guests. Over a year those touch points add up, even when individual ones lead nowhere.
- He uses social as a producer, not a consumer. When scrolling, he goes deeper (looks up a place, learns something) instead of just liking.
- In person, going the extra mile builds deep bonds; a friend of five-plus years flew to Michigan for his birthday.
- Good energy: how you make people feel matters (coffee line, Uber driver, podcast). He describes himself as a "delusional optimist" and says positive energy comes back "tenfold."

## Staying consistent with a long-term project

- Casey publishes at least one episode a week. The show began as "Rise of the Young" with 2-3 minute solo episodes, moved to Zoom interviews, then in-person interviews, then Zoom again during COVID.
- Around a year and a half ago he questioned why he was doing it. He concluded he loves interviewing and listening ("99% of your time listening"), and treats the show as a lifelong consistency play: he wants to be 49 having done "5,314 interviews since I was 17," regardless of view counts or revenue.
- He rebranded the show from "Rise of the Young" to "The Casey Adams Show" because he'd outgrown the name, and says "your name is forever going to be with you." That one change "reinvigorated so much of my passion."
- A second reinvigorating change: via mentor David Meltzer he got into Blue Wire's Las Vegas studio (a podcast network, mostly sports, with a business slice). Since last October he flies out about once a month or every other month and records four to six interviews in a day.
- His advice: figure out small things that rekindle the fire (new name, new recording place, a new laptop for editing). "Sometimes it's a small change that will make a big difference."

## Resilience and gratitude

- At 15-16, as a sophomore, he was injured playing football, spent six months in a neck brace, almost paralyzed, and could never play sports again after a decade as an athlete. He got depressed, then found personal development (Think and Grow Rich and other books) through social media, which reset his mindset.
- Gratitude is his tool for bad days. His tactics:
  - Journal future-based messages, such as "your future self thanks you for going through this because it's made you a better man."
  - Call his mom, dad or brothers, people disconnected from his business.
  - Reflect on why he's having a bad day instead of ignoring it.
- He took the leap young: moved from Virginia to Arizona at 18 with a few suitcases to run a media company with friends, and said it's a challenging jump a lot of people never make.
- He says the age you feel is quantified by your experience (business, travel, relationships), and that his maturity came from the mentors and people he spends time with.

## Media Kits: the startup Casey co-founded

- Media Kits lets creators connect social accounts (Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, Twitch, Twitter, YouTube) via first-party APIs and build a media kit with real-time data in about 30 seconds. A media kit is a "digital resume" for creators; brands also get a trusted way to see creator data at scale.
- Origin: in 2017-2018, Casey and co-founder Kieran made a media kit by hand in Canva/Photoshop for friend Jr Garage, a big automotive YouTuber, after Pennzoil asked for one. Three weeks later Ferrari asked, the numbers were already outdated, and they were "changing ones to twos and threes to fours." That prompted the idea of a media kit that never goes stale.
- They shelved it (Casey was in high school, Kieran had a marketing agency), then in 2020, with TikTok and the creator economy booming, they hired a designer and went all in.
- Raised a $1.25 million friends and family round (David Meltzer was the first investor). Launched in August 2021 with a launch party in LA featuring Wiz Khalifa; Wiz's manager is an investor.
- Now over 25,000 creators use it monthly, with a team of eight, mostly remote. It's a "set it and forget it" tool, with more planned beyond the media kit.
- Casey says tech and raising capital were new to him, and the last year taught him more than the previous four. He notes that in the current downturn (layoffs, few raises, crypto correcting), you have to think harder about how to put yourself in a position to win.
