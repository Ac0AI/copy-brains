## Starting from a setback in high school

Casey Adams grew up in Richmond, Virginia, in a modest household. His mother was a special ed teacher (earlier a babysitter earning about $18K a year) and his father worked at Philip Morris for roughly 25 years. He played hockey, lacrosse and football and expected to play college lacrosse.

- At 15, a football drill left him with an interspinous ligament injury (his helmet came off and he slammed his head). He was told he would need a neck brace for six months and probably could never play football again.
- He describes losing his identity, spending one to three months angry and depressed, then filling the free time with YouTube and social media.
- There he found entrepreneurs such as Tai Lopez, Gary Vaynerchuk and Tony Robbins, and "something just clicked."
- He latched onto business, fundraising, marketing and Facebook ads, and "became obsessed."
- He had always been a hustler (shoveling driveways when it snowed, mowing lawns with a mower he bought), with the mindset "nothing's gonna come to me, I have to go get it myself."

His takeaway: the pivot came from a forced loss of identity, and he says he has no regrets about how it played out.

## Staying grounded and keeping family on board

Casey earned more than his parents in a year while still in high school (age 17-18), living at home, running his first company and missing about 60 days of school to travel to LA and New York for interviews.

- His mom's advice: "stay true to your roots" and don't let outside circumstances change you for the worse.
- He kept a 4.0 GPA and took AP classes, because his parents' rule was that he could travel only if his grades held.
- Persuading his parents to let him travel alone at 16-17 was one of the hardest things he did. Their buy-in came through consistency and visible results, not arguments.
- His parents grew to love it. His mom watched Rob Dyrdek's Fantasy Factory, so when he interviewed Drama of Young and Reckless he FaceTimed her.
- He says his older brother got into real estate after attending a Seattle event Casey ran (a snowboard Mastermind).
- A regret: he never built a company with his brothers, and he cites Super Coffee (three brothers) as inspiration.

## Starting a podcast fast and scrappy

Casey started his podcast, Rise of the Young, at the end of 2017 after reading the last chapter of Gary Vaynerchuk's "Crushing It," which covered podcasting as a way to build relationships and create deal flow.

- He had no long-term plan and no mic. The first episode was five minutes recorded with Apple earbuds.
- His first guests came from his close circle: Caleb Maddox, Kieran, and a friend named Jeff.
- He set a target of 60 interviews in 90 days and moved fast: Zoom interviews and scrappy Facebook Lives, optimized for volume, not quality.
- His motive was to build his network and meet people he wanted to learn from. Every conversation left him "smarter" and excited.
- He frames a podcast as a way to get access to busy people who won't do coffee meetings, and as meaningful long-form content in contrast to quick-scroll platforms.
- Later guests included Larry King and Robert Greene.

## Advice for people starting a podcast

- Start now: "don't overthink it." Have a show idea, a great title and concept, then figure it out as you go. This is the most common question in his DMs (how to start, how to book guests).
- Quantity helps early, but he says quality matters more now because the space has grown (he says the industry has been doubling or tripling year over year) and there is a lot of noise.
- Differentiate. Many guests have been interviewed hundreds of times, so he thinks about what makes his show different (being young, asking different questions).
- Avoid making it a must-do, which would stress him out. Some weeks he does zero interviews and other weeks five (Monday through Friday during the NFT craze).
- Accept uneven results: some episodes get a few listens, and one YouTube interview got about 200,000.
- He names the real work as the back-end creation and uploading, plus wanting episodes to do well when they don't.

## Handling hate and criticism

Casey says he gets little hate, but he has had messages saying guests only come on his show to leverage his audience.

- His approach is to reply personally, often with a thorough video or voice message, addressing what they said without being aggressive and explaining his intentions.
- He says about 90% of people flip and sometimes apologize.
- If someone seems to be coming from anger, he sometimes sends an interview he recorded on the issue they may be going through, and says he tries to provide resources and feedback.
- He tries to keep his own feed positive (quotes, books, purpose- and education-driven interviews), while respecting people who are vulnerable about hardships.

## Advice for young entrepreneurs

- "Surround yourself with great people" and be careful about whose advice you take.
- Social media has real upside. He pushed back in a 10th/11th grade class that preached only its negatives, telling his own story, and says the teacher agreed.
- Avoid unhealthy comparison. Emulating others is fine, but he admits he sometimes catches himself comparing and wondering what others know that he doesn't.
- He says he has tried many things over a few years and sees them as lessons, but sometimes wonders whether he was focused enough.
- Everyone is "figuring it out" (a lesson he says he took from his interviews), and his identity changes as he grows.
- He doesn't aim to be the biggest influencer. His social presence supports the podcast, his brand and his network.

## NFTs and what he is building

Casey views NFTs as "just the infancy" and believes in crypto as an asset and NFTs as proof of ownership, while saying he is no expert.

- He explains non-fungible: a dollar bill is interchangeable, while an NFT is unique, rare or exclusive.
- Example use case: a limited run of 1,000 event tickets minted as NFTs, kept in your wallet as proof of attendance.
- He did an NFT drop with WorldStar (CEO Danny, a friend), whose media brand is focused on music NFTs such as albums, singles and exclusive content.
- Artist example: his friend Justin (a photographer) spent three years photographing 100 sets of twins, struggled to sell the framed set through galleries, then dropped 100 one-of-one NFTs. He told his story on his blog and social and sold out in seven days, mostly to real collectors who believed in the story.
- A key benefit he cites: the original artist can attach a roughly 5-10% commission on resales, putting power back in the artist's hands.

His current company is Media Kits (medikits.com), built with his best friend Kieran, who sat on the idea for two years before they started building the tech about eight months earlier. It is a real-time data analytics product for musicians, bloggers, influencers and podcasters to create a Media Kit with live data that never goes out of date.
