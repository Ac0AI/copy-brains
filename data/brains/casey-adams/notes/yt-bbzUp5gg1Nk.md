## How Casey's podcast and clip strategy evolved: from scrappy interviews to a business development vehicle

- Casey started his show in 2017 at 17, in high school, as a way to connect with founders. He has since had 500+ founders on, from Netflix founders to Elon Musk's mom and sisters.
- The first ~50 episodes were Facebook Live streams on a roughly $100 Blue Yeti mic; he downloaded the audio and uploaded it as the podcast.
- He set a goal of 60 interviews in 100 days to get reps in before a meeting with Gary Vee in his junior year. Early guests became friends and later his co-founder at his last startup.
- He says episode 151 felt like his first real episode: by then he knew what not to do, what his curiosity and format were. He didn't care about listeners or opportunity, and the reps opened doors.
- Two years in, at 19, he interviewed Larry King (then 87). King's reason for starting a new show: asking questions and having conversations is "what I'm built for."
- He frames the show as a business development vehicle, not a bid to build the biggest show. His last company had 37 angel investors, and nearly all were past guests.

## Casey on brand deals: host-read plus partnerships that extend beyond the mic

- He says he rarely did core programmatic advertising. He did host-read ads, but more often branded partnerships that went beyond the mic.
- Example: Adobe and Slack, years ago, three- or six-month campaigns with four to eight host-read ads, each with about three clips per episode.
- His creative pitch to Slack: because his show interviews founders and Slack wants to talk to founders, he would interview small business owners live around Small Business Saturday.
- The live stream ran on Slack's LinkedIn channel (about a million to a million and a half executives following, with paid promotion behind it) and reached about 200,000 live concurrent viewers over a roughly two-hour interview series.
- His view: the podcaster is the talent and must bring the creative ideas. Good brands will take a bet, as TBPN's did with logos at the bottom of the screen and large payments.
- Most deals beyond that came from founders he had already interviewed whose companies were testing podcast advertising. They were already in his ecosystem, so he didn't need to cold pitch.

## Advice for emerging shows: find brands you believe in that believe in you

- Casey's advice to small shows: don't cold pitch 100 brands that are allocating budget across the biggest shows in the world.
- Instead ask what brands you already use, what relationships you have, and (for interview shows) who you can interview and how to pull your existing ecosystem in.
- Look for brands that align closely with you, then give them the creative ideas, because at this stage of the market that is the host's job.
- Aim for long-term partnerships with brands you believe in and that believe in you.

## The Formula 1 model behind TBPN's sponsorships

- Casey's read: TBPN's Jordy said it was "not a podcast." You could list it on podcast apps, but they built a cutting-edge media company, the "ESPN of tech."
- Their sponsorship model was not CPM-based. It was effectively "get in or get out": brands who wanted to associate with them paid big budgets for long-term presence in all the clips and content.
- Casey says the talent mattered. The hosts had built and sold companies and worked in venture, so people came for them and not only for their guests. He also pointed out their unfair advantage of top CEOs appearing frequently.
- He adds that they were also tech influencers, active on X all day, in replies and posting funny things.
- His takeaway for interview shows: ask whether people care about the host or only the guest, and build the host into the draw.

## Beyond the download: measuring an episode as a cluster

- Casey's view on measurement: an episode is more than downloads. It is a cluster of the long-form YouTube, X and Apple/Spotify versions plus newsletter, short form and live event activations.
- A download is just a device fetching an episode. He notes you could download 12 episodes in a month and listen to none.
- His example of the gap: a show might have 1,000 downloads a month yet get 1 million to 8 million impressions on social. If an advertiser only sponsored the long form, they missed those impressions, and showing that missed opportunity is a sales tool.
- Practical consequence he describes: publishers should control how they pitch the show as an ecosystem, using retention data (for instance YouTube) and omni-channel campaign reporting, instead of handing advertisers a spreadsheet of numbers.
- He argues comparing 5,000 streams on one show with 10,000 downloads on another is not a fair equation, because the depth of trust and type of listener differ.

## How advertisers should diligence a podcast

- Casey's advice to advertisers: before looking at downloads, check whether an emerging listener base exists. Can you find a culture in social comments and shares?
- Using shows like All-In or My First Million as examples, he says you can tell from word of mouth and audience interaction whether people care, whether the show has 100 or 100,000 listeners.
- He says there are many ways to manipulate downloads, so advertisers can be left holding the bag.
- He suggests advertisers do their own diligence on a brand's presence across platforms in addition to reading the download report.

## Quality versus volume, and less-is-more as something you earn

- Casey's view: there is polarity in the space. Some creators put out hundreds of pieces a week, while shows like Acquired release a handful of long episodes a year and are still huge.
- He argues less-is-more has to be earned. His quote from Charlie Munger: "take a simple idea and take it serious," which he applies to David Senra's show, whose guests (Dana White, Evan Spiegel) make every episode a top-tier listen.
- His example on the short-form side is James from School of Hard Knocks: on the main account, which has about 8.5 million followers, he posts roughly 10 clips a month rather than more, and in over two and a half years there is no clip under a million views.
- His point is that once you find something that works, you can say "how are we the best in the world at what we do" and make less but higher quality.
- He says there is no one-size-fits-all format and you have to figure out which approach applies in your world.
- He also references Hormozi putting out about 100 clips a week from live conference Q&A after noticing a comment with 1,000 likes saying the new format was great, as an example of volume surfacing what resonates.
- He notes a signal of format mix: people say "love your podcast" when they mean they watch clips on Instagram, and that works, as with a Caleb Pressley show built for 1-3 minute clip-oriented brand deals.
