## Casey's path into podcasting and how he got started

- At 15, a football neck injury (spine unstable, neck brace for six months, no more football) pushed him toward business content. He followed Gary Vaynerchuk and Tony Robbins and came out "fueled up" for business and marketing.
- He had no entrepreneurial family background. His mom was a teacher and babysitter; his dad worked at Philip Morris for about 30 years.
- The podcast idea came from the last chapter of Gary Vee's "Crushing It" in 2017, when Casey was 17. He started with no strategy or plan, recording on a phone with wired Apple headphones. The first episode ran about three and a half minutes and was live within a day or two.
- His advice: many people spend six months to a year conceptualizing a podcast. In most cases, "just getting started is the best thing to do."
- The original goal was to interview successful founders, learn from them, and create business opportunities through networking.

## Getting guests: the 60 interviews in 90 days push

- Casey cold-DMed Gary Vee on Instagram asking what it would take to get five minutes of his time. Gary replied and Casey skipped three days of school to meet him in New York.
- Ahead of that meeting he set a goal of 60 interviews in 90 days.
- He began with friends of similar age, then "shot his shot" with bigger names. Early guests included Andy Frisella, Ed Mylett and Grant Cardone.
- By the time he met Gary he was doing 40 to 50 interviews, and Gary encouraged him to keep going because opportunity compounds from "micro occurrences."
- Six years in (December 2023) he had done about 450 interviews, with guests ranging from Larry King and Rick Ross to Elon Musk's mom and sister and the founders of Twitch and Netflix.

## Why long-form podcasting pays off

- He cites three benefits: meeting people and building depth in relationships faster, learning from others' stories, and the opportunities that follow.
- Hour-long conversations "expedite relationship building," which creates trust, respect and openness to working together later.
- Long form builds more trust with an audience than short clips, because listeners get to know you through depth.
- He acknowledges the downside: podcasting is hard to master and slow to reward compared with short-form video where someone "can just put out 10 TikToks."
- The podcast is "the backbone of everything," not his only income. It feeds conversations and opportunities across his businesses.
- Concrete payoff: his last company raised about $1.5M from 37 angel investors, and roughly 90% of those investors were former podcast guests.
- He stayed consistent through gaps (sometimes a month to a month and a half without an episode) by asking why he does it, and by thinking about compounding over 20 years.

## Building companies from problems he lived

- MediaKits.com launched in 2020. It let creators aggregate cross-platform data in one place to share with brands, updating in real time. It was acquired in September 2022, and he worked at the acquirer, Viral Nation, for six months.
- The idea came from a friend, a YouTuber with about a million subscribers, who was assembling screenshots of Instagram, TikTok and YouTube for a brand's media kit request. Casey and his co-founder started with the first step, a Media Kit built in Canva, to learn the pain points.
- His approach: "What are problems that creators deal with," then take the first step.
- Listener.fm launched about four months before the recording. It is a post-production tool for podcasters: upload an audio or video file and get SEO-optimized titles, descriptions, show notes, blog posts and newsletters.
- He built it because he deals with the problem himself and uses it every time he records. His stated direction is a "vertically integrated podcast network" with owned IP and unique shows, plus services for podcasters.
- He is also launching a new show with two co-hosts about a month after the recording, and says he now thinks harder about go-to-market and content strategy than he did six years ago because there are far more podcasts.

## Learning and questions as an operating principle

- He did not go to college. He moved to Arizona a month after high school to run a media company.
- He frames lifelong learning as being willing "to learn anything from anybody," and says he learns most from stories and others' experiences, which cuts his learning curve.
- He named Listener.fm for this idea. He cites Larry King: "I've never learned anything while I was talking."
- Practical habits: put yourself around people who have done it before. For his first marathon he interviewed ultra-marathoners and James Lawrence, who did 101 Ironmans.
- He warns that with abundant content, "a superpower" is choosing who to learn from, since there is "no one size fits all" in advice.
- Quality questions make for better listening. He tells the story of acquiring the Instagram handle @questions, which led to a podcast called "Questions" with Jordan Young (DJ Swivel) and Brandon Jenner.

## Recommended resources and habits

- Book: "How to Win Friends and Influence People," which he rereads about once a year.
- Podcast: All-In, and more broadly, find one to three podcasts to listen to consistently.
- Practice: find something that makes you feel inspired, such as journaling, sunrises or sunsets.
- Networking early on: he attended events (e.g. ones run by Dan Fleshman) as a teenager when he had little to offer beyond "a youthful spirit and a hardworking ambitious mindset," and mostly listened.

## Life priorities he builds around

- He describes four pillars: business, foundational relationships, health, and quality relationships.
- Health: he was training for a marathon about 25 days out in Palm Beach, Florida, after running his first in Hawaii the prior year, when his longest run before training had been three miles.
- He plans year-defining moments (a book, a company, an exit, a trip) and had just bought the "big ass calendar" from Jesse Itzler's team to map them.
- He values longevity in everything, quoting Warren Buffett that reputation "takes 20 years to build but two seconds to ruin."
- He treats mortality as a reminder to treat people better and check in on them.
