## How he got into podcasting and what it did for him

- Casey started his show in December 2017 at 16-17, in Richmond, Virginia. His mom is a special ed teacher and his dad worked in a tobacco factory for 30 years, and he had no business mentors.
- A football injury put him in a neck brace for almost 6 months and stripped away his athlete identity. He fell down social media rabbit holes (Gary Vaynerchuk, Tony Robbins, Jay Shetty), started a Facebook-ads agency for local businesses, then read the podcasting chapter of Gary Vee's *Crushing It*.
- He started a podcast "to meet entrepreneurs, learn from them and maybe create some level of business opportunity." He says there was "a naive aspect of not overthinking."
- He calls podcasting "the foundation of my career": it helped him launch companies, fundraise, and meet founders he later invested in.
- His first 15 episodes charted on the top business charts alongside Tim Ferriss and Gary V.
- He thought he was late back then, and cites "the best time to plant a tree was 20 years ago. The second best time is today."
- He did not go to college; he moved from Virginia to Scottsdale, Arizona a month after graduating high school. He calls his ~500 episodes his business school.

## Podcasts as a fundraising and relationship engine

- His startup MediaKits (2020) helps creators, podcasters and Twitch streamers aggregate their data and present it to brands.
- It raised just over $1.5M from 37 angel investors, against a $1M goal. He says "pretty much all of the investors were past guests on my podcast," and he had no prior fundraising experience.
- His view: an in-depth interview is "an expedited way to build trust and relationships." A 45-60 minute sit-down builds more depth than months of social interaction.
- He uses guests as free advisors for whatever he is doing. He interviewed endurance athletes before his first marathon in 2022. He interviewed CEOs about keeping a marriage strong while scaling a business when he was getting married.
- From interviewing nearly 500 founders, he took away that the key question is "what's your unique advantage," meaning founder-market fit.
- He says a big sample of data points beats one data point. Founders give contradictory advice, so you have to sort it for yourself rather than take it as is.

## Getting big guests: his outreach playbook

- Early on he set a goal of 60 interviews in 90 days, and describes the early intensity as what set him on his path.
- He used his youth as an advantage: "lean into your advantages." Guests like Andy Frisella were doing him a solid because he was 17 and DMing them 15 times a month. His rule is to be consistent and hard to ignore.
- The core rule is to make the pitch about what's in it for the guest, not about you or your show. Find what matters to them now (a new book, a sold company, a story they're telling on other shows) and build the pitch around it.
- Do the research. He notes AI can now dig through 20 years of someone's social content so you can reference things they don't usually hear.
- Name-drop past guests the person follows. Check their Instagram following for people you have had on, and reference them; it "expedites that level of trust" and lowers the guard.
- Example: he cold-DMed Rick Ross about 50 times, unsending and resending. The one that worked said he had just had Robert Greene (Rick's favorite author) on the show, offered to promote his new book, and promised only about 20 minutes. Rick later said he came on because Casey "presented yourself like a boss," not with a hand out, and respected his time.
- Be everywhere: cold email, cold DM, show up at events. The biggest guests came from consistency, follow-up and serendipity, like going to an event in LA where the person or their team would be.
- A short personal video DM still works. Send a quick Instagram video to someone you follow or have interacted with, because "you're going to watch the video." It is how he met one of his best friends, who was a groomsman at his wedding.

## Personal brand, niche and evolving the show

- Rise of the Young (2017) became The Casey Adams Show around 2020, when he was turning 20 and no longer identified with "young people" as the mission. A personal name brand is something you can "forever stand on."
- He has rebranded the cover and style many times and says each rebrand feels like a new season. People stall out because they stop identifying with the show and don't change it.
- He points to Justin Kan, who went through roughly six rebrands before Twitch (originally justin.tv, a GoPro on his forehead).
- Don't pigeonhole yourself early. His show's topics evolved with his interests: fundraising, marathon training, marriage.
- He argues we're moving from a social media economy to an interest economy, where a new account can get millions of views. Owned channels like podcasts and newsletters matter more because they give you direct access to your audience.
- If the name, format or set doesn't excite you and make you proud to promote it, reinvent it. Options include adding a co-host or changing the format.
- He admits he posts too little and has "left a lot of growth on socials on the table," because he lives much more offline now.

## The podcast industry and where it's going

- The industry is more commercial than in 2017: bigger budgets, video production, and more focus on how you gain awareness. He still thinks it's the best time to start because it's "really just getting started."
- Most podcasts quit early (the average podcast puts out about 10 episodes and stops). He calls the medium the antithesis of quick short-form content, which is why depth and trust with an audience are worth more.
- He sees podcasters of the future building a "modern media business" rather than just a show: podcast, newsletter, live events and tours, merchandise, brand deals, and their own products and services.
- He sees live tours and cinematic sets (Chris Williamson, Sweetgreen's location for a founder interview) as a differentiator. He cites a partnership with Eventbrite as evidence that live podcasting is rising.
- Past guests don't carry you forever: "you can't just stand on the shoulder of your past episodes, the past guests. You have to continue to reinvent it."
- Copy the blueprint of the top shows, then "crumble it up" and make it your own. Examples he gives are Caleb Pressley and Mel Robbins, who started podcasting at 52.
- He built Listener (listener.com) out of this experience. It is AI-powered analytics for podcast publishers running 10-50 shows (think The Athletic, The Ringer, Barstool). It aggregates audio, long-form video and social data for sales enablement and episode reporting, and is moving toward campaign management between brands and publishers.
- He could only see the need because of 8.5 years as a host across platforms (Spotify, Megaphone, Spreaker). Publishers were "living in spreadsheets."

## Doing hard things, seasons and consistency

- Andy Frisella's influence taught him to do hard things. He has done 75 Hard three times, and sees its value in following through on what you say you'll do, not in the program being optimal.
- He has run four marathons and plans to do all the world majors over about six years (New York, Boston, Chicago, Berlin, London, Tokyo, Sydney). Long runs are phone-free time with himself.
- He works in seasons: when not training, he lifts to build muscle; when marathon training, he spends about 3 months dialed in on zone 2 and says no to more things.
- He cites Jesse Itzler's "Misogi" idea: pick one year-defining hard thing, such as a physical pursuit, a business, a book or a podcast.
- He believes you can't be perfect across the board in every season of life, and recommends choosing where to give 100%.
- Quitting is also valid: if you truly believe it's a waste of time and the signs are there, pause or pivot. He notes YouTube began as a dating platform.
- He gave Matt Rife as a persistence example: roughly 15 years of grinding before an overnight breakout, which Dane Cook described to him.
- Closing advice: get started today, and "don't get in your own way," because people are mostly worried about themselves.
