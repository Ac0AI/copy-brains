## Starting a podcast young: the neck-brace origin and the "why"

- At 15-16, Casey was almost paralyzed playing football and spent six months in a neck brace. He lost his identity (he had planned to play lacrosse at UVA) and calls the first three months the most depressing period of his life.
- He rebuilt his identity by reading about founders and entrepreneurs, which planted the seed for his career.
- He started his podcast in December 2017 at age 17, recording in his bedroom on wired Apple headphones. Video wasn't an option then.
- His reason for starting: he realized he knew nothing about business, so interviewing business owners was a way to learn from them.
- He skipped college and moved to Scottsdale a month after high school.
- Advice to aspiring podcasters: "know why you're doing it." Long-form is hard and attention is hard to get, but with a clear why, "it should never feel like that heavy lift."
- His own why was threefold: meet great entrepreneurs, learn from them, and create business opportunity.
- He has now done the show for about 9 years and credits it as the foundation of his career.

## How he landed Larry King at 19: volume, asking, and showing up

- He set a goal of 60 interviews in 90 days, partly to have something to talk about when he got a meeting with Gary Vaynerchuk in New York. He interviewed anyone and everyone in business, including young founder friends aged 16-17.
- Larry King was on his dream guest list from the start because of his blunt, authentic interviewing style.
- Path to King: he interviewed Tilman Fertitta (owner of the Houston Rockets) around his book *Shut Up and Listen* (2019). Fertitta invited him and other creators who helped with the book promotion to a dinner at Mastro's in Beverly Hills.
- He sat next to Chance King (Larry's son) without knowing who he was, talked about his podcast, and left with a soft ask: if there was ever an opportunity to have his father on the show, it would be a privilege. He asked without expecting anything.
- A week later he spotted Larry King in a wheelchair at Century City Mall and worked up the courage to say hello, mentioned meeting Chance, and took a selfie. Chance then asked his dad, who agreed. The interview happened around December 10, 2019, when Casey was 19 and King was 87.
- Lessons he draws:
  - Know when to ask.
  - The 60-100 interviews beforehand are what prepared him for the opportunity.
  - Someone like King took the time for a 19-year-old's podcast, so "we're never too busy" for meetings or calls you're tempted to skip.

## Early hustle: Facebook ad agency and podcast as a business-development engine

- Before MediaKits, from ages 16 to 19, he ran a local Facebook advertising and social media management agency for his local dentist and chiropractor, on monthly retainers.
- That income paid for his own plane tickets. In junior and senior year of high school he missed roughly 80 days of school, persuading his parents to let him travel to LA to interview people like Jay Shetty and Jake Paul (both on one day in 2018, at age 17).
- The podcast served as his network and fundraising pipeline later: about 37 angel investors in his first company were past podcast guests, including a manager for Wiz Khalifa. He calls this "business development on steroids."

## MediaKits: idea, raise, launch, and exit

- Origin of the idea (2020): a friend, the automotive YouTuber JR Garage, asked Casey and his co-founder to make a media kit one-pager in Canva or Photoshop for a brand sponsorship. It took a couple of hours and he got the deal.
- Months later the friend had grown by 100,000 subscribers, had different engagement rates, and was bigger on Instagram and Facebook. He asked them to update the numbers.
- The insight: there was no easy way for social creators to aggregate their cross-platform data and represent it to brands.
- He moved to LA at about 19-20 to start the company. He and his co-founder were non-technical (this was before AI coding tools), so they needed to hire engineers and raised money.
- Raise: about $1.5 million from 37 angel investors, which took roughly eight months, done on SAFE notes with no product yet. His investor and board member Aristotle Loumis, whom he met through Steven Galanis (CEO of Cameo), guided them through how SAFEs and fundraising work.
- Launch: a launch party in 2021 with Wiz Khalifa performing and about 700 creators.
- Exit: about 18 months after the launch, MediaKits was acquired by Viral Nation, a Canadian talent agency. Viral Nation's CEO DM'd him on LinkedIn. He stayed under a year.
- Positioning: they were "an inch wide and a mile deep," a single-focus product named MediaKits.com ("the easiest way to build a media kit"), in a creator-tool market where he says many tools "did a lot of things good, but not one thing great."

## When to sell: taking the early win

- When the acquisition came, they were about to do a proper seed round after a harsh fundraising process and had finally got a term sheet. The acquisition was a chance to "just get a win on the board."
- He explains the reasoning: it was very uncertain, they had not figured everything out (cap table and other founder issues), and the exit could be used in future ventures.
- His framework:
  - A big part of business is proving to yourself that you can take something from idea to launch, growth, scale, and exit; completing that full lifecycle taught him a lot.
  - Weigh the time-and-effort-to-outcome ratio when the future is uncertain and partners may want different things.
  - There are exceptions (founders who turned down billion-dollar offers), but the early win is a lesson to build from.
- His observation about buyers: the endgame is often unclear to young founders and the right buyer is rarely the obvious one.
- Preparing for an exit: keep your books clean, keep a clean cap table, stay capital efficient, and plant seeds with potential strategic acquirers early.
- His former co-founder later sold a second company (Shop Genie, an auto-repair-shop CRM) two years in to a private-equity-backed company in Texas; Casey was a small partner. He says once you've done an exit, you understand the process.

## Listener.com: positioning and go-to-market

- Listener launched in April 2025 as an AI-powered analytics and intelligence system for modern podcast publishers.
- It aggregates audio, video, and social data (podcast hosting downloads, YouTube long-form data, and social clips) so the top 1% of publishers can represent their shows and campaigns to advertisers more effectively.
- Target customer: teams running networks of 20 to 50 shows. He went after the biggest shows first because they have the most analytics, monetize best, and have the biggest data-fragmentation problem across platforms.
- Thesis: social clips are part of the show, not just a promotional vehicle. Modern podcasts are sold and measured in a way that includes clips, so advertisers want to be part of that clip culture.
- Example he cites: Caleb Pressley (an investor and a podcaster/comedian), who has no Apple or Spotify feed but sells ads through clips, with each clip getting about a million views.
- His view of the market: the old-school podcast industry (audio natives) is disconnected from the creator ecosystem building the biggest shows, though both are moving to the same point.
- He used strategic investors with aligned interests (Caleb, Ben Kaplan, founder of Jerry Media and What Do You Meme).

## Fundraising approach for Listener: stay lean, keep optionality

- First company: raised $1.5M as first-time founders before having a product. For Listener he has not done a proper round; he took strategic capital from a former co-founder, a former board member, and people like Ben and Caleb.
- Team: three people, kept extremely lean, building for a need in the market.
- Why lean: in the AI era, customers ask "could we just build this?" so he wanted relief to shift product direction in the first six months. He's glad he didn't have a conviction he wasn't willing to move on, and notes that what Listener is has shifted a lot since last year.
- His biggest lesson as a founder: give yourself optionality.
  - He has the option to raise a proper round (he could commit to that path) or to keep growing sustainably with customers.
  - He doesn't want to over-raise before he's figured it out and then be forced to sell or return capital.
- He notes that his advice should be taken "with a grain of salt" since it comes from his own experience, and that he drew on about 500 conversations with founders.

## Long-term outlook and operating principles

- Family is a priority; he got married last year. He cites Mark Randolph (Netflix co-founder), who kept a hard stop every Friday.
- He wants to back founders at the ground floor as an angel (he has done an SPV and some angel investments), inspired by Harry Stebbings building a media-led fund; he has an appetite for something like that, not "a fund to raise a fund."
- He believes "what worked in the past isn't going to work in the future," and says to shed experience and stay curious about the world as it is today, rather than hold strong beliefs that block seeing what's ahead. He cites the shift toward AI, robotics, and space, and reads *Life at the Speed of Play* by the Zynga founder.
- He sees himself as an entrepreneur at his core who will always be starting things, and plans to take his podcast to the next level as a long-game commitment.
- Habit: he has run a marathon every year for the last four years (the Sydney Marathon was upcoming); the 2-3 hour runs give him a bird's-eye view and a chance to think.
