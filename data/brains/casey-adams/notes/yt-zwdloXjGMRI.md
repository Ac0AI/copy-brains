## How Casey got started: injury, Gary Vaynerchuk's book and a podcast at 16

- Casey grew up in a small town in Virginia with no business-minded friends or family. A football injury in sophomore year left him in a neck brace for over six months (inter-spinous ligament damage). He calls it "a blessing in disguise."
- During recovery he moved into personal development and entrepreneurship content (Gary Vaynerchuk, Tony Robbins). The last chapter of Gary's book *Crushing It* is about podcasting. Casey saw it as a low-effort, low-output format for learning from people he wanted to interview.
- The podcast launched in December 2017, when he was still in high school, with three motives:
  - Meet people and build a network, since nobody around him was going to help him level up.
  - Learn from them.
  - The business opportunity and long-term value of a strong network.
- Early episodes were solo talks about his neck injury and building a personal brand, then interviews with friends met on social media, including his later business partner Kieran O'Brien.
- Five and a half years in he had done over 400 interviews, from Larry King to Elon Musk's mom and sister to the founders of Twitch and Netflix. He says you can't predict which guest leads to which door, but one guest leading to the next creates a network effect.

## Booking guests: outputs, reference points and who they admire

- His first "wow" guest was Chris "Drama" Pfaff (Young and Reckless, Rob & Big). It took one DM about a book Drama had posted. Casey says you're often "one message away," though sometimes it takes 10 follow-ups.
- Early goal: 60 interviews in the first 90 days. That came from a 5-10 minute meeting he had lined up with Gary Vaynerchuk in New York in early 2018. Casey got it by DMing Gary after K-Swiss sent him early-access Gary Vee shoes. He skipped school for the day to go. He credits that sprint with building his interviewing momentum.
- His outreach rules, using the Rick Ross booking (2020, about 10 interactions over roughly a year) as the example:
  - Give a reference point. He told Ross he had just interviewed Robert Greene, one of Ross's favorite authors.
  - State the time frame up front ("20-25 minutes, short and sweet").
  - Ask for nothing else. Ross later said Casey "approached me like a boss."
- Be curious about the guest's sphere of influence. Before Ross he interviewed the CEO of Sovereign Brands, which runs Bel Air champagne. To reach Wiz Khalifa he interviewed Ludy, then Will Dzombak, CEO of Taylor Gang records.
- That chain led to Will investing in Media Kits and Wiz performing at the Media Kits launch party in 2021.

## The podcast as a long-term business asset, not just ad inventory

- Casey says the real value is not one-off sponsorships. About 90% of the investors in his $1.5M Media Kits round came through the podcast.
- He compares typical podcast ad rates, roughly $25 CPM, with how he structures deals: a "vertically integrated deal stack" across the whole personal brand.
- Slack example (three months, last year): podcast ads, Instagram Reels, TikTok and Twitter content, plus hosting a live workshop of about an hour and a half for small business owners on Slack's LinkedIn page. The page has millions of followers, and the workshop ran during small business appreciation month.
- He pursues equity over cash where possible. He is an advisor to Callin (the social podcasting app run by David Sacks) after doing a deal there.
- He uses guests as research. Before launching a SaaS company he interviewed about 50 SaaS founders. Before one day launching a venture fund he plans to interview about 50 fund GPs.
- His definition of luck: "planting seeds with the expectation of watering them," for example sending a sick contact green juice.

## Media Kits: origin, fundraise and launch

- The idea came in 2018. YouTuber Jeff Jarrett (Pennzoil, then Ferrari) kept asking Casey and Kieran to update his Canva media kit as his subscribers grew. They asked why creators couldn't have a media kit that never goes stale, shareable by link with real-time data, and why brands couldn't see verified data.
- They shelved it in 2018 because Casey was in high school and had no tech or SaaS experience. They revived it in mid-2020 as TikTok-driven creator economy growth took off, spending six months bootstrapping, ideating and collecting creator feedback.
- Fundraise (2021):
  - Raised about $1.5M pre-seed from 37 angel investors over roughly six months (January to June/July 2021, a rolling close).
  - Check sizes ranged from $15K to $25-50K, with a few $100-150K.
  - It took about 150-200 meetings to get the 37 yeses. Casey says the hardest check to get is the first one, and momentum is everything.
  - He notes it consumed a lot of founder time while they were still building and hiring.
- At launch (August 2021) the team was seven full-time: three engineers, two co-CEOs, a COO and marketing help.
- Mentor: Aristotle Loomis. He describes a true mentor as someone who will "get their hands dirty," such as helping craft an email or script a call.

## Launch party that backfired, and the organic growth that fixed it

- Launch was August 11, 2021: a party-plus-networking event at Dan Fleischman's Elevator Studio in downtown LA. Speakers included Tanner Fox and David Meltzer, with 700 creators attending and Wiz Khalifa performing.
- It put the brand on the map, but they scrambled to finish the site against the clock. The product shipped with bugs and crashes, signups from the event were roughly 1,000, and growth flatlined for two months (August and September).
- What got them to the first 10,000 users with no paid media (September to year-end):
  - Organic TikTok content showing creators' and investors' media kits (Josh Richards, Wiz Khalifa).
  - A Twitch integration, followed by going deep in the Twitch community on Twitter and running giveaways such as gifted subscriptions.
- That reached 25,000+ users by early 2022, then a B2B model for talent agencies and managers (WME Sports, Taylor Gang, Landing Records). It let them build 10-50 media kits for a talent roster in one place.
- Casey says lessons from the launch party include that things are usually more expensive and take longer when you match a product timeline to a fixed event.

## Acquisition and what he's building next

- In 2022, while deep in a seed raise with an elite lead investor lined up, the CEO of Viral Nation (a 400-person influencer marketing company that raised roughly $200M in private equity, per Casey) reached out on LinkedIn. After meeting the team in Toronto, they were acquired in September 2022, just over 12 months after the August 2021 product launch.
- He says the product fit Viral Nation because they manage thousands of creators exclusively and could drop the talent roster product straight in.
- Next: Listener.fm, a podcast post-production tool built with an engineer. You drop in an audio file and it outputs titles, descriptions, show notes and other assets using AI. He built it because he has the problem himself and uses it for every episode.
- He applies the venture "founder-market fit" idea: build where you have industry experience and access. He is also interested in angel investing and eventually raising a fund.

## Keep going, never quit, and doing hard things

- His core motto is "keep going, never quit." He says the biggest common thread among 400+ guests is that they kept going through adversity and didn't quit when a logical person would have. He adds that top performers know when to pivot, not just when to persist.
- "The day I think about quitting is the day you die." He argues for committing to the long game (relationships, business, process) over short-term games, and notes everyone gets the same amount of time. He cites the "time billionaire" idea and regular reflection on mortality (from Robert Greene) as a motivator.
- Hard physical challenges as a business practice:
  - 75 Hard (Andy Frisella); he was on day 63 when recorded and had done it once before in 2020.
  - Daily cold plunge. He says it never gets easier, you just get better.
  - Running a marathon a year for 20 years.
- He cites James Lawrence ("Iron Cowboy"): 50 Ironmans in 50 states in 50 days, then 101 in a row. Lawrence's lesson, per Casey: it starts with one step, so focus on the next step. He applies that "one foot in front of the other" framing to business.
- He says people can't obsess over outcomes. Obsess over the journey, because no one had it figured out until they did.

## Lessons from specific guests

- **Larry King:** "I've never learned anything while I was talking." Casey now treats intentional listening as his default. King's answer to what makes people great was that "those who are great are always the most driven." Casey met King's son Chance at a Tilman Fertitta dinner, then by coincidence ran into King at a mall a week later, and interviewed him in December 2019. Casey says it was one of King's last in-studio interviews, and he describes King as completely present for the 45 minutes.
- **Maye Musk (Elon's mom):** His takeaway is how tightly knit the Musk family unit was and how strongly it stayed aligned. He argues you should find your "family" (friends, masterminds) because it's too hard to go alone.
- **Robert Greene:** Casey calls him a friend. Greene handwrites every page, builds 150 note cards of research per chapter, works 12-18 hours a day for years per book, and wasn't successful until his late 30s or early 40s. Casey says 48 Laws is meant to equip you to recognize manipulation, and tells people that "no matter what business you're in, you're in the people business."
