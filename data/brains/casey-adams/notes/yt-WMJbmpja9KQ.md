## Early life, risk, and why Casey treats investing as math

- Casey grew up in a casino family (Vegas until age 11, then New Jersey). His dad worked in gaming, and as a teen Casey asked his uncle which casino game was best to win at.
- The answer he took on board: "they have those big buildings because you can't win at the games." Casino games are "games of math and the math says you lose," and apparent wins just keep you playing longer.
- Casey treats gambling as paid entertainment and nothing more. People who think they have a system to tilt the math their way are fooling themselves.
- He says this shapes how he invests. He sees investing as "very calculated math," a question of risk against reward, with the difference that investing can actually be won.
- The goal is to weigh the reward against the risk and mitigate the risk in ways you believe you can, without falling in love with the deal.
- Watching his dad drop from a casino VP earning $150-160K to an assistant manager at Wendy's within about 12 months (late 1980s to early 90s) taught Casey that a highly specialized skill set may not transfer. He wanted a baseline toolkit that would make money regardless of industry.

## Learning law school the hard way: find the best and copy them

- Casey graduated with an economics degree and a mediocre GPA (about a 2.7 at BYU). He decided to try law school after watching his wife practice, applied to only one school, the University of Utah, and got in.
- After a first semester where he was sure he was top 10 and landed in the bottom half, he refused to accept that "those kids are smarter than me."
- He went to every professor who would take a meeting and asked how to think about getting good grades. He identified the best students, asked what they did, compared it to his own approach, and built his own process.
- He graduated in the top 10% of his class.
- He calls interviewing the best in a field and copying them one of the highest-return habits there is, and says most people skip it.
- Step one of the pattern is being honest about a weakness: "I should be good at this and I'm not." Then list the experts, talk to them, and expand out through their network.

## Copy 99%, improve one thing

- Casey says he doesn't have business ideas to invent. He tries to take "99% copy and do one little thing a little bit better" (more charming, cheaper, nicer).
- His example is the iPhone vs. BlackBerry. The BlackBerry already had email, internet, phone, contacts and a clock, so the iPhone was "a good version of a BlackBerry."
- He knew Apple had something when his nearly one-year-old son slid the unlock block on a display iPhone and opened an app, while BlackBerry required a training course and a book at his law firm.
- He believes in not reinventing the wheel, since most things have been thought of before.

## Building strengths by necessity, not interest

- Casey describes himself as a well-rounded executive, but says it came from need: "I needed to not suck." At the company then called Visible (later Radial's parent), operations were weak, so he studied Six Sigma, lean and kaizen.
- He says he didn't choose finance or operations out of interest. Someone had to figure it out, and he did.
- On weakness vs. strength, he reframes weaknesses as undeveloped areas: "anything's a weakness until you develop it" (a person with great lung capacity is just that until it's developed for a use case).
- His dominant strength, as he puts it, is a willingness to dig into any topic, translate it into something familiar, and build capability.
- The key to kaizen, in his view, is going directly to the activity: be present, watch it, participate in it, and get smart. He says people skip this by circling the activity instead.

## Leaving law practice and how he split roles with Jared

- Casey left a top-100 law firm for a regional firm, then moved in-house at his friend Jared Starling's company (Visible), then roughly $10M in revenue with a couple hundred thousand dollars of earnings.
- Two things pushed him out: litigation was making him combative, and billing in 0.1-hour increments made his whole life a stopwatch. He recalls a moment at a movie thinking about the notepad with "0.3" next to his computer.
- He took a substantial pay cut and valued being proactive: getting in front of problems before they turned ugly.
- Early proof of value: a client wasn't paying Jared's company, and Casey spotted that the contract had no waiver of consequential damages. He told the other side's lawyer they'd owe for the damage to Jared's business too, and the money arrived by the Wednesday deadline.
- Their working split: Jared is the entrepreneur and engine who pushes the cadence and takes risk; Casey is "driving," mitigating risk and saying what he needs to be comfortable. Casey says "I'm not an entrepreneur, Jared is."

## Customer experience and the parcel insight that built the business

- Early on, their biggest clients kept firing them for operational failures, unclear invoicing, or thinking they could do it themselves. Casey took over client management so "we're not going to get fired again."
- Fulfillment was an awful category experience, and he tried to see what everyone did and copy it. The fixes:
  - Let customers come into the warehouse whenever they wanted, without 48 hours' notice, so they felt it was theirs.
  - Replace one-line summary invoices ("hard cost, 12% markup, no breakdown") with activity-level breakdowns: orders shipped, units, destinations, shipping cost.
  - Recognize that the bookkeeper paying the bill was the biggest detractor, so give them transparency ("know their business better than they know their business").
- Itemizing exposed activities they weren't invoicing for, so they likely ended up charging more. His line: "people will pay for value always."
- The superpower turned out to be parcel expertise. Jared saw a mis-routed order and noticed shipping via USPS would be about $2 cheaper per order than the carrier the customer chose, but the software couldn't calculate it.
- Most competitors treated shipments as numbers (distance, weight, cost); Jared treated them as boxes with dimensions, which matter to cost.
- Casey says it made them a big USPS reseller: about $1.5M a year of spend with the Postal Service early on, and about $1.2B when he sold to Maersk 11 years later. The company's real advantage became this parcel edge.

## Acquisitions: buying what's worth more to you than to the seller

- Roughly half of the company's growth by the Maersk sale was organic and half inorganic. Casey says the acquisitions worked because they were great operators first.
- Their test: is this business more valuable to us than to its current owner? They would look for targets broken in an area they were good at, then price what it would be worth with their contracts and cost advantage plugged in.
- Examples he gives:
  - A fulfillment company bought for around $27M that they believed would be worth $50-60M under their contracts.
  - A business with a $14M market value (private equity had looked at it) where the seller wanted $27-28M. Casey says they paid the seller's price because they believed it was worth $54M, and he thinks it was closer to $70M to them the next year. The business did about $2M of EBITDA with roughly $10M of revenue.
  - Another target the market valued as a SaaS business that they saw as a shipping business, plus others in freight and warehousing where they thought they could run it better.
- He says they were conservative in the math, so they could make big bets. One purchase took a full year of earnings, and another was half their own size.
- On exits: "if they offer a dollar more than I think it's worth I'll sell it to them." He says entrepreneurs fall in love with the business, while he doesn't, and he views a sale as selling something that is more valuable to the buyer than to him.
- He credits some of the outcome to luck: selling at a great point in the market.

## Faith, optimism, and playing long games

- Casey defines luck as doing the right things and good things happen (or the reverse for bad luck). He says his success includes big runs of luck and that outcomes outstrip his inputs.
- He views learning something and then doing it as an act of faith. He ties his business success to a deep belief in a positive outcome from the work.
- He says this makes him willing to play long games in business: "things will always work out, I'll just buy more time."
- In volleyball, people say "I always feel like I'm about to win," even when down by eight at game point.
- He says belief plus action is a good definition of faith, and that his religious background encouraged it.
- He describes his two-year mission as hard and not enjoyable but valuable, and credits his mission companion for helping him get his head right.
- On trust and teammates, volleyball with Jared taught honest, high-trust communication: they could cover each other's weaknesses without judgment, which carried into business.
