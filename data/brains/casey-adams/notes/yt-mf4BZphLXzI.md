## Why Casey's podcast runs as a vehicle for business, not just content

- Casey started his podcast at 17, after a neck injury in high school that nearly paralyzed him. His stated purpose has held steady for eight years: "to meet great people, learn from them, and hopefully create some type of business opportunity."
- He treats the show as a business engine. Roughly 90% of the 37 angel investors in his first tech startup's funding round were past podcast guests.
- He points to Harry Stebbings as the model for the same idea. Stebbings used 20VC to build up to a $400 million fund by age 28, after starting the show around 2015.
- His takeaway: "podcasting is a vehicle for so much more." He admires hosts who use the medium beyond the show itself, such as Stebbings with an investment fund and Steven Bartlett with a media company.
- His whole career, from his companies to the funds he has raised, "has sort of been built on the foundation of podcasting."

## How Casey's content focus follows his current business priorities

- Casey lets the show mirror where he is in life and business: "I tend to live my life through the lens of my podcast."
- Early on he interviewed young entrepreneurs, because he wanted to meet other young people doing things.
- When he started his first software company in 2020 and raised capital, he interviewed venture capitalists and startup founders.
- Over the last three years he has run a marathon every year, and he began interviewing ultra marathoners and athletes.
- While engaged, he had guests whose conversations drifted into marriage, kids and family.
- He argues that in an era of information abundance and AI, human conversation and learning from lived experience have become more valuable to him, not less.

## Casey's view of where the podcast industry is going

- He says podcasting has matured into a "media powerhouse": mainstream appeal, influence on elections, celebrity shows, Netflix moving in, and nine-figure deals (he cites Alex Cooper and Rogan).
- Even so, he calls it "still so incredibly early." More competition exists than ever, but there is still room to find a unique voice.
- Successful shows have large unseen operations: 30, 50 or 60 production and media team members, plus A/B testing.
- The definition of "podcast" is blurring. He notes YouTube's top podcast list included shows with no RSS feed that are not on Apple or Spotify.
- He cites Steven Bartlett's show crossing 10 million subscribers, and a Forbes report that it brings in $20-25 million a year.
- He frames his own listener-side work around this shift. YouTube is the biggest podcasting platform, so "what is a listener, where are they listening" has changed.

## Listener.com: a listener analytics and monetization layer

- Casey is building listener.com as a listener analytics tool for modern podcasts and podcast teams.
- The first go-to-market product is a listener dashboard that shows a show's value beyond downloads and plays.
- The pitch is that top shows sell merchandise, live events and brand deals, so they need a holistic view of on- and off-platform data.
- Customers use it to pitch brands, guests and partnerships.
- It came out of an acquisition of an existing podcast tech product, which was integrated into the tech stack.
- The longer-term vision: a sales enablement tool and monetization engine for what he calls "podcast 3.0," serving everyone from independent podcasters to large networks as an infrastructure layer.
- The company's name comes from a Larry King quote on his show: "I've never learned anything while I was talking, therefore be a great listener."

## How Casey prepares for and runs interviews

- He avoids a rigid question blueprint. He keeps topics and notes things he is curious about from his research.
- He deliberately keeps prep light so it never feels daunting. That is part of why he still has excitement and longevity after hundreds of interviews.
- His research checklist: understand the person's story and context, then ask what he does not know about them and what he would want to know.
- His format runs 45 to 60 minutes. He tries to "meet them where they are today" rather than spending 30 minutes on background and 10 on the present.
- He sometimes does less research on a guest so he doesn't "clutter my mind" and can keep curiosity open. He models this on Larry King, who asked direct questions and was curious about each person.
- He is considering testing 2 to 4 hour episodes, citing Chris Williamson, because he worries about defaulting to the same format.

## How Casey turns interviews into a personal operating system

- He says the value is often in stories and rules that surface later, not in an immediate takeaway from a given episode.
- Example: Tilman Fertitta's "955 rule," tied to picking up trash in his restaurants, meaning focus on the 5% of things people don't see.
- He applies the idea in daily life, such as picking up small trash on the ground, and says attaching meaning, rules and stories to habits is "super empowering."
- He now rereads old transcripts and revisits old episodes, and says he picks up different things as his experience grows.

## Casey's approach to opportunity, relationships and ambition

- He says there is no strategy behind spotting people who later became big: "it's just a curiosity." Past guests he cites include Jay Shetty and Jake Paul (interviewed in 2018 before his first boxing fight).
- Core belief: "the people that are right around you today are the movers and shakers of tomorrow." He chooses carefully who he spends time with and builds with.
- He says yes to opportunities without knowing where they lead. He is taking a last-minute London trip because he expects it to be valuable.
- His rule of thumb: the events he attends and coffees he takes are never regretted; the ones he skips stay with him.
- He believes entrepreneurship and growth are a "double-edged sword" in that it is "hard to be satisfied," and he tries to step back and recognize how far he has come.
- His biggest lesson from guests: "just keep going and never quit," with a built-in "element of delusion."
- On priorities, his advice to his future self is to know what matters most (family, wife, future kids) and not let lesser things crowd it out, and to stay present.
