## Personal branding as authentic perception and authority

- Casey defines personal branding as "building the most authentic perception of yourself online." Everyone already has an in-person first impression; the digital footprint is the same thing maintained online.
- He started in 2016 at age 16 after reading Gary Vaynerchuk's Crush It and Crushing It. He never tried to be someone he wasn't: he reviewed the self-development books he was reading, which led to daily Snapchat book reviews and Snapchat affiliate marketing.
- His view now: a personal brand is built on accomplishments. "You can put out the most content in the world but what gives you credibility?"
- His early credibility came from association. At 17-18 he interviewed successful entrepreneurs on a podcast while starting a digital marketing agency, then promoted the interviews across social media. The collaboration lent him authority.
- He quotes Jay-Z: "I'm not a businessman, I'm a business, man."
- He says personal brand does not guarantee success, since many successful people stay low-key and behind the scenes. But it speeds up what used to require knocking on doors, and gives instant authority in a niche.
- Your brand changes as you change; the work is maintaining it over time.

## How a personal brand attracts investors and equity partners

- Casey says business partners bet on him because of his relationships from the podcast and what he has shown on social. He has taken equity in multiple companies as a result.
- He describes a specific case: a man he was pitching for Media Kits wanted to invest after seeing him on Instagram as a young hustler, without knowing his background. Casey credits four years of consistent personal branding.
- Raising money for Media Kits was easier and faster (a roughly $1M round over about 30-60 days) because of the relationships and podcast built over the prior four to five years.
- His podcast and brand are "a vehicle" for the business. His current focus is deal structures, fundraising rounds, acquisition, and starting an angel investment fund, and he uses interviews with venture capitalists to feed that interest.

## Platform strategy: pick one and start small

- His early mistake was trying to do everything at once: Snapchat, Instagram, a Facebook page, viral videos, and thinking about numbers rather than content.
- His advice: go in on one thing. He argues TikTok is the best platform for new creators right now.
- Reasons he gives: you get more fulfillment when you have traction; starting a YouTube channel and reaching even 100 subscribers is hard without a viral video; Instagram is still very curated.
- Tactic: make 15 to 30 second TikTok clips, put out 25 or more in a month, and see what sticks.
- He admits he has under-used TikTok and YouTube himself, partly through procrastination. Now his social time is spent meeting people, pitching investors, and fueling his company.

## The podcast as deal flow, networking and relationships

- He started the podcast at the end of 2017, now about 300 episodes, initially interviewing friends and people he wanted to talk to. Notable guests include Larry King, Jordan Belfort, Grant Cardone, Robert Greene, Rick Ross and Chris Voss.
- He calls the podcast "deal flow, networking opportunities." Having it positions you as a person of value while you also learn from every guest.
- Example: he interviewed Aristotle Loomis (founder of Ellison Eyewear, later a cannabis tech startup that raised over $60M). After the interview Casey told him what he was working on, they set up a follow-up call, then texted daily. Within about six months Loomis became a mentor, investor and board member for Media Kits and helped with fundraising.
- Lesson: when someone is willing to give you time, keep them close. "If someone's giving you their time they could be spending their time anywhere else."
- Casey stresses that not every guest turns into a relationship; you find the few you really hit it off with.

## Showing up in person and where confidence comes from

- His biggest investment in himself over five years: travel to events, to meet people, and "to shake someone's hand in person." He cites hosting and attending events.
- In-person meetings make you easier to remember and build a deeper connection than Instagram DMs or Zoom.
- Story: in 2018 he messaged Grant Cardone, got a reply from his assistant, and when offered an in-person interview in Miami the following Thursday, he booked a flight despite never having been to Miami and having to miss school. He has since spoken with Cardone at events and talked with him often.
- His confidence comes from track record: Cardone was roughly his 30th-40th interview, so he could point to guests Cardone knew. His first 10 interviews were scripted and scattered. A sports upbringing with two older brothers also shaped his mentality.

## Media Kits: origin, problem and positioning

- Media Kits (mediakits.com) is a real-time data and analytics tool that lets influencers, creators, podcasters, musicians and bloggers build an online media kit, a "digital resume," with live data.
- Origin: about three years earlier, YouTuber friend Jeff (JR Garage) asked for a media kit when a company reached out, then asked for an update after Ferrari wanted to fly him to Daytona. His PDF was already outdated after growing by 100,000 subscribers. That sparked the idea of a media kit that never goes out of date.
- Timeline: the idea sat for about two years; when COVID gave them time, Casey and co-founder Kieran bootstrapped for five months, then built a team of about eight full-time. As of the interview it was in beta with a 5,000-person waitlist and a planned launch party.
- Casey sees the problem as a user too: guests pitch him with media kits, and when he pitches guests they ask for his podcast's media kit. He sees potential in influencers, musicians, podcasters, athletes and eventually gamers pitching brand deals and sponsorships.
- He believes every creator who wants to work with brands efficiently will need one, "like having a website or having an Instagram account."

## Routines, creativity and efficiency

- Sleep: he does not believe in staying up for days. He aims to sleep by 10 to 10:30 and wake at 5 to 7 depending on the day, and says it differs per person. An ideal pattern is asleep at 10, up at 5 or 5:30.
- He says it is about quality of time, not quantity: "Are you actually moving the needle?" He sets tangible daily, weekly and monthly goals, such as the million dollar raise, and notes missed goals slow the ship.
- Creativity comes from a run, the gym with a podcast, or reading. As co-CEO juggling team, investors, partnerships, design and developers, he says the work can "fog your mind"; 20-30 minutes of one of those activities puts him back on center.
- He likes biographies and company stories (he was reading Super Pumped on Uber) because seeing others' problems puts his own in perspective and gives him an idea he can apply to operations, team building, scaling or raising capital.

## NFTs as a learning experiment

- He first heard of NFTs in February 2021. With digital artist friend Television he dropped his own one-of-one piece depicting him at a table with Larry King, Elon Musk and Rick Ross.
- His goal was not the sale price but learning the process: minting, building hype, marketing, and promoting.
- He points to Gary Vaynerchuk's project raising $30-50M in two to three weeks and attaching NFTs to in-person events, and expects similar links to concerts and album releases.
- He considers himself not an expert but sees blockchain ownership, tickets and IDs as a change over the next decade, dependent on adoption.
