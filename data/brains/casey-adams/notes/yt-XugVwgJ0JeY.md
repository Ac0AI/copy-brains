## Podcasting is now a format, not an RSS feed

- Casey argues "podcast" has evolved from a technical delivery mechanism (RSS) into a format: an episodic, conversational show.
- He uses a book analogy: books began on paper, but Kindle and audiobooks are still books. The format survived the change of container.
- Netflix and Tubi signing podcast-style shows are, in his view, contracting for a talk show comparable to the big podcasts.
- He says YouTube, Spotify and social platforms raised the medium's value, business opportunity and cultural influence. Much of the RSS loyalty comes from people who remember the first 10 to 20 years, when it was neither visual nor social.
- He rejects the idea that audiences are wrong to call a Netflix or YouTube video show a "podcast". "Who am I to say the audience is wrong in how they perceive it?"
- His taxi/Uber analogy: both move you from A to B, and fighting the new delivery method means you lose out. He cites the car versus the horse in the same spirit.
- He ties this to a generational shift: people who grew up on social media met podcasts on YouTube and clips and have no context for RSS.

## Why Casey thinks the industry loses sight of the listener

- He says people in the industry get absorbed in internal debates and forget who they serve: the listener (or viewer), where they are, and how they consume today.
- That belief is behind the company name. He quotes Larry King: "I never learned anything while I was talking, so be a good listener."
- Brands sponsor and advertise on podcasts because they want to reach listeners, so the audience's behavior should drive the measurement.
- He accepts that RSS is a valid origin story worth honoring, but says the industry has matured well past downloads.
- His stance on whether he is still a podcaster: yes, 100%. The term describes the format, not only the pipe.
- He keeps a direct-to-audience channel in mind: RSS gave a direct line to listeners and trust. In a short-form, algorithmic world that is harder, so build direct links via email or a newsletter and be present on the platforms your audience already uses.

## Listener.com: aggregating a show's whole footprint

- Listener.com launched in May 2025 as an AI-based analytics and intelligence platform for modern podcast publishers, from the biggest publishers to large independents.
- Thesis: combine audio data, long-form video data and short-form content data (plus newsletters) in one place.
- Casey's framing is "Episode Clusters": one episode becomes a cluster of audio downloads, a long-form YouTube/Facebook/X video, and dozens of short clips on YouTube Shorts, Instagram, X, Facebook and TikTok.
- His example: an episode with about 16k audio downloads reported as roughly 1.3 million total reach once YouTube, audio and social are combined.
- Platforms are not equal. An X viewer, an Instagram viewer, a long-form YouTube viewer and an Apple/Spotify listener are different consumers, so each platform gets its own treatment and is not collapsed into a single impression number.
- Integrations (15+) fall into three groups:
  - **Audio:** Apple, Spotify, and hosts such as Megaphone and Simplecast.
  - **Content:** YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, X.
  - **Third-party:** newsletters (Beehiiv now, Substack in progress), added from customer requests in the last six months.
- Matching clips to episodes: if a guest or episode name appears in the title on Apple/Spotify and in the caption of social clips, the system suggests clusters automatically. He says it's imperfect, and building clusters manually takes minutes once the workflow is understood.
- He launched a Listener MCP so teams can query their data from Claude or ChatGPT, or via "Listener AI" in the product. Example questions: "What's our best content?" "How is our show growing across everything?"
- His line on AI: "We're not building an AI podcast." They use AI to understand data better and deliver more effective insights, not to produce shows.

## Reporting to advertisers and brands

- Casey says Listener now tracks tens of millions of dollars in ad campaigns for roughly 50 to 70 brands.
- Publishers send brands a Listener campaign report. His example: a brand spent $1M across five shows and 50 episodes, and sees performance across YouTube, audio and social, including host-read ads and social integrations.
- Brand feedback he cites: "This is exactly what we were looking for. How did this not exist before?"
- Advertisers sometimes learn they appeared in far more places than expected, such as 37 pieces of content with their logo or caption, when they thought they bought a single host-read ad.
- Data stays in the publisher's hands. Publishers share Listener links or dashboards with brands, and the platform supports verification through the underlying integrations.
- On measurement standards (Spotify's 30-second count, IAB's 60-second standard, YouTube's opaque view definition): Casey says Listener isn't leading those standards conversations. His position is to pull each platform's data as that platform counts it, and he expects eventual convergence.
- He advises against waiting for an ideal industry standard: "You shouldn't rely on the perfect scenario happening, because most of the time it never will."
- He wants Listener to define an industry-style format for reporting host-read ads across platforms, not a programmatic ad format.
- For small shows: a young podcaster may have modest downloads but a big social community and newsletter list. Showing the combined footprint stops advertisers from undervaluing them based on a downloads estimate alone.

## Who Listener sells to

- Go-to-market started with networks and large multi-show publishers, for three reasons: they have the most data to work with, they care most because the stakes are high, and they are the ones monetizing with big budgets and big brands.
- Customer example: Jomboy Media, a New York baseball media company (he compares it to Barstool Sports for baseball) with about 40 to 50 podcasts. He says MLB took a minority stake in it.
- Over the last 60 to 90 days, Listener started onboarding independent publishers using a self-serve model with a free trial. His view is that the big-publisher endorsement had to come first, and independents followed.
- Network customers get a guided onboarding because the product integrates into their workflow. He also offers demos via the site.

## What the new media winners do: TBPN as the case study

- Casey calls TBPN a model for modern media. Founders who are experienced operators with deep tech-ecosystem relationships built a live daily show with top guests.
- His account of their results: revenue went from $5M to $30M a year because they treated the show like a Formula 1 team, with many sponsor slots and exclusive year-long deals per sector.
- His takeaways:
  - Treat the show and network as an ecosystem: long-form, short-form and newsletter are all ways of reaching people.
  - Treat it as a media company. Greatness shows up in the studio investment, the daily suits, the 5-days-a-week, 3-hours-a-day commitment.
  - Be clear about whom you serve and what you want out of it, then build a business around it.
- On why TBPN was valued by OpenAI: brand trust and credibility with a community, plus influence on perception. He says the hosts advise OpenAI's comms team after each show.
- Control of narrative is why companies launch their own podcasts. He cites a16z's new media arm and Substack's founders' show as examples of controlling narrative with long-form, high-production content. He contrasts that with a short TV hit where you risk being misunderstood.
- Brand and trust matter because they sustain a show: a luxury brand and a plain product can be nearly identical but valued very differently.
- He rejects reading TBPN's deal as "a podcast that got a deal". In his framing it was a strategic deal, different from Joe Rogan's or Alex Cooper's.

## Execution, AI, and why the creator still decides

- On using AI for content strategy: tools like ChatGPT or Claude can give ideas and direction, but "execution is all that matters".
- His examples: AI can give you a rocket recipe or YouTube ideas, but who actually follows through and commits to being the best at it? He says no original idea exists and the cliche is true for a reason.
- Why tech fans didn't get OpenAI-sized deals: they didn't execute the format or put their own taste in front of an audience.
- His advice: "Your show today shouldn't look like your show a year from now." Keep adjusting week by week, as MrBeast's early videos compared to today show.
- Use AI as a thinking partner: connect your data to understand what is growing and what works, then add your creative touch.
- Casey's own motive for his show: not to be the biggest show, but to learn from great founders, build relationships and create opportunity. He says some of his best opportunities, investments and capital raised for companies came from conversations that began on the show.

## Honesty with the audience in sponsorship

- Casey describes early podcast advertising: hosts got products to try before reading ads. His example is Casper mattresses: hosts were sent beds for a week, and some reported a back problem on air.
- That honesty built trust in podcast advertising and gave brands useful feedback over the long term, even if it hurt in the short term.
- He applies the same standard today: audiences can read between the lines, "People can tell when they're being misled," and creators must stay honest and transparent with sponsors and acquirers.
