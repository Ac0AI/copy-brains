## Casey's drive comes from a neck injury and a "things could be worse" mindset

- At 16, Casey was a sophomore athlete (hockey, lacrosse, football, about five foot six, a running back). On the first day of hitting practice he landed on his neck during a "B drill." The doctor said it was like a guillotine to his spine.
- He wore a neck brace for six months and was told he could probably never play football again. His identity, daily routine and commitments disappeared, and he spent a stretch depressed and anxious.
- He frames this as the pivot of his life. The free time led him to social media and business content, and he says he could easily have become resentful instead.
- He grew up in a small Virginia town. He did not fly until age 16, and his parents were far from entrepreneurial (his father spent 30 years at Philip Morris, his mother is a special ed teacher). His original plan was college and a job.
- His standing reminder: "things could always be so much worse." He says that perspective, along with Robert Greene's ideas about death, sits at the top of his mind daily. His response is to make something of the day, the week and the year.

## How Casey turns negative events into identity instead of victimhood

- He argues that significant events, good or bad, become part of identity. People can attach purpose to a bad event or let it define their limits.
- He asks what question you ask yourself afterward: "Who am I now that this event has occurred in my life?" Questions tied to growth and change lead somewhere different than self-pity.
- He says to also mark positive milestones deliberately: the first customer, the day you started the company, the first client. Attaching meaning to those cements identity and gives something to build on.
- Momentum cuts both ways. A 5k, then a 10k, then a half marathon makes a marathon plausible, while circumstances you let define you stop you.
- He keeps the neck brace from that period turned into a trophy, with a plaque reading "Keep going, never quit."

## Starting the podcast with no plan and building on momentum

- Casey started his show at 17 in 2017, first called "Rise of the Young." He was inspired by the last chapter (on podcasting) of Gary Vaynerchuk's book *Crushing It*.
- His mentality was "let me just try it." He had no mic, only wired Apple headphones, no launch strategy and no perfect title. The first episode was a roughly three-minute audio recording made while walking around his bedroom.
- He had already run a Facebook advertising agency and done social media marketing for local businesses.
- He booked a 5 to 10 minute meeting with Gary Vee by DMing him on Instagram about four months out. He then set a goal of 60 interviews in 90 days so he would have something to talk about with Gary.
- He started with friends (his former business partner Kieran, Jeff from Jr Garage) and did Facebook Live interviews, releasing one to three a week.
- He caveats that in 2023 you do need to find a way to stand out, but that should not stop you from starting and building momentum.
- Preparation was the point: the interviews put him in position so that when he sat across from Gary, he knew what he was doing.

## What the podcast produced: relationships, learning and business opportunities

- He says the show gave him three things: meeting like-minded people (he cites Dale Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence People*), lifelong learning from people who have done it (he skipped college and moved to Arizona about a month after graduating), and business opportunities that followed.
- He has done over 400 episodes. Guests include Larry King, Rick Ross, Elon Musk's mom and sister, and the founders of Netflix and Twitch.
- In 2021 he raised $1.5 million before launching Media Kits, a real-time data and analytics product for creators. He says about 80 to 90 percent of the money came from past podcast guests, and he had roughly 250 guests by then.
- He says he did not interview people to pitch them. He had worked with large creators and agencies that build media kits, and the relationships were already there when he had an idea to share.
- He sees podcast depth as the opposite of what short-form rewards: "the ability to connect on a much deeper level."
- His current company is Listener FM; he previously built and sold Media Kits.

## What holds people back from creating content, and the "next step" fix

- Main blocker: comparison. Feeds show others' success, and people either try to be someone else or measure themselves against where they think they should be.
- He is fine with learning from others ("a good artist copies, great artists steal"), but the issue is comparing yourself instead of looking at the cards you are dealt.
- A common trap is "if only I had started earlier," which he calls a heavy thought that holds you back more than you realize. He asks instead what one thing you can do today.
- Minimum step examples: share a thought on a tweet, make a 15-second TikTok, talk to one customer, reach out to one person.
- He recommends using social media to get inspired and connect rather than to compare, and being aware of how your feed makes you feel.
- Scale the ask down: building an Amazon is incomprehensible, but selling one book today is doable.

## The "will this next step kill me?" lesson from Iron Cowboy James Lawrence

- Casey interviewed James Lawrence, who did 50 Ironman triathlons in 50 days in 50 states and later 101 Ironmans in 101 days, much of it on a fractured leg.
- Casey asked him for advice for when Casey would be deep in his first marathon (December), listening around miles 20 to 21.
- James called it "The Hurt Locker" and told him to focus on the next step and ask whether it will kill him. If not, keep moving.
- Casey applies this to 75 Hard, where day 29 feels different from day 1, but stacked small wins give proof of effort. He concludes: "today is all you have," so focus on winning the day.

## 75 Hard as a mental reset

- Casey has completed Andy Frisella's 75 Hard twice, the second time finishing in May 2023, and was invited onto Andy's show *Real AF* in St. Louis afterward.
- Rules: a gallon of water a day, two 45-minute workouts (one outdoors), read 10 pages, follow a diet with no alcohol and no cheat meals, and take a daily progress photo.
- He sees it as a mental toughness program rather than a fitness one, and says the small daily wins build discipline.
- He plans to do it once a year for the rest of his life as "a mental reset" because business days are fast-paced, and the outdoor time forces him to be intentional.

## Larry King: "Greatness is driven"

- Casey interviewed Larry King in 2019, when Larry was 87 and Casey was 19. Larry had done about 60,000 interviews and interviewed every living president of his era.
- Asked what makes people great, Larry answered that greatness is driven, and that those with drive who simply don't quit are the ones who succeed.
- Casey's takeaway: those who keep going through adversity, including a failed business that they move on from without letting it define them, are the ones you hear about, and the others are not.
- He says the weight of the lesson comes from who said it and his intentionality, and he applies it to the gym, relationships, business and perseverance in general.
