## Origin story: injury, social media, and the podcast as a foundation

- Casey grew up in a small town in Virginia as an athlete, with two older brothers. A football injury in sophomore year of high school ended his playing career and left him in a neck brace for six months.
- During recovery he found Tony Robbins and Gary Vaynerchuk on social media and went "all in" on making money online, building a brand, and meeting people he could learn from.
- He launched the Casey Adams Show in 2017, when he was 16 or 17, and has hosted it for about six and a half years. It has produced 400+ interviews, including Larry King, Rick Ross, and founders of Twitch and Netflix.
- He calls the podcast "the best foundation for a career" for a teenager. It connected him with people he could learn from, kept relationships alive, and generated business opportunities.
- He skipped college, graduated high school, and moved across the country at 18.
- The show was originally called "Rise of the Young." He renamed it the Casey Adams Show because it felt more long-term and personal-brand focused.

## Relationship building: listening, value-first, no "stay in touch" agenda

- A lesson from Larry King shapes how Casey approaches relationships. King's line: "I've never learned anything while I was listening." Casey tries to be a listener first.
- He interviews the person, not just the business: who they are, what they do, and the hardships they deal with.
- He argues podcasting "expedites relationships." Sitting down for 45 to 60 minutes (ideally in person) goes much deeper than a typical first meeting.
- He never treats staying in touch as a goal. He asks how he can be valuable to the guest, for example by trying their product, beta testing, giving feedback, or buying what they sell.
- Example: he had David Sacks on when Sacks ran Kin.com (a social podcasting app), then hosted 30 to 40 live shows on the platform.
- Dale Carnegie's "How to Win Friends and Influence People" shaped his approach from age 17.
- His principle: "no matter what business you're in, you're in the people business."

## Turning podcast relationships into a funded company

- His last company, MediaKits.com, was a software tool for creators to aggregate cross-platform social data in one place. It was acquired by ViralNation in 2022.
- MediaKits raised a round from 37 angel investors, and about 80 to 90% of them were past guests on his podcast.
- He says this wasn't the plan. Years of interviewing investors, creator economy operators, and media builders meant he had trusted people to ask for feedback and validation.
- Because he had never asked them for anything, he could say "I'm starting this new business, would love a couple minutes of your time" and get real feedback.
- He had talked to guests' teams and assistants, so intros and conversations came naturally.
- It doesn't have to be a podcast. He earlier hosted events for a media company, which similarly made him a connector people knew, liked, and trusted.
- People he has a rapport with are more likely to take his call than a cold email, though he says cold outreach is great too.

## Getting guests: cold DMs, the Rick Ross blueprint, and reps

- In 2017-2018 he had no network and a non-entrepreneurial family, so everything was cold DMs. Most guests still come from cold DMs on Instagram, Twitter/X, or LinkedIn.
- His blueprint is to make it the easiest possible yes:
  - Reference someone the guest likes (Rick Ross admired Robert Greene, whom Casey had just interviewed).
  - Tie it to something they care about now (Ross's upcoming book).
  - Ask for a short, specific window ("all we need is 20 to 25 minutes").
- Ross later told him on air, in effect: you presented yourself like a boss, you didn't come with your hand out, you came with a specific time frame, and my time is valuable.
- He reached out to Rick Ross about 15 times, resending the message, before getting a yes. His line: "You miss 100% of the shots you don't take."
- He frames it as asking what's in it for the guest and how he can serve them, a question that also applies to customers, audience, and partners.
- Advice for beginners: focus on reps, not big names. He says he was "terrible" around interview 13, and that getting better as a host matters more early than landing marquee guests.
- He also advises taking yourself seriously. As a young creator he did not feel he went unrespected, because he treated his own work seriously, and he says that translates.

## Listener.com: a vertically integrated podcast network

- Casey launched Listener.com about six months before this interview, after the MediaKits acquisition. He bought the listen.com domain because it represented the brand he wanted.
- It has three components:
  1. **Services:** getting CEOs and corporate leaders booked on top business podcasts. This is the bread and butter, driven mostly by inbound and referrals.
  2. **Software:** Listener FM, an AI post-production tool where podcasters upload audio or video and get SEO-optimized titles, descriptions, show notes, and blog posts. It is used internally and has a customer base.
  3. **Media/network:** the Casey Adams Show, a second show called Questions (hosted with DJ Swivel and Brandon Jenner), and a podcasting newsletter launched about five to six weeks earlier.
- The media thesis: to be for podcasting what Barstool was for sports media. The newsletter bridges the business of podcasting (acquisitions, investments) with culture (Cristiano Ronaldo on the Whoop podcast, LeBron's Uninterrupted).
- He sees a gap in dedicated podcast media and names Sounds Profitable as a brand he respects.
- It is bootstrapped, with no outside capital raised, and run step by step. The service side is the current focus, and social-first content around the newsletter is underway on Instagram.
- He describes the pieces as synergistic: guests become clients, then newsletter participants, then users of the software.

## Podcast industry view and monetization

- He says podcasting has matured. The 2020-2021 wave of investment (Spotify acquisitions and exclusives) has played out, and ad revenue keeps growing.
- Six years ago a poor-audio episode with no marketing could reach the top 200. Now quality is the baseline: strong audio, video for social clips, and polished production.
- His first 50 episodes were not even on video. He now says people should focus strongly on quality.
- He argues the medium is a better channel for some conversations than articles (Colin and Samir interviewing the YouTube CEO).
- Podcasting is a slow, consistency-driven medium unlike social. "You could have the biggest celebrities on for 10 episodes, but if you don't put out an 11th, it's not going to matter." Most shows stop at about 10 episodes.
- On brand deals, he did a Slack campaign in 2022 that went beyond ad reads. He hosted a LinkedIn Live panel on Slack's page for Small Business Saturday with four small business owners. His point is that hosts can package their skills creatively.
- PR firms and agencies are a hidden gem for both sides:
  - PR firms send him batches of strong guests.
  - Agencies working with brands (the Slack deal came through one) can open several brand opportunities from one conversation.
- Chris Williamson is his example of partnering well with aligned brands.

## Positioning a niche show beyond CPM

- When asked how a niche show sells against download counts and CPM, his answer is brand over data: "Why do people pay $7 for a Starbucks coffee and $3 for another?"
- At MediaKits he saw three creators with identical data command dramatically different rates, depending on who they are and what they mean to their community.
- Treat the podcast as a vertical of a personal brand. Sell integrated deals (podcast, social campaigns, stories, reels, live events) rather than only per-download ads.
- Stand out among millions of podcasts by being a specific human with a specific audience, and aim for long-term partnerships and client work rather than one-off ads.
- He expects human hosts to stay valuable even as brands launch AI-voiced podcasts.
- He notes that podcasting influences business results: a guest, the CEO of Momentous, discussed how a partnership with Huberman Lab impacted that company.

## Distribution, long-term commitment, and personal operating principles

- Instagram is his main lever. His approach is a long-form show plus 3 to 8 short-form pieces per episode, posted to Instagram, TikTok, and Reels, with more focus on LinkedIn this year.
- His simple view: "no one's going to listen to your show unless you put it out there," and trailers must be engaging. He cites Steven Bartlett's team (a tightly cut first minute of each episode) as the playbook to study, including split testing and team structure, and admits his own approach is still being refined.
- On a 10-year view, he says people overestimate what they can do in a year and underestimate what they can do in ten. He expects to still host his show and run Listener unless it is acquired.
- He says he commits long-term: the podcast since 16-17, a six-year plan to run all six major marathons (New York in November 2024, then Boston, Chicago, Tokyo, Berlin, London) by age 30.
- Success factors he names: adaptability to new technology, focus and commitment, openness to feedback, and learning something every day.
- He credits mentors and partners: Aristotle Loumis (met through Steven Galanis of Cameo) as his key mentor on dealmaking and raising capital, and his fiancée Jaclyn Brunett for pushing him to improve how he shows up.
