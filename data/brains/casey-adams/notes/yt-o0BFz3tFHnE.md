## Starting at 17: injury, pivot, and the podcast as a business engine

- At 16 a football neck injury (inner spinous ligament damage) ended his sports identity; he spent about 6 months in a neck brace, which he calls the "white space" that forced him to find a new path.
- He went from social media rabbit holes (Tony Robbins, Gary Vaynerchuk) to running a Facebook ad agency at 16, serving his dentist, chiropractor and local sports shop, then retainer clients.
- He had no entrepreneurial family background (mom a special ed teacher, dad at Philip Morris), though his dad ran a side-hustle asphalt business.
- He started his podcast December 22, 2017, at 17, with a three-point checklist: "meet great people, learn from them, and create business opportunities."
- Before the podcast his content was book reviews on Instagram and Snapchat stories: he didn't pretend to be an expert, he brought people along as he learned.
- Senior year he was making about $15K/month on average, including a $15K/month client who became a partner, and he dropped out of the college path (he had planned lacrosse at UVA) after taking only three classes.
- His advice to young people on skeptical family: "it's the actions and results" that make the conversation easier.

## Why the podcast is never judged on direct revenue

- He says the show was never required to make money; if it meets the three checklist items, he'll keep doing it "forever" because it is "foundational to my career."
- The payoff came indirectly: his media kits company raised $1.5 million in 2021 when he was 20, from 37 angel investors, and about 90% of them were past podcast guests.
- He built a live events company in Scottsdale (about October 2018) as an extension of the show, inviting past guests like Drama (Chris Pfaff) to speak with no budget.
- That led to two co-hosted LA events with Drama's Young and Reckless brand in 2019, where he met Robert Greene, who later led to Rick Ross.
- Takeaway he draws: one relationship (guest, then speaker, then partner) compounds into the next opportunity over years.

## Booking big guests: the cold outreach framework

- Fearlessness came from being the youngest of three brothers, playing with kids six years older: he wasn't afraid of rejection or a "no."
- Gary Vaynerchuk's book *Crushing It* (last chapter on podcasting, cold DMs) prompted the approach.
- First big guest was Drama (Rob Dyrdek's cousin from Rob & Big): he DM'd after Drama posted a book, saying he'd just started his podcast and done 20 interviews; Drama replied in about 10 minutes.
- Gary Vee: he sent a DM with a photo shoot of himself wearing Gary's K-Swiss sneaker; that led to a 15-minute in-person meeting in New York months later, and he used the wait to do as many interviews as possible so he had something to show.
- Rick Ross, two years after Drama: his message was short, referenced a mutual friend, respected time, and tied to something Ross cared about: "I recently had your friend Robert Green on my podcast. It would be such an honor to have you on my show for 15 minutes to talk about your new book."
- The framework: short message, social proof via someone they know, a specific small time ask (15-30 minutes), and a hook tied to what they're promoting right now (book, movie, charity).
- Most pitches get nothing: "I've probably had more nos or messages that didn't respond than I've ever had received," with hundreds of non-responses behind the guests people know.
- Timing matters: big names are on press tours for something specific, so know "why now."
- His assistant now sends outreach proactively to people he'd like to talk to; he believes the more personal the message, the better.

## Listening as a principle

- He credits Larry King (his 2019 interview was reportedly King's last long-form interview) with the line: "I've never learned anything while I was talking. Therefore, be a great listener."
- This shaped his show style (no scripted questions, lean on curiosity) and the company name Listener.
- He applies it to outreach too: learn what someone cares about before asking.

## Listener.com: product thesis and positioning

- Positioning: "AI listener analytics for modern podcasts and podcast networks," a single hub aggregating Apple, Spotify, social analytics and newsletter data.
- Core feature: a shareable public profile page (listener.com/casey) that works like DocSend: viewers enter name, email and company, and the host manually approves access, so the host controls who sees their data.
- Aim: let hosts pitch sponsors with confidence and "tell a story with your data"; two shows with 100,000 monthly downloads can differentiate on audience and impact.
- Extra use case he's noticed: public-figure guests' teams ask for listener counts and cross-platform reach before agreeing to appear.
- Origin: a separate AI podcast product, listener.fm, was a 2024 "testing year" (alongside a podcast network and services); an acqui-hire of a small podcast tech tool and a shift in Q3/Q4 2024 turned into listener.com.
- He bought the .com after the .fm product validated the idea; the starting price was over $250,000 and he got it well below that after about 10 months stuck at the $100-150K range. He didn't wait for the .com to launch.
- Funding: no formal round; a handful of strategic investors, including big podcasters and his former co-founder/board member.
- He sold media kits in 2022 to a Toronto company, then did brand partnerships and consulting before returning to tech.
