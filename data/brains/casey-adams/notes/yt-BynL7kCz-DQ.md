## Casey's view of how leaders build culture and trust

- Casey argues culture "is not about perks," it is about how a company handles and solves hard problems. He cites Ben Horowitz's *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* as the source of the idea.
- Degreed has roughly 10 operating values covering how people think, operate, treat each other, and balance work and relationships. He says those values earn their keep when applied to hard cases:
  - customer demands that would pull the company off its product roadmap
  - good or bad financial news
  - a market downturn and a possible reduction in force
- Treating people with dignity and respect in those moments shapes culture more than anything else.
- Every person added to or removed from a team shifts the culture. Hiring "the best people wrapped around a core set of values" matters more than adding controls.
- He prefers finding the right fit over forcing someone into an existing model.
- Degreed is virtual-first and hires the best people regardless of location. It was recently named one of the top 100 most flexible companies to work for in the US.

## The biggest betrayal a leader can commit

- Casey says the worst betrayal is breaking trust and confidence, not someone leaving or a role changing. He sees departures as normal business: "I don't take it personally. If I were to leave, I wouldn't want them to."
- A team member may admit "I'm actually not very good at these two things" out of vulnerability. A leader can use that to build a development plan and give mentoring and experience.
- Betrayal is repeating that confidence to others, or using it in a performance review to downgrade the person in comp. Casey says: "the biggest betrayal you can do is to create that environment and then take that trust and then put it somewhere else."
- An environment of trust creates a highly productive team. A leader's job is to build on people's vulnerabilities and lift them up.
- He endorses the principle from Jocko Willink and Leif Babin's *Extreme Ownership*: "there's no bad teams, only bad leaders." If people aren't lifted up, "it's on you."
- He wants to be "someone that people want to work for and work with" and sees his role as serving his people and making their jobs easier.

## Hiring: missionaries first, then specialists

- Early hiring at Degreed was around mission-driven people who believed in the mission to "jailbreak the degree" and make skills matter, giving people the path to own their careers.
- Later hiring shifted to filling specific roles and skills, while trying to keep the passion.
- Casey names these sources of hires:
  - customers, who bring industry and vertical knowledge and a customer perspective (he stresses "we're not poaching")
  - people from commoditized learning management system vendors who love the space but want something more innovative
- He notes there are over 400 learning management system providers that are "basically a commodity." Degreed gives those people a chance to work on something that isn't.

## Market categories: take the revenue, don't let the box define you

- The market labeled Degreed a "learning experience platform," a category it created but, Casey says, "never wanted to be."
- He sees real benefits in accepting the label:
  - You can make money.
  - The market understands what you do.
  - You stop fighting educational battles on many fronts.
- The risk is complacency. "To do that in an innovative tech space is a surefire way to become irrelevant."
- He credits leadership with not losing sight of the original goal and finding "the courage and sort of the right moment" to break out of the category. The category credibility now lets them push the market into new places.
- He admits Degreed "probably could have held on to who we wanted to be a little better."

## Educating the market, then re-educating it

- Around 2016-2017, Degreed had to teach the market that skills, not degrees, titles and prerequisites, are what make up a person. Companies thought in ladders; he describes the reality as lattice moves, where a skill set like marketing and demand work might make someone a great product designer or engineering leader.
- Once "everyone in the space just talks about skills," the word became "almost meaningless." Early on no one wanted to listen; today no one does because everyone claims it.
- The second round of education shifts to execution: how to compile and normalize skills data and use it for business impact (internal mobility, retention, faster time to market).
- He frames this as a head start. Degreed has been doing the hard data work for a long time.

## Selling learning to business leaders

- Learning has often been an afterthought or compliance, and it is hard to measure. It is the first thing dropped when budgets or tech get consolidated.
- Casey's approach is to stop selling only into the learning team. Sell to the business line leaders and executives who own the goals.
- The pitch runs like this:
  - a company has a goal, such as "a 2027 goal of being here"
  - the goal cascades into gaps in talent and capacity (his example is AI, a space the company has never built teams for)
  - if those reskilling and upskilling gaps aren't closed, "the greatest objectives for the business can never be attained"
- The value proposition is closing business gaps, not better content or leadership programs.
- Go-to-market is a standard enterprise mix: events, marketing, outbound sales, and original research on how the workforce learns.
- He reminds himself that the playbook is often "more of the same, then better," not a shiny new thing.

## Managing distractions and priorities as a product company

- Casey says customers will offer to "write you a check to do other things that will distract you." Degreed refused to build its own LMS despite customers asking, because it isn't the company's DNA and why be "401" among 400 providers.
- Some distractions can be positive. AI was a "helpful distraction" or "detour" that can shorten the time to the north star, through curated content, less time for instructional designers, and adaptive coaches.
- Which obstacles to say no to and which to say yes to is the core decision. Each yes creates new obstacles, so plan for them.
- Regulated customers like financial institutions can be averse to AI, which brings back the education problem.
- His example of build-versus-buy: voice agents progressed so fast that it makes little sense to build your own when you can use APIs from businesses that did.
- His "magic wand" wish is for a tech stack with interoperability, so tech debt stops competing with innovation for resources. That makes the company more responsive to customers and new ideas.
