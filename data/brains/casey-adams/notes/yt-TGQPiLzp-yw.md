## How Casey got into podcasting and why it became his career foundation

- Casey launched the Casey Adams Show in late 2017, at 17, from his bedroom in a small Virginia town. He had been an athlete (hockey for roughly 10-15 years, then lacrosse, then football), and a football injury put him in a neck brace for nearly 6 months.
- He picked podcasting after reading the last chapter of Gary Vee's *Crushing It*, which was about podcasting.
- His goal was clear from day one: "meet great entrepreneurs, learn from them, create business opportunity."
- The first six months were about 50-60 interviews, built by cold pitching anyone he could reach on social media. A few unexpected yeses gave him the confidence to keep showing up.
- Within two years he was interviewing Larry King. Over eight years he has interviewed 500+ founders and leaders and still releases roughly an episode a week.
- He credits that clarity of purpose for the show's longevity.

## Podcasting as a relationship engine: raising over $1M from past guests

- Casey moved from Virginia to Arizona right out of high school, skipped college, and started Media Kits in 2020 with a co-founder. The product let creators aggregate cross-platform social data and present it to brands.
- They raised just over $1 million from 37 angel investors, and pretty much all of them were past podcast guests.
- He says his intention was never to interview people in order to raise money from them. Years of building high-quality relationships meant that when he had a company to raise for, he already knew some of the smartest people in his network.
- His approach was to share what he was building, ask for thoughts and advice, and then offer the chance to invest.
- Takeaway he draws: podcasting "really is what you make it," and the less traditional ways to build, grow and monetize a show are often where the value is.
- Media Kits was acquired in 2022 by Viral Nation, a Toronto company.

## Why Casey built Listener: data fragmentation "beyond the download"

- After the Media Kits exit he wanted to build in podcasting. He had watched podcast tech for years (Riverside, Anchor, Spreaker) as well as the roll-ups and acquisitions.
- His pitch for Listener is an AI-powered listener analytics platform for the modern podcast and podcast network, tying together audio, content and commerce integrations for sales enablement and episode reporting.
- Core insight: podcast data is more fragmented than creator data was. People care about and track different metrics on different platforms.
- Hosting platforms don't track social views tied to episodes, even though Spotify and others tell podcasters to post clips on social to grow. Clips live on different platforms and mean different things, with no easy way to attribute them to an episode's growth.
- His view of the "modern podcast" is audio plus video, live events, private communities and merchandise, so a show is effectively a multimedia company.
- The market gap: the money that came in around 2020 for roll-ups has played out, and he sees an opening for a tool built for "podcast 2.0 or 3.0."
- Listener launched in May 2025 and, as he put it, "the lack of analytics to track and manage everything in the space is real."

## Selling sponsors on multi-platform campaigns

- His largest brand deals (Slack, Adobe) were vertically integrated, not just host-read ads. They included social clips, segments built to become clips, and newsletter blasts for specific episodes.
- He says those multi-platform campaigns led to the larger deals and to more successful campaigns for both sides.
- His conclusion: podcasting should be treated as "social first, video first," and measured that way.
- He did a six-month campaign with Slack that was tightly associated with his brand.

## How Listener uses AI: episode clusters and "conversation with your data"

- **Episode cluster reports:** when an episode is published via RSS, Listener scans the connected social integrations. It pulls the long-form YouTube video and the associated Shorts, Reels, Facebook reels and TikTok clips, and attributes them to the episode.
- The data refreshes every 24 hours, so a report shows growth for the episode's downloads plus its cross-platform clips.
- Episode Clusters 2.0 automates what used to be manual clip-to-episode linking. The AI suggests, for example, 15-20 clips using transcription data and sentiment analysis, and the user approves or denies.
- He frames the workflow as "inbox zero for your podcast" and accepts it won't be 100% accurate, which is why thumbs up/down review exists.
- **Listener AI (insights):** a ChatGPT-style interface scoped to the network's own data. Example questions: top-performing social platform, which platform is growing most over 3 months, views and downloads in April vs May.
- Core customer today: podcast networks managing 5 to 50 shows.
- His view of where AI fits: pulling insights and speeding up workflow, not replacing hosts.

## How a network workflow breaks down and where the small-team advantage is

- He describes a network or production company's work in four steps: produce, edit, publish, then track and manage content. Listener sits at the fourth step, reporting.
- Listener's team is five people, and his thesis is that AI coding platforms let a team of five feel like a team of 15.
- On speed: "if you spend 6 months building something, the reality is in 6 months things will be a lot better, be a lot different." He argues that being lean and shipping quickly to get product feedback on AI features is the main advantage against larger companies with podcast-tech acquisitions.
- On risk: the biggest risk isn't how you implement AI but "being unaware of the true capability and the impact of what is happening right now." He calls himself a "delusional optimist" who leans into the upside without being naive about the downside.
- He names a line for podcasting: he would not want to interview an AI version of a guest, and wouldn't want a world where more people listen to AI-narrated podcasts than to real people. He mentions Wondercraft (AI-generated host-read ads) and Delphi AI as examples of tools worth understanding.

## How brands should use podcasting

Casey splits brand involvement into three plays:

1. **Start a show.** Own your narrative. Variants include a simple one-person show, or hiring influencer talent to host and bring in guests.
2. **Advertise on shows.** Build long-term relationships, as BetterHelp and AG1 have done, not just one-off episode sponsorships.
3. **Get executives and founders booked as guests** on other shows to go direct to consumer and borrow their audiences. He cites the way major executives and politicians use appearances like Rogan's. He says he tries to bring value to executives by helping them get on other shows.

His hot take: brands should build internal podcast networks, not just produce their own shows. Example: a project management app could convince outside marketing or productivity podcasters to join its network, as HubSpot (HubSpot Podcast Network) and Morning Brew have done. He predicts that over the next 5 to 10 years many tech companies, large and small, will want such networks.

## Why the company is named Listener

- He's proud of the one-word domain, listener.com, and says the name reflects the thesis that everything in the industry "comes back down to the listener": advertisers want to reach listeners, and shows create content for them.
- The name came from his Larry King interview, where King said: "I've never learned anything while I was talking. Therefore, be a great listener."
- Product implication: the platform is meant to meet listeners where they are, across social, live events and private communities such as Patreon, and he sees the next era as reframing what a podcast is, with long-form in audio or video.
